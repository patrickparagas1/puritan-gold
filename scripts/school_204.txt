Knowing God — His Attributes


Welcome, young scholars. Today we gather in the tradition of the great Puritan academies, where every subject was studied under the lordship of Christ. No knowledge is secular when it is received from the hand of God.

Our Father, the Puritans prayed before every lesson, and so do we. We ask for Your blessing on today's studies. Let the truths we learn take root in our hearts and produce fruit in our lives. We study not for our own glory, but for Yours. Amen.

Let us still our hearts with the Word of God before we proceed.

Our Scripture for today is Psalm 89:14:

"Justice and judgment are the habitation of thy throne: mercy and truth shall go before thy face."

As you begin this day, consider the perfect justice of God. He has never once been unfair. He has never overlooked a wrong or failed to uphold what is right. His throne rests on justice and judgment. And yet, marvel of marvels, this perfectly just God has found a way to forgive your every sin without compromising His righteousness — through the blood of His Son shed at Calvary. Because of Christ, you stand before the Judge of all the earth not as a condemned criminal but as a justified saint. Let this truth fill you with both reverence and gratitude. Walk today in the light of His justice, seeking to be fair, honest, and upright in all your dealings, knowing that the God who sees all things judges all things rightly.

As we prepare for today's lesson, remember that everything we study flows from this one fountain: the Word of God. Our lesson today is part of Knowing God — His Attributes, and it is titled: God's Justice & Our Obedience. Let us proceed with minds engaged and hearts open.


Having laid the foundation in prayer and Scripture, let us build upon it.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes in Psalm 89:14.

The justice of God is that attribute by which He is infinitely righteous in His own nature and perfectly fair in all His dealings with His creatures. Psalm 89:14 provides a majestic description of the divine throne: "Justice and judgment are the habitation of thy throne: mercy and truth shall go before thy face." Notice that justice and judgment are not occasional activities of God but the very foundation — the "habitation" — of His throne. Everything God does proceeds from and rests upon perfect righteousness. His throne is not built on power alone, nor on love alone, but on justice — which means that His power is always exercised justly and His love is always expressed righteously.

The pairing of attributes in this verse is deeply instructive. Justice and judgment form the foundation; mercy and truth go before His face as attendants or heralds. This tells us that God's mercy never operates at the expense of His justice, and His truth never contradicts His compassion. In the economy of God's character, all attributes work in perfect harmony. This is what distinguishes the God of Scripture from every false god invented by human imagination. Pagan gods were either just but not merciful, or merciful but not just. Only the true God is both — perfectly, simultaneously, and without compromise.

Deuteronomy 32:4 gives us one of the most comprehensive statements of divine justice: "He is the Rock, his work is perfect: for all his ways are judgment: a God of truth and without iniquity, just and right is he." Moses calls God "the Rock" — a metaphor for stability, reliability, and unchanging strength. God's justice is not arbitrary or capricious; it is as solid and dependable as a rock. "His work is perfect" — there is no flaw, no error, no injustice in anything God has ever done or will ever do. "All his ways are judgment" — not some of His ways, but all of them. Every action, every decision, every decree of God is characterized by perfect justice. He is "without iniquity" — there is no crookedness, no bias, no partiality in Him. He is "just and right" — two words that together convey the comprehensive righteousness of His character.

Romans 3:23-26 takes us to the very heart of how God's justice and mercy are reconciled at the cross: "For all have sinned, and come short of the glory of God; Being justified freely by his grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus: Whom God hath set forth to be a propitiation through faith in his blood, to declare his righteousness for the remission of sins that are past, through the forbearance of God; To declare, I say, at this time his righteousness: that he might be just, and the justifier of him which believeth in Jesus." This passage is the Magna Carta of the gospel. The problem is stated clearly: all have sinned. The solution is stated clearly: justification by grace through redemption in Christ. But notice why God set forth Christ as a propitiation — "to declare his righteousness." God's justice demanded that sin be punished. God's love desired that sinners be saved. At the cross, both demands were met. Christ bore the just penalty for sin, satisfying divine justice; and through faith in His blood, sinners are freely justified, receiving divine mercy. Thus God is both "just" — because sin has been fully punished — "and the justifier" — because sinners who believe are fully forgiven.

This is the most important truth in all of theology: the cross is not merely an expression of love but a satisfaction of justice. If God had simply forgiven sin without punishment, He would have been merciful but not just. If God had simply punished sin without providing salvation, He would have been just but not merciful. But at the cross, justice and mercy meet, truth and grace embrace, and God is glorified in being simultaneously the righteous Judge and the gracious Savior.

Nahum 1:3 reveals the seriousness of God's justice: "The LORD is slow to anger, and great in power, and will not at all acquit the wicked." God's slowness to anger is not weakness or indifference — it is patience, giving sinners time and opportunity to repent. But His patience has a limit. He "will not at all acquit the wicked." The guilty will not go unpunished. Every sin will be dealt with — either at the cross of Christ, where believers find pardon through the blood of the Lamb, or at the final judgment, where unbelievers face the full weight of divine justice. There is no third option. Every human being will experience God's justice either in mercy (through Christ) or in wrath (at the judgment). This is why the gospel is so urgent and so precious.

The justice of God also governs His dealings with His own people. Hebrews 12:6 tells us, "For whom the Lord loveth he chasteneth, and scourgeth every son whom he receiveth." God's discipline of believers is an expression of His justice combined with His fatherly love. He will not overlook sin in His children any more than in the world. But His purpose in disciplining believers is not condemnation but correction, not destruction but sanctification. As Psalm 119:75 confesses, "I know, O LORD, that thy judgments are right, and that thou in faithfulness hast afflicted me." God's afflictions upon His people are acts of faithfulness — He cares too much about our holiness to leave us in our sin.

The practical implications of God's justice for the believer are profound. First, it establishes a foundation for moral living. Because God is just, right and wrong are not merely human conventions but reflections of the divine character. Second, it produces a healthy fear of sin. If God will not acquit the wicked, then sin is a far more serious matter than the world suggests. Third, it magnifies the gospel. The more we understand God's justice, the more we treasure Christ's sacrifice. Fourth, it assures us that God will right every wrong. In a world full of injustice, oppression, and cruelty, the justice of God guarantees that nothing will ultimately be overlooked. Every wrong will be addressed. Every victim will be vindicated. Every oppressor will give account. This is the hope of the righteous and the terror of the wicked.

This biblical foundation is essential. Without it, everything else we study today would be like a house built on sand. But with it, we stand on solid rock.


Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. Thomas Watson wrote extensively on this subject in "A Body of Divinity." The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life.

Thomas Watson treated the justice of God with both theological precision and pastoral warmth in his "Body of Divinity." Watson understood that the justice of God is not merely an attribute to be studied academically but a reality that should profoundly affect the way believers think, live, and worship. He began by defining God's justice as "that perfection of the divine nature whereby He is infinitely righteous in Himself and in all His dealings with His creatures." Watson emphasized that God's justice is not something imposed upon Him from outside but an expression of His very nature. God does not conform to an external standard of justice; He is the standard. What is just is just because it accords with the character of God.

Watson made a crucial distinction between God's remunerative justice and His punitive justice. Remunerative justice refers to God's fairness in rewarding those who obey Him. Watson pointed to texts such as Hebrews 6:10: "For God is not unrighteous to forget your work and labour of love, which ye have shewed toward his name." God takes notice of every act of obedience, every prayer offered in faith, every sacrifice made for His sake, and He will reward them — not because the believer has earned anything, but because God in His justice has promised to reward those who seek Him. Punitive justice refers to God's righteous punishment of sin. Watson wrote that because God is infinitely holy, He cannot look upon sin with indifference. Sin is an offense against the infinite majesty of God, and therefore it deserves infinite punishment. This is why hell is eternal — not because God is cruel, but because sin against an infinite Being warrants an infinite penalty.

Watson devoted considerable attention to explaining why the cross was necessary from the standpoint of divine justice. He argued that God could not simply overlook sin without denying His own nature. If a human judge released a convicted murderer without any penalty, we would rightly call that judge corrupt. How much more would it be unjust for the perfectly righteous Judge of all the earth to let sin go unpunished? Watson wrote: "If God should acquit the sinner without satisfaction being made to justice, this would make God unrighteous. The judge of all the earth must do right." The cross, therefore, was not an arbitrary decision but a divine necessity. God's justice required punishment; God's love provided a substitute. In Christ, both are perfectly satisfied.

Watson was particularly compelling in his application of God's justice to daily life. He taught that the justice of God should produce in the believer a deep respect for God's law. If God is just, then His commandments are not arbitrary rules but expressions of His righteous character. To obey God's law is to align oneself with the moral order of the universe. To disobey is to set oneself against the very foundation of reality. Watson urged believers to cultivate what he called "a tender conscience" — a sensitivity to sin that springs from an awareness of God's justice. He wrote: "The Christian who truly understands God's justice will tremble at the thought of offending such a God, not merely from fear of punishment but from reverence for His holiness."

Watson also applied the justice of God to the problem of suffering. He observed that many people question God's justice when they see the righteous suffer and the wicked prosper. But Watson counseled patience and faith. He pointed to Psalm 73, where Asaph was troubled by the prosperity of the wicked until he went into the sanctuary of God and understood their end. Watson taught that God's justice operates on an eternal timetable, not a temporal one. The wicked may prosper for a season, but their day of reckoning is certain. The righteous may suffer for a time, but their vindication is assured. Watson wrote: "Do not judge God's justice by a short chapter of providence. Wait until you have read the whole book."

Stephen Charnock complemented Watson's teaching by exploring the philosophical and theological dimensions of divine justice. Charnock argued that justice is essential to God in a way that makes it impossible for Him to act unjustly. Just as it is impossible for a circle to be square (because squareness contradicts the nature of a circle), so it is impossible for God to be unjust (because injustice contradicts the nature of God). This is not a limitation on God but a perfection. God cannot lie, cannot sin, and cannot act unjustly — not because He lacks power but because His nature is infinitely perfect.

Charnock also explored the relationship between God's justice and His mercy in the work of redemption. He taught that at the cross, these two attributes do not compromise with each other but are both fully satisfied. Justice receives full payment for sin in the death of Christ. Mercy extends full pardon to sinners who believe in Christ. Neither attribute is diminished; both are glorified. Charnock wrote that the cross is "the highest display of justice and the richest display of mercy in the history of the universe." In no other event do we see more clearly who God is — a God of infinite holiness who cannot overlook sin, and a God of infinite love who provides, at infinite cost to Himself, a way of salvation for sinners.

The Puritan emphasis on God's justice served as a corrective to two dangerous errors. On one hand, it corrected the error of presumption — the idea that God is so loving that He will overlook sin without punishment. Watson and Charnock insisted that God's love never cancels His justice; rather, the two work together at the cross. On the other hand, it corrected the error of despair — the idea that God is so just that sinners can never be saved. The Puritans proclaimed with joyful confidence that God has made full provision for the satisfaction of His justice in the person and work of His Son. The sinner who comes to Christ in faith finds that justice has already been satisfied on his behalf and that mercy flows freely to all who believe.

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.


True learning requires reflection. Let us discuss today's key ideas.

Discussion Question 1: Psalm 89:14 says that justice and judgment are the 'habitation' of God's throne. What does this metaphor tell us about the centrality of justice to God's character?

Discussion Question 2: How does Romans 3:23-26 explain the way God can be both 'just and the justifier' at the same time? Why is this the most important truth in the gospel?

Discussion Question 3: Watson distinguished between remunerative justice and punitive justice. Can you explain these two aspects of God's justice in your own words with biblical examples?

Discussion Question 4: How should God's justice affect the way we respond to injustice in the world? Does knowing that God will ultimately right every wrong change how we handle unfair situations?

Discussion Question 5: Watson said 'Do not judge God's justice by a short chapter of providence. Wait until you have read the whole book.' What does this mean for times when life seems unfair?

Take time with each question. The Puritans valued what they called "holy conference," the practice of discussing spiritual truths together. Do not settle for quick, surface answers. Push deeper. Ask follow-up questions. Listen carefully to one another.

Application is where truth becomes life. It is not enough to know what Psalm 89:14 says. We must ask: how does this change the way I live today? How does this shape my thoughts, my words, my actions? The Puritans insisted that a truth that does not transform is a truth that has not been truly understood.


Let us press deeper into the doctrine of divine justice by examining additional Scripture passages and the insights of other notable Puritan divines.

Consider the declaration of Abraham in Genesis 18:25 as he interceded for the city of Sodom: "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?" This question is not an expression of doubt but an affirmation of confidence. Abraham knew that whatever God decided to do with Sodom, it would be perfectly just. The title "Judge of all the earth" is one of the most significant titles given to God in Scripture. It means that God is the ultimate arbiter of right and wrong, the final court of appeal, the One before whom all creatures will eventually stand to give account. His judgments are not subject to review or reversal. There is no higher authority to which one can appeal. And because God is infinitely wise, infinitely holy, and infinitely good, His judgments are always perfectly right.

Psalm 97:2 adds, "Clouds and darkness are round about him: righteousness and judgment are the habitation of his throne." This verse acknowledges that God's ways are sometimes hidden from us — surrounded by "clouds and darkness" — but it simultaneously affirms that beneath those clouds, righteousness and judgment remain the unshakeable foundation of His government. We may not always understand what God is doing, but we can always trust that what He is doing is just. The obscurity of God's ways does not negate the righteousness of His character.

Isaiah 45:21 presents one of the most compressed and powerful statements of divine justice in all of Scripture: "Tell ye, and bring them near; yea, let them take counsel together: who hath declared this from ancient time? who hath told it from that time? have not I the LORD? and there is no God else beside me; a just God and a Saviour; there is none beside me." Notice the conjunction: God is both "just" and "a Saviour." He is not just without being a Savior, and He is not a Savior apart from His justice. The cross is the place where these two realities converge. At Calvary, God's justice was fully satisfied and His salvation was freely offered. This is the gospel in one verse: God is just — He does not ignore sin — and God is a Savior — He provides forgiveness for sinners through the substitutionary death of His Son.

The Puritan theologian John Owen (1616-1683) wrote one of the most significant works on divine justice and the atonement in his masterpiece "The Death of Death in the Death of Christ." Owen argued that Christ's death was a real, effectual, and complete satisfaction of divine justice on behalf of all for whom He died. Owen taught that when Christ hung upon the cross, He bore the exact penalty that God's justice demanded for the sins of His people. Not one sin was left unpunished, and not one demand of justice was left unsatisfied. Owen wrote that Christ "made a full, perfect, and sufficient satisfaction to the justice of God for all the sins of all the elect" and that this satisfaction was accepted by the Father as complete and final. This is why Paul could declare with such confidence in Romans 8:33-34: "Who shall lay any thing to the charge of God's elect? It is God that justifieth. Who is he that condemneth? It is Christ that died, yea rather, that is risen again, who is even at the right hand of God, who also maketh intercession for us."

Owen's emphasis on the completeness of Christ's satisfaction of divine justice has profound implications for the believer's assurance. If Christ has fully satisfied justice on my behalf, then no charge of guilt can ever be brought against me. The debt has been paid. The penalty has been endured. Justice has nothing further to demand from those who are in Christ Jesus. This is what theologians call "the double imputation": my sin was imputed to Christ at the cross, and His righteousness is imputed to me through faith. He bore what I deserved; I receive what He earned. Second Corinthians 5:21 states this with crystal clarity: "For he hath made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin; that we might be made the righteousness of God in him."

The Puritan pastor Thomas Brooks (1608-1680) applied the justice of God to the believer's daily battle against temptation in his famous work "Precious Remedies Against Satan's Devices." Brooks taught that one of the most powerful weapons against temptation is a clear understanding of God's justice. When a believer is tempted to sin, he should remember that every sin — no matter how small it seems — is an offense against the infinite justice of God. Brooks wrote that sin "is not a trifle; it is not an indifferent thing. It is a stab at the heart of God, a trampling upon the blood of Christ, and a grieving of the Holy Spirit." This sobering perspective helps the believer resist temptation, not merely out of fear of punishment but out of a holy reverence for the God whose justice sin offends.

Brooks also taught that the justice of God should produce in believers a deep compassion for the lost. If God will not acquit the wicked, and if every person who dies outside of Christ will face the full weight of divine justice in eternal punishment, then believers should be moved to tears and to action on behalf of those who are perishing. Ezekiel 33:11 records God's own heart: "Say unto them, As I live, saith the Lord GOD, I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked; but that the wicked turn from his way and live: turn ye, turn ye from your evil ways; for why will ye die, O house of Israel?" God takes no pleasure in the execution of justice upon sinners. He pleads with them to turn and live. And His people should share His heart, urgently proclaiming the gospel to a world that stands under the just condemnation of a holy God.

Consider also the teaching of Psalm 9:7-8: "But the LORD shall endure for ever: he hath prepared his throne for judgment. And he shall judge the world in righteousness, he shall minister judgment to the people in uprightness." This text looks forward to the final judgment, when God will judge every person who has ever lived. Acts 17:31 confirms this: "Because he hath appointed a day, in the which he will judge the world in righteousness by that man whom he hath ordained; whereof he hath given assurance unto all men, in that he hath raised him from the dead." The final judgment will be conducted "in righteousness" — with perfect justice, perfect knowledge, and perfect impartiality. Nothing will be hidden. Nothing will be overlooked. Every secret thing will be brought to light (Ecclesiastes 12:14). And the Judge — the Lord Jesus Christ Himself — will render a verdict that is absolutely just for every soul.

The justice of God in the final judgment should comfort believers and sober unbelievers. For believers, it means that every wrong they have suffered will be addressed, every tear they have shed will be acknowledged, and every sacrifice they have made for Christ will be rewarded. For unbelievers, it means that the opportunity to receive mercy through faith in Christ will not last forever. There is a day appointed. The Judge is coming. Now is the time to flee to the cross, where justice has already been satisfied, and mercy waits with open arms.


Before we proceed to the afternoon activity, let us consider how the justice of God should shape our daily conduct and attitudes.

First, let the justice of God make you a person of integrity. Because God is just, He values honesty, fairness, and uprightness in all our dealings. Proverbs 11:1 declares, "A false balance is abomination to the LORD: but a just weight is his delight." In your schoolwork, in your business dealings, in your conversations, and in every interaction with others, let the justice of God be your standard. Do not cheat. Do not deceive. Do not take advantage of those who are weaker than you. Be a person whose word can be trusted, whose actions are fair, and whose character reflects the righteousness of the God you serve.

Second, let the justice of God govern how you treat others. The Puritans taught that because all human beings are made in the image of God, they are owed just treatment regardless of their social standing, race, or economic condition. James 2:1-4 warns against the sin of partiality: "My brethren, have not the faith of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Lord of glory, with respect of persons." Showing favoritism to the rich or powerful while neglecting the poor and insignificant is a violation of God's justice. Treat every person you encounter as someone created in the image of God and deserving of just and compassionate treatment.

Third, let the justice of God shape your response to the wrongs you suffer. Romans 12:19 instructs, "Dearly beloved, avenge not yourselves, but rather give place unto wrath: for it is written, Vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord." Because God is just, you do not need to take justice into your own hands. You can entrust every wrong you suffer to the perfectly just Judge who will, in His own time and in His own way, make all things right. This does not mean that you should never seek justice through proper channels — the legal system and church discipline are God-ordained means of addressing wrongs. But it does mean that you should never harbor a vengeful spirit, because vengeance belongs to God alone.

Fourth, let the justice of God produce in you a deep hatred of sin. The Puritans understood that sin is not merely a personal failing but an offense against the justice of God. When you see sin clearly — as an assault on divine righteousness, as a violation of God's holy law, as the very thing that nailed Christ to the cross — you will hate it with a holy hatred. Not the sinner, but the sin. Not with self-righteous contempt, but with the grieved recognition that every sin dishonors the God whose justice we have been studying.

Fifth, let the justice of God fill you with wonder at the gospel. The more deeply you understand how just God is, the more astonishing it becomes that He would save sinners at all. Justice demanded our punishment. Love provided a substitute. Wisdom devised the plan. Power executed it. And grace applied it to undeserving sinners like us. As the old hymn expresses, the cross is where "mercy and truth are met together; righteousness and peace have kissed each other" (Psalm 85:10). Stand before the cross today and marvel that the just God has justified you — not by ignoring your sin but by punishing it in His own beloved Son.


The afternoon brings us to our activity time. Let us engage our hands as well as our minds.

Students will conduct a 'Justice and Mercy at the Cross' analysis. Working individually or in small groups, students will create a two-column chart on poster paper. The left column is titled 'The Demands of Justice' and the right column 'The Provision of Mercy.' Students will look up and write out key Scripture passages that describe each: passages about the penalty of sin, the wrath of God, and the necessity of punishment on the justice side; passages about God's love, Christ's sacrifice, and the offer of forgiveness on the mercy side. In the center between the two columns, students will draw or describe the cross, showing how it is the meeting point where justice and mercy converge. Each group will present their chart to the class, explaining how the cross satisfies both attributes simultaneously.

Materials needed: Large poster paper, markers in multiple colors, Bibles, concordance or Bible app for looking up references, ruler for creating columns

As you complete this activity, think about how it connects to what you studied in today's lesson. The goal is not just to do something, but to reinforce the truths you have been learning. The Puritans believed that education should engage the whole person: mind, heart, and hands.

Take your time with this activity. Quality matters more than speed. When you are finished, reflect on what you learned through the process of doing. Often we understand something far better after we have tried to put it into practice.


One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

The concept of justice has shaped legal systems throughout history. Study the development of law from the Code of Hammurabi (c. 1750 BC) through Roman law to the English common law tradition. Observe how biblical principles of justice — particularly the idea that law must be impartial and that punishment should be proportionate to the offense — influenced the development of Western legal systems. Discuss how the Puritans' understanding of divine justice shaped their approach to civil government in colonial New England.

Literature Connection:

Justice is one of the great themes of world literature. In Victor Hugo's 'Les Miserables,' the tension between justice and mercy is embodied in the characters of Javert and the Bishop. In Shakespeare's 'The Merchant of Venice,' Portia's famous speech on mercy argues that mercy must temper justice. Compare these literary treatments with the biblical teaching that at the cross, justice is not tempered but fully satisfied, and mercy flows not at the expense of justice but because of its satisfaction.

Science Connection:

The concept of natural law — the idea that the universe operates according to fixed, reliable principles — has its roots in the biblical teaching on God's justice and faithfulness. The scientific method depends on the assumption that nature behaves consistently and predictably. Discuss how this assumption is grounded in the character of a just and orderly God. The laws of cause and effect, action and reaction, and conservation of energy all reflect a universe governed by a God of perfect justice and order.

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.


Let us now explore the doctrine of God's justice as it relates to the final judgment, the vindication of the righteous, and the ultimate triumph of divine righteousness over all evil. The Puritans regarded the doctrine of the last judgment as one of the most solemn and practically important teachings in all of Scripture.

The certainty of the final judgment is established by numerous passages of Scripture. Acts 17:31 declares, "Because he hath appointed a day, in the which he will judge the world in righteousness by that man whom he hath ordained; whereof he hath given assurance unto all men, in that he hath raised him from the dead." God has appointed a day — a specific, fixed, unalterable day in the divine calendar — when He will judge the entire world. The judge will be the Lord Jesus Christ, the God-man whom the Father has ordained for this purpose. And the guarantee of this future judgment is the resurrection of Christ from the dead. Because Christ rose, the judgment is certain. Because the tomb is empty, the courtroom will be full.

Revelation 20:11-12 provides the most detailed description of the final judgment in all of Scripture: "And I saw a great white throne, and him that sat on it, from whose face the earth and the heaven fled away; and there was found no place for them. And I saw the dead, small and great, stand before God; and the books were opened: and another book was opened, which is the book of life: and the dead were judged out of those things which were written in the books, according to their works." The throne is great — signifying the supreme authority of the judge. The throne is white — signifying the perfect purity and righteousness of the judgment. Every person who has ever lived will stand before this throne. None will be excused, none will be overlooked, and none will escape. The books of works will be opened, revealing every deed, every word, and every thought. And the book of life will be opened, revealing who belongs to Christ by faith.

Ecclesiastes 12:14 confirms the comprehensiveness of this judgment: "For God shall bring every work into judgment, with every secret thing, whether it be good, or whether it be evil." Every secret thing. The sins committed in darkness, the thoughts harbored in the hidden chambers of the heart, the motives that no human eye could discern — all will be brought into the light of God's perfect justice. Jesus Himself warned, "For there is nothing covered, that shall not be revealed; neither hid, that shall not be known" (Luke 12:2). The Puritans took these warnings with the utmost seriousness. They lived with what they called "the eye of God upon them" — the constant awareness that every moment of their lives was observed by the Judge of all the earth, before whom they would one day give account.

The Puritan pastor Thomas Manton (1620-1677) devoted extensive commentary to the doctrine of the final judgment in his exposition of Jude. Manton taught that the judgment should produce three effects in the believer's life. First, it should produce sobriety. The knowledge that we will give account for every idle word (Matthew 12:36) should make us serious and deliberate in our speech, our conduct, and our use of time. Second, it should produce comfort. For the believer who is in Christ, the judgment holds no terror — because Christ has already borne the penalty for all their sins, and they will stand before the Judge clothed in the perfect righteousness of their Savior. Romans 8:1 assures us, "There is therefore now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus, who walk not after the flesh, but after the Spirit." Third, it should produce urgency in evangelism. If those who die outside of Christ face eternal condemnation, then sharing the gospel is the most important task in the world.

The justice of God is also displayed in His providential governance of history. The Puritans observed that while justice is not always immediately visible in the events of this world — the wicked often prosper and the righteous often suffer — God's justice operates on an eternal timeline. Psalm 37:1-2 counsels, "Fret not thyself because of evildoers, neither be thou envious against the workers of iniquity. For they shall soon be cut down like the grass, and wither as the green herb." The prosperity of the wicked is temporary; the vindication of the righteous is eternal. David continues in Psalm 37:25, "I have been young, and now am old; yet have I not seen the righteous forsaken, nor his seed begging bread." Over the course of a lifetime, David had observed the faithfulness of God's justice. The righteous are never ultimately forsaken, and the wicked never ultimately triumph.

The Puritan divine William Perkins (1558-1602), the great Cambridge theologian who shaped the entire Puritan movement, wrote extensively on the justice of God in relation to the doctrine of predestination. Perkins taught that God's justice is perfectly displayed in both the salvation of the elect and the condemnation of the reprobate. In saving the elect, God demonstrates His justice by punishing their sins in Christ — not one sin goes unpunished, but the punishment falls on the Substitute rather than on the sinner. In condemning the reprobate, God demonstrates His justice by punishing them for their own sins — sins they freely chose to commit and for which they are fully responsible. In neither case is God unjust. In both cases, His justice is perfectly satisfied.

Perkins was also careful to address the common objection that God's justice seems unfair because some are chosen for salvation while others are not. Perkins responded by pointing out that justice and mercy are different attributes with different operations. Justice gives people what they deserve; mercy gives people what they do not deserve. No one deserves mercy — if they did, it would not be mercy but obligation. God is under no obligation to save anyone. The fact that He saves any is a display of mercy; the fact that He condemns some is a display of justice. Both are fully consistent with His perfect character. As Paul argues in Romans 9:22-23, God bears with "vessels of wrath fitted to destruction" in order to make known "the riches of his glory on the vessels of mercy, which he had afore prepared unto glory."

Consider the justice of God as displayed in the cross of Christ from the perspective of Psalm 85:10: "Mercy and truth are met together; righteousness and peace have kissed each other." This verse, which the Puritans frequently cited in their discussions of the atonement, describes the cross as the meeting place of God's seemingly conflicting attributes. At the cross, mercy and truth — which might seem to pull in opposite directions — meet together in perfect harmony. Righteousness and peace — which might seem irreconcilable when dealing with guilty sinners — kiss each other in an embrace of divine satisfaction. The cross is not a compromise between justice and mercy; it is the full and simultaneous expression of both. Justice is fully satisfied because sin is fully punished. Mercy is fully expressed because sinners are fully forgiven. This is the glory of the gospel, and it is the supreme display of God's justice in all of history.

The prophet Isaiah captures the same truth in Isaiah 53:5-6: "But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed. All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned every one to his own way; and the LORD hath laid on him the iniquity of us all." The chastisement that brings us peace was laid upon Christ. The stripes that bring us healing were inflicted upon the innocent Lamb of God. God "laid on him the iniquity of us all" — every sin, every transgression, every rebellion was transferred to the sinless Son of God, and He bore its full penalty in His body on the tree. This is substitutionary atonement, and it is the answer to the question: How can a just God forgive sinners without compromising His justice?

Practical Application — Living in Light of God's Justice:

1. Live each day with the awareness that you will give account to God for how you spent it. The Puritans called this "living coram Deo" — living before the face of God. Every word you speak, every action you take, every thought you think is observed by the perfectly just Judge. Let this awareness produce not paralyzing fear but reverent diligence. Use your time wisely, your words carefully, and your talents faithfully, knowing that "every one of us shall give account of himself to God" (Romans 14:12).

2. When you witness injustice in the world, comfort yourself with the certainty that God will right every wrong. The Puritans lived in an age of great injustice — they were ejected from their pulpits, imprisoned for their beliefs, and fined for worshipping according to their conscience. Yet they endured with patience because they knew that the Judge of all the earth would do right (Genesis 18:25). Entrust every injustice you suffer to the hands of the righteous Judge, and leave vengeance to Him (Romans 12:19).

3. Marvel daily at the gospel — that the just God has justified you through the death of His Son. Never lose your wonder at the cross. Never take for granted the staggering reality that the Judge Himself became the prisoner, the Lawgiver bore the penalty, and the Holy One was made sin so that you might be made the righteousness of God in Him (2 Corinthians 5:21).

4. Let the justice of God govern all your dealings with others. Be scrupulously honest in your business. Be fair in your assessments of people. Be impartial in your treatment of the rich and the poor, the powerful and the weak. Leviticus 19:15 commands, "Ye shall do no unrighteousness in judgment: thou shalt not respect the person of the poor, nor honour the person of the mighty: but in righteousness shalt thou judge thy neighbour."

5. Share the gospel with urgency, knowing that those who die outside of Christ will face the full weight of God's justice. The love of Christ constrains us (2 Corinthians 5:14) to warn the perishing and to point them to the only Savior who can deliver them from the wrath to come. Every day that passes without a person hearing the gospel is a day of infinite loss. Be faithful in proclaiming the message that justice has been satisfied at the cross and that mercy is freely offered to all who believe.
