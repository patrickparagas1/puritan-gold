PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Pastoral Encouragement, Day 23
"The Rare Jewel of Christian Contentment"
Jeremiah Burroughs — Pastoral Counsel on Contentment


Welcome, friend. There are nights when what we need most is not more information but more encouragement. Tonight is one of those nights. Let the Lord speak directly to your heart.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Philippians 4:11-13:

"Not that I speak in respect of want: for I have learned, in whatsoever state I am, therewith to be content. I know both how to be abased, and I know how to abound: every where and in all things I am instructed both to be full and to be hungry, both to abound and to suffer need. I can do all things through Christ which strengtheneth me."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


Contentment is one of the most elusive and most necessary virtues in the Christian life. The apostle Paul says he has 'learned' it — the word implies a process, a discipline, a lesson that does not come naturally. Contentment is not the natural disposition of the human heart; it is a supernatural grace that must be cultivated through faith and experience.

Jeremiah Burroughs, in his classic work 'The Rare Jewel of Christian Contentment,' provides the most thorough and practical treatment of this virtue in all of Puritan literature. Burroughs was a pastor who knew both abundance and deprivation. He was ejected from his pulpit for nonconformity and spent years in difficult circumstances. His teaching on contentment was forged in personal experience, not academic speculation.

Burroughs defines contentment with memorable precision. It is, he writes, 'that sweet, inward, quiet, gracious frame of spirit, which freely submits to and delights in God's wise and fatherly disposal in every condition.' Notice the key elements: contentment is sweet, not bitter; inward, not merely outward; quiet, not restless; gracious, not natural; and it involves both submission and delight. The contented soul does not merely endure God's providence; it embraces it.

Burroughs identifies several characteristics of true contentment. First, contentment is an inward work. It does not depend upon outward circumstances. The man who is content only when things are going well is not truly content; he is merely comfortable. True contentment operates independently of circumstances — the contented soul is at peace whether abounding or abased, full or hungry, prospering or suffering.

Second, contentment does not mean passivity. Burroughs is careful to distinguish contentment from fatalism or laziness. The contented man is not the man who does nothing because he accepts everything. He works diligently, pursues lawful goals, and uses proper means to improve his situation. But he does all of this without anxiety, without envy, and without the desperate grasping that characterizes the discontented heart.

Third, Burroughs teaches that contentment is learned, not achieved overnight. Paul says 'I have learned' — past tense, implying a process. Contentment grows through trials, through the experience of God's faithfulness in difficulties, through the repeated exercise of faith in God's sovereign goodness. The man who has never been tested has never had the opportunity to learn contentment.

Burroughs also identifies the roots of discontent. The primary root is a disproportionate valuation of earthly things. The man who treasures the gifts more than the Giver will always be discontented, because earthly gifts are by nature unstable and insufficient. Only God can fill the soul, and the soul that is filled with God will not be destabilized by the loss of anything else.

Another root of discontent is comparison. The man who compares his lot with that of others will always find someone who has more, and that comparison breeds envy, resentment, and ingratitude. Burroughs counsels believers to compare their condition not with others but with what they deserve: eternal judgment. Any blessing, however small, is infinitely more than a sinner deserves. The man who truly understands grace will be grateful for everything and resentful of nothing.

Patrick, discontent is one of the most insidious sins because it is so easily justified. We call it 'ambition' or 'drive' or 'wanting the best for our families.' But beneath the respectable exterior, discontent is a refusal to trust God's provision and a rejection of His wise disposal of our lives. Are you content? Not merely resigned, not merely suppressing your frustration, but genuinely at peace with God's fatherly disposal of your circumstances? If not, Burroughs would counsel you to look not at your circumstances but at your God — and in Him to find the sufficiency that no earthly condition can provide.


Before we close, take a few moments with these reflection questions.

Question 1: Am I genuinely content with my current circumstances, or am I merely tolerating them while longing for something different?

Question 2: Which of Burroughs's roots of discontent — overvaluing earthly things or comparing myself with others — is most active in my own heart?

Question 3: What would it look like for me to cultivate a 'sweet, inward, quiet, gracious frame of spirit' in the specific circumstances I face today?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Father, I confess that my heart is often restless and discontented. I compare my lot with others, I overvalue earthly comforts, and I fail to rest in Your wise and fatherly disposal of my life. Teach me the rare jewel of contentment. Help me to find in You a sufficiency that does not depend on my circumstances. I want to be able to say with Paul: 'I have learned, in whatsoever state I am, therewith to be content.' Through Christ who strengthens me, Amen.

Until next time. May the Lord deepen your faith with every passing day.
