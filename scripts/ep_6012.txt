PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"Unto Thee Will I Cry, O Lord My Rock"
June 12, 2026

---

"Unto thee will I cry, O Lord my rock; be not silent to me: lest, if thou be silent to me, I become like them that go down into the pit. Hear the voice of my supplications, when I cry unto thee, when I lift up my hands toward thy holy oracle. Draw me not away with the wicked, and with the workers of iniquity, which speak peace to their neighbours, but mischief is in their hearts." -- Psalm 28:1-3

David addresses the Lord as his rock, and in that single metaphor he lays the foundation of everything that follows. A rock is stable when all else is shifting. A rock is strong when all else is weak. A rock is permanent when all else is passing away. And so the Lord is to the believing soul: the one fixed point in a world of flux, the one sure foundation in a universe where every other ground of confidence is sinking sand. To call God "my rock" is to make a confession of faith that covers every circumstance of life, for whatever storms may rage and whatever floods may rise, the man who stands upon the rock shall not be moved.

"Be not silent to me: lest, if thou be silent to me, I become like them that go down into the pit." Here David reveals the deepest terror of the believing heart, which is not death, not suffering, not persecution, but the silence of God. A God who speaks, even in rebuke, is a God who is present. A God who answers, even with a denial, is a God who cares. But a God who is silent -- this is the ultimate desolation. For the silence of God seems to say that He has turned His back, that He has ceased to regard, that the cry of the sufferer has risen no higher than the ceiling of the room in which it was uttered. And if God is truly silent, then the believer is no different from the dead, who lie in the pit beyond all hope and all help.

John Owen, in his meditations upon the spiritual life of the believer, wrote extensively about the difference between God's real presence and His felt presence. God is always present with His people, for He has promised never to leave them nor forsake them. But the sense of His presence may be withdrawn for wise and holy reasons, and when it is withdrawn, the soul experiences a darkness and a silence that can be more painful than any physical suffering. It is in these seasons that faith is most severely tested and most gloriously vindicated, for the man who continues to cry out to God when God seems silent is proving that his faith rests not upon feeling but upon the unshakeable promise of the covenant.

"Hear the voice of my supplications, when I cry unto thee, when I lift up my hands toward thy holy oracle." The lifting up of hands was a posture of prayer in ancient Israel, signifying both dependence and expectation. The empty hands stretched upward toward heaven are a picture of the soul that has nothing to offer God but its need, and nothing to hope for but His mercy. And the direction of the hands -- toward the holy oracle, the inner sanctuary of the temple where God had promised to meet with His people -- shows that David's prayer was not a vague and general cry sent out into the void, but a specific appeal directed to the God who had bound Himself by covenant to hear and answer the prayers of His people.

Spurgeon loved to preach upon the posture of prayer, and he taught that while no particular bodily position is essential to true worship, the attitude of the body often reflects and reinforces the attitude of the soul. The man who kneels in prayer is reminded of his humility before God. The man who lifts his hands is reminded of his dependence upon God. And the man who turns his face toward the sanctuary is reminded that God has appointed a specific place and a specific way in which He may be approached -- not through the inventions of human religion, but through the sacrifice and mediation that He Himself has ordained.

"Draw me not away with the wicked, and with the workers of iniquity, which speak peace to their neighbours, but mischief is in their hearts." David is not merely asking for deliverance from external enemies; he is asking for preservation from the moral and spiritual corruption that surrounds him. He is praying that God will not allow him to be swept along in the current of wickedness, to be carried away by the tide of iniquity that seems to be pulling everyone else down. He sees the wicked all around him, speaking peace to their neighbours with smooth and friendly words while harbouring malice and mischief in their hearts, and he trembles at the thought that he might become like them.

Thomas Brooks wrote with great earnestness upon the danger of being conformed to the world, and he warned that this conformity does not happen suddenly but gradually, by imperceptible degrees, as the constant dripping of water wears away the hardest stone. A man does not wake up one morning and decide to become a hypocrite. He drifts into hypocrisy by small and seemingly harmless compromises, each one taking him a little further from the truth, until at last he finds himself speaking peace with his lips while harbouring mischief in his heart, and he cannot remember when the transformation began.

William Gurnall likewise taught that the prayer for preservation is one of the most important prayers a Christian can pray. We are weak, and we dwell in the midst of strong temptations. The current of the world flows against us with relentless force, and without the sustaining hand of God, we would be carried away in a moment. Therefore we must cry daily, with David, "Draw me not away with the wicked." Keep me, Lord. Hold me. Preserve me. Let me not be swept along by the flood of ungodliness that is carrying so many to destruction. For Thou art my rock, and in Thee I put my trust. Amen.
