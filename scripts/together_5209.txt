PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 9
"Be Angry and Sin Not"
Ephesians 4:26-27 — Communication and Holy Living

---

Good evening, dear husband and wife. Tonight we come to a passage that speaks directly to one of the most common and most destructive forces in marriage: unresolved anger. Every couple experiences anger. The question is not whether anger will visit your home, but what you will do when it arrives.

Let us read our passage. Ephesians 4:26-27 (KJV):

"Be ye angry, and sin not: let not the sun go down upon your wrath: Neither give place to the devil."

--- Teaching ---

This passage contains a remarkable concession: be angry. Paul does not say that anger is always sinful. There is a righteous anger that responds to injustice, that grieves over sin, that burns against what is evil. Even our Lord Jesus was angry when He cleansed the temple. Anger itself is not the problem. What we do with our anger is what determines whether it becomes sin.

Paul gives two boundaries for anger. First, be angry and sin not. This means that our anger must be expressed in a manner that does not violate the law of God. We do not curse. We do not demean. We do not throw things, slam doors, or use our bodies to intimidate. We do not assassinate our spouse's character. We do not retreat into punishing silence that is designed to wound. Sinful anger seeks to destroy. Righteous anger, properly expressed, seeks to restore.

Second, let not the sun go down upon your wrath. This is one of the most practical commands in all of Scripture for married couples. Do not go to sleep angry. Do not let the wound fester overnight. Do not let the sun set on an unresolved conflict if it is within your power to resolve it. The reason is given immediately: "Neither give place to the devil." Unresolved anger is an open door for the enemy. When you go to bed angry, you give Satan a foothold in your marriage. You allow bitterness to take root. You invite resentment to settle into the soil of your relationship, where it will grow into something far worse than the original offense.

William Gouge was deeply concerned about anger in the home. He observed that many couples who conducted themselves with perfect civility in public became tyrants behind closed doors. The home, which should be the safest place on earth, became a battlefield. Gouge urged husbands in particular to control their tempers, noting that a husband's anger carries a unique weight because of his position of authority. When a husband rages, the entire household trembles. Gouge wrote that the man who cannot govern his own spirit is unfit to govern his household.

But Gouge was equally direct with wives. He warned against the use of sharp, nagging, or contemptuous speech, which he regarded as a form of anger that is no less destructive for being quieter. A cutting remark, a roll of the eyes, a tone of contempt can wound as deeply as a raised voice. Both husband and wife must learn to express their anger in ways that honor God and preserve the dignity of their spouse.

Richard Baxter offered this counsel: when you feel anger rising, pause. Do not speak in the heat of the moment. Take a breath. Pray silently. Ask yourself: what is the true cause of my anger? Am I angry because of a genuine wrong, or am I angry because my pride has been wounded? Is this about something that truly matters, or am I making a mountain out of a molehill? Baxter said that most of the anger in marriage is disproportionate to its cause. We erupt over small things because we have not dealt with the large things beneath the surface.

Dear couple, make a covenant with each other tonight. Commit to the discipline of not letting the sun go down on your anger. This does not mean that every disagreement must be fully resolved before bedtime. Some issues are complex and require time. But you can at least acknowledge the conflict, affirm your love, and agree to continue the conversation with cool heads and warm hearts. Say to each other: "I am upset, but I love you. We will work through this together."

--- For Discussion ---

Question 1: How do each of us typically express anger? Is it in a way that honors God and respects our spouse?

Question 2: Have we allowed any unresolved anger to take root in our marriage? What needs to be addressed tonight?

Question 3: What practical steps can we take to obey the command not to let the sun go down on our wrath?

--- Prayer ---

Merciful God, we confess that we have not always handled our anger well. We have spoken words in haste that we cannot take back. We have let the sun go down on our wrath and given place to the devil in our marriage. Forgive us, Lord. Teach us to be angry without sinning. Give us the discipline to resolve our conflicts quickly and the humility to seek reconciliation before the day is done. Guard our marriage from the poison of bitterness. Fill us with the peace that passes understanding. Through Christ our Lord, Amen.
