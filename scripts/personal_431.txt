PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Guided Puritan Reading, Day 31
"Pressing On Toward the Prize"
John Owen — A Life of Mortification and Glory


Welcome to our guided reading. The book before us tonight has nourished Christians for centuries. We approach it not as critics but as students, not as experts but as hungry learners. Let us read together, slowly and prayerfully.

Tonight we draw from the work of John Owen, specifically from "The Mortification of Sin / The Glory of Christ." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is 2 Timothy 4:7-8:

"I have fought a good fight, I have finished my course, I have kept the faith: Henceforth there is laid up for me a crown of righteousness, which the Lord, the righteous judge, shall give me at that day: and not to me only, but unto all them also that love his appearing."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


As we come to the final day of this month's personal growth journey, we stand at the intersection of two great themes from John Owen's life and work: the mortification of sin and the glory of Christ. These are not two separate subjects; they are two sides of the same coin. The man who mortifies sin does so in order to see Christ more clearly. The man who gazes upon the glory of Christ finds his sin losing its power.

Owen's final work, published shortly before his death in 1683, was 'Meditations and Discourses on the Glory of Christ.' On his deathbed, when a friend came to tell him that his last book had been published, Owen reportedly said: 'I am glad to hear it; but, oh, the long-wished-for day is come at last, in which I shall see that glory in another manner than I have ever done, or was capable of doing, in this world.'

This is the destination of the mortifying life: the vision of Christ's glory. Owen's entire theology of sanctification is oriented toward this end. We kill sin not merely to be free from guilt but to be free for glory. We put off the old man not merely to avoid shame but to put on the new man who is created in the image of Christ. The fight against sin is ultimately a fight for the fullest possible enjoyment of Christ in this life and the next.

In 'The Glory of Christ,' Owen argues that meditation on Christ's glory is both the highest privilege and the most effective means of sanctification available to the believer. When the soul is occupied with the beauty of Christ — His person, His work, His offices, His love — there is simply less room for the allurements of sin. Owen writes: 'No man shall ever behold the glory of Christ by sight hereafter, who does not in some measure behold it by faith here in this world.'

Owen describes several dimensions of Christ's glory that the believer should meditate upon. The glory of His person: the eternal Son of God, the second Person of the Trinity, who existed in the form of God before all worlds. The glory of His incarnation: the mystery of God becoming man, of infinite condescension, of the Word made flesh. The glory of His obedience: the perfect life lived under the law, fulfilling every righteous requirement. The glory of His cross: the willing sacrifice, the bearing of sin, the satisfaction of divine justice. The glory of His resurrection: the triumph over death, the vindication of His claims, the firstfruits of the believer's own resurrection. The glory of His exaltation: seated at the right hand of the Father, ruling over all things, interceding for His people.

Owen writes that the man who regularly meditates on these glories will be progressively transformed by them. Paul teaches this in 2 Corinthians 3:18: 'But we all, with open face beholding as in a glass the glory of the Lord, are changed into the same image from glory to glory, even as by the Spirit of the Lord.' We become what we behold. The more we gaze upon Christ, the more we are conformed to His likeness.

This is the great secret that Owen spent his life discovering and commending: the fight against sin and the vision of glory are one fight. The man who is killing sin is the man who is seeing Christ. The man who is seeing Christ is the man in whom sin is dying. And the end of this dual movement is the day when sin is finally and forever destroyed, and the believer sees face to face the One who was always, from the beginning, the object of his desire.

Paul's words to Timothy serve as both a testimony and an invitation. Paul could say, at the end of his life: 'I have fought a good fight. I have finished my course. I have kept the faith.' This is the aspiration of every Christian man. Not to have lived a perfect life (Paul knew his imperfections better than anyone), but to have fought faithfully, to have finished the course, and to have kept the faith.

And the promise: 'Henceforth there is laid up for me a crown of righteousness.' This crown is not earned by merit but received by grace. It is the gift of 'the Lord, the righteous judge,' who gives it not only to Paul but 'unto all them also that love his appearing.'

Do you love His appearing? Then fight the good fight. Mortify sin by the Spirit's power. Fix your eyes on the glory of Christ. Press on toward the prize. And know that the One who called you will be faithful to bring you home.


Here are some questions to carry with you into the coming days.

Question 1: As I look back over this month of study, what has been the most significant change in my understanding of the Christian life?

Question 2: Am I actively gazing upon the glory of Christ as the primary means of growth and sanctification?

Question 3: Can I say, with honest aspiration if not yet with Paul's confidence: 'I am fighting the good fight, I am running the course, I am keeping the faith'?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Lord Jesus Christ, in whose glory all things find their meaning: I thank You for this month of growth. I have seen more deeply the seriousness of my sin and the surpassing greatness of Your grace. I press on toward the prize. Finish what You have started in me. Bring me at last to that day when I shall see Your glory face to face. Until then, keep me faithful. Amen.

Rest in His grace tonight. Tomorrow is a new day of growth.
