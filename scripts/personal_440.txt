PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Doctrine Deep-Dive, Day 9
"The Nature of the Church"
Ecclesiology Explored Through the Puritans


Welcome. Doctrine is not a dry, academic exercise. It is the scaffolding on which the entire Christian life is built. Tonight we examine a foundational truth that, rightly understood, will transform the way you worship, pray, and live.

Tonight we draw from the work of John Owen, specifically from "The True Nature of a Gospel Church." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Ephesians 1:22-23:

"And hath put all things under his feet, and gave him to be the head over all things to the church, Which is his body, the fulness of him that filleth all in all."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


What is the church? This question, seemingly simple, has generated centuries of controversy and confusion. Is the church a visible institution with hierarchical authority? Is it an invisible body known only to God? Is it a gathering of believers in a local congregation, or the totality of the redeemed across all ages? The Puritans, standing in the Reformed tradition, gave careful and nuanced answers to these questions, and their answers remain profoundly relevant today.

John Owen, in 'The True Nature of a Gospel Church,' provides the most thorough Puritan treatment of ecclesiology. Owen begins with a fundamental distinction that governs his entire discussion: the distinction between the church universal and the church local. The church universal is the whole body of the elect — all those who have been, are, or will be united to Christ by faith. This church is invisible in the sense that its boundaries are known perfectly only to God. No human institution can claim to be identical with the church universal; no organizational membership guarantees inclusion in it.

But Owen does not stop there. He insists with equal force that the church must also be visible and local. The New Testament knows nothing of a Christianity lived in isolation from the gathered assembly. Believers are commanded to meet together, to partake of the Lord's Supper together, to exercise discipline toward one another, to bear one another's burdens, and to use their gifts for the building up of the body. None of this can happen without concrete, local, visible congregations.

Owen identifies several essential marks of a true church. First, a true church is founded upon the Word of God. The Scriptures are the church's constitution, its rule of faith and practice. A church that departs from the authority of Scripture has forfeited its claim to be a church of Christ, however ancient or impressive its pedigree. Owen writes that the Word of God alone is sufficient to determine what the church believes, how it worships, how it is governed, and how it disciplines its members.

Second, a true church confesses the gospel. Owen is specific: the church exists to proclaim and guard the message that sinners are saved by grace alone, through faith alone, in Christ alone. A church that obscures, distorts, or denies this message has lost its essential character. Owen writes that the gospel is not merely one truth among many that the church teaches; it is the truth around which all other truths are organized. Remove the gospel and the church becomes a social club with religious decoration.

Third, Owen addresses the government of the church. He argues for a congregational form of church government, in which the authority of Christ is exercised through the gathered congregation rather than through a hierarchy of bishops or synods. Owen does not deny the legitimacy of pastors, elders, and deacons — he insists upon them — but he argues that their authority is derived from Christ through the congregation, not imposed upon the congregation from above.

Fourth, Owen addresses church discipline. A church without discipline, Owen writes, is like a garden without a fence: it will be overrun by every kind of weed. Discipline is not punitive but restorative. Its goal is not to destroy the offender but to bring him to repentance. And it is the duty of the whole congregation, not merely the officers. Owen laments that the neglect of church discipline is one of the chief causes of the church's weakness, for where sin is tolerated without correction, the entire body is infected.

The Puritan Richard Baxter adds a pastoral dimension to Owen's theological framework. In 'The Reformed Pastor,' Baxter pleads for churches that are communities of genuine spiritual care. Baxter envisions a church where every member is known personally by the pastor, where families are visited regularly, where catechesis is faithful and thorough, where the ordinances are administered with reverence, and where the sick, the struggling, and the backsliding are pursued with patient love.

The Puritan Thomas Watson adds yet another dimension by emphasizing the church as a foretaste of heaven. Watson writes that the fellowship of the saints on earth is a preview of the fellowship we will enjoy for eternity. When believers gather to worship, to pray, to encourage one another, to bear each other's burdens, they are participating in something that will continue, in perfected form, forever. The local church, with all its imperfections, is the closest thing to heaven that exists on earth. The man who despises the church despises the very community he will inhabit for eternity.

Patrick, how do you think about the church? Is it merely a place you attend on Sundays, or is it the body to which you belong, the community in which you are accountable, the family in which you serve and are served? The Puritans would remind you that there is no Christianity without the church. Christ loved the church and gave Himself for it. Can you claim to love Christ if you are indifferent to the community He purchased with His own blood?


Before we close, take a few moments with these reflection questions.

Question 1: Am I committed to a local church — not merely attending but actively participating, serving, and submitting to its fellowship?

Question 2: How does the Puritan emphasis on church discipline challenge my own tendency toward spiritual autonomy and independence?

Question 3: Do I view the church primarily as a place that serves my needs, or as a body to which I owe sacrificial love and service?

Question 4: What would it look like for me to invest more deeply in the life of my local congregation this month?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.

Deepen my commitment to the local body where You have placed me. Help me to serve, to love, to submit, and to build up my fellow members for Your glory. Amen.
