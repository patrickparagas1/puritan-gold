Good morning. Every day is a gift from God, and every lesson is an opportunity to see His glory more clearly. Welcome to the Puritan Academy. Let us open this day in prayer.

Father, we thank You for the privilege of education. Not everyone has the opportunity we have today. Help us to steward it well, to study with excellence, and to use what we learn in service of Your kingdom. In Jesus' name. Amen.

Let us begin with a time of prayerful reading, in the Puritan tradition.

Our Scripture for today is James 1:2-4:

"My brethren, count it all joy when ye fall into divers temptations; Knowing this, that the trying of your faith worketh patience. But let patience have her perfect work, that ye may be perfect and entire, wanting nothing."

If you are walking through a valley of suffering today, take heart. You are not alone. The Shepherd walks with you, His rod and staff guiding and comforting. The trial that weighs upon you is not meaningless — it is the refining fire of a wise and loving Father, purifying your faith and producing patience, character, and hope. Remember that this valley is not your final destination. It is a passage — a necessary passage — on the way to the Celestial City. The same Lord who walked through the valley of the shadow of death before you will walk through it with you now. Count it all joy, for the trying of your faith is producing something precious in you.

As we prepare for today's lesson, remember that everything we study flows from this one fountain: the Word of God. Our lesson today is part of The Pilgrim's Journey, and it is titled: The Valley of Humiliation — Suffering Well. Let us proceed with minds engaged and hearts open.


It is time for our main lesson. Give this your full attention.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes in James 1:2-4.

Suffering is one of the most universal and most perplexing aspects of the Christian experience. Every believer will face trials, hardships, and afflictions in this life. The question is not whether we will suffer but how we will respond to suffering. James 1:2-4 provides a startling instruction: "My brethren, count it all joy when ye fall into divers temptations; Knowing this, that the trying of your faith worketh patience. But let patience have her perfect work, that ye may be perfect and entire, wanting nothing."

The word "temptations" here is the Greek word "peirasmos," which encompasses both temptations to sin and trials of various kinds. James is addressing the full spectrum of difficulties that believers face — persecution, poverty, illness, bereavement, disappointment, and every other form of hardship. And his counsel is astonishing: "count it all joy." He does not say count it all joy when you feel joyful about your trials. He says count it — that is, reckon it, consider it, deliberately regard it — as joy. This is not a feeling but a decision, not an emotion but a judgment of faith. We are to look at our trials through the lens of God's purposes and conclude that they are reasons for rejoicing.

Why? Because "the trying of your faith worketh patience." The word "trying" refers to the testing or proving of faith, like the testing of metal in a furnace. Gold is purified by fire — the heat drives out impurities and leaves the gold more precious than before. In the same way, trials purify faith by exposing and removing the impurities of self-reliance, worldliness, and spiritual complacency. And the result is "patience" — the Greek word "hupomone," which means steadfast endurance, the ability to remain faithful under pressure without giving up.

Romans 5:3-5 presents the same truth with an extended chain of reasoning: "And not only so, but we glory in tribulations also: knowing that tribulation worketh patience; And patience, experience; and experience, hope: And hope maketh not ashamed; because the love of God is shed abroad in our hearts by the Holy Ghost which is given unto us." Here Paul traces the spiritual progression that flows from suffering rightly received. Tribulation produces patience (steadfast endurance). Patience produces experience (proven character — the Greek word "dokime" refers to something that has been tested and approved). Experience produces hope (confident expectation of God's faithfulness). And hope does not put us to shame because it is undergirded by the love of God poured into our hearts by the Holy Spirit. Suffering, when received in faith, produces a chain of spiritual growth that terminates in unshakeable hope.

First Peter 4:12-13 adds another dimension: "Beloved, think it not strange concerning the fiery trial which is to try you, as though some strange thing happened unto you: But rejoice, inasmuch as ye are partakers of Christ's sufferings; that, when his glory shall be revealed, ye may be glad also with exceeding joy." Peter tells us not to be surprised by suffering — it is not strange, unusual, or unexpected for Christians. Rather, suffering is the normal experience of those who follow a crucified Savior. Furthermore, our sufferings connect us to Christ. When we suffer for His sake, we become "partakers of Christ's sufferings" — we share in the fellowship of His pain, and we will share in the revelation of His glory.

Psalm 23:4 provides one of the most comforting images of suffering in all of Scripture: "Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil: for thou art with me; thy rod and thy staff they comfort me." The valley of the shadow of death represents the darkest, most frightening experiences of life. But the psalmist does not say "if I walk" but "though I walk" — it is a certainty, not a possibility. And he does not say "I will not enter the valley" but "I will fear no evil." The promise is not exemption from suffering but the presence of God in suffering. "Thou art with me" — this is the ultimate comfort. God does not send His people through the valley alone. He goes with them, guiding them with His rod and staff, providing protection and direction even in the darkest hours.

The book of Job provides the most extended biblical treatment of suffering. Job lost everything — his wealth, his children, his health — yet he declared, "The LORD gave, and the LORD hath taken away; blessed be the name of the LORD" (Job 1:21). Job's suffering was not punishment for sin but a sovereign permission for testing. God allowed Satan to afflict Job in order to demonstrate the reality of Job's faith and to bring Job into a deeper knowledge of God. At the end of the book, Job confesses, "I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear: but now mine eye seeth thee" (Job 42:5). Suffering had brought Job from secondhand knowledge of God to firsthand experience of God.

The ultimate example of suffering is Christ Himself. Isaiah 53:3 prophesied, "He is despised and rejected of men; a man of sorrows, and acquainted with grief." Hebrews 2:10 tells us that God made Christ "perfect through sufferings" — not that Christ had any moral imperfection, but that His human experience of suffering was necessary for His role as our High Priest. Because He suffered, He can sympathize with our weaknesses. Because He endured the cross, He can help those who are tempted. Hebrews 4:15-16 assures us: "For we have not an high priest which cannot be touched with the feeling of our infirmities; but was in all points tempted like as we are, yet without sin. Let us therefore come boldly unto the throne of grace, that we may obtain mercy, and find grace to help in time of need."

The proper response to suffering is neither stoic denial nor despairing complaint but faithful endurance rooted in the knowledge of God's sovereign purpose, God's loving presence, and God's promised outcome. As Romans 8:18 declares, "For I reckon that the sufferings of this present time are not worthy to be compared with the glory which shall be revealed in us.'

This biblical foundation is essential. Without it, everything else we study today would be like a house built on sand. But with it, we stand on solid rock.


Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. John Bunyan wrote extensively on this subject in "The Pilgrim's Progress." The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life.

John Bunyan's depiction of the Valley of Humiliation in "The Pilgrim's Progress" is one of the most theologically rich and pastorally sensitive sections of the entire allegory. After passing through the Interpreter's House and losing his burden at the cross, Christian must descend into the Valley of Humiliation — a low, dark place where he encounters his most fearsome enemy, the fiend Apollyon. This sequence illustrates a profound spiritual truth: the path to glory often leads through deep valleys of suffering, and some of the fiercest spiritual battles occur not in moments of obvious temptation but in seasons of humiliation, weakness, and apparent defeat.

Apollyon — whose name means "Destroyer" and is taken from Revelation 9:11 — confronts Christian with a threefold attack. First, he uses accusation, reminding Christian of his past sins and failures. Second, he uses discouragement, pointing to the hardships of the Christian path and suggesting that it would be easier to turn back. Third, he uses violence, throwing flaming darts at Christian and nearly overwhelming him. Bunyan drew these three strategies from his own experience and from his study of Scripture. The devil is called "the accuser of our brethren" (Revelation 12:10), the tempter who tries to make believers lose heart, and the adversary who "walketh about, seeking whom he may devour" (1 Peter 5:8).

Christian's victory over Apollyon comes through the Word of God. When all seems lost, Christian seizes "the sword of the Spirit, which is the word of God" (Ephesians 6:17) and strikes Apollyon with a decisive blow, declaring, "Rejoice not against me, O mine enemy: when I fall, I shall arise" (Micah 7:8). Apollyon flees, and Christian is healed by leaves from the Tree of Life. Bunyan here illustrates the great Puritan conviction that the Word of God is the believer's primary weapon against spiritual attack. When the devil accuses, we answer with the promises of God. When he discourages, we answer with the faithfulness of God. When he attacks, we answer with the truth of God.

But Bunyan does not allow the Valley of Humiliation to be entirely negative. In Part Two of "The Pilgrim's Progress," when Christiana and her companions pass through the same valley, their guide Great-heart gives a different perspective. He tells them that the Valley of Humiliation is actually one of the most fruitful and pleasant places on the journey — if one has the heart to appreciate it. Great-heart says that the Lord Jesus Himself once had His country house in this valley, and that He loved to walk in these meadows because the air was pleasant and the ground was fruitful. This remarkable reversal illustrates the Puritan understanding that humiliation and suffering, rightly received, are not curses but blessings. The valley that seems terrifying to the proud is actually a place of rich spiritual growth for the humble.

Bunyan's personal experience of suffering informed his writing profoundly. He spent twelve years in Bedford jail for the crime of preaching without a license. During this imprisonment, he was separated from his wife and children, including his blind daughter Mary, whom he loved with particular tenderness. Bunyan wrote in "Grace Abounding" that the thought of his family's suffering — especially Mary's — was like "the pulling of the flesh from my bones." Yet he refused to stop preaching, and he used his imprisonment to write the works that have blessed millions. Bunyan embodied the truth he depicted in his allegory: suffering is not the end of the journey but a necessary passage on the way to the Celestial City.

The Puritan approach to suffering was fundamentally different from the world's approach. The world sees suffering as meaningless misfortune to be avoided at all costs. The Puritans saw suffering as purposeful discipline to be received in faith. They drew this understanding directly from Scripture. Watson, in his treatise on affliction, taught that God uses suffering for at least four purposes. First, to wean believers from the world. When earthly comforts are removed, the soul learns to find its satisfaction in God alone. Second, to purify the believer's character. Just as fire purifies gold, affliction purifies faith by burning away the dross of worldliness, pride, and self-reliance. Third, to deepen the believer's prayer life. Watson observed that God's people pray more fervently and more frequently in times of affliction than in times of prosperity. Fourth, to prepare believers for greater usefulness. Those who have suffered and been comforted by God are uniquely equipped to comfort others in their suffering (2 Corinthians 1:3-4).

Bunyan also warned against two dangers in responding to suffering. The first is murmuring — complaining against God's providence and questioning His goodness. In "The Pilgrim's Progress," Christian passes through the Valley of the Shadow of Death, where he hears blasphemous whispers that he initially mistakes for his own thoughts. This illustrates how in seasons of suffering, the devil may inject ungodly thoughts into the believer's mind — thoughts of resentment, self-pity, or doubt — hoping to provoke complaint against God. The second danger is despair — giving up hope entirely and concluding that God has abandoned us. Bunyan addressed this danger most fully in the episode of Doubting Castle (which we will study in a later lesson), but even in the Valley of Humiliation, the temptation to despair is present.

The Puritan remedy for both murmuring and despair was the same: trust in the character of God and submission to His sovereign purposes. Bunyan and his fellow Puritans did not pretend that suffering was pleasant. They acknowledged its pain, its confusion, and its loneliness. But they insisted that behind every affliction stood a loving, wise, and sovereign God who was working all things together for the good of His people and the glory of His name. This is the faith that sustained Bunyan through twelve years of imprisonment and enabled him to produce the literary masterpiece that has guided countless pilgrims through their own valleys of humiliation.

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.

Discussion Question 2: In Pilgrim's Progress, Apollyon uses accusation, discouragement, and violence against Christian. Which of these three strategies do you think is most common in your own experience?

Discussion Question 3: Great-heart says that the Valley of Humiliation is actually one of the most fruitful places on the journey. What does this teach us about the potential benefits of suffering?

Discussion Question 5: Romans 5:3-5 traces a chain from tribulation to hope. Can you identify each link in the chain and explain how one leads to the next from your own experience or from Scripture?

Take time with each question. The Puritans valued what they called "holy conference," the practice of discussing spiritual truths together. Do not settle for quick, surface answers. Push deeper. Ask follow-up questions. Listen carefully to one another.

Application is where truth becomes life. It is not enough to know what James 1:2-4 says. We must ask: how does this change the way I live today? How does this shape my thoughts, my words, my actions? The Puritans insisted that a truth that does not transform is a truth that has not been truly understood.


Let us now explore additional dimensions of the biblical teaching on suffering and the insights of other Puritan divines who understood suffering not merely from books but from personal experience.

Consider the profound declaration of 2 Corinthians 4:16-18: "For which cause we faint not; but though our outward man perish, yet the inward man is renewed day by day. For our light affliction, which is but for a moment, worketh for us a far more exceeding and eternal weight of glory; While we look not at the things which are seen, but at the things which are not seen: for the things which are seen are temporal; but the things which are not seen are eternal." Paul, who had endured beatings, shipwrecks, stonings, imprisonments, hunger, and constant danger, calls his sufferings "our light affliction, which is but for a moment." This is not the statement of a man who has not suffered but of a man who has weighed his sufferings against the glory to come and found them comparatively light. The key is the contrast between temporal and eternal. When we view our sufferings through the lens of eternity, they shrink in proportion. When we view them through the lens of the present moment, they seem overwhelming. The Puritans understood this contrast and constantly urged believers to lift their eyes from the temporal to the eternal.

First Peter 1:6-7 adds a crucial dimension to our understanding of suffering: "Wherein ye greatly rejoice, though now for a season, if need be, ye are in heaviness through manifold temptations: That the trial of your faith, being much more precious than of gold that perisheth, though it be tried with fire, might be found unto praise and honour and glory at the appearing of Jesus Christ." Peter uses the phrase "if need be" — suggesting that God does not send suffering arbitrarily but only when it is necessary for our spiritual development. And the purpose of the trial is explicitly stated: that our faith, tested and refined by suffering, might be found "unto praise and honour and glory" at the appearing of Christ. The suffering is temporary; the glory is eternal. The fire is painful; the refined gold is precious beyond measure.

Psalm 119:71 provides a remarkable personal testimony: "It is good for me that I have been afflicted; that I might learn thy statutes." The psalmist does not merely endure affliction with patience — he declares it good. He recognizes that affliction has taught him things about God's Word that he could not have learned in any other way. The classroom of suffering is where some of the deepest lessons of faith are taught. The Puritans understood this principle and embraced it. They did not seek suffering, but when it came, they received it as a harsh but valuable teacher appointed by a wise and loving God.

The Puritan pastor Thomas Brooks (1608-1680) wrote "The Mute Christian Under the Smarting Rod," one of the most pastoral treatments of suffering in the entire Puritan corpus. Brooks urged believers who were suffering to silence their complaints and submit to God's sovereign purposes. He identified what he called "ten cords" that bind the suffering believer to patience. Among these were: the consideration that God never afflicts His people without just cause; the recognition that the suffering of this life is infinitely less than what our sins deserve; the knowledge that God's afflictions are always mixed with mercy; the assurance that affliction is a sign of God's fatherly love (for "whom the Lord loveth he chasteneth," Hebrews 12:6); and the confidence that every affliction has a divinely appointed end and will not last one moment longer than God's purposes require.

Brooks was especially effective in counseling believers not to judge God's love by their circumstances. He wrote: "God's love does not always ride on the fair weather of outward prosperity. Sometimes His dearest children are in the greatest afflictions." Brooks pointed to the example of Lazarus, whom Jesus loved (John 11:5) and yet whom He allowed to die. The disciples might have concluded that Jesus did not love Lazarus because He did not prevent his illness. But Jesus' love was demonstrated not in preventing the suffering but in using it for a greater purpose — the resurrection of Lazarus, which brought glory to God and strengthened the faith of many.

The Puritan divine Thomas Manton (1620-1677), one of the most prolific Puritan writers, devoted extensive commentary to James 1:2-4. Manton taught that the "divers temptations" mentioned by James include not only external trials such as persecution and poverty but also internal struggles such as spiritual dryness, besetting sins, and the warfare between flesh and spirit. Manton emphasized that every kind of trial, whether outward or inward, is under the sovereign control of God and serves His sanctifying purposes. He wrote: "God does not waste the sufferings of His people. Every tear is counted, every groan is heard, and every trial is measured to produce the precise degree of patience and maturity that God intends."

Manton also explored the concept of "patience" (hupomone) in James 1:3 with great precision. He distinguished between passive patience — the mere endurance of suffering without complaint — and active patience — the courageous, faith-filled endurance that continues to trust God, obey God, and serve God in the midst of suffering. Manton taught that James is calling for active patience — not the grim stoicism of the Stoic philosophers, who suppressed their emotions and endured suffering by sheer willpower, but the vibrant, hopeful endurance of the Christian, who suffers with eyes fixed on the promises of God and a heart sustained by the Holy Spirit. This active patience, Manton wrote, "is not the fruit of natural temperament but of supernatural grace."

Consider also the teaching of Hebrews 5:8, which says of Christ: "Though he were a Son, yet learned he obedience by the things which he suffered." Even the Son of God, in His human nature, learned obedience through suffering. This does not mean that Christ was ever disobedient, but that His obedience was tested, proven, and made experientially complete through the sufferings He endured. If the sinless Son of God learned through suffering, how much more do sinful believers need the school of affliction to shape their character and deepen their obedience? The path of suffering is the path that Christ Himself walked, and those who follow Him should not be surprised when they are called to walk it as well.

The testimony of the apostle Paul in 2 Corinthians 12:7-10 provides a master class in suffering well: "And lest I should be exalted above measure through the abundance of the revelations, there was given to me a thorn in the flesh, the messenger of Satan to buffet me, lest I should be exalted above measure. For this thing I besought the Lord thrice, that it might depart from me. And he said unto me, My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness. Most gladly therefore will I rather glory in my infirmities, that the power of Christ may rest upon me. Therefore I take pleasure in infirmities, in reproaches, in necessities, in persecutions, in distresses for Christ's sake: for when I am weak, then am I strong." Paul prayed three times for the removal of his thorn — and God said no. But in saying no, God gave Paul something far better than relief: He gave him the revelation that divine grace is sufficient and that divine power is perfected in human weakness. Paul's response was not resentment but rejoicing — he would "glory in infirmities" because they drove him into deeper dependence upon Christ. This is the Puritan ideal of suffering well: not pretending the pain does not exist, but finding in the midst of pain a deeper experience of Christ's sustaining grace.


Before we proceed to the afternoon activity, let us apply the doctrine of suffering to the concrete realities of daily life.

First, when suffering comes, resist the temptation to ask "Why me?" and instead ask "What is God teaching me?" The Puritans understood that suffering is a school, and every trial is a lesson. Instead of wasting your suffering on self-pity and complaint, look for the lesson God is teaching. Is He weaning you from the world? Is He deepening your prayer life? Is He developing patience, compassion, or humility? Is He preparing you for future ministry? Romans 8:28 assures us that God is working all things together for good — but we must have eyes of faith to see it.

Second, in times of suffering, run to the Word of God, not away from it. The Puritans found their greatest comfort in Scripture during their darkest hours. Psalm 119:50 declares, "This is my comfort in my affliction: for thy word hath quickened me." When you are suffering, immerse yourself in the promises of God. Read the Psalms, which express every human emotion with raw honesty before God. Read Romans 8, which assures you that nothing can separate you from the love of God. Read Revelation 21, which promises a day when every tear will be wiped away. Let the Word of God be your anchor in the storm.

Third, do not suffer in isolation. The Puritans placed great emphasis on the communion of saints — the fellowship of believers who bear one another's burdens (Galatians 6:2). When you are suffering, resist the temptation to withdraw from fellowship. Instead, seek out mature believers who can pray with you, encourage you, and point you to Christ. And when others are suffering, do not stand at a distance — draw near, listen, weep with those who weep (Romans 12:15), and offer the comfort you yourself have received from God (2 Corinthians 1:4).

Fourth, let suffering deepen your compassion for others. One of the greatest fruits of suffering is an enlarged capacity for empathy. Those who have walked through the valley of the shadow of death are uniquely equipped to comfort others who are walking the same path. The Puritans taught that God never wastes a tear — every sorrow you endure prepares you for a ministry of comfort that you could never have without it. Second Corinthians 1:4 states this principle explicitly: God "comforteth us in all our tribulation, that we may be able to comfort them which are in any trouble, by the comfort wherewith we ourselves are comforted of God."

Fifth, keep your eyes fixed on the eternal weight of glory. The Puritans lived with one eye on this world and one eye on the world to come. They understood that the sufferings of this present time, however painful, are preparing for them "a far more exceeding and eternal weight of glory" (2 Corinthians 4:17). When the trial seems unbearable, lift your eyes to heaven. The Celestial City is real. The promises of God are certain. The day is coming when every tear will be wiped away, every wrong will be righted, and every faithful pilgrim will hear the words, "Well done, thou good and faithful servant; enter thou into the joy of thy lord" (Matthew 25:21).

Drawing from the biblical teaching and Bunyan's allegory, students will reflect on a time of difficulty or trial in their own lives (or in the life of a biblical character they identify with) and analyze it through the lens of this lesson. The essay should address: (1) What was the nature of the trial? (2) How did it feel at the time? (3) What spiritual attacks or temptations accompanied it? (4) How did God's Word and God's presence sustain you (or the biblical character) through it? (5) What spiritual fruit came from the experience? Students should reference at least two Scripture passages and one insight from Bunyan or the Puritans. This is a personal and devotional exercise, so students should write honestly and reflectively.

Materials needed: Notebooks or lined paper, pens, Bibles, access to or copies of relevant sections of Pilgrim's Progress

As you complete this activity, think about how it connects to what you studied in today's lesson. The goal is not just to do something, but to reinforce the truths you have been learning. The Puritans believed that education should engage the whole person: mind, heart, and hands.

Take your time with this activity. Quality matters more than speed. When you are finished, reflect on what you learned through the process of doing. Often we understand something far better after we have tried to put it into practice.


One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

The history of the Christian church is largely a history of suffering. From the Roman persecutions of the first three centuries to the Reformation martyrs to the Puritan ejections of 1662 to the persecution of Christians in the modern world, believers have consistently faced opposition for their faith. Study specific examples of Christians who suffered well — such as Polycarp, John Huss, the Marian martyrs, and Bunyan himself — and discuss what sustained them and what fruit their suffering produced.

Literature Connection:

Suffering is one of the great themes of world literature. From the ancient Greek tragedies of Sophocles and Euripides to the modern novels of Dostoevsky and Solzhenitsyn, writers have grappled with the meaning of human pain. Compare how secular literature approaches suffering (often as meaningless absurdity) with how Christian literature (especially Bunyan) approaches it (as purposeful discipline leading to growth and glory). Discuss which perspective provides more genuine comfort and deeper meaning.

Science Connection:

The process of refinement and purification is well documented in metallurgy. Gold is purified by heating it to extreme temperatures, causing impurities to rise to the surface where they can be removed. The higher the heat, the purer the gold. This scientific process provides a powerful analogy for the biblical teaching that trials purify faith (1 Peter 1:7). Study the actual process of gold refining and discuss how each stage parallels the spiritual experience of being refined through suffering.

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.


Let us now examine the deeper theology of the cross as it relates to the believer's experience of suffering, exploring how union with Christ transforms the meaning and purpose of every trial we endure. The Puritans understood that the Christian's suffering is never meaningless — it is always connected to the sufferings of Christ and directed toward the glory of God.

The apostle Paul articulates this connection with remarkable clarity in Colossians 1:24: "Who now rejoice in my sufferings for you, and fill up that which is behind of the afflictions of Christ in my flesh for his body's sake, which is the church." This is a startling statement. Paul says he is filling up what is "behind" — what remains, what is lacking — in the afflictions of Christ. This does not mean that Christ's atoning work was insufficient. The cross fully and perfectly satisfied the justice of God for the sins of His people. Rather, Paul means that there are afflictions appointed for the body of Christ — the church — that must be endured by its members as they carry the gospel to the world. Christ suffered to accomplish redemption; His people suffer to proclaim redemption. And Paul finds joy in this suffering because it connects him to Christ and serves the church.

Philippians 3:10-11 takes us even deeper into this theology of suffering: "That I may know him, and the power of his resurrection, and the fellowship of his sufferings, being made conformable unto his death; If by any means I might attain unto the resurrection of the dead." Paul's supreme desire is to know Christ — not merely to know about Him but to know Him in the fullness of His person and work. And this knowledge includes "the fellowship of his sufferings." The word "fellowship" is the Greek koinonia — partnership, participation, shared experience. Paul wants to participate in Christ's sufferings, to share in the kind of suffering that Christ endured. This is not masochism; it is love. The one who truly loves Christ desires to share in every aspect of His experience, including His sufferings, because suffering with Christ leads to deeper knowledge of Christ and ultimately to sharing in His resurrection glory.

First Peter 2:21 establishes suffering as an integral part of the Christian calling: "For even hereunto were ye called: because Christ also suffered for us, leaving us an example, that ye should follow his steps." We are called to suffer. This is not an accident, a mistake, or a sign of God's displeasure. It is part of the divine design for the Christian life. Christ left us an example — the Greek word is hupogrammon, which literally means a writing pattern that a student traces to learn penmanship. Christ's life of suffering is the pattern; our lives are to be tracings of that pattern. We follow in His steps, walking the path He walked, enduring what He endured, trusting as He trusted.

The Puritan pastor Richard Baxter (1615-1691) wrote with extraordinary pastoral tenderness about the connection between the believer's sufferings and the sufferings of Christ. In "The Saints' Everlasting Rest," Baxter taught that suffering is one of the primary means by which God prepares His people for heaven. He compared the believer's life to a journey through a wilderness — necessary, temporary, and purposeful. The wilderness is not the destination; Canaan is. The suffering is not the end; glory is. Baxter wrote that "God would not have His children too much at home in this world" — He uses suffering to loosen their grip on earthly things and tighten their grip on heavenly things.

Baxter also taught that suffering produces a peculiar beauty in the Christian character that cannot be produced in any other way. He compared it to the process of sculpting — the sculptor must chip away at the stone, removing everything that does not belong, in order to reveal the beautiful form within. The chipping is painful, but the result is glorious. In the same way, God uses the chisel of suffering to remove from the believer's character everything that does not resemble Christ — pride, self-sufficiency, worldliness, complacency — until the image of Christ is clearly visible.

Consider the testimony of Psalm 66:10-12: "For thou, O God, hast proved us: thou hast tried us, as silver is tried. Thou broughtest us into the net; thou laidst affliction upon our loins. Thou hast caused men to ride over our heads; we went through fire and through water: but thou broughtest us out into a wealthy place." The psalmist acknowledges that God Himself is the author of his suffering: "Thou hast proved us... thou hast tried us... thou broughtest us into the net... thou laidst affliction upon our loins." There is no attempt to blame Satan or circumstances. God is sovereign over every trial. And yet the suffering has a purpose and an end: "but thou broughtest us out into a wealthy place." God does not leave His people in the furnace forever. He brings them out — refined, purified, and enriched — into a place of abundance and blessing.

The Puritan divine Samuel Bolton (1606-1654), who served as Master of Christ's College, Cambridge, wrote about suffering in his important work "The True Bounds of Christian Freedom." Bolton taught that the believer's sufferings are fundamentally different from the sufferings of the unconverted, even when they appear identical from the outside. The unconverted person suffers as a result of living in a fallen world, but his suffering has no redemptive purpose — it does not draw him closer to God, purify his character, or prepare him for glory. The believer's suffering, by contrast, is always supervised by a loving Father, always directed toward a sanctifying purpose, and always limited in both intensity and duration by the sovereign wisdom of God. Bolton wrote that "affliction to the believer is like the pruning of a vine — it hurts, but it produces more fruit."

The prophet Isaiah provides one of the most tender promises for suffering believers in Isaiah 43:1-3: "But now thus saith the LORD that created thee, O Jacob, and he that formed thee, O Israel, Fear not: for I have redeemed thee, I have called thee by thy name; thou art mine. When thou passest through the waters, I will be with thee; and through the rivers, they shall not overflow thee: when thou walkest through the fire, thou shalt not be burned; neither shall the flame kindle upon thee. For I am the LORD thy God, the Holy One of Israel, thy Saviour." Three assurances are given. First, God's ownership: "thou art mine." You belong to God. You are His purchased possession, His covenant child, His beloved. Second, God's presence: "I will be with thee." Not merely watching from a distance, but present in the midst of the suffering, walking through the fire and the water alongside His people. Third, God's protection: "they shall not overflow thee... thou shalt not be burned." The waters may rise, but they will not drown you. The flames may burn, but they will not consume you. God sets limits on every trial and ensures that His people emerge safely on the other side.

Romans 8:17-18 draws the ultimate connection between present suffering and future glory: "And if children, then heirs; heirs of God, and joint-heirs with Christ; if so be that we suffer with him, that we may be also glorified together. For I reckon that the sufferings of this present time are not worthy to be compared with the glory which shall be revealed in us." If we are God's children, we are His heirs — heirs of God and joint-heirs with Christ. But the inheritance comes through suffering. We suffer with Christ so that we may be glorified with Christ. And when Paul weighs the suffering against the glory, the comparison is not even close: the sufferings of this present time are "not worthy to be compared" with the glory to come. The Puritans took great comfort in this proportion. Whatever they endured in this life — persecution, poverty, imprisonment, bereavement — it was infinitely outweighed by the glory that awaited them in the Celestial City.

Practical Application — Suffering Well in Daily Life:

1. When suffering comes, anchor your soul in the truth that God is sovereign over your trial. He has not lost control. He has not forgotten you. He has not abandoned you. Your suffering is measured, purposeful, and temporary. Cling to Psalm 31:15: "My times are in thy hand." Every moment of your suffering is held in the wise and loving hand of your heavenly Father.

2. Look for Christ in your suffering. The Puritans taught that Christ is never closer to His people than when they are in the furnace of affliction. Seek His face in prayer. Listen for His voice in Scripture. Look for the evidences of His sustaining grace in the small mercies that surround you even in your darkest hours. As the hymn writer put it, "He giveth more grace when the burdens grow greater."

3. Use your suffering as an occasion for intercessory prayer. When you are in pain, you are in a unique position to pray for others who are also suffering. Your own experience of affliction gives you insight, empathy, and urgency that you would not otherwise possess. Turn your suffering into ministry by lifting up others before the throne of grace.

4. Resist the temptation to withdraw from the body of Christ during times of suffering. The devil wants to isolate you, to convince you that no one understands, that no one cares, that you are better off alone. But Scripture commands the opposite: "Bear ye one another's burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ" (Galatians 6:2). Let others bear your burden with you. Receive their prayers, their presence, and their comfort as gifts from a faithful God.

5. Keep your eyes fixed on the eternal weight of glory. The Puritans lived with one foot in this world and one foot in the next. They endured present suffering by contemplating future glory. Let the vision of the Celestial City sustain you through every valley, every trial, and every dark night of the soul. The day is coming when suffering will be no more, when tears will be wiped away forever, and when you will enter into the joy of your Lord.
