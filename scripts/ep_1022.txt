PURITAN GOLD -- Study Topic: The Book of Enoch, Part 2 of 10
"The Watchers (1 Enoch 1-5)"
The Book of Enoch — Angels who left their proper domain

---

Welcome back, dear friends, to our continuing study of the Book of Enoch. In our last episode, we introduced this ancient Jewish text and established the principles by which we approach it — as students of Scripture who value historical context, not as those who would elevate any work above the canonical Word of God. Today, we turn to the opening chapters of 1 Enoch, commonly known as the Book of the Watchers.

Let us read from Jude, verse 6:

"And the angels which kept not their first estate, but left their own habitation, he hath reserved in everlasting chains under darkness unto the judgment of the great day."

--- Teaching ---

The opening five chapters of 1 Enoch constitute a grand theophany — a vision of God coming in judgment upon the earth. It is, in many ways, a meditation upon the themes found throughout the canonical prophets: the day of the Lord, the vindication of the righteous, and the destruction of the wicked. The language is majestic and soaring, drawing upon imagery familiar to any reader of Isaiah, Ezekiel, or the Psalms.

Enoch begins with a declaration that has echoed through the ages: "The Holy Great One will come forth from His dwelling, and the eternal God will tread upon the earth, even on Mount Sinai, and appear in His camp, and appear in the strength of His might from the heaven of heavens." Compare this with the language of Deuteronomy 33:2, "The LORD came from Sinai, and rose up from Seir unto them; he shined forth from mount Paran, and he came with ten thousands of saints." The parallels are unmistakable. The author of 1 Enoch was steeped in the language and theology of the Torah and the Prophets.

These opening chapters establish the cosmic framework for everything that follows. God is presented as the sovereign Lord of creation — the One who has set the stars in their courses, who commands the seasons and the tides, who governs all things by His eternal decree. Nature itself testifies to God's faithfulness. The sun rises and sets according to His command; the trees blossom and shed their leaves in their appointed times; the oceans observe their boundaries. All of creation obeys its Maker — all, that is, except the sinners who have turned aside from His ways.

This contrast between the obedience of creation and the rebellion of sinners is a theme that runs throughout canonical Scripture. The prophet Jeremiah declared, "Yea, the stork in the heaven knoweth her appointed times; and the turtle and the crane and the swallow observe the time of their coming; but my people know not the judgment of the LORD" (Jeremiah 8:7). The apostle Paul would later write that the wrath of God is revealed against those who suppress the truth, for what may be known of God is manifest in creation (Romans 1:18-20). The Book of Enoch stands firmly in this tradition, using the natural world as a witness against human wickedness.

But the primary concern of these chapters — and indeed of the entire Book of the Watchers — is the angelic rebellion. The term "Watchers" itself is found in the canonical Scriptures, though only in the book of Daniel. In Daniel 4:13, Nebuchadnezzar recounts his dream: "I saw in the visions of my head upon my bed, and, behold, a watcher and an holy one came down from heaven." The Aramaic term is 'ir, meaning "wakeful one" or "watcher" — a designation for angelic beings who serve as God's sentinels over creation.

In the Enochic tradition, the Watchers are a class of angels appointed to watch over humanity. Their tragedy — and the central narrative of the Book of the Watchers — is that a group of them abandoned their heavenly station and descended to earth, drawn by desire for the daughters of men. This narrative expands upon the mysterious passage in Genesis 6:1-4: "And it came to pass, when men began to multiply on the face of the earth, and daughters were born unto them, That the sons of God saw the daughters of men that they were fair; and they took them wives of all which they chose."

The interpretation of "sons of God" in Genesis 6 has been debated throughout church history. The early church, influenced by the Enochic tradition, largely understood these as angelic beings. This interpretation finds support in the fact that the phrase "sons of God" (bene ha-elohim) is used elsewhere in the Old Testament to refer to angels (Job 1:6, 2:1, 38:7). The Septuagint — the Greek translation of the Old Testament used by the early church — renders the phrase as "angels of God" in some manuscripts.

Later interpreters, including Augustine and many of the Reformers, preferred to understand the "sons of God" as the godly line of Seth intermarrying with the ungodly line of Cain. This interpretation has merit and avoids certain theological difficulties. Yet the epistles of Jude and 2 Peter seem to support the angelic interpretation. Jude 6, which we read at the outset, speaks of angels who "kept not their first estate" — the Greek word arche, meaning their proper domain or principality — "but left their own habitation." Peter likewise refers to "the angels that sinned" being cast down to Tartarus (2 Peter 2:4), a term borrowed not from the Old Testament but from the language of Greek mythology, suggesting that Peter was drawing upon a broader tradition about fallen angels.

The Book of Enoch presents this rebellion in vivid detail. The leader of the rebellious Watchers is named Semjaza (also spelled Shemihazah), and he is joined by a company of two hundred angels who swear an oath together on Mount Hermon to carry out their plan. The choice of Mount Hermon is significant — it was a real mountain on the northern border of Israel, long associated in Canaanite tradition with the divine realm. The very name "Hermon" is related to the Hebrew word herem, meaning "devoted to destruction" or "placed under a ban."

What concerns us as students of Scripture is not the historical accuracy of these details, but the theological truths they convey. The Book of Enoch, like the canonical Scriptures, teaches that the angelic rebellion was a real event with catastrophic consequences for humanity. It teaches that sin, even among the highest created beings, brings inevitable judgment. It teaches that God is sovereign over all powers, whether earthly or heavenly, and that no rebellion, however grand, can finally thwart His purposes.

The Puritan divine Thomas Goodwin, in his great work on Ephesians, reflected at length upon the reality of the angelic world and the significance of the conflict between faithful and fallen angels. Though he did not comment directly on the Enochic text, his theology of cosmic spiritual warfare resonates deeply with the themes of the Book of the Watchers. For Goodwin, the fall of the angels was the first act in the great drama of redemption — a drama that would find its resolution only in the person and work of Christ.

--- Application ---

1. Take seriously the reality of the unseen spiritual world. The Bible teaches that we wrestle "not against flesh and blood, but against principalities, against powers" (Ephesians 6:12). The Watchers tradition, whatever its literary character, points to this sober reality.

2. Learn from the Watchers the danger of abandoning your God-given station. Every believer has a calling, a "first estate" to keep. To abandon the post where God has placed you — whether in family, church, or vocation — is to follow the pattern of the fallen angels.

3. Let the obedience of creation rebuke your disobedience. If the sun and moon and stars faithfully perform their appointed courses, how much more should rational, redeemed creatures walk in faithful obedience to their Maker?

4. Rest in the sovereignty of God over all angelic powers. No rebellion in heaven or on earth can overturn God's decree. Christ has triumphed over all principalities and powers, having made an open show of them at the cross (Colossians 2:15).

--- Closing ---

Almighty God, Thou who art Lord of hosts and sovereign over all angelic powers, we praise Thee that no rebellion can undo Thy purposes. Teach us to keep our first estate — to remain faithful in the station where Thou hast placed us — and to find our strength in Thee alone. Through Jesus Christ our Lord, who reigns victorious over every power and principality. Amen.
