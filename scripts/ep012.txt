PURITAN GOLD — Episode 12
"The Danger of Sin's Allure"
Week 2, Day 5 (Friday) — Proverbs 7

---

Let me tell you a story. It's not a new story. In fact, it's one of the oldest stories in the world. But it plays out every single day, in every city, in every neighborhood, in every human heart.

A young man is walking down a street at dusk. He doesn't have a destination — not really. He's just... wandering. Restless. Bored, maybe. Looking for something, though he couldn't tell you what. And as the streetlights come on and the shadows deepen, he turns a corner he didn't need to turn, walks past a door he should have avoided, and there she is. Waiting.

That's Proverbs 7. And if you think this chapter is just about one ancient Israelite kid making a bad decision on a dark night... you're missing the point entirely.

This is Puritan Gold. And today we're going to look at what may be the most cinematic chapter in all of Proverbs — a chapter so vivid, so psychologically precise, so terrifyingly modern that it could have been written yesterday. Solomon pulls back the curtain on how temptation actually works — step by step, from the first idle thought to the final devastating consequence.

Let's start at verse 6. Solomon is looking out through the lattice of his window. He's watching. And he sees something that grieves him:

"For at the window of my house I looked through my casement, and beheld among the simple ones, I discerned among the youths, a young man void of understanding."

There's our first clue. This young man is "void of understanding." Now, that doesn't mean he's stupid. It doesn't mean he can't read or hold a job or carry on a conversation. It means he lacks moral discernment. He lacks the kind of wisdom that sees around corners — that anticipates where a decision will lead before it gets there. He is, in the most dangerous sense of the word, naive.

And I want to pause here, because this is important. Naivety is not innocence. Innocence is beautiful. It's the state of someone who hasn't been touched by evil. But naivety is different. Naivety is the state of someone who doesn't take evil seriously. Who thinks he can handle it. Who believes he's the exception to the rule.

Verse 8: "Passing through the street near her corner; and he went the way to her house."

He's near her corner. He went the way to her house. Do you see? He's not being dragged there against his will. He's choosing a path. Maybe he tells himself he's just taking a walk. Maybe he tells himself he'll turn around before he gets too close. But his feet know where they're going, even if his mouth won't admit it.

This is how temptation works, friends. It almost never begins with the act itself. It begins long before — with a decision about where to walk. What to look at. What to click on. Whose company to keep. The young man in Proverbs 7 was lost before he ever met the woman, because he chose to wander near the danger without any purpose strong enough to keep him away.

Now the scene intensifies. Verses 10 through 13:

"And, behold, there met him a woman with the attire of an harlot, and subtil of heart. She is loud and stubborn; her feet abide not in her house: Now is she without, now in the streets, and lieth in wait at every corner. So she caught him, and kissed him, and with an impudent face said unto him..."

Notice how she's described. Subtil of heart. Crafty. Calculating. She knows exactly what she's doing. And she meets him — she doesn't chase him down. She doesn't have to. She simply positions herself where the wandering man will inevitably end up.

A.W. Pink made a penetrating observation about this in his writings. He noted that Satan doesn't need to overpower us if he can simply outlure us. The devil is a hunter, and a good hunter doesn't chase his prey — he sets traps in the places his prey naturally wanders. If the young man had been home that night, studying, praying, in the company of the wise — there would have been no trap to fall into. The trap only works because the prey is already in the wrong place.

And then comes the sales pitch. Look at verses 14 through 18:

"I have peace offerings with me; this day have I payed my vows. Therefore came I forth to meet thee, diligently to seek thy face, and I have found thee. I have decked my bed with coverings of tapestry, with carved works, with fine linen of Egypt. I have perfumed my bed with myrrh, aloes, and cinnamon."

Do you hear the craft in her words? She begins with religion. "I have peace offerings." She's just been to the temple. She's a good, religious woman. This isn't sordid — this is respectable. Then she flatters: "I came to seek thy face. I've been looking for you." You're special. You're the one I wanted. And then she appeals to the senses: tapestry, fine linen, perfume. She's creating an atmosphere. Painting a picture. Making sin look beautiful, luxurious, desirable.

This is exactly what sin always does. It never presents itself honestly. It never says, "Come with me, and I'll destroy your marriage, wreck your health, empty your bank account, and leave you broken and ashamed." Instead, it says, "Come with me, and I'll give you pleasure. Excitement. Fulfillment. Everything you've been missing."

Myrrh and aloes. Tapestry and linen. The honeycomb of Proverbs 5 revisited.

And the young man? Verse 21 tells us his response:

"With her much fair speech she caused him to yield, with the flattering of her lips she forced him."

She caused him to yield. She forced him — not with physical strength, but with words. With flattery. With the sheer persistent pressure of a seductive voice telling him exactly what his flesh wanted to hear.

And now the most devastating verse in the chapter. Verse 22:

"He goeth after her straightway, as an ox goeth to the slaughter, or as a fool to the correction of the stocks."

An ox to the slaughter. Think about that image. Have you ever watched an animal being led to slaughter? It doesn't know. It doesn't understand. It walks calmly, even contentedly, toward its own death. That's the young man. That's what sin does. It leads you by the nose, with a gentle hand, toward destruction — and you go willingly, because the hand feels so gentle.

Solomon drives the point home in verses 26 and 27:

"For she hath cast down many wounded: yea, many strong men have been slain by her. Her house is the way to hell, going down to the chambers of death."

Many strong men. Not just the weak. Not just the naive. Strong men. Men with reputations, with accomplishments, with seemingly unshakeable character. And they've all been slain. Her house — that perfumed, tapestried, beautiful house — is the way to hell. The road to the chambers of death.

Now, I want to bring this into our world. Because the temptation of Proverbs 7, while literally about sexual sin, is a template for every form of allurement that the world, the flesh, and the devil place before us.

Think about the way addiction works. It starts with wandering — just a drink, just a taste, just a scroll through the feed. Then comes the encounter — the substance, the screen, the rush that meets you right where your restlessness took you. Then comes the sales pitch — the feeling of relief, the promise of escape, the momentary numbness that feels like peace. And then comes the surrender — "straightway," without deliberation, without resistance. And then comes the slaughter.

Think about how materialism works. The wandering through stores or websites. The product that catches your eye. The voice that says, "You deserve this. You've worked hard. One more purchase won't hurt." And before you know it, you're drowning in debt, owned by the things you thought you owned.

Think about how bitterness works. The idle rehearsal of an old wound. The entertaining of vengeful thoughts. The inner voice that says, "They deserve your anger. Holding this grudge is protecting you." And slowly, the bitterness becomes a prison — and you are both the prisoner and the warden.

The pattern is always the same. Always. Wandering. Encounter. Flattery. Surrender. Death.

So how do we protect ourselves?

Solomon already told us at the beginning of the chapter. Verses 1 through 4:

"My son, keep my words, and lay up my commandments with thee. Keep my commandments, and live; and my law as the apple of thine eye. Bind them upon thy fingers, write them upon the table of thine heart. Say unto wisdom, Thou art my sister; and call understanding thy kinswoman."

There's the armor. God's Word, kept close. Not on a shelf, not in an app you never open — on the table of your heart. Written on your fingers. As close to you as family. The man who walks the streets armed with the Word of God is not easily seduced. The woman who has hidden God's commandments in her heart has an answer for every flattering lie.

And there's a second defense that's implied in the whole narrative: don't wander. Don't be "void of understanding." Don't take idle strolls past known danger zones. Know where the traps are, and stay away. Have a destination. Have a purpose. Have someone expecting you at home.

Pink wrote about this with characteristic directness. He said that the believer who does not actively cultivate communion with God will inevitably be drawn into communion with the world. There is no neutral ground. Your heart will be filled with something. The question is whether you fill it deliberately with what is good... or allow it to be filled passively with what is deadly.

Friends, we live in a world saturated with the voice of the woman of Proverbs 7. She speaks from every billboard, every screen, every algorithm designed to exploit your restlessness. She's louder than she's ever been, more accessible, more persistent. And the only defense — the only reliable, unbreakable defense — is the Word of God written on the table of your heart, and the wisdom of God embraced as a sister and kinswoman.

Stop wandering. Start walking with purpose. And when the flattering voice begins to speak — and it will — answer it with Scripture. Answer it with truth. Answer it with the voice of the One who said, "I am the way, the truth, and the life." That voice will never lead you to the chambers of death. It will lead you home.

Let's pray.

O Lord, we are more vulnerable than we dare to admit. We are that young man on the street, wandering without purpose, telling ourselves we can handle the proximity to danger. Shatter our illusions. Open our eyes to see sin for what it truly is — not the tapestried bed but the chamber of death. Write Your Word so deeply on our hearts that when the seductive voice speaks, we already have our answer. Keep our feet on Your path. Give us the wisdom to flee, the humility to admit our weakness, and the grace to find in You a satisfaction so deep that every counterfeit loses its power. In the name of Jesus, who defeated every temptation for us. Amen.
