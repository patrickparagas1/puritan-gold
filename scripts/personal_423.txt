PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Pastoral Encouragement, Day 23
"When the Soul Is Cast Down"
Puritan Counsel for the Discouraged Man


Welcome, friend. There are nights when what we need most is not more information but more encouragement. Tonight is one of those nights. Let the Lord speak directly to your heart.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Psalm 42:5:

"Why art thou cast down, O my soul? and why art thou disquieted in me? hope thou in God: for I shall yet praise him for the help of his countenance."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


Every Christian man faces seasons of discouragement. The psalmist knew it. The apostles knew it. The great Puritans knew it. And you will know it too. The question is not whether discouragement will come, but how you will respond when it does.

William Bridge, a Puritan pastor who himself knew deep seasons of spiritual darkness, wrote 'A Lifting Up for the Downcast' to minister to discouraged believers. Bridge begins by normalizing the experience: discouragement is not, in itself, a sign of spiritual failure. Some of the most godly men in Scripture were profoundly discouraged — David, Elijah, Jeremiah, even Paul. Bridge writes that 'the most fruitful trees are sometimes the most shaken by the wind.'

Bridge identifies several common causes of spiritual discouragement. The first is the weight of remaining sin. The man who takes holiness seriously is often the man most discouraged by his failures. He knows what he should be, he sees how far he falls short, and the gap fills him with grief. Bridge counsels this man: your grief over sin is itself evidence of grace. The unregenerate man does not grieve over his sin; he enjoys it. Your discouragement, far from proving you are not a Christian, actually proves you are.

The second cause is unanswered prayer. The man who prays earnestly for something — his child's conversion, a difficult marriage, healing from illness, relief from temptation — and does not see results may conclude that God does not hear him. Bridge responds: God always hears the prayers of His children. But He does not always answer on our timeline or in the way we expect. Bridge writes that delayed answers are not denied answers; they are opportunities to deepen trust and exercise patient faith.

The third cause is comparison. The discouraged man looks at other believers who seem more joyful, more fruitful, more assured, and concludes that something is wrong with him. Bridge warns against this comparison: 'Every soul has its own seasons, its own pace of growth, its own relationship with God. Do not measure your inner life by another man's outward appearance.'

The fourth cause is the feeling of uselessness. Some men are discouraged because they feel they are accomplishing nothing for the kingdom. Their ministry seems fruitless, their influence negligible, their gifts unused. Bridge counsels: faithfulness, not fruitfulness, is the measure of your obedience. God does not hold you accountable for results; He holds you accountable for faithfulness. Plant the seed and trust God with the harvest.

Bridge then offers remedies for discouragement. His primary remedy is the one the psalmist uses: 'Hope thou in God.' The discouraged soul must preach to itself. This is not positive self-talk; it is gospel self-talk. It is reminding yourself of what is true when your feelings tell you otherwise. God is still sovereign. Christ is still your righteousness. The Spirit still dwells within you. The promises are still sure. The end is still glory.

Thomas Watson adds counsel from his own pastoral experience. He writes that the discouraged man should return to the basics of the spiritual life: read the Word, even when it feels dry. Pray, even when the heavens seem brass. Attend worship, even when the heart is cold. Seek fellowship, even when you feel like withdrawing. Watson writes that these means of grace are most important precisely when we feel they are least effective.

Richard Sibbes, in 'The Bruised Reed,' offers perhaps the most comforting Puritan word for the discouraged soul. Sibbes reminds us that Christ does not break the bruised reed or quench the smoking flax. The soul that is bruised by failure, the faith that is barely flickering — these are precisely the souls that Christ treats with the greatest tenderness. Sibbes writes: 'Where He finds a spark of grace, He will not pour water on it, but blow upon it and fan it to a flame.'

Take courage today. Your discouragement is not the final word. Hope in God, for you shall yet praise Him.


Before we close, take a few moments with these reflection questions.

Question 1: What is the primary source of my discouragement right now — remaining sin, unanswered prayer, comparison, or a sense of uselessness?

Question 2: Am I preaching the gospel to my own soul, or am I allowing my feelings to dictate my assessment of reality?

Question 3: Which means of grace have I been neglecting in my season of discouragement, and can I commit to returning to them this week?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Lord, my soul is cast down, but You are my hope. I preach Your truth to my own discouraged heart: You are sovereign, Christ is mine, and Your Spirit has not left me. Fan the smoking flax of my faith into flame. I shall yet praise You. Amen.

Until next time. May the Lord deepen your faith with every passing day.
