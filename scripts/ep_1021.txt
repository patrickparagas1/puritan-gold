PURITAN GOLD -- Study Topic: The Book of Enoch, Part 1 of 10
"Introduction — What Is the Book of Enoch?"
The Book of Enoch — Its history and relationship to Scripture

---

Beloved friends, welcome to this new study series on Puritan Gold. Over the next ten episodes, we shall undertake a careful and reverent examination of the Book of Enoch — that ancient Jewish text which has fascinated, perplexed, and instructed readers for over two thousand years. Our purpose is not to elevate this work to the level of Holy Scripture, but rather to understand it in light of the canonical Word of God, and to see how it illuminates the world of thought in which our Lord and His apostles ministered.

Let us begin with a reading from the Epistle of Jude, verses 14 and 15:

"And Enoch also, the seventh from Adam, prophesied of these, saying, Behold, the Lord cometh with ten thousands of his saints, To execute judgment upon all, and to convince all that are ungodly among them of all their ungodly deeds which they have ungodly committed, and of all their hard speeches which ungodly sinners have spoken against him."

--- Teaching ---

The Book of Enoch, known more precisely as 1 Enoch, is a composite Jewish work written in stages between roughly the third century before Christ and the first century before Christ. It is classified among the pseudepigrapha — writings attributed to biblical figures but composed at a later date. The work is attributed to Enoch, that remarkable patriarch of whom Genesis 5:24 declares, "And Enoch walked with God: and he was not; for God took him."

Let us be clear at the outset about what this book is and what it is not. The Book of Enoch is not canonical Scripture. It was not received into the Hebrew Bible, nor was it accepted by the vast majority of the Christian church as divinely inspired. The early church fathers debated its status — Tertullian spoke favorably of it, while Augustine and Jerome regarded it as outside the canon. The Protestant Reformers, following the Hebrew canon, did not include it among the inspired books. The Ethiopian Orthodox Church alone among major Christian traditions regards it as canonical Scripture.

Yet the Book of Enoch holds a peculiar and important place in the study of Scripture for several reasons. First, as we have already read, the apostle Jude quotes directly from it. This does not mean Jude regarded it as Scripture in the same sense as the Pentateuch or the Prophets — the apostle Paul quoted pagan poets without endorsing their theology — but it does mean that the early church was familiar with this text and that at least some of its content was regarded as containing truth.

Second, the Book of Enoch provides invaluable context for understanding the world of Second Temple Judaism. Many of the concepts, phrases, and images found in the New Testament — particularly in the teachings of our Lord concerning the Son of Man, the final judgment, and the fate of fallen angels — find parallels and background in the Enochic literature. To study Enoch is to better understand the intellectual and spiritual landscape in which the gospel was first proclaimed.

Third, the theological themes of the Book of Enoch are remarkably consonant with many themes in canonical Scripture: the sovereignty of God over all creation, the reality of angelic beings both faithful and fallen, the certainty of divine judgment, the hope of a messianic deliverer, and the ultimate triumph of righteousness over wickedness.

The book itself is divided into five major sections, each of which we shall examine in turn. The first section, chapters 1 through 36, is known as the Book of the Watchers. It tells of angelic beings who rebelled against God by descending to earth and corrupting humanity — a narrative that expands upon the enigmatic passage in Genesis 6:1-4 concerning the sons of God and the daughters of men. The second section, chapters 37 through 71, contains the Parables or Similitudes of Enoch, which present a remarkable vision of a messianic figure called the Son of Man. The third section, chapters 72 through 82, is the Astronomical Book or Book of Luminaries, dealing with the movements of the sun, moon, and stars. The fourth section, chapters 83 through 90, presents the Dream Visions, including the famous Animal Apocalypse — an allegorical retelling of Israel's history. The fifth section, chapters 91 through 108, is the Epistle of Enoch, containing moral exhortation, prophetic woes, and a vision of the final judgment.

Now, how ought we as Reformed and evangelical Christians to approach such a text? The Puritan divines, though they did not devote extensive commentary to the Book of Enoch as we know it today — for the full Ethiopic text was not available in Europe until the late eighteenth century — nevertheless established principles of interpretation that serve us well. They taught us to hold fast to the sufficiency of Scripture while remaining charitable and curious students of all ancient literature that casts light upon the sacred text.

John Owen, that prince of Puritan theologians, wrote extensively on the nature of divine revelation and the boundaries of the canon. He affirmed that the sixty-six books of the Protestant Bible are the sole infallible rule of faith and practice, while also acknowledging that extra-canonical writings can be profitable for historical understanding. This is the spirit in which we approach the Book of Enoch.

We must also reckon with the fact that the Book of Enoch was enormously influential in the early church. Fragments of it were found among the Dead Sea Scrolls at Qumran — more copies, in fact, than many canonical books — suggesting that the community there regarded it with great seriousness. The earliest Christians, many of whom were Jewish, would have been familiar with its contents. When Peter writes that God "spared not the angels that sinned, but cast them down to hell, and delivered them into chains of darkness, to be reserved unto judgment" (2 Peter 2:4), he may well have had the Enochic tradition in view.

Let us therefore approach this study with humility, with reverence for the canonical Scriptures as our supreme authority, and with gratitude for the light that ancient texts can shed upon the glorious truths of God's Word. We do not read Enoch to find new doctrine, but to deepen our understanding of the old. We do not elevate it above Scripture, but we use it as a tool to better appreciate the richness of the biblical world.

In the episodes that follow, we shall walk through the major sections of the Book of Enoch, always tethering our study to the canonical Scriptures and drawing out those spiritual lessons that edify the soul and magnify the glory of God.

--- Application ---

1. Recognize the difference between canonical Scripture and valuable ancient literature. The Bible alone is our infallible authority, but other texts can illuminate the world of the Bible and deepen our understanding of its truths.

2. Cultivate a spirit of humble curiosity. The Puritans were voracious readers who studied widely — not to undermine their faith, but to strengthen it. Let us follow their example by engaging ancient texts with discernment and charity.

3. Let the figure of Enoch himself inspire your walk with God. Genesis tells us he "walked with God" in an age of great wickedness. Whatever else we learn in this series, let that simple testimony be our highest aspiration — to walk faithfully with God in our own generation.

4. Remember that all study must lead to worship. The purpose of examining the Book of Enoch is not mere intellectual curiosity, but a deeper apprehension of God's sovereignty, His judgment, and His mercy — truths that should drive us to our knees in adoration.
