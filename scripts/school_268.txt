PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 8
"Of Christ the Mediator"
The Westminster Standards — Lesson 8

---

Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we come to the heart of the Christian faith — the person and work of Jesus Christ as the Mediator between God and man. This is the doctrine upon which all other doctrines depend. Without Christ, there is no covenant of grace. Without Christ, there is no forgiveness of sin. Without Christ, there is no hope for fallen humanity. The Westminster Standards devote some of their most careful and exalted language to this subject, and we must give it our most attentive study. Let us pray and begin.

Lord Jesus Christ, Thou art the Mediator of the new covenant, the only hope of sinners, the joy of angels, and the glory of heaven. As we study Thy person and Thy work today, open the eyes of our understanding to behold Thy majesty and Thy mercy. Let us see Thee as Thou truly art — fully God, fully man, Prophet, Priest, and King — and let that sight transform our hearts and lives. For Thy name's sake, Amen.

--- Lesson ---

Part One: The Need for a Mediator

Before we can appreciate who the Mediator is and what He has done, we must understand why a Mediator was needed in the first place. The fall of man, which we studied in Lesson 6, created an impassable gulf between God and humanity. Man had sinned against an infinitely holy God. The penalty of that sin was death — spiritual, physical, and eternal. Man's nature was corrupted, his will was enslaved, and his relationship with God was shattered. He was, in the words of the Apostle Paul, "dead in trespasses and sins" (Ephesians 2:1), "without Christ, being aliens from the commonwealth of Israel, and strangers from the covenants of promise, having no hope, and without God in the world" (Ephesians 2:12).

How could this situation be remedied? Man could not save himself. His best efforts at righteousness were, as Isaiah declared, "as filthy rags" (Isaiah 64:6). He could not undo his guilt, repair his nature, or bridge the chasm between himself and God. Nor could any mere creature save him. No angel, however powerful, could bear the infinite weight of God's wrath against sin. No human being, however noble, could offer a righteousness sufficient to satisfy God's justice.

What was needed was a Mediator — someone who could stand between God and man, someone who could represent both parties, someone who could satisfy God's justice and secure man's salvation. As the Apostle Paul writes in 1 Timothy 2:5: "For there is one God, and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus."

The Westminster Confession, Chapter 8, Section 1, sets forth the eternal decree behind this provision: "It pleased God, in his eternal purpose, to choose and ordain the Lord Jesus, his only begotten Son, to be the Mediator between God and man, the Prophet, Priest, and King, the Head and Saviour of his church, the Heir of all things, and Judge of the world; unto whom he did, from all eternity, give a people to be his seed, and to be by him in time redeemed, called, justified, sanctified, and glorified."

This is a passage of breathtaking scope. Before the world was made, before man had sinned, before the need for redemption had arisen in human experience, God had already purposed to provide a Mediator. The plan of salvation was not an afterthought or a contingency plan. It was the eternal purpose of the triune God — the Father choosing and ordaining the Son, giving Him a people to redeem, and appointing Him as Prophet, Priest, King, Head, Saviour, Heir, and Judge.

Part Two: The Person of the Mediator — Fully God

The Westminster Standards teach that the Mediator must be both fully God and fully man. The Shorter Catechism, Question 21, asks: "Who is the Redeemer of God's elect?" and answers: "The only Redeemer of God's elect is the Lord Jesus Christ, who, being the eternal Son of God, became man, and so was, and continueth to be, God and man in two distinct natures, and one person, for ever."

Let us consider first the deity of the Mediator. Christ is "the eternal Son of God." He did not begin to exist at Bethlehem. He has existed from all eternity as the second Person of the Holy Trinity — fully God, co-equal and co-eternal with the Father and the Holy Spirit. The Gospel of John opens with this majestic declaration: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. The same was in the beginning with God. All things were made by him; and without him was not any thing made that was made" (John 1:1-3).

The Apostle Paul affirms Christ's deity in equally strong terms. In Colossians 1:15-17 he writes: "Who is the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of every creature: For by him were all things created, that are in heaven, and that are in earth, visible and invisible, whether they be thrones, or dominions, or principalities, or powers: all things were created by him, and for him: And he is before all things, and by him all things consist." And in Colossians 2:9: "For in him dwelleth all the fulness of the Godhead bodily."

Why must the Mediator be God? The Westminster Larger Catechism, Question 38, provides the answer: "It was requisite that the Mediator should be God, that he might sustain and keep the human nature from sinking under the infinite wrath of God, and the power of death; give worth and efficacy to his sufferings, obedience, and intercession; and to satisfy God's justice, procure his favour, purchase a peculiar people, give his Spirit to them, conquer all their enemies, and bring them to everlasting salvation."

This is a remarkable list. Only God could endure the infinite weight of divine wrath without being destroyed. Only God could give infinite value to a finite act of suffering. Only God could satisfy the justice of God. Only God could send the Holy Spirit. Only God could conquer sin, death, and hell. Only God could secure everlasting salvation. If Christ were merely a creature — however exalted — His death could not have accomplished these things. But because He is the eternal Son of God, His sacrifice has infinite worth and infinite efficacy.

The early church fought fierce battles to defend the deity of Christ. At the Council of Nicaea in 325 AD, convened by the Emperor Constantine in the city of Nicaea in Asia Minor (modern-day Turkey), the church condemned the heresy of Arius, a priest of Alexandria who taught that the Son of God was a created being — the first and greatest of God's creatures, but a creature nonetheless. The Council declared that the Son is "of one substance" (homoousios) with the Father — that is, fully and completely God, possessing the same divine nature as the Father. This Nicene faith was reaffirmed at the Council of Constantinople in 381 AD and has been the bedrock confession of the Christian church ever since.

The Westminster divines stood squarely in this Nicene tradition. When they confessed that Christ is "the eternal Son of God," they were affirming what the church has always believed: that Jesus Christ is God the Son, the second Person of the Trinity, fully divine, possessing all the attributes of deity — omnipotence, omniscience, omnipresence, eternity, immutability, and infinite holiness.

Part Three: The Person of the Mediator — Fully Man

But the Mediator must also be fully man. The Confession, Chapter 8, Section 2, declares: "The Son of God, the second person in the Trinity, being very and eternal God, of one substance, and equal with the Father, did, when the fulness of the time was come, take upon him man's nature, with all the essential properties and common infirmities thereof; yet without sin."

The phrase "when the fulness of the time was come" is drawn from Galatians 4:4: "But when the fulness of the time was come, God sent forth his Son, made of a woman, made under the law." The incarnation did not happen at a random moment in history. It happened at the precise moment ordained by God from all eternity — a moment prepared by centuries of prophecy, history, and providence.

Jesus Christ took upon Himself genuine human nature — not an appearance of humanity, but the reality of it. He had a real human body that could hunger, thirst, grow weary, suffer, bleed, and die. He had a real human soul that could grieve, rejoice, experience temptation, and agonize in prayer. He possessed "all the essential properties" of human nature — a human mind, a human will, human emotions, and human physical constitution. He also shared in "the common infirmities" of human existence — He experienced fatigue, hunger, sorrow, and pain. The only thing He did not share was sin. He was, as the writer of Hebrews declares, "in all points tempted like as we are, yet without sin" (Hebrews 4:15).

The Larger Catechism, Question 39, explains why the Mediator must be man: "It was requisite that the Mediator should be man, that he might advance our nature, have a fellow-feeling of our infirmities; that he might undergo the punishment due to us, and which we should have borne and suffered, though he could not have done so had he not been made like us in all things, sin only excepted; and that he might sympathize with us in all our afflictions."

The incarnation is the most stupendous event in the history of the universe. The infinite became finite. The eternal entered time. The Creator became a creature — not by ceasing to be God, but by adding to His divine nature a genuine human nature. The early church father Athanasius of Alexandria, who served as bishop from 328 to 373 AD and was the great champion of Nicene orthodoxy, summarized the purpose of the incarnation with matchless conciseness: the Son of God became man so that men might become sons of God.

John Owen, the prince of Puritan theologians who served as Vice-Chancellor of Oxford University during the 1650s, devoted some of his most brilliant writing to the incarnation. In his work Christologia, published in 1679, Owen argued that the incarnation was the crowning display of divine wisdom. In the incarnation, God found a way to satisfy both His justice and His mercy — to punish sin and to pardon sinners — by uniting the divine and human natures in one Person. The divine nature gave infinite value to Christ's sufferings. The human nature made it possible for Him to suffer and die in our place. Together, the two natures constituted the perfect Mediator — one who could represent God to man and man to God.

Part Four: The Hypostatic Union — Two Natures, One Person

The Confession, Chapter 8, Section 2, further states that Christ's two natures were "inseparably joined together in one person, without conversion, composition, or confusion. Which person is very God and very man, yet one Christ, the only Mediator between God and man."

This is the doctrine of the hypostatic union — the teaching that in the one Person of Jesus Christ, two complete natures (divine and human) are united without confusion, without change, without division, and without separation. This was the great christological definition of the Council of Chalcedon, convened in 451 AD in the city of Chalcedon, near Constantinople.

The Council of Chalcedon was called to address several heresies that had arisen regarding the person of Christ. Nestorius, the Patriarch of Constantinople, had been accused of dividing Christ into two persons — one divine and one human. Eutyches, a monk in Constantinople, went to the opposite extreme, teaching that Christ's two natures were mixed together into a single, hybrid nature that was neither fully divine nor fully human. The Council rejected both errors and affirmed that Christ is one Person in two natures, united "without confusion, without change, without division, without separation."

The Westminster divines embraced the Chalcedonian definition fully. Their language in the Confession carefully guards against every christological error. "Without conversion" — the divine nature was not converted or changed into a human nature. "Without composition" — the two natures were not blended into a third, composite nature. "Without confusion" — the properties of each nature remain distinct and are not transferred from one to the other. Christ's divine nature is not made finite, and His human nature is not made infinite. Each nature retains its own properties, yet they are inseparably united in the one Person of Christ.

This is a mystery that transcends human comprehension, but it is not a contradiction. It is the teaching of Scripture, confessed by the church for centuries, and essential to our salvation. If Christ's two natures were confused or mingled, He could not be a true Mediator — for a Mediator must fully represent both parties. If His two natures were divided into two persons, there would be no unity of the Mediator, and salvation would be impossible. The hypostatic union is the theological foundation upon which the entire work of redemption rests.

Part Five: The Three Offices of Christ — Prophet, Priest, and King

The Westminster Standards present Christ's work under the rubric of His three offices: Prophet, Priest, and King. This "threefold office" (munus triplex) was a framework used extensively by the Reformers and the Puritans to organize the biblical teaching about what Christ does as Mediator.

The Shorter Catechism, Question 23, states: "Christ, as our Redeemer, executeth the offices of a prophet, of a priest, and of a king, both in his estate of humiliation and exaltation."

Christ as Prophet: The Shorter Catechism, Question 24, asks: "How doth Christ execute the office of a prophet?" and answers: "Christ executeth the office of a prophet, in revealing to us, by his Word and Spirit, the will of God for our salvation."

Moses had prophesied that God would raise up a prophet like himself from among the people of Israel: "The LORD thy God will raise up unto thee a Prophet from the midst of thee, of thy brethren, like unto me; unto him ye shall hearken" (Deuteronomy 18:15). Jesus Christ is that Prophet — the final, definitive revelation of God to man. During His earthly ministry, He taught with an authority that astounded His hearers: "And it came to pass, when Jesus had ended these sayings, the people were astonished at his doctrine: For he taught them as one having authority, and not as the scribes" (Matthew 7:28-29). He revealed the Father's will not only in His spoken words but in His entire life — His compassion for sinners, His zeal for His Father's glory, His confrontation of hypocrisy, His tender care for the weak and the broken.

But Christ's prophetic office did not end with His ascension. He continues to teach His church through His Word (the Scriptures) and His Spirit (who illuminates the Word and applies it to the hearts of believers). Every faithful sermon, every Spirit-led reading of Scripture, every moment of divine illumination in the believer's heart is an exercise of Christ's ongoing prophetic ministry.

Christ as Priest: The Shorter Catechism, Question 25, asks: "How doth Christ execute the office of a priest?" and answers: "Christ executeth the office of a priest, in his once offering up of himself a sacrifice to satisfy divine justice, and reconcile us to God, and in making continual intercession for us."

The priestly office has two dimensions: sacrifice and intercession. As our sacrificial Priest, Christ offered Himself on the cross as the perfect, once-for-all sacrifice for sin. The book of Hebrews develops this theme at length, showing that Christ's sacrifice fulfills and surpasses every sacrifice offered under the Old Testament system. "Neither by the blood of goats and calves, but by his own blood he entered in once into the holy place, having obtained eternal redemption for us" (Hebrews 9:12). And again: "But this man, after he had offered one sacrifice for sins for ever, sat down on the right hand of God" (Hebrews 10:12).

As our intercessory Priest, Christ continually pleads on our behalf before the Father. "Wherefore he is able also to save them to the uttermost that come unto God by him, seeing he ever liveth to make intercession for them" (Hebrews 7:25). Every prayer we offer is heard by the Father because Christ, our High Priest, presents it in His own name. Every sin we commit is covered because Christ, our Advocate, pleads His blood on our behalf. "My little children, these things write I unto you, that ye sin not. And if any man sin, we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous: And he is the propitiation for our sins: and not for ours only, but also for the sins of the whole world" (1 John 2:1-2).

The Puritan Hugh Martin, along with many of the Westminster divines, placed special emphasis on Christ's intercession as the ongoing application of His sacrificial work. Christ does not offer a new sacrifice in heaven. He presents the merit of His completed sacrifice — the blood already shed, the atonement already made — before the Father on behalf of His people. This intercession is the guarantee that every benefit of Christ's death will be fully and finally applied to every one of His elect.

Christ as King: The Shorter Catechism, Question 26, asks: "How doth Christ execute the office of a king?" and answers: "Christ executeth the office of a king, in subduing us to himself, in ruling and defending us, and in restraining and conquering all his and our enemies."

Christ's kingship has a threefold exercise. He subdues His people to Himself — that is, by His Spirit and Word, He conquers the rebellious hearts of sinners and brings them willingly into His kingdom. He rules and defends His people — governing His church by His Word, His Spirit, and His providential care, and protecting them from all their foes. And He restrains and conquers all His enemies — Satan, sin, death, and hell — none of which can ultimately prevail against His people.

The Apostle Paul describes the ultimate triumph of Christ's kingly office in 1 Corinthians 15:25-26: "For he must reign, till he hath put all enemies under his feet. The last enemy that shall be destroyed is death." Christ's kingdom is not a future aspiration. It is a present reality. He reigns now, at the right hand of the Father. And He will continue to reign until every enemy is subdued and every knee bows before Him.

Part Six: The Humiliation of Christ

The Westminster Standards distinguish between Christ's "estate of humiliation" and His "estate of exaltation." The Shorter Catechism, Question 27, asks: "Wherein did Christ's humiliation consist?" and answers: "Christ's humiliation consisted in his being born, and that in a low condition, made under the law, undergoing the miseries of this life, the wrath of God, and the cursed death of the cross; in being buried, and continuing under the power of death for a time."

The Larger Catechism, Question 46, provides even greater detail. Christ's humiliation began with His incarnation — the eternal Son of God taking on the nature of a servant. He was born not in a palace but in a stable, in the small and insignificant town of Bethlehem, around 5 or 4 BC. He was laid in a manger — a feeding trough for animals. He was circumcised on the eighth day, submitting to the sign of the covenant as one who had come to fulfill the law. He grew up in Nazareth, a village of no great reputation, in the household of a carpenter named Joseph and his wife Mary.

Throughout His earthly life, Jesus experienced the full range of human suffering. He knew hunger (Matthew 4:2), thirst (John 19:28), fatigue (John 4:6), homelessness (Matthew 8:20), grief (John 11:35), and rejection (Isaiah 53:3). He was tempted by Satan in the wilderness (Matthew 4:1-11). He was misunderstood by His family (Mark 3:21). He was betrayed by one of His own disciples (Matthew 26:47-50). He was denied by another (Matthew 26:69-75). He was abandoned by all of them (Matthew 26:56).

But the deepest dimension of Christ's humiliation was His bearing of the wrath of God. In the Garden of Gethsemane, on the night before His crucifixion, Jesus experienced an agony so intense that His sweat became "as it were great drops of blood falling down to the ground" (Luke 22:44). He cried out: "Father, if thou be willing, remove this cup from me: nevertheless not my will, but thine, be done" (Luke 22:42). The "cup" was the cup of divine wrath — the full, unmitigated fury of God against sin, concentrated upon the sinless Son of God.

On the cross, that wrath was poured out. Jesus bore our sins in His body on the tree (1 Peter 2:24). He was made a curse for us (Galatians 3:13). He was made sin for us (2 Corinthians 5:21). The full penalty that our sins deserved — every last measure of divine justice — fell upon Him. And in the darkness of that Friday afternoon, He cried out with the most agonizing words ever uttered: "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?" (Matthew 27:46). This was not a cry of confusion. It was a cry of real, experienced dereliction. The Father, who had loved the Son with infinite love from all eternity, turned His face away as the Son bore the sins of the world.

Thomas Goodwin, who served as president of Magdalen College, Oxford, and was one of the leading Independent divines at the Westminster Assembly, wrote with profound reverence about the sufferings of Christ. Goodwin argued that the physical sufferings of Christ — the scourging, the crown of thorns, the nails, the cross — were the least part of His suffering. The true anguish was spiritual: the bearing of God's wrath, the experience of divine abandonment, the weight of the sins of all the elect concentrated upon one innocent soul. Goodwin marveled that Christ endured this voluntarily — not because He was compelled, but because He loved His people with an everlasting love.

Part Seven: The Exaltation of Christ

But humiliation was not the end. The Shorter Catechism, Question 28, asks: "Wherein consisteth Christ's exaltation?" and answers: "Christ's exaltation consisteth in his rising again from the dead on the third day, in ascending up into heaven, in sitting at the right hand of God the Father, and in coming to judge the world at the last day."

The resurrection of Christ is the vindication of everything He claimed and everything He accomplished. By raising Jesus from the dead, God the Father declared that the sacrifice had been accepted, the penalty had been paid, and death itself had been conquered. "He was delivered for our offences, and was raised again for our justification" (Romans 4:25). The resurrection is not an appendix to the gospel. It is the foundation of the gospel. "And if Christ be not risen, then is our preaching vain, and your faith is also vain" (1 Corinthians 15:14).

The historical evidence for the resurrection is compelling. Jesus was crucified under the Roman governor Pontius Pilate, likely on April 3, 33 AD. He was buried in the tomb of Joseph of Arimathea. On the third day — the first day of the week — the tomb was found empty. Over the following forty days, the risen Christ appeared to numerous witnesses: to Mary Magdalene, to the other women, to Peter, to the two disciples on the road to Emmaus, to the assembled apostles, and to more than five hundred brethren at once (1 Corinthians 15:3-8). These appearances transformed a group of frightened, discouraged men into bold, fearless preachers who were willing to die for the truth of the resurrection — and many of them did.

After His resurrection, Christ ascended into heaven from the Mount of Olives, as described in Acts 1:9-11: "And when he had spoken these things, while they beheld, he was taken up; and a cloud received him out of their sight. And while they looked stedfastly toward heaven as he went up, behold, two men stood by them in white apparel; Which also said, Ye men of Galilee, why stand ye gazing up into heaven? this same Jesus, which is taken up from you into heaven, shall so come in like manner as ye have seen him go into heaven."

Christ now sits at the right hand of God the Father — a position of supreme authority, power, and glory. This is His "session" at God's right hand, and it signifies His completed work and His ongoing reign. As the Apostle Peter declared on the day of Pentecost: "Therefore being by the right hand of God exalted, and having received of the Father the promise of the Holy Ghost, he hath shed forth this, which ye now see and hear" (Acts 2:33).

And Christ will come again. He will return in glory to judge the living and the dead. "For the Lord himself shall descend from heaven with a shout, with the voice of the archangel, and with the trump of God: and the dead in Christ shall rise first: Then we which are alive and remain shall be caught up together with them in the clouds, to meet the Lord in the air: and so shall we ever be with the Lord" (1 Thessalonians 4:16-17).

Part Eight: The Scope and Nature of Christ's Obedience

Before we examine the atonement specifically, we must understand a distinction that the Puritans and the Westminster divines considered vital: the distinction between Christ's active obedience and His passive obedience.

Christ's active obedience refers to His perfect, lifelong keeping of the law of God in thought, word, and deed. From the moment of His conception to the moment of His death, Jesus Christ never committed a single sin. He never entertained an impure thought. He never spoke a careless word. He never performed a selfish act. He loved God with all His heart, soul, mind, and strength, and He loved His neighbor as Himself — perfectly, consistently, and without interruption. He fulfilled every requirement of the moral law. He submitted to every ordinance of the ceremonial law. He obeyed every legitimate human authority placed over Him. As He Himself testified: "I do always those things that please him" (John 8:29).

This active obedience was not performed for His own benefit. Christ did not need to earn righteousness for Himself — He was already infinitely righteous as the eternal Son of God. His active obedience was performed on behalf of His people. It is this perfect righteousness that is imputed to believers in justification. When God declares a sinner righteous, He does so on the basis of Christ's active obedience — Christ's spotless record of law-keeping is credited to the believer's account.

Christ's passive obedience refers to His suffering and death — His bearing of the penalty that our sins deserved. Throughout His life, but especially in the final hours before and during His crucifixion, Christ endured suffering of every kind: physical, emotional, and spiritual. He was betrayed, arrested, tried before an unjust tribunal, scourged with a Roman flagellum — a whip embedded with pieces of bone and metal that tore the flesh — crowned with thorns, mocked, spat upon, stripped of His garments, and nailed to a Roman cross.

But the physical agony, terrible as it was, was the least part of His suffering. The true weight of Christ's passion was the spiritual suffering — the bearing of divine wrath against sin. On the cross, the sins of all the elect were laid upon Him, and the full fury of God's holy justice was poured out upon Him. He who knew no sin was made sin for us (2 Corinthians 5:21). He who was the beloved of the Father experienced the abandonment of the Father. He who was the source of all life tasted the bitterness of death.

The Puritan divine Thomas Manton, who served as one of the clerks to the Westminster Assembly and later as a prominent Nonconformist minister in London, preached powerfully on the sufficiency of Christ's obedience. Manton argued that Christ's obedience was sufficient to save because it was the obedience of a divine Person. The value of an act of obedience is determined by the dignity of the person who performs it. Because Christ is the eternal Son of God — infinite in worth and dignity — His obedience has infinite value and is therefore sufficient to secure the salvation of all the elect.

Robert Traill, the Scottish Puritan who suffered imprisonment for his faith and later ministered in London until his death in 1716, wrote a series of sermons on the sufficiency of Christ's obedience and the danger of adding anything to it. Traill warned against every form of human merit, every attempt to supplement Christ's work with our own works, every subtle suggestion that salvation depends in any measure upon human effort. "The righteousness of Christ," Traill insisted, "is the only ground of a sinner's acceptance with God."

Part Nine: The Atonement — What Christ Accomplished on the Cross

The Confession, Chapter 8, Section 5, provides a comprehensive summary of what Christ accomplished through His death: "The Lord Jesus, by his perfect obedience, and sacrifice of himself, which he, through the eternal Spirit, once offered up unto God, hath fully satisfied the justice of his Father; and purchased, not only reconciliation, but an everlasting inheritance in the kingdom of heaven, for all those whom the Father hath given unto him."

Several truths are packed into this statement. First, Christ's work includes both His "perfect obedience" and His "sacrifice of himself." Theologians distinguish between Christ's "active obedience" (His lifelong, perfect keeping of God's law) and His "passive obedience" (His suffering and death on the cross). Both are essential. Christ's active obedience provides the positive righteousness that is imputed to believers. Christ's passive obedience provides the atonement for sin — the removal of guilt and the satisfaction of divine justice. Together, they constitute the complete ground of our salvation.

Second, Christ's sacrifice "fully satisfied the justice of his Father." This is the doctrine of penal substitutionary atonement. Christ bore the penalty that our sins deserved. He took our place. He suffered in our stead. And His sacrifice fully, completely, and finally satisfied the demands of God's justice. Nothing more needs to be done. Nothing more can be done. The work is finished — as Christ Himself declared from the cross: "It is finished" (John 19:30).

Third, Christ's work "purchased not only reconciliation, but an everlasting inheritance." Reconciliation means that the broken relationship between God and man has been restored. We who were enemies of God have been made friends — more than friends, children. And the inheritance is "everlasting" — an eternal, unlosable, incorruptible inheritance in the kingdom of heaven.

The Puritan theologian John Owen devoted what is arguably the greatest work of theological reasoning in the English language to the doctrine of the atonement: The Death of Death in the Death of Christ, published in 1648. In this work, Owen argued with devastating logic that Christ's death actually accomplished salvation for all those for whom it was intended. It did not merely make salvation possible — it made salvation certain. Christ did not die to make the human race "saveable." He died to save His people from their sins. "For the Son of man is come to seek and to save that which was lost" (Luke 19:10).

Part Ten: The Extent of the Atonement — For Whom Did Christ Die?

The Westminster Confession addresses this question with clarity. Chapter 8, Section 8 states: "To all those for whom Christ hath purchased redemption, he doth certainly and effectually apply and communicate the same." And Chapter 3, Section 6, speaking of God's decree of election, declares that the elect are "redeemed by Christ, are effectually called unto faith in Christ by his Spirit working in due season, are justified, adopted, sanctified, and kept by his power through faith unto salvation."

The logic of the Confession is that Christ's death actually secures salvation for all those for whom it was intended. It is not a general, indefinite atonement that merely makes salvation possible for everyone without actually saving anyone. It is a particular, definite, effectual atonement that accomplishes salvation for the elect.

This does not mean that the value of Christ's death is limited. His death has infinite value — sufficient for the sins of the entire world ten thousand times over. But its design and intention are particular — it was offered for the specific purpose of actually saving those whom the Father had given to the Son. As Jesus Himself said: "I lay down my life for the sheep" (John 10:15). And again: "I pray for them: I pray not for the world, but for them which thou hast given me; for they are thine" (John 17:9).

John Owen's great work The Death of Death in the Death of Christ, published in 1648, remains the most thorough treatment of this subject in the Reformed tradition. Owen argued that if Christ bore the penalty for the sins of every person who ever lived, then no one could be condemned, for their penalty would already have been paid. But Scripture clearly teaches that some are condemned. Therefore, Christ's death must have been designed to secure the salvation of the elect specifically. This reasoning, while rigorous, flows from a heart of pastoral concern — Owen wanted believers to know that Christ's death actually, certainly, and infallibly saves them.

Part Eleven: Christ the Mediator and the Believer's Life

The person and work of Christ are not merely doctrines to be studied in the classroom. They are realities that transform every aspect of the believer's life.

Because Christ is our Prophet, we have a sure and certain word from God. We are not left to grope in darkness, guessing at the truth. Christ has revealed the Father to us and continues to teach us by His Word and Spirit. Therefore, we are to be people of the Word — reading, studying, memorizing, meditating upon, and obeying the Scriptures.

Because Christ is our Priest, we have access to God. The veil that separated man from God has been torn in two (Matthew 27:51). We can come boldly to the throne of grace (Hebrews 4:16). We can pray with confidence, knowing that our prayers are heard because our great High Priest presents them to the Father. And we can face our sins with honesty, knowing that we have an Advocate who has already paid for every one of them.

Because Christ is our King, we have a Lord to obey and a kingdom to serve. We are not our own — we have been bought with a price (1 Corinthians 6:19-20). Our lives are to be lived under Christ's authority, in Christ's service, and for Christ's glory. This is not bondage but freedom — the glorious liberty of the children of God.

Samuel Rutherford, the saintly Scottish commissioner to the Westminster Assembly who ministered at Anwoth and later taught at St. Andrews, was known above all for his passionate devotion to Christ. In his letters, written during a period of exile in Aberdeen from 1636 to 1638, Rutherford poured out his heart in language of extraordinary beauty and tenderness. He wrote of Christ as the altogether lovely one, the fairest among ten thousand, the one in whom all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge are hidden. For Rutherford, theology was not a dry academic exercise but a living, breathing encounter with the risen Christ.

Isaac Ambrose, the Puritan minister who served in Lancashire during the tumultuous years of the English Civil War and who was ejected from his living in 1662, wrote a devotional masterpiece entitled Looking Unto Jesus, first published in 1658. In this remarkable work, Ambrose guided his readers through every stage of Christ's life — from His eternal existence before all worlds, through His incarnation, ministry, death, resurrection, and ascension, to His present intercession and future return. Ambrose intended the work to be used as a daily devotional aid, encouraging believers to fix their gaze upon Christ in every circumstance of life. When they were tempted, they should look unto Jesus, who was tempted in all points yet without sin. When they were suffering, they should look unto Jesus, who suffered more than any man. When they were dying, they should look unto Jesus, who conquered death and holds the keys of hell and death.

The writer of Hebrews captures this devotional focus with matchless simplicity: "Looking unto Jesus the author and finisher of our faith; who for the joy that was set before him endured the cross, despising the shame, and is set down at the right hand of the throne of God" (Hebrews 12:2).

Let us follow Rutherford and Ambrose in their example. Let the doctrines we have studied today not remain merely in our heads but descend into our hearts. The Christ confessed in the Westminster Standards is the same Christ who stands at the door of your heart and knocks. He is the same Christ who says: "Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest. Take my yoke upon you, and learn of me; for I am meek and lowly in heart: and ye shall find rest unto your souls. For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light" (Matthew 11:28-30).

--- Closing ---

We have covered much ground today, but every truth we have examined points in one direction — to the matchless glory of our Mediator. Jesus Christ — fully God, fully man, Prophet, Priest, and King — is the center of the Christian faith and the hope of every sinner. He is the Mediator who bridges the infinite gulf between a holy God and fallen humanity. He is the Lamb who was slain, the Priest who intercedes, the King who reigns, and the Saviour who will return. Know Him. Trust Him. Love Him. Worship Him. And build your life upon the unshakeable foundation of His person and work. Until next time, students.
