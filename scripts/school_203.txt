SEGMENT 2: MAIN LESSON PART 1 -- BIBLICAL FOUNDATION
----------------------------------------

We are now ready for the core of today's study.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes in 1 John 4:7-12.

The love of God is one of the most frequently referenced yet least understood attributes of the divine nature. Many people speak of God's love in sentimental terms, reducing it to a vague benevolence or permissive acceptance. But the biblical teaching on God's love is far richer, deeper, and more demanding than popular sentiment suggests. The apostle John provides the most concentrated theological treatment of divine love in 1 John 4:7-12, a passage that rewards careful study and meditation.

John begins with a command rooted in a theological truth: "Beloved, let us love one another: for love is of God; and every one that loveth is born of God, and knoweth God." The ability to love others with genuine, self-sacrificing love is not a natural human capacity but a supernatural gift. Love "is of God" — it originates in Him, flows from Him, and can only be truly practiced by those who have been born of God. This means that the command to love is simultaneously a test of spiritual life. Those who truly love demonstrate that they have been born again; those who do not love demonstrate that they do not truly know God, regardless of their religious profession.

Then comes the foundational declaration: "He that loveth not knoweth not God; for God is love." This is one of only two places in all of Scripture where an attribute of God is expressed as an identity statement. We are told "God is light" (1 John 1:5) and "God is love" (1 John 4:8). God is not merely loving — love is essential to His very being. Yet we must be careful not to reverse this statement. John says "God is love," not "Love is God." God defines love; love does not define God. We do not start with a human conception of love and then apply it to God. Rather, we start with God and learn from Him what true love is.

And what does true love look like? John tells us in verses 9-10: "In this was manifested the love of God toward us, because that God sent his only begotten Son into the world, that we might live through him. Herein is love, not that we loved God, but that he loved us, and sent his Son to be the propitiation for our sins." The love of God is not an abstract feeling or a philosophical concept — it is manifested, displayed, made visible in a concrete historical act: the sending of His only begotten Son into the world. The word "propitiation" is crucial. It means a sacrifice that turns away wrath and satisfies justice. God's love did not bypass His holiness or ignore His justice. Rather, God's love found a way to satisfy justice while saving sinners — and that way was the cross of Christ.

Romans 5:8 adds another dimension to this truth: "But God commendeth his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us." The word "commendeth" means to demonstrate, to prove, to establish beyond doubt. God did not wait for us to become lovable before He loved us. He did not wait for us to seek Him before He sought us. He loved us while we were sinners — while we were rebels, enemies, and objects of His just wrath. This is what makes God's love so astonishing: it is not a response to anything good in us but a free, sovereign, unconditional act of grace.

John 3:16, perhaps the most well-known verse in all of Scripture, confirms this: "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life." The little word "so" does not merely indicate the degree of God's love ("God loved the world very much") but the manner of it ("God loved the world in this way — by giving His Son"). The giving of the Son is not merely an evidence of God's love; it is the supreme expression and definition of it. Everything we know about God's love, we learn at the cross.

Jeremiah 31:3 reveals that God's love is not merely an act but an eternal disposition: "The LORD hath appeared of old unto me, saying, Yea, I have loved thee with an everlasting love: therefore with lovingkindness have I drawn thee." God's love is everlasting — it has no beginning and no end. It does not fluctuate with our faithfulness or diminish with our failures. This is what theologians call the immutability of divine love: because God does not change, His love does not change. He loved His people before the foundation of the world, and He will love them throughout all eternity.

The practical implications of God's love are spelled out by John in verses 11-12: "Beloved, if God so loved us, we ought also to love one another. No man hath seen God at any time. If we love one another, God dwelleth in us, and his love is perfected in us." The love of God is not merely something to be received and enjoyed; it is something to be reflected and extended to others. If God loved us when we were unlovable, we are called to love others even when they are difficult, unpleasant, or hostile. The love of God in us is "perfected" — brought to completion, made mature — when it flows through us to others.

This love is not mere emotion. Biblical love is covenantal, sacrificial, and active. It seeks the highest good of its object regardless of the cost to the one who loves. It is the love described in 1 Corinthians 13:4-7: love that is patient and kind, that does not envy or boast, that bears all things, believes all things, hopes all things, endures all things. This kind of love is impossible apart from the regenerating work of the Holy Spirit. It is the fruit of the Spirit (Galatians 5:22), produced in the hearts of those who abide in Christ. It is the distinguishing mark of true discipleship, as Jesus Himself declared: "By this shall all men know that ye are my disciples, if ye have love one to another" (John 13:35).

The love of God also provides the ultimate security for the believer. Romans 8:35-39 asks, "Who shall separate us from the love of Christ?" and answers with a resounding list of impossibilities: neither tribulation, distress, persecution, famine, nakedness, peril, sword, death, life, angels, principalities, powers, things present, things to come, height, depth, nor any other creature "shall be able to separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord." This is the ground of Christian assurance: not our love for God, which is weak and wavering, but God's love for us, which is infinite, eternal, and unchangeable.

This biblical foundation is essential. Without it, everything else we study today would be like a house built on sand. But with it, we stand on solid rock.

SEGMENT 3: MAIN LESSON PART 2 -- PURITAN TEACHING
----------------------------------------

Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. Stephen Charnock wrote extensively on this subject in "Discourses upon the Existence and Attributes of God." The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life.

Stephen Charnock's treatment of God's goodness and love in his "Discourses upon the Existence and Attributes of God" stands as one of the most thorough Puritan expositions of divine benevolence. Charnock understood that God's love cannot be properly grasped apart from His other attributes. He taught that God's love is a holy love — it never compromises His righteousness. It is a sovereign love — it flows freely from God's own will, not from any obligation to the creature. It is a wise love — it always knows what is truly best for its objects. And it is an eternal love — it had no beginning and will have no end.

Charnock was particularly insightful in distinguishing between different aspects of God's love. He recognized a general love or benevolence that God extends to all His creatures. Psalm 145:9 declares, "The LORD is good to all: and his tender mercies are over all his works." God provides rain and sunshine, food and sustenance, to all people indiscriminately. Jesus pointed to this common grace as evidence of the Father's character: "He maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good" (Matthew 5:45). But Charnock also recognized a special, saving, covenantal love that God reserves for His elect — those whom He has chosen in Christ before the foundation of the world. This special love is described in Ephesians 1:4-5: "According as he hath chosen us in him before the foundation of the world, that we should be holy and without blame before him in love: Having predestinated us unto the adoption of children by Jesus Christ to himself, according to the good pleasure of his will."

Charnock was careful to emphasize that the love of God is not a response to human merit. God does not love us because we are lovable; He makes us lovable by His love. Charnock drew an important analogy: human love is typically attracted by something beautiful or worthy in its object, but divine love creates beauty and worth in its object. When God set His love upon Israel, He did not choose them because they were great or numerous or righteous. Deuteronomy 7:7-8 makes this explicit: "The LORD did not set his love upon you, nor choose you, because ye were more in number than any people; for ye were the fewest of all people: But because the LORD loved you." God's love is its own reason. He loves because He loves. This is the freedom and sovereignty of divine grace.

Thomas Watson added his own rich reflections on the love of God. Watson delighted in cataloging the evidences of God's love toward His people. He pointed to creation itself as an act of love — God made the world not because He needed it but because He desired to share His goodness with creatures. He pointed to providence as an ongoing expression of love — God sustains His people day by day, providing for their needs, protecting them from dangers they never see, and guiding their steps in ways they often do not recognize. But above all, Watson pointed to the cross as the supreme manifestation of divine love. Watson wrote with characteristic warmth: "God's love is like a river that overflows its banks. At the cross, the river of divine love burst all boundaries and flooded the world with mercy."

Watson was especially effective in showing how the love of God should transform the believer's experience. He identified several practical responses that should flow from a true apprehension of divine love. First, gratitude. If God has loved us with an everlasting love, given His Son for us, forgiven our sins, and secured our eternal salvation, then ingratitude is the most monstrous of all sins. Watson urged believers to cultivate a habit of thankfulness, beginning each day by recounting the mercies of God and ending each day by acknowledging His faithful love. Second, love for God in return. Watson acknowledged that our love for God will always be imperfect and inadequate compared to His love for us, but he insisted that it must be real, fervent, and growing. "If God is the fountain of love," Watson wrote, "then let our hearts be vessels that overflow with love for Him."

Third, Watson taught that the love of God should produce love for others. This was a constant theme in Puritan pastoral theology. The Puritans were not cold, stern moralists but warmhearted Christians who believed that genuine love for God must inevitably produce genuine love for one's neighbor. Watson observed that many professing Christians claim to love God while treating their fellow believers with coldness, suspicion, and cruelty. He regarded this as a contradiction that exposes the falsehood of their profession. If the love of God truly dwells in a person's heart, it will flow outward toward others just as water flows downhill — naturally, inevitably, and abundantly.

Charnock addressed a question that has troubled many believers: How can we be sure that God truly loves us? His answer was both theological and experiential. Theologically, the love of God is proved by the cross. If God gave His Son — the highest gift imaginable — for sinners, then nothing else can be in doubt. As Paul argues in Romans 8:32, "He that spared not his own Son, but delivered him up for us all, how shall he not with him also freely give us all things?" Experientially, the love of God is confirmed by the witness of the Holy Spirit in the believer's heart. Romans 5:5 declares, "The love of God is shed abroad in our hearts by the Holy Ghost which is given unto us." Charnock taught that the Spirit's work in assurance is not merely an intellectual conviction but a warm, experiential awareness of being loved by God — what the Puritans often called the "sense of God's love."

The Puritan understanding of divine love was thus profoundly balanced. It was theological without being cold, experiential without being subjective, practical without being shallow. Charnock and Watson together help us see that the love of God is the greatest truth in the universe — not because it tells us what we want to hear, but because it tells us what we most need to know: that the holy, sovereign, just, and faithful God has set His affection upon undeserving sinners and will never, ever let them go. This is the love that passes knowledge (Ephesians 3:19), the love from which nothing can separate us (Romans 8:39), and the love that will be the theme of our worship throughout all eternity.

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.

SEGMENT 4: DISCUSSION AND APPLICATION
----------------------------------------

The Puritans valued conference -- the practice of discussing truth together. Let us do so now.

Discussion Question 1: John says 'God is love' but does not say 'Love is God.' Why is this distinction important? What happens when we reverse the statement?

Discussion Question 2: How does the word 'propitiation' in 1 John 4:10 connect God's love to His holiness and justice? Why is this connection essential?

Discussion Question 3: Charnock distinguished between God's general love for all creatures and His special saving love for His elect. How does this distinction help us understand the biblical teaching on love?

Discussion Question 4: Watson taught that ingratitude is 'the most monstrous of all sins' in light of God's love. Do you agree? How can we cultivate a habit of genuine thankfulness?

Discussion Question 5: If God loved us 'while we were yet sinners' (Romans 5:8), how should this affect the way we love people who are difficult or hostile toward us?

Take time with each question. The Puritans valued what they called "holy conference," the practice of discussing spiritual truths together. Do not settle for quick, surface answers. Push deeper. Ask follow-up questions. Listen carefully to one another.

Application is where truth becomes life. It is not enough to know what 1 John 4:7-12 says. We must ask: how does this change the way I live today? How does this shape my thoughts, my words, my actions? The Puritans insisted that a truth that does not transform is a truth that has not been truly understood.

SEGMENT 4B: DEEPER THEOLOGICAL ANALYSIS
----------------------------------------

Let us now explore additional dimensions of God's love through further Scripture passages and the writings of other Puritan divines.

Consider the profound declaration of Deuteronomy 33:27: "The eternal God is thy refuge, and underneath are the everlasting arms: and he shall thrust out the enemy from before thee; and shall say, Destroy them." Moses, at the end of his life, blesses the tribes of Israel with this magnificent image. The everlasting arms of God are underneath His people — supporting them, upholding them, carrying them through every trial. This is not the distant love of a deity who watches from afar but the intimate, sustaining love of a God who is nearer to us than we are to ourselves. The Puritans delighted in this image because it conveyed both the strength and the tenderness of divine love. God's arms are everlasting — they never tire, never weaken, never let go. And they are underneath — catching us when we fall, supporting us when we stumble, bearing us up when our own strength fails.

Zephaniah 3:17 presents one of the most remarkable pictures of God's love in the entire Old Testament: "The LORD thy God in the midst of thee is mighty; he will save, he will rejoice over thee with joy; he will rest in his love, he will joy over thee with singing." This verse reveals that God not only loves His people but rejoices over them. He sings over them. The infinite, almighty Creator of heaven and earth — who has no need of any creature — delights in His people with singing. This is not the cold love of duty but the warm, exuberant love of joy. The Hebrew word translated "rest" carries the sense of being quiet and satisfied — God is satisfied in His love for His people. His love is not anxious or uncertain; it is calm, settled, and deeply content. What a staggering truth: the God of the universe sings over you with joy.

The apostle Paul, writing from a Roman prison, penned the great prayer of Ephesians 3:17-19: "That Christ may dwell in your hearts by faith; that ye, being rooted and grounded in love, May be able to comprehend with all saints what is the breadth, and length, and depth, and height; And to know the love of Christ, which passeth knowledge, that ye might be filled with all the fulness of God." Paul prays that believers may comprehend the four dimensions of Christ's love — its breadth, length, depth, and height. The breadth of God's love extends to every nation, tribe, and tongue. The length of God's love stretches from eternity past to eternity future. The depth of God's love reached down to the lowest pit of human sin and degradation to save us. The height of God's love lifts us up to the very throne of God as adopted sons and daughters. And yet, even with all four dimensions explored, Paul says this love "passeth knowledge" — it exceeds our capacity to fully understand. We can know it experientially, but we can never exhaust it intellectually.

The Puritan pastor Samuel Rutherford (c. 1600-1661) was one of the most eloquent writers on the love of Christ in the entire Puritan tradition. Rutherford was a Scottish Presbyterian who was banished to the remote town of Aberdeen for his Nonconformist views. During his exile, he wrote a series of letters to friends and parishioners that are among the most devotionally rich writings in the English language. Rutherford's letters overflow with rapturous expressions of love for Christ. He wrote from his place of banishment: "I have been in the cellars of affliction, and I have found the wines of Christ's love there." Rutherford taught that Christ's love is experienced most deeply not in times of prosperity but in times of suffering, when all earthly comforts are stripped away and the soul is left alone with its Redeemer.

Rutherford also wrote: "O what a fair and sweet-smelling flower is the love of Christ! Come and see a fair garden. I urge you to go in and pluck the flowers, for Christ's love surpasses all the fragrances of the earth." For Rutherford, the love of Christ was not merely a doctrine to be believed but a reality to be tasted, savored, and delighted in. He represents the experiential side of Puritan theology — the conviction that true Christianity involves not just the understanding of the mind but the engagement of the heart. The Puritans were not cold intellectualists; they were men and women who pursued the felt experience of God's love with passionate devotion.

Richard Sibbes, whom we mentioned in a previous lesson, also wrote beautifully about divine love. In "The Bruised Reed," Sibbes emphasized that God's love is especially tender toward the weak and the broken. He pointed to Psalm 103:13-14: "Like as a father pitieth his children, so the LORD pitieth them that fear him. For he knoweth our frame; he remembereth that we are dust." God does not expect from us more than we can give. He knows our frame — He knows that we are frail, weak, prone to failure, and easily discouraged. And knowing this, He pities us with the tenderness of a father who stoops down to comfort a weeping child. Sibbes taught that this fatherly compassion of God should give courage to every struggling believer. No matter how weak your faith, no matter how many times you have failed, God's love has not changed. He remembers that you are dust, and He loves you still.

The parable of the prodigal son in Luke 15:11-32 provides one of the most moving illustrations of God's love in all of Scripture. When the wayward son returns home, his father does not wait at the door with folded arms and a stern rebuke. Instead, "when he was yet a great way off, his father saw him, and had compassion, and ran, and fell on his neck, and kissed him" (Luke 15:20). The father ran — a deeply undignified act for an older man in the ancient Near East — because his love was so great that it could not wait. He fell on his son's neck and kissed him — not after the son had cleaned himself up and made proper amends, but while he was still filthy from the pig pen. This is the love of God toward returning sinners. He does not wait for us to become worthy of His love. He runs to meet us in our unworthiness, embraces us in our filth, and clothes us in the best robe of Christ's righteousness.

John 15:9-13 records Christ's own teaching on the nature of divine love: "As the Father hath loved me, so have I loved you: continue ye in my love. If ye keep my commandments, ye shall abide in my love; even as I have kept my Father's commandments, and abide in his love. These things have I spoken unto you, that my joy might remain in you, and that your joy might be full. This is my commandment, That ye love one another, as I have loved you. Greater love hath no man than this, that a man lay down his life for his friends." Christ loves us with the same love with which the Father loves Him — this is a love of infinite quality and intensity. And the proof of this love is His willingness to lay down His life for us. There is no greater love than this. The cross is love's final word, love's ultimate demonstration, love's everlasting monument.

SEGMENT 4C: PRACTICAL APPLICATION
----------------------------------------

Let us now consider how the love of God should shape our daily lives in concrete and practical ways.

First, let the love of God be the foundation of your identity. The world will tell you that your worth is based on your performance, your appearance, your achievements, or the opinions of others. But the gospel tells you that your worth is based on the love of God, who chose you before the foundation of the world, redeemed you with the precious blood of His Son, and sealed you with His Holy Spirit. When you are tempted to base your identity on anything other than God's love, return to this truth: "Behold, what manner of love the Father hath bestowed upon us, that we should be called the sons of God" (1 John 3:1).

Second, let the love of God drive out your fear. First John 4:18 declares, "There is no fear in love; but perfect love casteth out fear: because fear hath torment. He that feareth is not made perfect in love." Fear of failure, fear of rejection, fear of the future, fear of death — all of these fears are conquered by the love of God. If God is for us, who can be against us? If God loves us with an everlasting love, what have we to fear from any creature? The remedy for fear is not willpower or positive thinking but a deeper apprehension of how much God loves you.

Third, let the love of God overflow into love for the difficult people in your life. It is easy to love those who love us in return. But Christ commands something far more radical: "Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and pray for them which despitefully use you, and persecute you" (Matthew 5:44). This kind of love is impossible apart from the grace of God. But when you remember that God loved you while you were His enemy — while you were still in rebellion against Him — it becomes possible to love others who mistreat you. You love them not because they deserve it but because God has first loved you.

Fourth, let the love of God produce a generous spirit. Second Corinthians 8:9 connects divine love directly to generosity: "For ye know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, that, though he was rich, yet for your sakes he became poor, that ye through his poverty might be rich." Christ gave up the riches of heaven for the poverty of the manger, the carpenter's shop, and the cross — all for your sake. When you are tempted to cling tightly to your possessions, your time, or your comfort, remember the generosity of Christ's love and let it loosen your grip on earthly things. The Puritans were known for their remarkable generosity precisely because they understood that everything they had was a gift from a loving God, entrusted to them for the good of others and the glory of His name.

Fifth, let the love of God sustain you in seasons of spiritual dryness. Every believer will experience times when God's love does not feel real — when prayers seem to bounce off the ceiling, when Scripture seems dry, and when the heart is cold. In these seasons, do not trust your feelings. Trust the objective truth of God's Word. His love has not changed, even when your experience of it has dimmed. The sun does not stop shining simply because clouds have obscured it from your view. Persevere in the means of grace — prayer, Scripture, fellowship, worship — and in due time, the warmth of God's love will break through the clouds once more.

SEGMENT 6: CROSS-SUBJECT CONNECTIONS
----------------------------------------

One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

The concept of self-sacrificing love has shaped the course of Western civilization. Study how the early Christians' practice of radical love — caring for the sick during plagues, rescuing abandoned infants, sharing resources with the poor — transformed the Roman Empire from within. Historians like Rodney Stark have documented how the practical love of Christians was one of the primary reasons for the explosive growth of the early church. Connect this to John's teaching that the love of God must overflow into love for others.

Literature Connection:

The theme of sacrificial love is central to some of the greatest works of English literature. In Charles Dickens' 'A Tale of Two Cities,' Sydney Carton gives his life for another. In George MacDonald's fairy tales, love consistently triumphs through self-sacrifice. Discuss how these literary expressions of sacrificial love reflect — however imperfectly — the supreme sacrifice of Christ on the cross. Consider also how the Puritans influenced the literary tradition of writing about divine and human love.

Science Connection:

Modern psychology and neuroscience have studied the effects of love and attachment on human development. Research consistently shows that children who receive consistent, unconditional love develop stronger emotional health, greater resilience, and better cognitive function. The science of attachment theory confirms what Scripture has always taught: we are designed to thrive in relationships of secure love. Discuss how the biblical teaching on God's unfailing love provides the ultimate 'secure attachment' for the human soul.

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.

SEGMENT 4D: THE LOVE OF GOD IN COVENANT AND COMMUNION
----------------------------------------

Let us now press still deeper into the inexhaustible riches of God's love, examining its covenantal dimensions and its expression in the believer's intimate communion with Christ. The Puritans believed that the love of God is not merely a theological concept to be studied but a living reality to be experienced daily in the heart of every true believer.

The covenantal nature of God's love is powerfully expressed in Hosea 2:19-20: "And I will betroth thee unto me for ever; yea, I will betroth thee unto me in righteousness, and in judgment, and in lovingkindness, and in mercies. I will even betroth thee unto me in faithfulness: and thou shalt know the LORD." God uses the most intimate of human relationships — marriage — to describe His love for His people. The word "betroth" signifies a covenant commitment that is binding, exclusive, and permanent. God's love is not a passing sentiment but a covenant pledge sealed in righteousness, judgment, lovingkindness, mercy, and faithfulness. Every attribute of God undergirds and adorns His covenantal love. And the result of this covenant union is the deepest form of knowledge: "thou shalt know the LORD" — not merely intellectual knowledge, but the experiential, intimate knowledge of a bride who knows her bridegroom.

The Song of Solomon, which the Puritans regarded as one of the most spiritually rich books in all of Scripture, presents the love between Christ and His church under the figure of the love between a bridegroom and his bride. Song of Solomon 2:4 declares, "He brought me to the banqueting house, and his banner over me was love." The Puritans understood this verse as describing the believer's experience of being overwhelmed by the love of Christ. The "banqueting house" represents the rich provisions of the gospel — the Word, the sacraments, the fellowship of the saints — where the soul feasts upon the goodness of God. The "banner" of love is a military image — a standard raised over the beloved to claim ownership, provide protection, and declare allegiance. When God's love is our banner, we are marked as His own, protected by His power, and identified before all the world as the objects of His eternal affection.

The Puritan pastor John Flavel (c. 1627-1691) wrote with extraordinary tenderness about the love of Christ in his treatise "The Fountain of Life." Flavel taught that every aspect of Christ's earthly ministry — His incarnation, His obedience, His suffering, His death, His resurrection, and His ascension — was an expression of divine love. Flavel wrote that Christ's love is "a love that waters were not able to quench, a love that many floods could not drown, a love stronger than death itself." Flavel urged believers to trace the love of Christ through every stage of His redemptive work: "See His love in the manger, where He was born in poverty for your sake. See His love in the carpenter's shop, where He labored in obscurity for your sake. See His love in Gethsemane, where He sweat great drops of blood for your sake. See His love on Calvary, where He bore the wrath of God for your sake. See His love at the right hand of the Father, where He intercedes for you even now."

Flavel also addressed the question of how believers can know and experience the love of Christ in a personal way. He taught that the love of Christ is communicated to the soul primarily through the ministry of the Holy Spirit. Romans 5:5 declares, "The love of God is shed abroad in our hearts by the Holy Ghost which is given unto us." The word "shed abroad" suggests a lavish, overflowing outpouring — not a trickle but a flood. The Spirit takes the objective truth of God's love revealed in Scripture and makes it a subjective reality in the believer's heart. This is what the Puritans called "the sealing of the Spirit" or "the witness of the Spirit" — the internal testimony by which the Holy Spirit assures the believer that he is loved by God and belongs to God.

Consider the remarkable prayer of Psalm 63:1-3, written by David while he was in the wilderness of Judah: "O God, thou art my God; early will I seek thee: my soul thirsteth for thee, my flesh longeth for thee in a dry and thirsty land, where no water is; To see thy power and thy glory, so as I have seen thee in the sanctuary. Because thy lovingkindness is better than life, my lips shall praise thee." David declares that God's lovingkindness — His covenant love, His chesed — is better than life itself. This is an extraordinary evaluation. Life is the most precious possession any human being has. Yet David, who was no stranger to danger and death, says that God's love is more valuable than life. The Puritans frequently cited this verse to demonstrate that the experience of God's love is the highest and most satisfying experience available to the human soul. All other pleasures — health, wealth, fame, friendship, comfort — are pale shadows compared to the rich, deep, soul-satisfying experience of being loved by the living God.

The apostle Paul's prayer in Ephesians 3:14-21 carries this theme to its zenith. Paul prays that believers would be "strengthened with might by his Spirit in the inner man; That Christ may dwell in your hearts by faith; that ye, being rooted and grounded in love, May be able to comprehend with all saints what is the breadth, and length, and depth, and height; And to know the love of Christ, which passeth knowledge, that ye might be filled with all the fulness of God." This prayer reveals that the love of Christ is so vast that it requires supernatural strength to even begin to comprehend it. Its breadth extends to every nation under heaven. Its length stretches from eternity past to eternity future. Its depth reaches down to the lowest depths of human sin and misery. Its height lifts the redeemed to the very throne of God. And yet, after exploring all four dimensions, Paul says this love "passeth knowledge" — it surpasses the capacity of the human mind to fully grasp. We can know it experientially through the Spirit, but we can never exhaust it intellectually. It is an ocean without a shore, a sky without a horizon, a treasury without a bottom.

The Puritan divine Thomas Goodwin (1600-1680) devoted some of his most powerful writing to the experience of God's love in the believer's heart. In his work "The Heart of Christ in Heaven Towards Sinners on Earth," Goodwin explored the present-tense love of the glorified Christ for His struggling people on earth. Goodwin taught that Christ in heaven is not distant or indifferent toward His people but is actively, tenderly, and compassionately concerned for every one of them. He pointed to Hebrews 4:15: "For we have not an high priest which cannot be touched with the feeling of our infirmities." The word "touched" translates a Greek word meaning to suffer together with, to feel sympathetic pain. Christ in His glorified humanity still feels compassion for His people's sufferings. Goodwin wrote that "the same love that brought Christ down from heaven to die for sinners keeps Him in heaven to intercede for them." Christ's love did not end at the cross; it continues at the right hand of the Father, where He lives to make intercession for all who come to God through Him (Hebrews 7:25).

Psalm 136 provides one of the most striking literary expressions of God's enduring love in all of Scripture. Twenty-six times in twenty-six verses, the refrain is repeated: "for his mercy endureth for ever." The Hebrew word for "mercy" is again chesed — covenant love, steadfast love, lovingkindness. The psalmist traces God's chesed through creation, through the exodus from Egypt, through the wilderness wanderings, through the conquest of Canaan, and through the ongoing life of Israel. At every stage, through every event, the refrain remains the same: His love endures forever. This relentless repetition is intentional — it is designed to drive the truth deep into the heart of the worshiper. No matter what happens, no matter what changes, no matter what trials come, the love of God endures. It has no end. It has no limit. It has no condition that can nullify it. For His mercy endureth for ever.

Practical Application — Living in the Love of God:

1. Make the love of God the first truth you preach to your own soul each morning. Before your feet touch the floor, remind yourself: "The God who created the universe loves me personally, specifically, and eternally. He proved it at the cross. He sealed it with the Holy Spirit. And nothing in all creation can separate me from His love" (Romans 8:38-39).

2. Practice the discipline of tracing God's love in the ordinary details of your daily life. The Puritans called these "common mercies" — the breath in your lungs, the food on your table, the sunlight on your face, the friendship of fellow believers, the freedom to read God's Word. Each of these is an expression of divine love. Train yourself to see God's love not only in the dramatic moments of life but in the quiet, everyday provisions that are so easily taken for granted.

3. When you fail and sin, do not flee from God's love — flee to it. The prodigal son ran home to his father, and his father ran to meet him. God's love is not diminished by your failures; it is the very thing that picks you up, cleanses you, restores you, and sets you on your feet again. First John 2:1 assures us, "If any man sin, we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous."

4. Let the love of God overflow from your heart into the lives of those around you. Love is not truly received until it is truly given away. The more deeply you experience the love of God, the more naturally you will love others — even the unlovable, even the hostile, even the indifferent. As Christ loved you when you were unworthy, so love others regardless of their worthiness.

5. Meditate frequently upon the cross of Christ as the supreme demonstration of divine love. The Puritans spent hours contemplating the sufferings of Christ, not out of morbid fascination but out of adoring gratitude. At the cross, you see the full measure of God's love — love that held nothing back, love that paid the ultimate price, love that conquered sin and death and hell for your eternal salvation. Let the cross be the lens through which you view every circumstance of your life.
