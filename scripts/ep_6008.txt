PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"Hear the Right, O Lord"
June 8, 2026

---

"Hear the right, O Lord, attend unto my cry, give ear unto my prayer, that goeth not out of feigned lips. Let my sentence come forth from thy presence; let thine eyes behold the things that are equal. Thou hast proved mine heart; thou hast visited me in the night; thou hast tried me, and shalt find nothing; I am purposed that my mouth shall not transgress." -- Psalm 17:1-3

David comes before the Lord with a prayer of singular boldness, and the foundation of his boldness is not self-righteousness but sincerity. He does not claim to be without sin in every respect, but he does claim that his prayer is genuine, that it proceeds from an honest heart, that it is not the performance of a hypocrite but the cry of a soul that truly means what it says. "Hear the right, O Lord, attend unto my cry, give ear unto my prayer, that goeth not out of feigned lips." In this opening petition there are three requests for the same thing -- hear, attend, give ear -- and this threefold repetition reveals the urgency of a man who is in earnest, who cannot bear the thought that his prayer might go unheeded.

Spurgeon observed that there is nothing God despises more than feigned worship, and nothing He welcomes more readily than the sincere cry of a humble heart. The Pharisee who stands in the temple and makes his long and eloquent prayer, all the while thinking of the impression he is making upon the bystanders, is an abomination to the Lord. But the publican who smites upon his breast and can barely lift his eyes to heaven, crying "God be merciful to me a sinner," is heard in the courts of glory before the echo of his words has died away in the temple. God looks not upon the outward appearance of our prayers -- their length, their eloquence, their theological precision -- but upon the heart from which they spring.

"Let my sentence come forth from thy presence; let thine eyes behold the things that are equal." David appeals to God as his judge, and he does so with confidence because he knows that God's judgment is perfectly equitable. Unlike human judges, who may be swayed by bribes, or blinded by prejudice, or deceived by false testimony, the Lord sees all things exactly as they are. His eyes behold the things that are equal -- that is, the things that are right, just, and true. And the man who is in the right may go before this judge without fear, knowing that perfect justice will be done.

Thomas Manton expounded this passage with great care, noting that David's appeal to divine judgment is itself an act of extraordinary faith. Most men shrink from the scrutiny of God. They would rather be judged by their peers, who cannot see the heart, than by the Almighty, from whom nothing is hidden. But David invites the divine examination. "Thou hast proved mine heart; thou hast visited me in the night; thou hast tried me, and shalt find nothing." God has tested him in the furnace of affliction. God has visited him in the solitary watches of the night, when a man's true character is revealed, when there is no audience to impress and no reputation to maintain. And in all these trials, David's integrity has held.

Now this does not mean that David was sinless. He himself confesses elsewhere that he is a man of many transgressions. But in the specific matter that was the occasion of this prayer -- the false accusations brought against him by his enemies -- his conscience was clear before God. And this is a most important distinction that every believer must learn to make. We cannot claim universal innocence, for we are sinners saved by grace. But we can, in particular cases and specific accusations, appeal to God as our witness that the charge laid against us is false.

"I am purposed that my mouth shall not transgress." Here is a resolution of the highest order. David has determined, by the grace of God, that whatever his enemies may say about him, whatever provocations may be thrown in his face, whatever temptations may arise to respond in kind, he will govern his tongue. He will not repay slander with slander, nor railing with railing. He will keep his mouth as with a bridle, and will commit his cause to the righteous Judge.

John Flavel wrote extensively upon the government of the tongue, and he taught that the tongue is the best index of the heart. Out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh, and therefore the man who would govern his tongue must first govern his heart. A bitter fountain cannot send forth sweet waters, and a heart full of resentment and anger will inevitably produce words of bitterness and wrath. But a heart that is stayed upon God, a heart that trusts in His justice and rests in His providence, will produce words of patience, meekness, and grace, even in the face of the most grievous provocation.

Richard Baxter, that indefatigable Puritan pastor, urged his people to make this resolution of David their own. Purpose in your heart, he would say, that your mouth shall not transgress. Make this determination before the temptation comes, not in the heat of the moment when passion clouds the judgment and anger loosens the tongue. The man who enters the battle without having first put on his armour will surely be wounded. But the man who has resolved beforehand, in the strength of divine grace, that he will bridle his tongue, has already won half the victory.

Let us therefore come before God with the sincerity that David exemplifies in this psalm. Let us bring to Him prayers that are not feigned, hearts that are not divided, and tongues that are purposed not to transgress. And let us rest in the assurance that the Lord, whose eyes behold the things that are equal, will hear the right and will vindicate His people in the day of judgment. Amen.
