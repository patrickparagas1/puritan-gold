PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Pastoral Encouragement, Day 8
"Fighting Your Besetting Sin"
Thomas Brooks — Pastoral Counsel for the Struggling Believer


Good evening, dear brother or sister. Tonight I speak to you not as a lecturer but as a pastor, as a fellow traveler on the road of faith. Wherever you are tonight, whatever burden you carry, you are not alone. God is near, and He has a word for you.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Hebrews 12:1-2:

"Wherefore seeing we also are compassed about with so great a cloud of witnesses, let us lay aside every weight, and the sin which doth so easily beset us, and let us run with patience the race that is set before us, Looking unto Jesus the author and finisher of our faith; who for the joy that was set before him endured the cross, despising the shame, and is set down at the right hand of the throne of God."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


Every believer has a besetting sin — a particular weakness, a recurring temptation, a sin that clings more closely than any other. The author of Hebrews acknowledges this reality when he speaks of 'the sin which doth so easily beset us.' The Greek word suggests a sin that wraps itself around us, entangles us, trips us up at every turn. It is the sin we have confessed a hundred times, fought against a thousand times, and yet still struggle with. It is the sin that makes us wonder whether we are truly Christians at all.

Thomas Brooks, in his classic work 'Precious Remedies Against Satan's Devices,' addresses the believer's struggle against besetting sin with both realism and hope. Brooks is one of the most readable of the Puritan writers — warm, vivid, full of memorable illustrations. But beneath his engaging style is a mind of exceptional theological precision.

Brooks begins by identifying Satan's strategy regarding besetting sins. The devil knows your weakness. He has studied you for years. He knows the sin to which you are most inclined, the circumstances that make you most vulnerable, the rationalizations that your heart finds most convincing. And he exploits this knowledge ruthlessly. Brooks writes that Satan is like a skilled fisherman who uses different bait for different fish. He does not tempt the miser with lust or the proud man with sloth. He tempts each person with the sin to which that person is most naturally inclined.

But Brooks also offers remedies, and these are profoundly practical. First, know your besetting sin. Many believers live in a fog of vague spiritual dissatisfaction without ever identifying the specific sin that is most damaging their souls. Brooks counsels honest, prayerful self-examination. What sin do you fall into most frequently? What sin do you find most difficult to confess? What sin do you most readily excuse or minimize? That is likely your besetting sin.

Second, starve the sin. Brooks uses the vivid image of feeding and starving. Every sin has its food — the circumstances, habits, relationships, and thought patterns that nourish it. The man who wants to kill a particular sin must identify what feeds it and cut off the supply. If your besetting sin is anger, examine what triggers it: fatigue, frustration, unrealistic expectations. If your besetting sin is lust, examine what feeds it: what you watch, what you read, how you use your phone. Do not merely resist the sin when it erupts; trace it to its source and cut off the nourishment.

Third, Brooks counsels the believer to keep the cross ever in view. The most powerful weapon against sin is not fear of consequences but love for Christ. Brooks writes that the man who truly sees what his sin cost the Savior will find that sin losing its attractiveness. Gaze upon the suffering Christ — the agony in the garden, the scourging, the crown of thorns, the nails, the cry of dereliction — and then ask yourself: will I continue in the sin that crucified my Lord?

Fourth, Brooks emphasizes the importance of accountability. The man who fights his besetting sin alone is fighting at a serious disadvantage. Sin thrives in secrecy and isolation. Bring your struggle into the light. Confess to a trusted brother. Ask for prayer. Submit to questions. Brooks writes that Satan trembles when believers break the power of secrecy, for his greatest weapon is shame that keeps the sinner silent.

Patrick, what is the sin that most easily besets you? Name it. Not in vague terms — 'I struggle with sin' — but in specific terms. And then apply Brooks's remedies: identify it, starve it, look at the cross, and bring it into the light. The race is set before you. Christ, who has already finished His race, is both your example and your power. Run with patience, fixing your eyes on Him.


Let us pause and reflect. Consider these questions in the quiet of your heart.

Question 1: Can I name my besetting sin with specificity, or have I been avoiding honest self-examination?

Question 2: What circumstances, habits, or patterns feed my besetting sin, and what practical steps can I take to cut off that nourishment?

Question 3: Is there a trusted brother to whom I can confess my struggle and ask for accountability?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Father, I confess that there is a sin which does so easily beset me. I name it before You now without excuse or minimization. Give me the wisdom to identify what feeds it, the courage to starve it, and the faith to fix my eyes on the cross where my Savior bore its penalty. Break the power of secrecy in my life. Help me to run with patience the race set before me. In Jesus' name, Amen.

Goodnight, friend. Walk with God tomorrow.
