PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 12
"A Garden Enclosed"
Song of Solomon 4:12-16 — The Sacred Privacy of Marriage


Welcome, beloved couple. Tonight we enter one of the most intimate passages in the Song — the image of the bride as a garden enclosed, a spring shut up, a fountain sealed. This is a passage about the sacred privacy of marriage, the exclusive intimacy that belongs to no one else. Draw close and listen.

Song of Solomon 4:12-16:

"A garden enclosed is my sister, my spouse; a spring shut up, a fountain sealed. Thy plants are an orchard of pomegranates, with pleasant fruits; camphire, with spikenard, Spikenard and saffron; calamus and cinnamon, with all trees of frankincense; myrrh and aloes, with all the chief spices: A fountain of gardens, a well of living waters, and streams from Lebanon. Awake, O north wind; and come, thou south; blow upon my garden, that the spices thereof may flow out. Let my beloved come into his garden, and eat his pleasant fruits."


"A garden enclosed is my sister, my spouse; a spring shut up, a fountain sealed." In the ancient Near East, a walled garden was a private paradise — a place of beauty, fragrance, and refreshment hidden behind walls that protected it from the outside world. The bride is this garden. Her beauty, her intimacy, her deepest self is enclosed — reserved for one person alone.

The Puritans were deeply protective of marital privacy. William Gouge wrote that the physical and emotional intimacy of marriage is "a holy enclosure" that must never be violated by sharing it with others. What happens between husband and wife — in their bedroom, in their deepest conversations, in their most vulnerable moments — belongs to them alone. To share these things with friends, family, or the public is to break down the garden wall and invite the world into a space where it does not belong.

This principle is deeply counter-cultural in our age. Social media tempts us to publicize everything. Casual conversation invites complaints about our spouses. The culture normalizes sharing intimate details with friends. But the Song says the garden is enclosed. The spring is shut up. The fountain is sealed. There is a sacred exclusivity to marriage that must be fiercely guarded.

But look at what grows within this enclosed garden. The inventory is staggering: pomegranates, camphire, spikenard, saffron, calamus, cinnamon, frankincense, myrrh, aloes — "all the chief spices." This is not a sparse, bare enclosure. It is an overflowing paradise. The privacy of marriage is not a restriction; it is the condition for abundance. When the walls are secure, everything inside flourishes.

Thomas Gataker applied this to the emotional life of marriage. He wrote that true vulnerability — the kind that produces deep intimacy — is only possible within the safety of exclusive commitment. A wife who knows that her husband will never share her weaknesses with others can afford to be fully honest. A husband who knows that his wife will never ridicule his failures to her friends can afford to be fully vulnerable. The enclosed garden creates the safety that makes abundance possible.

"A fountain of gardens, a well of living waters, and streams from Lebanon." The bride is not just a garden but a fountain — a source of refreshment that flows and flows and never runs dry. Every spouse is called to be a source of life to the other. Not a drain, not a demand, but a well of living water. Richard Sibbes wrote that the best marriages are those where each spouse is more concerned with giving refreshment than receiving it. When both partners are pouring out, neither runs dry.

Then comes the bride's extraordinary invitation: "Awake, O north wind; and come, thou south; blow upon my garden, that the spices thereof may flow out." She calls upon the winds — both the cold north wind and the warm south wind — to blow upon her garden. Why? So that her fragrance would go out to her beloved. She is willing to endure both the cold and the warm — both difficulty and comfort — if it means that her love becomes more fragrant for her husband.

The Puritans saw in this a profound spiritual truth. It is often the trials of marriage — the north winds of conflict, suffering, and hardship — that produce the deepest fragrance. Richard Baxter wrote that "the best marriages are not those that have avoided all storms, but those that have been made fragrant by them." When you endure hard things together, the spices of patience, compassion, fortitude, and tenderness are released.

"Let my beloved come into his garden, and eat his pleasant fruits." The bride opens the garden. She invites her beloved in. The enclosed garden is not permanently locked; it is reserved for one person who has the right of entry. This is the picture of marital intimacy at its most beautiful — the free, willing, joyful opening of oneself to the one who belongs there.

Tonight, consider the garden of your marriage. Are the walls intact? Is the privacy guarded? And within those walls, is the garden flourishing or neglected? Water it tonight with conversation, with touch, with prayer, with laughter.


Turn to each other and consider these questions.

Question 1: Is there any area where the "wall" of your marital privacy has been breached — sharing too much with friends, family, or social media? How can you rebuild that wall?

Question 2: What "spices" grow in the garden of your marriage? What are the particular beauties and treasures of your relationship that exist nowhere else?

Question 3: The bride invites both north and south winds. How have the difficulties you have faced together actually made your marriage more fragrant and beautiful?


Tonight's prayer focus: Pray for the protection of your marriage's sacred privacy. Ask God to strengthen the walls of your garden and to make everything within it flourish. Pray that you would be a fountain of living water to each other — refreshing, life-giving, and never running dry.


Your marriage is an enclosed garden — a private paradise that God has planted and watered. Guard its walls. Tend its plants. And open its gates to each other with joy. Goodnight, beloved couple.
