--- Lesson ---

The Westminster Confession of Faith begins, fittingly, with the doctrine of Scripture. Chapter 1 is titled "Of the Holy Scripture," and it is one of the most important chapters in the entire Confession. The divines understood that before you can say anything authoritative about God, creation, salvation, or the church, you must first establish the authority of the source from which those truths are drawn. If the Bible is not trustworthy, then nothing built upon it is secure. But if the Bible is what it claims to be — the inspired, infallible Word of God — then its teachings carry the weight of divine authority.

The Confession opens with a remarkable statement about the necessity of Scripture. While the light of nature and the works of creation and providence reveal something of God's goodness, wisdom, and power, this general revelation is not sufficient to give us the knowledge of God necessary for salvation. Therefore, "it pleased the Lord, at sundry times, and in divers manners, to reveal himself, and to declare that his will unto his church." Over time, God committed this revelation to writing, "which maketh the holy Scripture to be most necessary."

This teaches us something fundamental: we need the Bible. The beauty of creation can point us toward God, but it cannot tell us the gospel. The voice of conscience can tell us something about right and wrong, but it cannot tell us how to be forgiven. Only the Scriptures reveal the plan of salvation — who God is, what He has done for us in Christ, and how we are to respond. Without the Bible, we would be lost in spiritual darkness.

The Confession then addresses the canon of Scripture — the list of books that belong in the Bible. It enumerates all sixty-six books of the Old and New Testaments by name and affirms that these books, "all of which are given by inspiration of God, to be the rule of faith and life." The Confession also addresses the books commonly called the Apocrypha, stating that while they may be read for historical interest, they are not part of the inspired canon and have no authority in matters of doctrine.

Next, the Confession makes a crucial affirmation about the authority of Scripture. The Bible's authority does not depend on the testimony of any man or church. The authority of Scripture rests on God alone, who is its author. The church recognizes and receives the Bible as authoritative, but it does not make the Bible authoritative. This is an important distinction. The Roman Catholic Church at the time of the Reformation claimed that the Bible's authority derived from the church's endorsement. The Westminster divines rejected this claim. The Bible is authoritative because God spoke it, period.

But how do we know that the Bible is truly from God? The Confession addresses this question with characteristic care. It acknowledges that external evidences — the majesty of Scripture's style, the unity of its parts, the scope of its teaching, the testimony of history — are all valuable confirmations. But the ultimate assurance that the Bible is God's Word comes from the internal testimony of the Holy Spirit. The Spirit who inspired the Scriptures also testifies in the hearts of believers that these writings are indeed divine. This is not a subjective feeling divorced from evidence but the Spirit's work of opening our eyes to see what is objectively true.

The Confession also affirms the sufficiency of Scripture. The Bible contains "the whole counsel of God, concerning all things necessary for his own glory, man's salvation, faith, and life." We do not need additional revelations, visions, or traditions to know what God requires of us. The Bible is enough. This principle — called "sola Scriptura" by the Reformers — was one of the foundational convictions of the Protestant Reformation and remains essential to the Reformed faith.

Two more affirmations deserve attention. First, the Confession teaches the clarity (or "perspicuity") of Scripture. While not every passage of the Bible is equally easy to understand, "those things which are necessary to be known, believed, and observed, for salvation, are so clearly propounded and opened in some place of Scripture or other, that not only the learned, but the unlearned, in a due use of the ordinary means, may attain unto a sufficient understanding of them." The Bible is not a sealed book reserved for scholars. Its central message — the gospel of Jesus Christ — is clear enough for anyone to understand.

Second, the Confession affirms that "the infallible rule of interpretation of Scripture, is the Scripture itself." When a passage is difficult to understand, we should look to clearer passages on the same subject for guidance. Scripture interprets Scripture. This principle protects us from imposing our own meanings on the text and keeps us anchored to the Bible's own testimony about its meaning.

These truths about Scripture are not merely academic. They are the foundation of your entire education, your faith, and your life. The Bible you hold in your hands is the inspired, authoritative, sufficient, clear Word of God. Treasure it. Study it. Trust it. And let it shape everything you think, say, and do.

--- Review Questions ---

Review Question 1: Why does the Westminster Confession begin with the doctrine of Scripture? Why is this the logical starting point for theology?

Review Question 2: According to the Confession, what is the ultimate basis for the authority of Scripture? Why does the Bible's authority not depend on the church?

Review Question 3: What does the Confession mean by the "sufficiency" and "clarity" of Scripture?

--- Activity ---

Read Chapter 1 of the Westminster Confession of Faith in its entirety. Then create a one-page summary chart with the following categories: Necessity of Scripture, Canon of Scripture, Authority of Scripture, Sufficiency of Scripture, Clarity of Scripture, and Interpretation of Scripture. Under each heading, write a one-sentence summary of the Confession's teaching and a supporting Bible verse.

--- Closing ---

The doctrine of Scripture is the bedrock of the Christian faith. Everything else we will study in this unit rests upon the conviction that the Bible is the trustworthy Word of God. Hold fast to this truth. Until next time, students.
