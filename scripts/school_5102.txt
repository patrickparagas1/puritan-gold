The Westminster Standards — Lesson 2

Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we examine what the Westminster Confession teaches about the Holy Scriptures — the foundation upon which every other doctrine rests. This is not merely one doctrine among many. It is the doctrine that makes all other doctrines possible, for if we cannot trust the Bible, we have no reliable source from which to learn about God, salvation, or the Christian life. The Westminster divines understood this with crystal clarity, which is why they placed the doctrine of Scripture first in their Confession. Let us pray and begin.

Lord, Thy Word is a lamp unto our feet and a light unto our path. As we study what the Westminster divines taught about the Bible, deepen our love for Scripture and our confidence in its truth. Open our eyes that we may behold wondrous things out of Thy law. Help us not merely to study this doctrine but to live under its authority with joy and reverence. In Christ's name, Amen.

--- Lesson ---

Part One: Why Scripture Comes First

The Westminster Confession of Faith begins, fittingly, with the doctrine of Scripture. Chapter 1 is titled "Of the Holy Scripture," and it is one of the most important chapters in the entire Confession — comprising ten carefully worded sections that address virtually every aspect of the Bible's nature and authority. The divines understood that before you can say anything authoritative about God, creation, salvation, or the church, you must first establish the authority of the source from which those truths are drawn. If the Bible is not trustworthy, then nothing built upon it is secure. But if the Bible is what it claims to be — the inspired, infallible Word of God — then its teachings carry the weight of divine authority, and every other doctrine flows from this foundation with unshakeable certainty.

This principle — that the doctrine of Scripture is logically prior to all other doctrines — was a hallmark of Reformed theology from the earliest days of the Reformation. Martin Luther, standing before the Diet of Worms in 1521, declared: "Unless I am convinced by Scripture and plain reason — I do not accept the authority of popes and councils, for they have contradicted each other — my conscience is captive to the Word of God." John Calvin, in the opening chapters of his "Institutes of the Christian Religion," established the authority of Scripture before proceeding to any other topic. The Westminster divines followed this same pattern, and for the same reason: everything depends on whether the Bible is what it claims to be.

The Puritan William Ames, whose "Marrow of Theology" was enormously influential in the Reformed tradition, wrote that Scripture is "the instrument of God through which the whole of theology is taught." If the instrument is flawed, the teaching is unreliable. If the instrument is perfect — as the divines believed — then the teaching carries divine authority. This is why Chapter 1 of the Confession is not merely an introductory formality. It is the foundation on which the entire edifice of Reformed theology is built.

Part Two: The Necessity of Scripture

The Confession opens with a remarkable statement about the necessity of special revelation. Section 1 reads: "Although the light of nature, and the works of creation and providence, do so far manifest the goodness, wisdom, and power of God, as to leave men inexcusable; yet are they not sufficient to give that knowledge of God, and of his will, which is necessary unto salvation; therefore it pleased the Lord, at sundry times, and in divers manners, to reveal himself, and to declare that his will unto his church; and afterwards for the better preserving and propagating of the truth, and for the more sure establishment and comfort of the church against the corruption of the flesh, and the malice of Satan and of the world, to commit the same wholly unto writing; which maketh the holy Scripture to be most necessary; those former ways of God's revealing his will unto his people being now ceased."

This carefully constructed sentence teaches several vital truths. First, it acknowledges what theologians call "general revelation" — God's self-disclosure through creation and providence. The Psalmist declared, "The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork. Day unto day uttereth speech, and night unto night sheweth knowledge" (Psalm 19:1-2, KJV). The apostle Paul affirmed that "the invisible things of him from the creation of the world are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made, even his eternal power and Godhead; so that they are without excuse" (Romans 1:20, KJV). General revelation is real and sufficient to render humanity "without excuse" before God.

But — and this is the crucial point — general revelation is "not sufficient to give that knowledge of God, and of his will, which is necessary unto salvation." The beauty of a sunset can point you toward God, but it cannot tell you the gospel. The voice of conscience can tell you that you have sinned, but it cannot tell you how to be forgiven. The order of nature can suggest the existence of a wise Creator, but it cannot reveal the plan of redemption. For the saving knowledge of God — who He is, what He requires, how sinners can be reconciled to Him through Christ — we need something more than general revelation. We need special revelation. We need the Bible.

The Confession further notes that God committed His revelation "wholly unto writing" for three reasons: for the "better preserving" of the truth against corruption and loss, for the "propagating" of the truth to all nations and generations, and for the "more sure establishment and comfort of the church." Oral tradition, no matter how carefully maintained, is vulnerable to distortion, addition, and loss. Written revelation is stable, verifiable, and transmissible. The written Word of God can be copied, translated, distributed, and studied with an accuracy and consistency that oral tradition simply cannot match.

The final clause — "those former ways of God's revealing his will unto his people being now ceased" — affirms the Reformation principle that the canon of Scripture is closed. God no longer reveals new doctrines through prophets, visions, or direct speech. The Bible contains everything we need for faith and life. This does not mean that God no longer speaks to His people — He speaks through His Word, by His Spirit, in the preaching of the gospel. But it does mean that no new revelations are to be expected or accepted as authoritative alongside Scripture. The Puritan John Owen made this point with characteristic force: "God doth not, nor ever did, reveal any new doctrine which is not contained expressly, or by necessary consequence, in this His written Word."

Part Three: The Canon of Scripture

The Confession then addresses the canon of Scripture — the list of books that belong in the Bible. Section 2 enumerates all sixty-six books of the Old and New Testaments by name, from Genesis to Revelation, and affirms that these books, "all of which are given by inspiration of God, to be the rule of faith and life." This enumeration was not accidental or unnecessary. In the seventeenth century, as in our own day, questions about the extent of the biblical canon were very much alive.

The Roman Catholic Church, at the Council of Trent in 1546, had officially declared the books of the Apocrypha — including Tobit, Judith, Wisdom of Solomon, Ecclesiasticus (Sirach), Baruch, and First and Second Maccabees — to be part of the canonical Scripture. The Reformers rejected this addition, noting that the Apocryphal books were not part of the Hebrew canon, were not quoted as Scripture by Christ or the apostles, contained historical errors and theological inconsistencies, and were not received as canonical by the early church fathers. Jerome, who translated the Latin Vulgate in the late fourth century, distinguished between the canonical books and the Apocryphal books, considering the latter useful for reading but not authoritative for establishing doctrine.

The Westminster Confession addresses the Apocrypha directly in Section 3: "The books commonly called Apocrypha, not being of divine inspiration, are no part of the Canon of the Scripture; and therefore are of no authority in the Church of God, nor to be any otherwise approved, or made use of, than other human writings." This is a clear and unambiguous statement. The Apocryphal books may be read for historical interest — they contain valuable information about the intertestamental period — but they have no authority in matters of doctrine. The sixty-six books of the Old and New Testaments, and no others, constitute the inspired and authoritative Word of God.

Part Four: The Authority of Scripture

Section 4 of the Confession makes a crucial affirmation about the basis of the Bible's authority: "The authority of the holy Scripture, for which it ought to be believed and obeyed, dependeth not upon the testimony of any man or church, but wholly upon God (who is truth itself), the author thereof; and therefore it is to be received, because it is the Word of God."

This statement was directed against a specific claim of the Roman Catholic Church. At the time of the Reformation, Roman Catholic theologians argued that the Bible's authority derived, at least in part, from the church's endorsement. The church, they said, had determined which books were canonical, and therefore the church was in some sense the authority behind the Bible. The Reformers — and the Westminster divines after them — rejected this argument decisively. The church did not create the canon. The church recognized the canon. The books of the Bible were authoritative from the moment they were written, because they were the Word of God. The church's role was to acknowledge and receive what God had already given, not to confer authority that the books did not already possess.

The distinction is important and has far-reaching implications. If the Bible's authority depends on the church, then the church stands above the Bible and can, in principle, overrule it. If the Bible's authority depends on God alone, then the Bible stands above the church and serves as its judge and guide. The Reformers' rallying cry of "sola Scriptura" — Scripture alone — expressed this conviction. The Bible alone is the final authority in all matters of faith and practice. Tradition, councils, creeds, and confessions are valuable — even the Westminster Confession acknowledges their usefulness — but they are all subordinate to Scripture and must be tested by Scripture.

The apostle Paul declared this principle when he wrote to Timothy: "All scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness: that the man of God may be throughly furnished unto all good works" (2 Timothy 3:16-17, KJV). The word "inspiration" in this passage translates the Greek "theopneustos," which literally means "God-breathed." Scripture is not merely a human production about God. It is God's own speech — breathed out by Him through human authors, carrying His authority, expressing His will, and accomplishing His purposes.

Part Five: The Internal Testimony of the Holy Spirit

But how do we know that the Bible is truly from God? How can we be certain that these sixty-six books, written by dozens of human authors over a span of roughly 1,500 years, are indeed the Word of the living God? The Confession addresses this question with characteristic care in Section 5.

First, the Confession acknowledges that there are many external evidences that support the divine origin of Scripture: "the heavenliness of the matter, the efficacy of the doctrine, the majesty of the style, the consent of all the parts, the scope of the whole (which is to give all glory to God), the full discovery it makes of the only way of man's salvation, the many other incomparable excellencies, and the entire perfection thereof, are arguments whereby it doth abundantly evidence itself to be the Word of God."

These arguments are sometimes called the "indicia" or marks of Scripture's divinity. The unity of the Bible — written over fifteen centuries by approximately forty authors, yet presenting a coherent and consistent message — is remarkable and without parallel in human literature. The heavenliness of its subject matter, the transforming power of its message, the majesty of its style, and the completeness of its teaching all point to a divine origin that transcends mere human authorship.

But the Confession does not rest the case for Scripture's authority on these external evidences alone. Section 5 continues: "Yet, notwithstanding, our full persuasion and assurance of the infallible truth, and divine authority thereof, is from the inward work of the Holy Spirit, bearing witness by and with the Word in our hearts."

This is the doctrine of the internal testimony of the Holy Spirit — the "testimonium Spiritus Sancti internum" — and it is one of the most distinctive contributions of Reformed theology. John Calvin articulated this doctrine with particular clarity in the first book of his "Institutes": "The testimony of the Spirit is more excellent than all reason. For as God alone is a fit witness of himself in his Word, so also the Word will not find acceptance in men's hearts before it is sealed by the inward testimony of the Spirit." The same Spirit who inspired the Scriptures also illuminates the hearts of believers to recognize and receive them as God's Word.

This is not a subjective feeling divorced from evidence. It is the Spirit's work of opening our eyes to see what is objectively true. The external evidences are real and valuable. But the ultimate certainty that the Bible is God's Word comes not from arguments and proofs but from the Spirit's work in the heart. This is why a simple, unlearned believer can have a deeper conviction of Scripture's authority than a brilliant scholar who lacks the Spirit's illumination. The Spirit seals the truth of Scripture upon the conscience with a certainty that no human argument can match.

Part Six: The Sufficiency of Scripture

Section 6 of the Confession affirms what is perhaps the most practically important doctrine about the Bible — its sufficiency: "The whole counsel of God, concerning all things necessary for his own glory, man's salvation, faith, and life, is either expressly set down in Scripture, or by good and necessary consequence may be deduced from Scripture: unto which nothing at any time is to be added, whether by new revelations of the Spirit, or traditions of men."

This is the doctrine of the sufficiency of Scripture, and it has enormous practical implications. It means that the Bible contains everything we need for faith and life. We do not need additional revelations — whether claimed by Roman Catholic tradition, Quaker inner light, charismatic prophecy, or any other source — to know what God requires of us. The Bible is enough.

The phrase "by good and necessary consequence may be deduced from Scripture" is worth careful attention. It acknowledges that not every truth the church needs to affirm is stated in so many words in the Bible. Some truths must be inferred from the explicit teaching of Scripture by logical deduction. The doctrine of the Trinity, for example, is not stated in a single proof text, but it is the necessary conclusion of the Bible's teaching that there is one God, that the Father is God, that the Son is God, that the Holy Spirit is God, and that the Father, Son, and Spirit are distinct persons. The Westminster divines recognized that faithful theology involves not only repeating what Scripture says explicitly but also drawing out the implications of what Scripture teaches.

At the same time, the Confession warns against adding to Scripture: "unto which nothing at any time is to be added, whether by new revelations of the Spirit, or traditions of men." This was a double-edged sword, cutting against both Roman Catholic claims for the authority of tradition alongside Scripture and enthusiast claims for new revelations of the Spirit beyond Scripture. The Reformation principle of "sola Scriptura" does not mean that tradition is worthless or that the Spirit is silent. It means that tradition must be judged by Scripture, and that the Spirit always works in accord with Scripture, never contradicting or supplementing the written Word.

The Confession also makes a helpful distinction in the same section: "Nevertheless we acknowledge the inward illumination of the Spirit of God to be necessary for the saving understanding of such things as are revealed in the Word; and that there are some circumstances concerning the worship of God, and the government of the church, common to human actions and societies, which are to be ordered by the light of nature and Christian prudence, according to the general rules of the Word, which are always to be observed." This acknowledges that while Scripture is sufficient for doctrine, there are practical matters — the time of worship services, the arrangement of church buildings, the details of church administration — that are not dictated by Scripture and must be decided by wisdom and prudence within the general principles of God's Word.

Part Seven: The Clarity of Scripture

Section 7 teaches what is often called the "perspicuity" or clarity of Scripture: "All things in Scripture are not alike plain in themselves, nor alike clear unto all; yet those things which are necessary to be known, believed, and observed, for salvation, are so clearly propounded and opened in some place of Scripture or other, that not only the learned, but the unlearned, in a due use of the ordinary means, may attain unto a sufficient understanding of them."

This is a carefully balanced statement. It does not claim that every passage of the Bible is equally easy to understand. The apostle Peter himself acknowledged that some things in Paul's letters are "hard to be understood" (2 Peter 3:16, KJV). But the Confession affirms that the central message of the Bible — the gospel of Jesus Christ, the way of salvation, the essential duties of the Christian life — is clear enough for anyone to understand who reads or hears it "in a due use of the ordinary means."

The phrase "in a due use of the ordinary means" is important. The Confession is not claiming that the Bible interprets itself without any effort on the reader's part. It is saying that when people use the ordinary means God has appointed — reading, studying, listening to preaching, praying for illumination — they can understand what the Bible teaches about the things that matter most. The Bible is not a sealed book reserved for scholars, priests, or theologians. Its central message is accessible to all.

The Puritan William Bridge, a member of the Westminster Assembly, captured this truth beautifully when he wrote concerning the Scriptures: "Though there be things in Scripture that are deep, yet there are shallows in this stream where a lamb may wade as well as depths where an elephant may swim." The essential truths of Scripture — the existence of God, the sinfulness of man, the saving work of Christ, the way of faith — are not hidden behind walls of academic complexity. They are presented with a clarity that the simplest reader can grasp.

This was a revolutionary claim in the seventeenth century. The Roman Catholic Church had long maintained that the Bible was too difficult and dangerous for ordinary people to read without the guidance of the church's Magisterium. The Reformers — Luther with his German translation, Tyndale and Coverdale with their English translations, Calvin with his commentaries and sermons — threw open the doors of Scripture to the common people. William Tyndale, who was martyred in 1536 for translating the Bible into English, reportedly told a critic: "If God spare my life, ere many years I will cause a boy that driveth the plough shall know more of the Scripture than thou doest." The Westminster divines shared Tyndale's conviction. The Bible is God's Word for all His people, not just for an educated elite.

Part Eight: Scripture Interprets Scripture

Section 9 of the Confession establishes a vital principle of biblical interpretation: "The infallible rule of interpretation of Scripture, is the Scripture itself; and therefore, when there is a question about the true and full sense of any Scripture (which is not manifold, but one), it must be searched and known by other places that speak more clearly."

This principle — that Scripture interprets Scripture — is sometimes called the "analogy of faith" or the "analogy of Scripture." It means that the Bible is its own best commentary. When we encounter a difficult passage, we should not impose our own interpretation or rely on human tradition. Instead, we should look to clearer passages on the same subject for guidance. The Bible is a unified book, inspired by a single divine Author, and its parts illuminate one another.

This principle has profound practical implications for how we study the Bible. It means we should always read passages in context — not pulling individual verses out of their literary and theological setting. It means we should compare Scripture with Scripture — looking at how the same themes, words, and doctrines are treated in different parts of the Bible. It means we should let the clearer passages guide our understanding of the more obscure passages, rather than building doctrines on isolated or ambiguous texts.

The Puritans excelled at this method of interpretation. Thomas Watson, in his "Body of Divinity," demonstrated remarkable skill in weaving together texts from across the entire Bible to illuminate individual doctrines. Matthew Henry, whose commentary on the whole Bible was published between 1708 and 1710, consistently interpreted Scripture by Scripture, drawing connections between passages that enriched the understanding of each. The Westminster Confession's insistence on this principle was not merely theoretical. It reflected the actual practice of the assembly's members, who spent years comparing text with text, passage with passage, in their effort to summarize the Bible's teaching faithfully.

Part Nine: The Supreme Judge in Controversies

The final section of Chapter 1, Section 10, brings the doctrine of Scripture to its practical climax: "The Supreme Judge, by which all controversies of religion are to be determined, and all decrees of councils, opinions of ancient writers, doctrines of men, and private spirits, are to be examined, and in whose sentence we are to rest, can be no other but the Holy Spirit speaking in the Scripture."

This is a comprehensive statement of biblical authority in its most practical form. When theological disputes arise — as they inevitably do — the final court of appeal is not a pope, a council, a creed, a confession, or a theologian. It is the Holy Spirit speaking in the Scripture. All human authorities — including the Westminster Confession itself — must bow before the authority of God's Word.

Notice the comprehensiveness of this statement: "all controversies of religion" — not some, but all; "all decrees of councils" — including the most ancient and venerable; "opinions of ancient writers" — including the church fathers; "doctrines of men" — including the most learned theologians; "and private spirits" — including claims of personal revelation. All of these must be examined by the standard of Scripture, and Scripture's verdict is final.

The phrase "the Holy Spirit speaking in the Scripture" is carefully chosen. The Bible is not a dead letter. It is the living voice of God, spoken by the Spirit through human authors, and continuing to speak by the Spirit to every generation. When we read the Bible, we are not merely encountering ancient human words about God. We are hearing God Himself address us through His appointed instrument. This is why the preaching of the Word is so powerful — not because the preacher is eloquent, but because the Spirit accompanies the faithful exposition of Scripture with divine power.

Part Ten: The Preservation of Scripture

Before we turn to the Puritans' love of Scripture, we must address a vital truth that undergirds the entire doctrine: the providential preservation of the Bible throughout history. The Westminster Confession affirms in Section 8 that the Old Testament was originally written in Hebrew and the New Testament in Greek, and that these Scriptures have been "kept pure in all ages" by God's "singular care and providence."

This doctrine of preservation is of immense importance. It would do us little good to know that the Bible was originally inspired by God if we could not be confident that it had been faithfully transmitted to us through the centuries. The Confession assures us that God's providence has watched over the transmission of His Word with singular care, ensuring that the Scriptures we possess today faithfully represent what the prophets and apostles originally wrote.

The history of the transmission of Scripture is a remarkable testimony to God's providential care. The Old Testament was copied by hand for centuries by Jewish scribes who developed meticulous procedures to ensure accuracy. The Masoretes — Jewish scholars who worked between the sixth and tenth centuries after Christ — developed an elaborate system of checks and cross-checks to preserve the accuracy of the Hebrew text. They counted the letters in each book, identified the middle letter, and compared their copies against these standards to detect and correct errors. The discovery of the Dead Sea Scrolls in 1947 — manuscripts dating from the third century before Christ to the first century after Christ — dramatically confirmed the reliability of the Masoretic text by showing that it had been transmitted with remarkable accuracy over more than a thousand years.

The New Testament text is the best attested of any ancient document. More than 5,800 Greek manuscripts of the New Testament survive — ranging from small fragments to complete copies — along with thousands of manuscripts in Latin, Syriac, Coptic, and other ancient languages, and extensive quotations in the writings of the early church fathers. The sheer volume of textual evidence is staggering. By comparison, the writings of the ancient Roman historian Tacitus survive in only a handful of manuscripts. The plays of Sophocles survive in fewer than a hundred. The New Testament's manuscript tradition is in a category entirely its own.

The Westminster divines, who lived before the great manuscript discoveries of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, did not have access to the full extent of this textual evidence. But they affirmed by faith what scholarship has since confirmed: that God has preserved His Word through the centuries, and that the Scriptures we possess are a faithful and reliable representation of the original inspired text.

The Confession also affirms, in the same section, that the Scriptures should be "translated into the vulgar language of every nation unto which they come" so that all people may worship God acceptably and have hope through the Scriptures. This commitment to translation was a hallmark of the Reformation. Martin Luther translated the Bible into German in 1534. William Tyndale began his English translation in the 1520s, a work that cost him his life. The Geneva Bible of 1560, produced by English Reformers in exile, became the most widely read English Bible of the Elizabethan era and was the Bible of Shakespeare, John Bunyan, and the Pilgrims who sailed to America on the Mayflower. The King James Version of 1611 — formally known as the Authorized Version — drew upon the work of Tyndale and the Geneva translators to produce what many consider the finest English prose ever written. The Westminster divines themselves used and quoted from the King James Version, and its language permeates the Confession and Catechisms.

The principle that the Bible should be translated into every language reflects the doctrine of the clarity of Scripture. If the Bible's message is accessible to all — "not only the learned, but the unlearned" — then it must be made available in languages that people can actually understand. The great translation efforts of the Reformation era, from Luther's German Bible to the King James Version, were practical expressions of the theological conviction that Scripture is clear, sufficient, and intended for all of God's people.

Part Eleven: The Relationship Between the Old and New Testaments

Section 6 of the Confession also addresses the relationship between the Old and New Testaments, teaching that the covenant of grace "was differently administered in the time of the law, and in the time of the gospel." The Old Testament administers the covenant through "promises, prophecies, sacrifices, circumcision, the paschal lamb, and other types and ordinances delivered to the people of the Jews," all of which pointed forward to Christ. The New Testament administers the same covenant through "the preaching of the Word, and the administration of the sacraments of baptism and the Lord's Supper."

This is a vital point. The Old and New Testaments are not two different books teaching two different religions. They are two parts of one unified story — the story of God's redemption of His people through Christ. The Old Testament anticipates Christ through prophecy, type, and shadow. The New Testament reveals Christ in His fullness. Together they form one coherent, progressive revelation of God's saving purposes.

Understanding this unity is essential for reading the Bible rightly. When we read the sacrificial system of Leviticus, we are reading about Christ — the true Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world. When we read the Psalms, we are reading the prayers and praises that find their ultimate fulfillment in Christ. When we read the prophets, we are reading promises that Christ came to fulfill. The Westminster divines understood this, and their Confession reflects a deeply Christocentric reading of all of Scripture.

Part Twelve: The Puritan Love of Scripture

Before we close, it is worth pausing to appreciate the Puritans' deep and fervent love for the Bible. The doctrine of Scripture, for the Westminster divines, was not merely an abstract theological proposition. It was the expression of a passionate devotion to the Word of God that shaped every aspect of their lives.

The Puritans read the Bible constantly. They read it in family worship — gathering their households morning and evening for the reading of Scripture, prayer, and catechetical instruction. They read it in private devotion — spending hours in personal Bible study, meditation, and prayer. They read it in public worship — devoting the largest portion of the Sunday service to the reading and expounding of Scripture. They read it in their studies — producing a body of biblical commentary, sermons, and theological works that remains one of the greatest achievements of Christian scholarship.

Thomas Watson urged believers to "read the word with a holy attention and reverence" and to "come to it as to the very oracles of God." William Gurnall, author of "The Christian in Complete Armour," wrote that the Bible is "the field wherein Christ is found; go and dig in that mine." Richard Baxter counseled, "Spend much of thy time in meditating on the word. Let it not be enough to hear it and read it; but meditate on it day and night." John Bunyan, the author of "The Pilgrim's Progress," though not a university-trained theologian, was so saturated in the language and theology of the Bible that his masterwork is essentially a dramatization of biblical truth.

The Westminster Confession's doctrine of Scripture, then, was not written by detached academics. It was written by men who loved the Bible with all their hearts, who staked their lives on its truth, and who wanted future generations to share their confidence in its divine authority. As the Psalmist declared: "O how love I thy law! it is my meditation all the day" (Psalm 119:97, KJV). And again: "Thy word is very pure: therefore thy servant loveth it" (Psalm 119:140, KJV). And once more: "The law of the LORD is perfect, converting the soul: the testimony of the LORD is sure, making wise the simple. The statutes of the LORD are right, rejoicing the heart: the commandment of the LORD is pure, enlightening the eyes" (Psalm 19:7-8, KJV).

Part Thirteen: The Doctrine of Scripture Under Attack

It is important to understand that the doctrine of Scripture has been under sustained attack since the Enlightenment of the eighteenth century, and that the Westminster Confession's affirmations remain as relevant and necessary today as when they were first written.

In the seventeenth century, the primary challenge to the Reformed doctrine of Scripture came from Roman Catholicism, which elevated tradition alongside Scripture, and from the radical sects, which claimed new revelations of the Spirit beyond Scripture. The Westminster Confession addressed both of these challenges directly.

In subsequent centuries, however, new challenges emerged. The rise of historical criticism in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries questioned the authorship, dating, and historical reliability of many biblical books. Scholars like Julius Wellhausen proposed that the Pentateuch was not written by Moses but was compiled from four separate sources centuries later. Others questioned the historical reliability of the Gospels, the authorship of the Pauline epistles, and the supernatural elements of the biblical narrative. These challenges were not mere academic exercises. They struck at the heart of the Bible's authority. If the Bible contains errors — historical, scientific, or theological — then it cannot be the infallible Word of God, and its authority is undermined.

The Reformed tradition has responded to these challenges with vigor and conviction. Scholars like B.B. Warfield of Princeton Seminary, writing in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, produced detailed defenses of biblical inerrancy that drew on the deepest insights of the Westminster Confession. Warfield argued that the Bible's own testimony about itself — that it is "God-breathed" and therefore carries divine authority — must be taken seriously as part of any honest evaluation of its claims. He distinguished between the original autographs of Scripture, which he affirmed were without error, and the copies and translations, which might contain minor scribal variations that do not affect any doctrine of the faith.

In the twentieth century, J. Gresham Machen, the founder of Westminster Theological Seminary, argued passionately that the doctrine of Scripture was the dividing line between historic Christianity and theological liberalism. In his landmark work "Christianity and Liberalism" (1923), Machen contended that a Christianity that abandons the authority of Scripture is no longer Christianity at all — it is a different religion masquerading under the same name. The Westminster Confession's insistence on the inspiration, authority, sufficiency, and clarity of Scripture provides the theological framework for this defense of the faith.

As you study the doctrine of Scripture, then, you are not merely learning about an ancient theological debate. You are equipping yourself to stand firm in a world that constantly questions, undermines, and attacks the authority of God's Word. The Westminster Confession's Chapter 1 is not only a statement of what the divines believed in 1647. It is a bulwark for the faith of every generation that receives it.

Part Fourteen: Practical Application

These truths about Scripture are not merely academic. They are the foundation of your entire education, your faith, and your life. Consider the following applications.

If the Bible is necessary, then you must read it. There is no substitute for regular, disciplined engagement with the Word of God. The Puritans recommended reading through the entire Bible at least once a year, and many of them read it far more frequently. Establish a daily reading plan and keep to it.

If the Bible is authoritative, then you must submit to it. The Bible is not a book of suggestions or opinions. It is the Word of the living God, and it claims your obedience. When Scripture speaks, the debate is over. The only appropriate response is humble submission and willing obedience.

If the Bible is sufficient, then you must trust it. You do not need new revelations, mystical experiences, or human philosophies to supplement what God has already given you in His Word. The Bible contains everything you need for faith and life. Rest in it.

If the Bible is clear, then you must study it. The central message of Scripture is accessible to you. You do not need a seminary degree to understand the gospel. But you do need to apply yourself — reading carefully, thinking deeply, comparing Scripture with Scripture, and praying for the Spirit's illumination.

If Scripture interprets Scripture, then you must know the whole Bible. Mastering individual verses is good, but understanding how the entire Bible fits together is better. The more of Scripture you know, the better you will understand any individual part of it.

If the Bible is the supreme judge in all controversies, then you must test everything by it. Do not accept any teaching — no matter how popular, how ancient, or how seemingly reasonable — without measuring it against the Word of God. The Bereans in the book of Acts were commended because "they received the word with all readiness of mind, and searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so" (Acts 17:11, KJV). Be like the Bereans. Search the Scriptures. Test all things. Hold fast to what is true.

If God has preserved His Word through the centuries, then you can have confidence in the Bible you hold in your hands. The same providential care that inspired the Scriptures has watched over their transmission, ensuring that the Word of God has come down to us faithfully and reliably. You are not reading a corrupted or unreliable text. You are reading the Word that God breathed out through His prophets and apostles, preserved by His sovereign care for your instruction and salvation.

The great Puritan pastor Thomas Brooks wrote: "The Word of God is the only rule to direct us how we may glorify and enjoy him." Let this be your conviction as well. The Bible you hold in your hands is the inspired, authoritative, sufficient, clear Word of God. Treasure it. Study it. Trust it. Obey it. And let it shape everything you think, say, and do.

--- Closing ---

The doctrine of Scripture is the bedrock of the Christian faith. Everything else we will study in this unit — the doctrine of God, creation, the fall, Christ, salvation, the church, and the last things — rests upon the conviction that the Bible is the trustworthy Word of God. If this foundation crumbles, everything built upon it collapses. But if this foundation holds — and it does, for it rests on the faithfulness of God Himself — then we can build our lives, our families, our churches, and our studies upon it with unshakeable confidence. As the prophet Isaiah declared: "The grass withereth, the flower fadeth: but the word of our God shall stand for ever" (Isaiah 40:8, KJV). Hold fast to this eternal Word. Let it be your daily bread, your guiding light, and the foundation upon which you build your entire life. Until next time, students.
