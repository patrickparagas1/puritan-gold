"Here I Stand — Martin Luther"
The Reformation — Lesson 2


Welcome back, students. Today we meet the man whose courage ignited the Protestant Reformation — Martin Luther. His story is one of the most dramatic in the history of the church, and its consequences are still felt around the world today. We have seen how the morning stars — John Wycliffe and Jan Hus — shone in the darkness of the late medieval period, preparing the way for the recovery of the gospel. Now the dawn has arrived, and the man whom God raised up to usher in a new day was an Augustinian monk from Saxony who discovered the gospel of grace in the pages of Holy Scripture.


THE WORLD INTO WHICH LUTHER WAS BORN

To understand Martin Luther and the revolution he unleashed, we must first understand the world of early sixteenth-century Europe. The medieval church still exercised enormous power over every aspect of life. The pope claimed supreme authority over both spiritual and temporal affairs. The sacramental system of the church — baptism, confirmation, the Eucharist, penance, extreme unction, holy orders, and marriage — was understood as the sole channel of saving grace. Salvation was not a free gift received by faith but a process administered by the church through its priests, its rituals, and its accumulated treasury of merit.

The system of indulgences had grown into a vast and corrupt enterprise. An indulgence was originally understood as a remission of the temporal punishment due for sin — punishment that a repentant sinner would otherwise endure in purgatory before being admitted to heaven. But by the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries, indulgences had been commercialized to a shocking degree. They were sold openly, and preachers promised that the purchase of an indulgence could release the souls of deceased loved ones from the sufferings of purgatory. The money flowed into the coffers of Rome, funding the construction of magnificent churches, the lavish lifestyles of the papal court, and the political ambitions of the papacy.

At the same time, a new intellectual movement — the Renaissance — was transforming European culture. The recovery of classical Greek and Latin literature, the development of humanist scholarship, and the invention of the printing press by Johannes Gutenberg around 1440 created a new world of learning and communication. The great humanist scholar Desiderius Erasmus of Rotterdam published a critical edition of the Greek New Testament in 1516, making it possible for scholars to read the apostolic writings in their original language rather than through the filter of Jerome's Latin Vulgate. These developments would prove crucial to the Reformation, for they gave men like Luther the tools to study the Scriptures with unprecedented accuracy and to spread their ideas with unprecedented speed.

LUTHER'S EARLY LIFE AND EDUCATION

Martin Luther was born on November 10, 1483, in Eisleben, in the German state of Saxony, the eldest surviving son of Hans and Margarethe Luther. His father was a copper miner and smelter who, through hard work and business acumen, rose to become a prosperous member of the middle class. Hans Luther was a man of fierce ambition for his son. He provided Martin with the best education available, sending him to schools in Mansfeld, Magdeburg, and Eisenach before enrolling him at the University of Erfurt in 1501.

Erfurt was one of the finest universities in Germany, known for its rigorous curriculum in the liberal arts and its thriving intellectual life. Luther excelled. He earned his Bachelor of Arts degree in 1502 and his Master of Arts degree in January 1505, ranking second in a class of seventeen candidates. His father was overjoyed and began addressing him with the formal German pronoun "Ihr" — a mark of respect. Hans Luther expected his son to pursue a career in law, a path that would bring the family wealth, status, and influence.

But God had other plans. The Scripture declares: "A man's heart deviseth his way: but the Lord directeth his steps" (Proverbs 16:9). The steps of Martin Luther were about to take a turn that would change not only his life but the course of Western civilization.

THE THUNDERSTORM AND THE MONASTERY

In July 1505, while traveling back to the university after visiting his parents, Luther was caught in a violent thunderstorm near the village of Stotternheim. A bolt of lightning struck the ground so close to him that it threw him to the earth. Terrified, convinced that he was about to die, he cried out in desperation: "Help me, Saint Anna! I will become a monk!" Saint Anna was the patron saint of miners — a figure dear to his father's profession and, in that moment of terror, the first name that came to Luther's lips.

Luther survived the storm, and he kept his vow. Two weeks later, on July 17, 1505, to the dismay and fury of his father, Luther entered the Augustinian monastery in Erfurt — one of the strictest monastic orders in Germany. He later reflected on this decision with characteristic honesty, acknowledging that his vow had been made in fear rather than in genuine devotion. But he believed he was bound by it, and he threw himself into the monastic life with the same intensity he had brought to his academic studies.

Luther was a model monk — indeed, he was an excessive one. He fasted beyond what the Rule required, sometimes going three days without food. He spent hours in prayer and confession, sometimes confessing for six hours at a stretch, cataloguing every sin he could recall and torturing himself with the possibility that he had overlooked some transgression. He subjected himself to vigils, sleeping without blankets in the bitter German winters. He later said that if any monk could have earned heaven through monastic discipline, it was he.

But nothing brought him peace. The great Puritan theologian John Owen would later write, with Luther clearly in mind: "The law discovers sin, and the wrath of God against it, but how to be delivered from it, it discovers not." This was precisely Luther's dilemma. The medieval system of salvation — the sacraments, the penances, the pilgrimages, the accumulated merit of the saints — was designed to address the problem of sin. But for Luther, the more he tried to satisfy the demands of the law, the more acutely he felt his own sinfulness. The question that haunted him was devastating in its simplicity: "How can a sinful man stand before a holy God?"

Luther's struggle was not merely psychological — it was profoundly theological. The medieval church taught a system of salvation that was, at its core, synergistic: God provides grace, but human beings must cooperate with that grace through the performance of good works, participation in the sacraments, and obedience to the commandments of the church. The problem, as Luther experienced it with excruciating clarity, was that this system could never provide certainty. How much cooperation is enough? How many good works are sufficient? How can a sinful human being ever be confident that he has done all that God requires? The answer, within the medieval framework, was that he cannot — and this uncertainty was considered a feature of the system, not a flaw. The church taught that Christians should maintain a state of holy anxiety about their salvation, trusting in the mercy of God but never presuming upon it.

For a man of Luther's moral seriousness and spiritual intensity, this teaching was spiritual torture. He did not approach God casually or carelessly. He approached God with the full weight of his intellect and his conscience, and what he found was a holy God before whom no human being could stand. The words of the psalmist expressed his anguish perfectly: "If thou, Lord, shouldest mark iniquities, O Lord, who shall stand?" (Psalm 130:3). Luther felt the crushing weight of divine holiness pressing down upon him, and all the religious exercises of the medieval church could not lift it.

His confessor and spiritual director, Johann von Staupitz, the vicar-general of the Augustinian order in Germany, tried to help. Staupitz was a kind and perceptive man who recognized that Luther's spiritual torment was not the result of unusual wickedness but of an unusually sensitive conscience. He directed Luther's attention away from his own sins and toward the wounds of Christ. He encouraged Luther to study the Scriptures and to pursue an academic career in theology. In 1508, Staupitz arranged for Luther to be transferred to the University of Wittenberg, and in 1512, Luther earned his Doctor of Theology degree and was appointed to the chair of biblical theology — a position he would hold for the rest of his life.

THE DISCOVERY OF THE GOSPEL

The Apostle Paul's experience on the road to Damascus transformed a persecutor of the church into its greatest missionary. In a similar fashion, though through the slow and agonizing process of scholarly study rather than through a single blinding vision, God was preparing to transform an anguished monk into the instrument of the greatest spiritual revolution since the apostolic age. As Isaiah had prophesied: "I will bring the blind by a way that they knew not; I will lead them in paths that they have not known: I will make darkness light before them, and crooked things straight. These things will I do unto them, and not forsake them" (Isaiah 42:16). God was leading Luther through darkness toward a light that would illuminate all of Europe.

It was in the lecture hall at Wittenberg that Luther's spiritual breakthrough came. As he prepared his lectures on the Psalms (1513-1515) and then on Paul's epistles to the Romans (1515-1516) and to the Galatians (1516-1517), Luther wrestled with the meaning of a phrase that appeared repeatedly in Scripture: "the righteousness of God."

Luther had always understood this phrase in its medieval sense — the righteousness by which God judges sinners. The "righteousness of God" was the standard of perfect holiness against which all human beings are measured and found wanting. It was the attribute of God that condemned Luther and drove him to despair. He later confessed that he had actually hated the phrase "the righteousness of God" because it seemed to speak only of judgment and wrath.

But as he studied Romans 1:16-17, the light broke through. Paul wrote: "For I am not ashamed of the gospel of Christ: for it is the power of God unto salvation to every one that believeth; to the Jew first, and also to the Greek. For therein is the righteousness of God revealed from faith to faith: as it is written, The just shall live by faith." Luther stared at those words — "The just shall live by faith" — and suddenly he saw what he had never seen before. The "righteousness of God" in this passage is not the active righteousness by which God judges sinners. It is the passive righteousness by which God justifies sinners — the righteousness that God freely gives to those who believe in Christ, received not by works but by faith alone.

Luther later described this moment as a spiritual earthquake: "I felt that I was altogether born again and had entered paradise itself through open gates. Here a totally other face of the entire Scripture showed itself to me." The entire landscape of his theology was transformed. If righteousness is a gift of God received by faith, then the whole medieval system of merit, works, indulgences, pilgrimages, and priestly mediation collapses. Salvation is not a process that the church administers; it is a verdict that God pronounces — a verdict of "not guilty" declared over the sinner who trusts in Christ alone. This doctrine — justification by faith alone, sola fide — became the cornerstone of the Reformation. It is the article, Luther would later say, on which the church stands or falls.

The apostle Paul had written to the Ephesians: "For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: Not of works, lest any man should boast" (Ephesians 2:8-9). And again to the Romans: "Therefore we conclude that a man is justified by faith without the deeds of the law" (Romans 3:28). And in his letter to the Galatians: "Knowing that a man is not justified by the works of the law, but by the faith of Jesus Christ, even we have believed in Jesus Christ, that we might be justified by the faith of Christ, and not by the works of the law: for by the works of the law shall no flesh be justified" (Galatians 2:16). These texts, which had been in the church's Bible for fifteen hundred years, now blazed with a light that had been obscured by centuries of tradition and institutional self-interest.

THE NINETY-FIVE THESES AND THE INDULGENCE CONTROVERSY

The immediate trigger for the Reformation was the sale of indulgences. In 1517, Pope Leo X — born Giovanni de' Medici, a member of the powerful Florentine banking family — authorized a massive indulgence sale to fund the construction of the new Saint Peter's Basilica in Rome. In Germany, the indulgence was promoted by Johann Tetzel, a Dominican friar of considerable rhetorical skill and questionable theology. Tetzel traveled through the German states with a dramatic sales pitch, reportedly declaring: "As soon as the coin in the coffer rings, the soul from purgatory springs." He told crowds that they could purchase the forgiveness of sins not only for themselves but for their deceased relatives languishing in purgatory. The people, desperate for assurance of their loved ones' salvation, opened their purses.

What many of the common people did not know was that the indulgence revenue was being divided. Half went to Rome for the construction of Saint Peter's. The other half went to pay off the massive debts of Albert of Brandenburg, the young Archbishop of Mainz, who had borrowed an enormous sum from the Fugger banking house to purchase his appointment to one of the most lucrative positions in the German church. The sale of indulgences was, at its root, a financial scheme designed to enrich the papacy and a German prince at the expense of the spiritual welfare of ordinary believers.

Luther was appalled. His parishioners in Wittenberg were crossing the border into Tetzel's territory, buying indulgences, and returning home with the belief that their sins had been forgiven and their deceased relatives released from purgatory — all without any genuine repentance or faith. Luther saw this as a direct assault on the gospel he had discovered in the pages of Romans.

Luther's pastoral heart was grieved. He saw the damage that the indulgence trade was doing to the spiritual lives of his people. They were being taught to trust in a piece of paper rather than in the mercy of God. They were being told that money could accomplish what only the blood of Christ could achieve. As a faithful shepherd of souls, Luther could not remain silent. The prophet Ezekiel had thundered against the false shepherds of Israel: "Woe be to the shepherds of Israel that do feed themselves! should not the shepherds feed the flocks? ... The diseased have ye not strengthened, neither have ye healed that which was sick, neither have ye bound up that which was broken, neither have ye brought again that which was driven away, neither have ye sought that which was lost" (Ezekiel 34:2, 4). Luther saw in Tetzel and the indulgence sellers the very same false shepherds that Ezekiel had condemned — men who fed themselves at the expense of the flock rather than feeding the flock with the bread of God's Word.

On October 31, 1517 — the eve of All Saints' Day — Luther posted his Ninety-Five Theses on the door of the Castle Church in Wittenberg. This was not, in itself, an act of rebellion. The church door served as the university's bulletin board, and posting theses was a standard invitation to academic debate. Luther's theses were written in Latin, the language of scholars, and were addressed to his fellow theologians. He was calling for a scholarly discussion of the theology and practice of indulgences.

But the content of the theses was explosive. Luther challenged the pope's authority to remit the punishments of purgatory. He argued that true repentance is an inward turning of the heart, not an outward act of purchasing a document. He declared that the pope would do better to empty purgatory out of love rather than for money. And he insisted that Christians should be taught that one who gives to the poor or lends to the needy does a better work than one who buys an indulgence.

The newly invented printing press transformed Luther's academic protest into a continental sensation. Within two weeks, copies of the Ninety-Five Theses had been translated into German and were circulating throughout the German-speaking lands. Within two months, they had reached every corner of Europe. Luther had struck a nerve that reverberated across the entire Western world.

Let us pause here to consider the specific content of some of Luther's most important theses. Thesis 1 declared: "When our Lord and Master Jesus Christ said 'Repent,' He willed the entire life of believers to be one of repentance." This was a direct challenge to the church's understanding of penance as a sacramental act performed before a priest. Luther was arguing that repentance is not a one-time ritual but a lifelong posture of the heart before God. Thesis 27 stated: "They preach only human doctrines who say that as soon as the money clinks into the money chest, the soul flies out of purgatory." Thesis 36 declared: "Every truly repentant Christian has a right to full remission of penalty and guilt, even without indulgence letters." And Thesis 62 proclaimed: "The true treasure of the church is the most holy gospel of the glory and grace of God."

These theses struck at the very heart of the medieval system. If the gospel of grace is the true treasure of the church, then indulgences are not merely unnecessary — they are a distraction from the true message of salvation. If every repentant Christian already has full remission through faith in Christ, then the entire edifice of papal indulgences, purgatorial punishments, and priestly mediation begins to crumble.

THE ESCALATION OF CONFLICT

Over the next several years, the conflict between Luther and Rome escalated rapidly. In 1518, Luther was summoned to appear before Cardinal Cajetan at the Imperial Diet in Augsburg. Cajetan demanded that Luther recant. Luther refused unless he could be shown from Scripture where he was wrong. The meeting ended in a stalemate, and Luther fled Augsburg by night.

In 1519, Luther participated in the famous Leipzig Debate with Johann Eck, a brilliant Catholic theologian. Eck maneuvered Luther into acknowledging that some of his views were shared by Jan Hus — the Bohemian reformer who had been condemned and burned by the Council of Constance in 1415. When Eck pressed him, Luther made a stunning declaration: he asserted that church councils could err and had erred, and that Scripture alone, not popes or councils, is the final authority in matters of faith. This was a decisive break with the Roman Catholic understanding of authority and placed Luther squarely in the tradition of Wycliffe and Hus.

In 1520, Luther produced three landmark treatises that laid out the theological program of the Reformation. In "To the Christian Nobility of the German Nation," he called on secular rulers to reform the church, arguing that the papacy had built three walls of defense — the claim that spiritual authority is superior to temporal authority, the claim that only the pope can interpret Scripture, and the claim that only the pope can call a council — and that all three walls must be torn down. In "The Babylonian Captivity of the Church," he attacked the entire sacramental system of Rome, arguing that the church had held the laity captive through a system of seven sacraments when Scripture recognized only two — baptism and the Lord's Supper. And in "The Freedom of a Christian," he articulated the doctrine of justification by faith and the priesthood of all believers with a beauty and clarity that captivated readers across Europe.

Pope Leo X responded by issuing the papal bull Exsurge Domine on June 15, 1520, condemning forty-one of Luther's propositions and threatening him with excommunication unless he retracted his teachings within sixty days. Luther's response was extraordinary. On December 10, 1520, before a crowd of students and faculty at the Elster Gate in Wittenberg, Luther publicly burned the papal bull, along with volumes of canon law and scholastic theology. It was an act of defiance that stunned Europe. Luther was declaring, in the most public way possible, that the authority of the pope was subordinate to the authority of Scripture.

THE DIET OF WORMS

The climax of Luther's confrontation with Rome came in April 1521, when he was summoned to appear before the Imperial Diet at the city of Worms, presided over by the young Emperor Charles V — the most powerful ruler in Europe, sovereign of the Holy Roman Empire, Spain, the Netherlands, and the vast Spanish possessions in the New World.

Luther traveled to Worms under a safe conduct from the emperor — a guarantee of protection that must have been cold comfort, given what had happened to Jan Hus under similar circumstances a century earlier. Along the way, crowds gathered to see the famous monk who had dared to defy the pope. Some warned him not to go. Luther reportedly replied: "Though there were as many devils in Worms as tiles on the rooftops, I would still go."

On April 17, 1521, Luther appeared before the Diet. He was shown a stack of his books and asked two questions: "Are these your books?" and "Do you recant what you have written?" Luther acknowledged the books as his own but asked for time to consider his answer — a request that surprised and disappointed many of his supporters, who expected the bold reformer to stand firm immediately.

The next day, April 18, Luther returned to the Diet and delivered one of the most famous speeches in the history of Western civilization. Speaking first in Latin and then in German, he distinguished between three categories of his writings: those that dealt with matters of faith and morals that even his opponents acknowledged as valuable; those that attacked the corruption of the papacy, which he could not retract without encouraging tyranny; and those directed against specific individuals, where he acknowledged that he may have been more harsh than necessary, though the substance of his arguments remained true. He concluded with words that have echoed through the centuries:

"Unless I am convinced by the testimony of the Scriptures or by clear reason — for I do not trust either in the pope or in councils alone, since it is well known that they have often erred and contradicted themselves — I am bound by the Scriptures I have quoted, and my conscience is captive to the Word of God. I cannot and I will not recant anything, since it is neither safe nor right to go against conscience. Here I stand. I can do no other. God help me. Amen."

Whether Luther actually spoke the precise words "Here I stand, I can do no other" has been debated by historians, as they do not appear in all contemporary accounts. But the substance of his declaration is beyond dispute: Martin Luther, a single monk armed with nothing but the Word of God and a conscience bound to its authority, stood alone against the combined might of the papacy and the empire and refused to yield.

THE WARTBURG CASTLE AND THE GERMAN BIBLE

The Diet of Worms issued the Edict of Worms on May 25, 1521, declaring Luther an outlaw and a heretic. His books were to be burned, and anyone who sheltered him was subject to punishment. But Luther's protector, Frederick the Wise, Elector of Saxony, had arranged a plan. On Luther's return journey from Worms, a group of armed horsemen staged a "kidnapping" and spirited Luther away to the Wartburg Castle, a fortress in the Thuringian forest near Eisenach.

Luther spent approximately ten months at the Wartburg, from May 1521 to March 1522, living under the assumed name "Junker Jorg" (Knight George) and growing a beard to disguise his appearance. Despite bouts of depression, physical illness, and what he described as spiritual assaults from the devil, Luther used this period of forced isolation with extraordinary productivity. In just eleven weeks, working from Erasmus's Greek New Testament, Luther translated the entire New Testament into German.

The period at the Wartburg was not without its struggles. Luther suffered from severe constipation, insomnia, and what he described as attacks by the devil — periods of intense spiritual darkness and doubt that the German language calls Anfechtungen. There is a famous legend that Luther threw an inkwell at the devil during one of these encounters; while the story is almost certainly apocryphal, it captures something of the intensity of Luther's spiritual warfare during this period. He wrote to Melanchthon during these months: "I sit here at ease, hardened and unfeeling — alas! praying little, grieving little for the Church of God, burning rather in the fierce fires of my untamed flesh." Luther was no plaster saint; he was a flesh-and-blood human being who struggled with temptation, doubt, and despair even as he performed the greatest literary and spiritual labor of his life.

Luther's German New Testament, published in September 1522 (and therefore known as the "September Testament"), was a literary and spiritual masterpiece. Luther did not merely translate the words of Scripture; he translated their meaning into the living, breathing language of the German people. He famously said that a translator must "look at the mouth of the mother in the home, the children in the street, the common man in the marketplace" to find the words that ordinary people actually use. The result was a translation of extraordinary power and beauty that not only gave the German people access to the Word of God but helped to shape and standardize the German language itself.

Luther went on to translate the entire Old Testament over the following years, completing the full German Bible in 1534 with the assistance of a team of scholars including Philip Melanchthon, his closest collaborator and the most brilliant humanist scholar of the German Reformation. The Luther Bible became the most widely read book in Germany and remained the standard German translation for centuries.

LUTHER'S MARRIAGE AND FAMILY LIFE

In June 1525, Luther took a step that scandalized many and delighted others: he married Katharina von Bora, a former Cistercian nun who had escaped from the convent at Nimbschen with a group of other nuns — reportedly smuggled out in a fish merchant's wagon among barrels of herring. Luther had helped arrange marriages for most of the escaped nuns, but Katharina remained unmatched. Luther, who had initially expressed no interest in marriage, eventually proposed — partly out of practical compassion, partly to please his father, and partly, as he frankly admitted, to spite the devil and the pope.

The marriage proved to be one of the happiest aspects of Luther's often turbulent life. Katharina was a woman of intelligence, energy, and practical ability. She managed the Luther household — the former Augustinian monastery known as the Black Cloister, which the Elector of Saxony had given to the couple as a wedding gift. She raised their six children (Hans, Elizabeth, Magdalena, Martin Jr., Paul, and Margarethe), managed the family finances, brewed beer, tended a garden and livestock, and hosted the endless stream of students, refugees, and visitors who filled the Luther home. Luther called her "my lord Katie" and "the morning star of Wittenberg," and his table conversations, recorded by students who boarded with the family, provide a vivid and often humorous portrait of domestic life in the Reformation era.

Luther's decision to marry was itself a theological statement. By renouncing his monastic vows and taking a wife, Luther was declaring that the celibate life is not spiritually superior to married life — that marriage is a holy calling ordained by God, not a lesser path for those who cannot attain the supposed perfection of celibacy. This conviction, rooted in the creation narrative of Genesis and in Paul's teaching that "marriage is honourable in all" (Hebrews 13:4), would transform the Protestant understanding of family life and vocation for centuries to come.

THE SPREAD OF THE REFORMATION IN GERMANY

Luther's message did not remain confined to Wittenberg. Throughout the 1520s and 1530s, the Reformation spread rapidly across the German-speaking lands. Princes, city councils, and entire territories embraced the Reformed faith, breaking with Rome and establishing Lutheran churches with the support of their secular governments. The imperial free cities of Nuremberg, Strasbourg, and Augsburg became early centers of the Reformation. Entire principalities — Saxony, Hesse, Brandenburg, and many others — adopted Lutheran worship and church organization.

The spread of the Reformation was not peaceful. The Peasants' War of 1524-1525, a massive uprising of the rural poor, invoked Luther's teachings about Christian liberty to justify their demands for economic and social justice. Luther initially sympathized with some of the peasants' grievances but was horrified by the violence of the revolt and eventually wrote a harsh pamphlet — "Against the Murderous, Thieving Hordes of Peasants" — calling on the princes to suppress the rebellion. The peasants were crushed with terrible brutality, and Luther's reputation among the common people was permanently damaged. This episode remains one of the most controversial chapters in Luther's life and a reminder that even the greatest instruments of God are fallible human beings.

Throughout these tumultuous decades, Luther continued to preach and teach at Wittenberg with unwavering devotion. His sermons, delivered in a plain and vigorous style, drew students and visitors from across Germany and beyond. His lecture hall was one of the most famous classrooms in Europe, and students who sat at his feet carried his teachings to every corner of the Protestant world. Luther's pastoral heart was evident in his preaching. He did not merely expound theological abstractions; he applied the gospel to the daily lives of his hearers — to their marriages, their work, their sufferings, their doubts, and their fears. He preached Christ crucified with a directness and power that transformed lives, as the apostle Paul had written: "For I determined not to know any thing among you, save Jesus Christ, and him crucified" (1 Corinthians 2:2).

In 1529, Luther and Zwingli met at the Marburg Colloquy in an attempt to resolve their disagreement over the Lord's Supper. Luther insisted on the real presence of Christ in the bread and wine, while Zwingli understood the Supper as a memorial. The two reformers agreed on fourteen of fifteen articles of faith but could not reach agreement on this crucial point. Luther famously chalked the words "Hoc est corpus meum" — "This is my body" — on the table and refused to budge. The failure at Marburg was a tragedy for Protestant unity and a reminder that even those who agree on the great doctrines of grace can divide over secondary matters.

In 1530, Philip Melanchthon presented the Augsburg Confession before Emperor Charles V — a formal statement of Lutheran doctrine that became the foundational confessional document of Lutheranism. The Augsburg Confession, with its careful and irenic articulation of Protestant theology, demonstrated that the Reformation was not merely a protest movement but a constructive theological enterprise capable of producing a coherent and systematic statement of Christian doctrine.

In 1546, shortly after Luther's death, Emperor Charles V launched the Schmalkaldic War against the Protestant Schmalkaldic League — an alliance of Lutheran princes and cities formed in 1531 for mutual defense. The emperor won an initial victory at the Battle of Muhlberg in 1547, capturing the Elector of Saxony and the Landgrave of Hesse. But the Protestant cause proved impossible to crush by military force. The princes regrouped, and the emperor, weary of decades of religious conflict, was eventually forced to negotiate.

The Peace of Augsburg in 1555, reached after decades of intermittent warfare between Catholic and Protestant princes, established the principle of cuius regio, eius religio — "whose realm, his religion" — granting each German prince the right to determine the official religion of his territory. While far from a guarantee of individual religious freedom, the Peace of Augsburg effectively ended the attempt to suppress Lutheranism by force and established the legal framework within which Protestantism could flourish in Germany and beyond.

LUTHER'S BROADER LEGACY

Luther's legacy extends far beyond the events of 1517 to 1521. Over the remaining twenty-five years of his life — he died on February 18, 1546, in Eisleben, the same town where he was born — Luther produced an enormous body of work. He wrote commentaries on Genesis, Galatians, the Psalms, and other books of the Bible. He composed catechisms — the Small Catechism for families and the Large Catechism for pastors — that became the standard tools of Lutheran religious education. He wrote hundreds of hymns, the most famous of which, "Ein feste Burg ist unser Gott" ("A Mighty Fortress Is Our God"), based on Psalm 46, became the anthem of the Reformation and is still sung in churches around the world.

Luther's five "solas" — sola Scriptura (Scripture alone), sola fide (faith alone), sola gratia (grace alone), solus Christus (Christ alone), and soli Deo gloria (to God alone be the glory) — became the defining principles of Protestant theology. These five principles were not merely academic propositions; they were the distilled essence of the gospel itself, recovered from centuries of distortion and proclaimed anew to a world that desperately needed to hear them.

The Puritan writer William Perkins, writing in the late sixteenth century, reflected on the significance of what Luther had recovered: "The doctrine of justification is the very sinew of all Christian doctrine. Take away this article, and it is impossible to rightly understand any other article of faith." Luther would have agreed entirely. Justification by faith alone was not one doctrine among many; it was the article on which the church stands or falls — articulus stantis et cadentis ecclesiae.

On February 18, 1546, Martin Luther died in his birthplace of Eisleben, where he had traveled to arbitrate a dispute between two local counts. He was sixty-two years old. His final days were marked by preaching, praying, and the same characteristic blend of faith and humor that had defined his entire life. On the night of his death, his companions found a slip of paper on which Luther had written his last recorded words: "We are beggars. This is true." It was a fitting summary of the doctrine of grace that had been the driving force of his entire ministry — the conviction that human beings bring nothing to God but their need and receive everything from Him as a free gift.

Luther was buried in the Castle Church in Wittenberg — the very church on whose door he had posted the Ninety-Five Theses twenty-nine years earlier. His grave lies beneath the pulpit from which he had preached thousands of sermons, and it remains a place of pilgrimage for Protestants from around the world.

Luther was not a perfect man. He could be intemperate, harsh, and even vulgar in his polemical writings. His later writings against the Jewish people are a source of deep shame and have been rightly condemned by Christians of every tradition. But for all his flaws, Luther was used by God to accomplish something of incalculable significance: the recovery of the gospel of free grace in Jesus Christ and the liberation of the Word of God from the chains of institutional control.

Luther's influence reached far beyond Germany. His ideas inspired reformers across Europe: Huldrych Zwingli in Zurich, Martin Bucer in Strasbourg, and eventually John Calvin in Geneva all built upon the foundations that Luther had laid. The Reformation in Scandinavia — Denmark, Sweden, Norway, and Finland — was directly inspired by Luther's writings and was led by men who had studied at Wittenberg. Even in England, where the Reformation took a different path under Henry VIII, Luther's theological influence was deeply felt. William Tyndale, the father of the English Bible, was profoundly shaped by Luther's writings, and the English Reformation owed an enormous debt to the German monk who had dared to stand alone at Worms.

Luther also transformed Christian worship. He believed that the congregation should participate actively in the service rather than passively observing the rituals performed by the priest. He introduced congregational singing in the vernacular, composing dozens of hymns that became the foundation of the Lutheran chorale tradition. His most famous hymn, "Ein feste Burg ist unser Gott" ("A Mighty Fortress Is Our God"), based on Psalm 46, captures the essence of Luther's faith: "A mighty fortress is our God, a bulwark never failing; our helper He amid the flood of mortal ills prevailing." This hymn has been called the "Battle Hymn of the Reformation," and it is still sung by Christians of every tradition around the world.

The prophet Jeremiah recorded the word of the Lord: "Is not my word like as a fire? saith the Lord; and like a hammer that breaketh the rock in pieces?" (Jeremiah 23:29). The Word of God, which had been locked away in Latin and buried under centuries of tradition, was now unleashed upon Europe — and it proved to be everything that Jeremiah said it was: a fire that consumed falsehood and a hammer that shattered the rock of institutional corruption. The Reformation had begun, and the world would never be the same.

The Puritan Thomas Brooks wrote: "An ounce of heart-knowledge is worth a pound of head-knowledge." Luther had both — and it was the union of profound theological understanding with deep personal experience of the grace of God that made him the instrument of reformation. Luther's courage was not his own — it was the courage that comes from a conscience captive to the Word of God. He stood before emperors and popes, and he did not flinch, because he stood upon the promises of the Almighty. The psalmist declared: "The Lord is on my side; I will not fear: what can man do unto me?" (Psalm 118:6). Luther knew that the God who inspired the Scriptures was the same God who would sustain those who trusted in them.
