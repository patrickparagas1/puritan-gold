PURITAN GOLD -- Study Topic: Female Piety, Part 8 of 10
"Trials and Suffering — Finding Grace in Affliction, with Puritan Strength"
John Angell James — Female Piety

---

Welcome back, dear listener, to Puritan Gold. We are deep in our study of Female Piety by John Angell James, and today we come to a subject that no honest treatment of the Christian life can avoid: the reality of trials and suffering. James devoted some of his most compassionate and theologically rich pages to this theme, knowing that the women he served faced sorrows of every kind — bereavement, poverty, sickness, loneliness, and the heartbreak of wayward children. He wanted them to know that the grace of God is sufficient for every trial. Let us hear him.

Let us read from Romans 5:3-5, in the King James Version:

"And not only so, but we glory in tribulations also: knowing that tribulation worketh patience; And patience, experience; and experience, hope: And hope maketh not ashamed; because the love of God is shed abroad in our hearts by the Holy Ghost which is given unto us."

And from 2 Corinthians 12:9:

"And he said unto me, My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness. Most gladly therefore will I rather glory in my infirmities, that the power of Christ may rest upon me."

--- Teaching ---

John Angell James did not write about suffering from a comfortable distance. He was a pastor for more than fifty years, and in that time he stood beside hundreds of women as they endured the deepest sorrows of human existence. He buried their children. He visited them on sickbeds. He counseled them through the collapse of marriages, the ruin of fortunes, and the long, slow decline of health. He knew the landscape of suffering intimately, and he wrote about it not with glib assurances but with the hard-won compassion of a man who had walked through the valley of the shadow of death with his people.

James begins his treatment of trials by establishing a fundamental theological truth: suffering is not random, not meaningless, and not outside the sovereign control of God. Whatever affliction comes to the child of God comes by divine appointment, through the hand of a Father who loves perfectly and who works all things together for good. This does not mean that suffering is pleasant or that the believer should pretend it does not hurt. It means that suffering has a purpose — a purpose rooted in the wisdom, love, and faithfulness of God.

The Puritans developed this theology of suffering with extraordinary depth and beauty. Thomas Watson, in his treatise All Things for Good, wrote that God can bring sweetness out of the bitterest providence. He compared the believer's trials to the refiner's fire, which does not destroy the gold but purifies it, burning away the dross and leaving behind something more precious than before. Watson argued that God uses affliction to wean us from the world, to draw us closer to Himself, to expose the hidden sins of our hearts, and to prepare us for the glory that is to come.

John Flavel, in his Mystery of Providence, made a similar argument. He urged believers to trace the hand of God through every affliction, to look for the lessons He is teaching, and to trust that the same God who ordained the trial has also ordained its end. Flavel wrote with particular tenderness about the suffering of women, noting that their trials are often of a peculiarly intimate and domestic nature — the loss of a child, the illness of a husband, the poverty of a household, the loneliness of a widow. These are trials that are felt in the very depths of the heart, and they require a grace that is equally deep.

James identifies several specific forms of suffering that women commonly face, and for each he offers the consolation of Scripture and the wisdom of pastoral experience.

First, bereavement. James writes with great feeling about the grief of a mother who has lost a child or a wife who has lost a husband. He does not minimize the pain. He acknowledges that bereavement is one of the most devastating experiences a human being can endure. But he points the grieving woman to the promises of God: that those who die in the Lord are not lost but safe in His keeping; that the separation is temporary and the reunion eternal; that the God who gave and took away is worthy of praise even in the darkest hour. "The LORD gave, and the LORD hath taken away; blessed be the name of the LORD" (Job 1:21). James urges the bereaved woman to cling to this confession, not as a denial of her grief but as the anchor of her hope.

Second, sickness and physical suffering. James observes that many women endure chronic illness, pain, and physical limitation with a patience that astonishes those who witness it. He points to the sufficiency of God's grace, which is made perfect in weakness. The woman who is confined to her bed is not confined from prayer. The woman who cannot attend public worship can still worship in the sanctuary of her own heart. The woman whose body is failing can still rejoice in the inward renewal of the spirit. "For which cause we faint not; but though our outward man perish, yet the inward man is renewed day by day" (2 Corinthians 4:16).

Third, the sorrow of a wayward child. James recognized that few sorrows cut deeper into a mother's heart than the sight of a child who has departed from the faith, who has fallen into sin, or who has embraced the ways of the world. To these mothers, James offers the counsel of patience, persevering prayer, and trust in the sovereignty of God. He reminds them that God is able to reach the most hardened heart, that the prayers of a faithful mother are never forgotten before the throne of grace, and that the story of the prodigal son is a story of hope — hope that the wanderer may yet return.

Fourth, poverty and financial hardship. James was keenly aware that many women in his congregation lived on the edge of destitution. He did not offer them empty platitudes. He pointed them to the God who feeds the birds of the air and clothes the lilies of the field, who has promised never to leave nor forsake His people, and who is able to supply all their needs according to His riches in glory by Christ Jesus. He also urged the church to fulfill its obligation to care for the poor, the widowed, and the fatherless — a duty that James took with utmost seriousness.

Through all of these discussions, James returns again and again to the central theme of his work: the sufficiency of divine grace. Whatever the trial, whatever the sorrow, whatever the loss — the grace of God is enough. Not just barely enough, but abundantly, overflowingly enough. The apostle Paul discovered this in his own thorn in the flesh, and James wants every suffering woman to discover it in hers.

Richard Sibbes, the great Puritan divine, wrote in The Bruised Reed that Christ will never break the bruised reed or quench the smoking flax. James treasures this image. The pious woman who is crushed by affliction is not abandoned by her Savior. She is held by Him. She is carried by Him. She is sustained by Him. And in the end, she will be glorified with Him. This is the hope that sustains the suffering saint, and it is a hope that will never be put to shame.

--- Application ---

1. If you are currently passing through a season of trial, take time to bring your suffering honestly before the Lord. Do not pretend that it does not hurt. Pour out your heart to Him, as David did in the Psalms, and then wait upon Him for comfort and strength.

2. Meditate upon 2 Corinthians 12:9 this week. Ask the Lord to show you how His grace is being made perfect in your weakness. Look for the evidences of His sustaining power in the midst of your trial.

3. Reach out to a woman in your church or community who is suffering — whether through bereavement, illness, financial hardship, or the sorrow of a wayward child. Offer her the comfort of your presence, your prayers, and your practical help.

4. Read Thomas Watson's All Things for Good or John Flavel's Mystery of Providence alongside this study. Let the Puritans deepen your understanding of God's purposes in suffering and strengthen your faith in His sovereign goodness.

--- Closing ---

Let us pray.

O Lord, Thou man of sorrows, who art acquainted with grief — we come to Thee in our afflictions, knowing that Thou dost understand our pain as none other can. We thank Thee that Thy grace is sufficient for every trial, that Thy strength is made perfect in our weakness, and that Thou art working all things together for our good and Thy glory. Comfort those among us who are bereaved. Strengthen those who are sick. Sustain those who are anxious for their children. Provide for those who are in need. And give us all the grace to trust Thee when we cannot trace Thee, to hope when all seems hopeless, and to rest in the assurance that nothing can separate us from Thy love in Christ Jesus our Lord. Amen.

Go in peace, dear listener. Your trials are not wasted, and your tears are not unseen. The God who counts the hairs of your head also bottles your tears. Trust Him. Until next time.
