PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Pastoral Encouragement, Day 29
"The Whole Counsel of God"
Preaching and Receiving All of God's Truth — Nothing Omitted, Nothing Added


Good evening, friend. Tonight we consider a phrase that should define the ministry of every church and the diet of every Christian: the whole counsel of God. In a day of selective theology and comfortable Christianity, the Puritans call us back to the fullness of divine truth — every doctrine, every command, every promise, every warning — received with gladness and proclaimed without apology.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Acts 20:26-27:

"Wherefore I take you to record this day, that I am pure from the blood of all men. For I have not shunned to declare unto you all the counsel of God."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


When the apostle Paul stood before the elders of the Ephesian church for the last time, he made a remarkable claim: he was pure from the blood of all men because he had not shunned to declare the whole counsel of God. This is a staggering statement. Paul understood that a minister who omits uncomfortable truths bears a measure of responsibility for the spiritual harm that follows. And he could say with a clear conscience that he had held nothing back.

The Puritans took this standard with absolute seriousness. They believed that the preacher's task was not to select the portions of Scripture that his audience would enjoy but to deliver the full message of God — the hard truths alongside the comforting ones, the demands alongside the promises, the warnings alongside the invitations. William Perkins, in his influential work "The Art of Prophesying," taught that the faithful minister must preach the law to the secure and the gospel to the broken, always applying the right word to the right condition of heart. To preach only law is to crush the penitent. To preach only gospel is to comfort the impenitent. Both are failures of pastoral care.

Richard Baxter echoed this conviction throughout his ministry. Baxter was known for his exhaustive pastoral visits in Kidderminster, where he met with every family in his congregation to discuss the state of their souls. He did not tell people what they wanted to hear. He told them what they needed to hear. And by God's grace, the town was transformed. Baxter wrote that a minister who withholds difficult truths to maintain his popularity is like a physician who refuses to diagnose a disease for fear of upsetting his patient. The patient may feel better in the moment, but the disease will kill him.

But the whole counsel of God is not only the responsibility of preachers. It is also the responsibility of hearers. Every Christian is called to receive all of God's truth, not merely the portions that suit his temperament or confirm his preferences. Thomas Watson warned against the danger of selective hearing. Some believers love the doctrines of grace but ignore the commands of holiness. Some love the promises of comfort but skip the warnings of judgment. Some embrace the love of God but resist the sovereignty of God. Watson counseled that a balanced diet of Scripture is as essential to the soul as a balanced diet of food is to the body.

The Puritans identified several doctrines that were commonly neglected in their day — and many of the same doctrines are neglected in ours. The doctrine of sin, with its uncomfortable insistence that the human heart is desperately wicked. The doctrine of hell, with its sobering reminder that judgment is real and eternal. The doctrine of self-denial, with its countercultural call to take up the cross daily. The doctrine of church discipline, with its unpopular insistence that the purity of the body matters. John Owen wrote that any church that omits these truths from its teaching will eventually produce a generation of believers who are doctrinally malnourished and spiritually weak.

At the same time, the whole counsel of God includes truths that the overly severe are tempted to neglect: the free offer of the gospel to all who will come, the tender compassion of Christ for sinners, the assurance of salvation for those who trust in Him, and the promise of ultimate victory over every enemy. A Christianity that is all law and no gospel is as distorted as a Christianity that is all gospel and no law. The Puritans held these truths in tension, and the result was a faith that was both rigorous and warm, both demanding and comforting.

The practical question for each of us is this: am I receiving the whole counsel of God? Am I sitting under teaching that challenges me as well as comforts me? Am I reading portions of Scripture that I find difficult, or am I staying in my favorite passages? Am I willing to be corrected by God's Word, even when the correction is painful?


First, examine your reading habits. Have you been avoiding certain books of the Bible or certain doctrines because they make you uncomfortable? This week, open a portion of Scripture you have been neglecting and ask God to speak to you through it.

Second, evaluate the preaching and teaching you are receiving. Does your church proclaim the whole counsel of God, or does it consistently avoid certain truths? If the latter, consider whether you need to supplement your spiritual diet through additional reading, study, or perhaps a conversation with your pastor.

Third, cultivate a willingness to be corrected. When you encounter a truth in Scripture that challenges your assumptions or confronts your behavior, do not dismiss it or explain it away. Receive it as a gift from a loving Father who knows what you need better than you do.


O Lord, Thy Word is a lamp unto our feet and a light unto our path — all of it, not merely the parts we prefer. Give us ears to hear the whole counsel of Thy truth. Give our pastors and teachers the courage to proclaim it faithfully. And give us the humility to receive it gladly, even when it wounds our pride and overturns our assumptions. Through Christ, who is the living Word, Amen.

Go in peace tonight, friend. The God who speaks hard truths speaks them because He loves you.
