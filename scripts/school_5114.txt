PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 14
"The Problem of Evil and Suffering"
Apologetics for Young Christians — Lesson 4


Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we confront the most emotionally powerful objection to the Christian faith — the problem of evil and suffering. This is the question that has troubled more hearts, shaken more faith, and caused more people to doubt the existence of God than perhaps any other. We will examine it with both intellectual rigor and pastoral sensitivity, and we will discover that the Christian worldview provides the most comprehensive and satisfying response to this profound challenge. Let us pray and begin.

Lord, Thou art good, and yet suffering fills the world Thou hast made. We come before Thee as students who seek understanding, knowing that Thy ways are higher than our ways, and Thy thoughts higher than our thoughts. Help us to think clearly and compassionately about this difficult question, and to find in Thy Word answers that satisfy both the mind and the heart. Comfort those among us who are suffering, and give us wisdom to comfort others. In Christ's name, Amen.


Of all the objections raised against the Christian faith, none is more common, more emotionally powerful, or more personally painful than the problem of evil. The Scottish philosopher David Hume, born in 1711 in Edinburgh, articulated it with characteristic precision when he paraphrased the ancient Greek philosopher Epicurus: "Is God willing to prevent evil, but not able? Then He is impotent. Is He able, but not willing? Then He is malevolent. Is He both able and willing? Whence then is evil?"

The argument, stated in its modern form, goes like this: If God is all-powerful (omnipotent), He has the ability to prevent evil and suffering. If God is all-good (omnibenevolent), He has the desire to prevent evil and suffering. But evil and suffering exist — in staggering, heartbreaking abundance. Therefore, either God is not all-powerful, or He is not all-good, or He does not exist at all.

This is a serious objection, and Christians should not dismiss it lightly. People who raise this question are often not being clever or hostile — they are hurting. They have experienced genuine suffering — the death of a child, the cruelty of abuse, the devastation of disease, the horror of war — and they are genuinely struggling to reconcile their pain with the existence of a loving, powerful God. Our response must be both intellectually rigorous and pastorally sensitive. We must speak truth, but we must speak it with tears in our eyes.

Let us begin by distinguishing between two forms of the problem of evil: the logical problem and the evidential problem.

The logical problem of evil claims that there is a logical contradiction between the existence of God and the existence of evil — that it is logically impossible for both to be true. This is the stronger form of the argument, and it was the dominant version through most of the twentieth century.

However, the logical problem of evil has been widely recognized by philosophers — including atheist philosophers — as having been decisively refuted. The philosopher Alvin Plantinga, born in 1932 in Ann Arbor, Michigan, who served as John A. O'Brien Professor of Philosophy at the University of Notre Dame, developed what is known as the Free Will Defense in his landmark work God, Freedom, and Evil, published in 1974. Plantinga demonstrated that there is no logical contradiction between the existence of God and the existence of evil, because it is possible that God has morally sufficient reasons for permitting evil.

Plantinga argued that it is logically possible that God created a world containing creatures with genuine free will — the ability to choose between good and evil — and that such creatures, left to their own choices, would sometimes choose evil. God could have created a world without free creatures, but such a world would also have been a world without genuine love, genuine virtue, genuine worship, or genuine relationship — because all of these require freedom. The value of a world containing free creatures who choose to love God and one another outweighs the risk that those same creatures will sometimes choose evil.

This argument was so successful that even the atheist philosopher J.L. Mackie, who had been one of the leading proponents of the logical problem of evil, acknowledged in his 1982 book The Miracle of Theism that Plantinga's Free Will Defense had effectively solved the logical problem. Mackie admitted that the logical contradiction he had argued for did not, in fact, exist.

But while the logical problem has been largely resolved in academic philosophy, the evidential problem of evil remains a powerful challenge. The evidential problem does not claim that God and evil are logically incompatible. Instead, it claims that the sheer amount, intensity, and distribution of suffering in the world makes the existence of God improbable. Even if God could have morally sufficient reasons for permitting some evil, is it really plausible that He has morally sufficient reasons for permitting the horrors we actually observe — the Holocaust, childhood cancer, devastating earthquakes, the rape of innocents?

This is a more difficult version of the problem, and it requires a more extensive response. Let us examine several resources that the Christian worldview provides for addressing it.

The first resource is the doctrine of divine omniscience and human limitation. The Christian faith teaches that God is omniscient — He knows all things, including things that we do not and cannot know. He sees the end from the beginning. He understands how every event — including every evil event — fits into the grand narrative of His purposes. We, by contrast, are finite creatures with severely limited knowledge. We see only a tiny fragment of reality. We understand only a small portion of the complex web of causes and effects that governs the course of events.

Consider an analogy. A child visits the doctor and receives a painful vaccination. From the child's perspective, a good and powerful parent is allowing something painful to happen — indeed, is holding the child still while a stranger sticks a needle into the child's arm. The child may conclude that the parent is either not loving or not powerful enough to prevent the pain. But the child is wrong. The parent has a morally sufficient reason for permitting the pain: the vaccine will protect the child from a far greater harm. The parent's perspective is larger than the child's.

In the same way, God's perspective is infinitely larger than ours. He sees the end from the beginning. He knows how every event — including every evil event — fits into the grand narrative of His purposes. As God declares through the prophet Isaiah, "For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways, saith the Lord. For as the heavens are higher than the earth, so are my ways higher than your ways, and my thoughts than your thoughts" (Isaiah 55:8-9).

This does not mean that suffering is trivial or that God is indifferent to our pain. It means that He is working on a canvas so vast that we cannot see the whole picture from our limited vantage point. The book of Job — one of the oldest books in the Bible, and the Bible's most extended treatment of the problem of suffering — makes exactly this point. Job, a righteous man, suffers terrible loss — his children, his wealth, his health. His friends insist that his suffering must be a punishment for some secret sin. Job protests his innocence and demands an explanation from God.

And what does God say when He finally speaks? He does not explain Job's suffering. He does not give Job a detailed account of the cosmic purposes behind his affliction. Instead, He asks Job a series of questions that reveal the vast gap between divine wisdom and human understanding: "Where wast thou when I laid the foundations of the earth? declare, if thou hast understanding. Who hath laid the measures thereof, if thou knowest? or who hath stretched the line upon it? Whereupon are the foundations thereof fastened? or who laid the corner stone thereof; When the morning stars sang together, and all the sons of God shouted for joy?" (Job 38:4-7).

God's response to Job is not an explanation but a revelation — a revelation of His infinite wisdom, power, and sovereignty. God is saying, in essence, "Job, you do not have the capacity to understand everything I am doing. But I do. Trust me." And Job, overwhelmed by the majesty and wisdom of God, responds with humility: "I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear: but now mine eye seeth thee. Wherefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes" (Job 42:5-6).

The Puritan pastor and theologian Thomas Boston, born in 1676 in Duns, Berwickshire, Scotland, and who served as minister of the parish of Ettrick from 1707 until his death in 1732, wrote about the mystery of suffering in his classic work The Crook in the Lot, published in 1737. Boston took his title from Ecclesiastes 7:13: "Consider the work of God: for who can make that straight, which he hath made crooked?" Boston argued that every person has a "crook in the lot" — some trial, some suffering, some difficulty that God has sovereignly appointed. And the proper response is not to rebel against the crook but to trust the God who appointed it and to seek the purposes He intends to accomplish through it. Boston wrote with the authority of personal experience — he endured the deaths of several of his children, the chronic illness of his wife, and the hardships of ministry in a remote Scottish parish.

The Puritan pastor Jeremiah Burroughs, born in 1600 in Essex, England, and who served as a preacher at Stepney and at Cripplegate, London, wrote one of the most beloved Puritan books on this theme: The Rare Jewel of Christian Contentment, published in 1648. Burroughs defined contentment as "that sweet, inward, quiet, gracious frame of spirit, which freely submits to and delights in God's wise and fatherly disposal in every condition." Notice that Burroughs does not define contentment as understanding everything God is doing. He defines it as trusting God's wisdom and fatherly care even when we do not understand. This is the posture of faith in the face of suffering — not blind optimism, not stoic resignation, but confident trust in a Father whose wisdom and love are infinitely greater than our own.

The Puritan theologian John Calvin, whose influence on the Puritan movement was immense, addressed the problem of evil in his Institutes of the Christian Religion (1536, final edition 1559). Calvin argued that God's sovereignty extends over all events, including evil ones, but that God is never the author of evil. Rather, God permits evil for purposes that are wise, just, and good — even when those purposes are hidden from our view. Calvin wrote, "God's providence does not exculpate our wickedness," meaning that human beings remain morally responsible for the evil they commit, even though God sovereignly governs all things. This is a mystery — the coexistence of divine sovereignty and human responsibility — but it is a mystery that Scripture clearly teaches.

Calvin also pointed to the example of the cross itself as the supreme illustration of this principle. The crucifixion of Jesus was the most evil act in human history — the murder of the sinless Son of God by wicked men. And yet God had ordained it from before the foundation of the world as the means of salvation for all who believe. Acts 2:23 states this with stunning clarity: "Him, being delivered by the determinate counsel and foreknowledge of God, ye have taken, and by wicked hands have crucified and slain." The same event was simultaneously the wicked act of sinful men and the predetermined plan of a sovereign God. God brought the greatest good out of the greatest evil.

The second resource the Christian worldview provides is the doctrine of free will, which we have already touched upon. God created human beings with genuine freedom — the ability to choose between good and evil. This freedom is a great and precious good, but it comes with the possibility — indeed, the certainty — of being misused. Much of the evil in the world — indeed, the vast majority of it — is the direct result of human beings making wicked choices. War, genocide, slavery, oppression, cruelty, abuse, exploitation, theft, murder — all of these are the products of human free will exercised in rebellion against God.

God could have created a world without free agents — a world of robots, programmed to obey and incapable of disobedience. But such a world would also have been a world without love, since love requires freedom. A forced love is not love at all. A coerced obedience is not genuine obedience. The possibility of evil is the necessary price of freedom, and freedom is the necessary prerequisite for love. As C.S. Lewis wrote in The Problem of Pain (1940), "Try to exclude the possibility of suffering which the order of nature and the existence of free wills involve, and you find that you have excluded life itself."

The third resource is the doctrine of the fall. The Christian worldview does not teach that God created a world of suffering. Genesis 1:31 records that when God surveyed His completed creation, He pronounced it "very good." There was no suffering, no disease, no death, no natural disaster in God's original creation. These entered the world through the rebellion of the first human beings against their Creator. In Romans 8:20-22, Paul describes the cosmic consequences of the fall: "For the creature was made subject to vanity, not willingly, but by reason of him who hath subjected the same in hope, Because the creature itself also shall be delivered from the bondage of corruption into the glorious liberty of the children of God. For we know that the whole creation groaneth and travaileth in pain together until now."

The pain, disease, natural disasters, and death that we experience are consequences of the fall — a world that has been broken by sin. The problem of evil, from a Christian perspective, is not a problem of God's design but a problem of humanity's rebellion. We live in a fallen world, and the suffering we experience is the fruit of that fall.

The Puritan theologian Thomas Goodwin, born in 1600 in Rollesby, Norfolk, England, who served as President of Magdalen College, Oxford, and was one of the leading theologians of the Westminster Assembly, wrote extensively about the effects of the fall on creation and on human nature. Goodwin argued that the fall did not merely affect human hearts — it affected the entire created order. The ground was cursed. Nature was disordered. Death entered the world. Every form of suffering we experience is traceable, ultimately, to the catastrophe of Genesis 3. Goodwin wrote that the proper response to suffering is not to blame God but to recognize the gravity of sin — to see suffering as a reminder of how devastating human rebellion against God truly is.

The fourth resource — and the most important — is the doctrine of redemption. This is the heart of the Christian answer to the problem of evil, and it is what sets Christianity apart from every other worldview's response to suffering.

God is not a passive spectator of our suffering. He has not stood at a distance, watching us writhe in pain, unmoved and unconcerned. He has entered into it. In the incarnation, God the Son — the eternal, infinite, all-powerful second Person of the Trinity — took on human flesh and entered the world of human suffering. He was born in poverty. He grew up as a refugee. He experienced hunger, thirst, fatigue, loneliness, rejection, betrayal, mockery, and physical agony. He wept at the tomb of His friend Lazarus (John 11:35). He agonized in the Garden of Gethsemane until His sweat was "as it were great drops of blood falling down to the ground" (Luke 22:44). He was beaten, scourged, crowned with thorns, spat upon, and nailed to a Roman cross.

The cross of Christ is God's ultimate answer to the problem of evil. On the cross, God did not merely explain suffering — He bore it. He took upon Himself the worst evil the world could produce — the unjust execution of the only truly innocent man who ever lived — and He transformed it into the greatest good the world has ever known: the salvation of sinners, the defeat of death, and the beginning of the restoration of all things.

Isaiah 53:4-5 describes this substitutionary suffering with breathtaking clarity: "Surely he hath borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows: yet we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted. But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed."

The cross does not remove all suffering from our lives in the present. But it does three things that no other worldview can offer. First, it assures us that God understands our suffering because He has experienced it Himself. The God we worship is not a distant deity who knows nothing of pain. He is a God who has entered the furnace of human suffering and emerged victorious. Second, it demonstrates that God can bring good out of the worst evil — that He is powerful enough and wise enough to take the darkest moment in human history and transform it into the brightest hope the world has ever known. If God can bring the salvation of the world out of the murder of His Son, then He can bring good out of any evil we experience. Third, it promises that suffering is not the final word — that because Christ has conquered death, we too shall conquer it.

The great Puritan pastor Richard Baxter, born in 1615 in Rowton, Shropshire, England, who suffered greatly from chronic illness throughout his life, wrote about suffering with the authority of personal experience. In his work The Saints' Everlasting Rest (1650), Baxter described the heavenly rest that awaits those who trust in Christ — a rest from all suffering, all sorrow, all pain, and all death. Baxter wrote, "O blessed day, when I shall rest with God! When I shall rest in the arms of love!" For Baxter, the hope of future glory was not a mere abstraction. It was the anchor that held him steady through decades of physical suffering.

The Puritan theologian Thomas Watson wrote about the cross with characteristic clarity in his Body of Divinity: "Here is wisdom in God, that He can turn evil to good. He can make the devil's arrow turn backward. He makes the sins of men subservient to His holy purposes." Watson understood that the cross reveals a God who does not merely prevent evil but who conquers it — who takes the worst the world can produce and transforms it into the instrument of its own redemption.

The apostle Paul experienced this reality firsthand. In 2 Corinthians 12:7-10, Paul describes his "thorn in the flesh" — some affliction, the specific nature of which Paul does not reveal, that caused him persistent suffering. Paul prayed three times for God to remove it, and God's answer was not removal but grace: "My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness." Paul's response was remarkable: "Most gladly therefore will I rather glory in my infirmities, that the power of Christ may rest upon me. Therefore I take pleasure in infirmities, in reproaches, in necessities, in persecutions, in distresses for Christ's sake: for when I am weak, then am I strong." Paul discovered that suffering, rather than disproving God's love, became a vehicle for experiencing God's power in a deeper way.

This is a truth that Christians throughout history have confirmed from their own experience. The church has always been strongest in times of persecution. The martyrs have always been the most powerful witnesses to the truth of the gospel. And believers who have walked through the valley of the shadow of death have consistently testified that they found God closer, more real, and more precious in their suffering than in their prosperity.

Corrie ten Boom, the Dutch Christian who was imprisoned in the Ravensbruck concentration camp during World War II for hiding Jews from the Nazis, wrote about her experience with a faith that astonishes even the hardest skeptic. Despite enduring unimaginable suffering — the death of her sister Betsie in the camp, the cruelty of the guards, the degradation of the conditions — Corrie testified to the sustaining power of God's grace and the reality of His presence even in the darkest of circumstances. Her story is a powerful refutation of the claim that suffering disproves God. It demonstrates, rather, that the God of the Bible is a God who meets His people in the furnace of affliction and sustains them with a grace that is truly sufficient.

The fifth resource is the doctrine of future glory. The Bible promises that evil and suffering are temporary. They are real, they are painful, they are grievous — but they are not the final chapter of the story. The final chapter is glory.

Revelation 21:4 promises, "And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away." One day, God will set all things right. He will wipe away every tear. He will heal every wound. He will punish every injustice. He will establish a new heaven and a new earth where righteousness dwells (2 Peter 3:13). The suffering of this present world is not the end of the story. It is the painful middle chapter of a story that ends in unimaginable glory.

The apostle Paul, who suffered shipwreck, imprisonment, beatings, stoning, and constant persecution for the sake of the gospel, wrote in Romans 8:18, "For I reckon that the sufferings of this present time are not worthy to be compared with the glory which shall be revealed in us." And in 2 Corinthians 4:17, he described our afflictions as "light" and "momentary" in comparison with the "far more exceeding and eternal weight of glory" that awaits us. Paul was not minimizing suffering. He was putting it in perspective — the perspective of eternity.

The book of Romans provides one of the most powerful expressions of this hope. In Romans 8:28, Paul writes the words that have sustained countless suffering Christians: "And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose." This does not mean that all things are good. Many things are genuinely evil. But it means that God is sovereignly weaving all things — including evil things — into a tapestry of good for those who love Him. This is not wishful thinking. It is a settled conviction grounded in the character of God and confirmed by the testimony of Scripture and the experience of the saints.

Romans 8:31-39 then rises to one of the greatest crescendos in all of Scripture, as Paul enumerates the trials that threaten to separate us from the love of God and then declares with triumphant certainty that none of them can succeed: "Who shall separate us from the love of Christ? shall tribulation, or distress, or persecution, or famine, or nakedness, or peril, or sword? ... Nay, in all these things we are more than conquerors through him that loved us. For I am persuaded, that neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor principalities, nor powers, nor things present, nor things to come, Nor height, nor depth, nor any other creature, shall be able to separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord."

Nothing can separate us from the love of God in Christ. Not suffering. Not evil. Not death itself. This is the ultimate Christian answer to the problem of evil — not that evil does not exist, not that suffering is an illusion, not that God is indifferent, but that God's love is stronger than any evil, and that His purposes will ultimately triumph over every form of darkness.

The Puritan pastor Samuel Rutherford, born in 1600 in Nisbet, Scotland, who was exiled to Aberdeen for his faith in 1636, wrote some of the most beautiful letters in the English language about the relationship between suffering and glory. Rutherford's Letters, written from his exile, overflow with a deep, experiential knowledge of both suffering and the comfort of Christ. He wrote, "I find that the saints' joys and Christ's cross are not severed." Rutherford discovered that the presence of Christ in suffering was sweeter than any earthly comfort. He wrote, "I never knew by my nine years' preaching so much of Christ's love as He taught me in Aberdeen by six months' imprisonment."

There is also the important distinction between different types of evil that we should understand. Philosophers distinguish between moral evil and natural evil. Moral evil is the suffering caused by the free choices of moral agents — war, murder, theft, oppression, and cruelty. The free will defense addresses this type of evil directly. Natural evil is the suffering caused by the natural world — earthquakes, hurricanes, disease, famine, and death. This type of evil is often seen as more difficult to explain, since it does not seem to involve human free will.

However, the Christian worldview provides resources for addressing natural evil as well. As we noted, the doctrine of the fall teaches that the natural world has been affected by human sin. The creation was subjected to futility and corruption as a consequence of the fall (Romans 8:20-22). Natural disasters, disease, and death are not part of God's original design — they are consequences of the fall, part of the groaning of a creation that longs to be set free from the bondage of corruption.

Additionally, natural evil often serves purposes that we can sometimes discern. The beauty and fruitfulness of volcanic soil, which makes some of the most productive agricultural land in the world, is a consequence of volcanic activity. The tectonic processes that cause earthquakes are the same processes that make the earth habitable by recycling carbon, regulating the atmosphere, and creating the conditions necessary for life. The existence of bacteria and viruses, while sometimes causing disease, is essential for the functioning of ecosystems and even for human health. This does not mean that every instance of natural suffering has a discernible purpose. But it does suggest that the natural processes that sometimes cause suffering are not random or purposeless — they are part of a complex, interconnected system that, as a whole, serves good purposes.

Furthermore, suffering often produces genuine goods that could not have been achieved in any other way. The development of courage, compassion, perseverance, humility, and faith often requires the furnace of affliction. James 1:2-4 teaches, "My brethren, count it all joy when ye fall into divers temptations; Knowing this, that the trying of your faith worketh patience. But let patience have her perfect work, that ye may be perfect and entire, wanting nothing." Suffering produces character. It refines faith. It develops virtues that cannot be developed in any other way.

The Puritan pastor John Bunyan, imprisoned for twelve years in Bedford jail for preaching the gospel, wrote The Pilgrim's Progress from that prison cell — a book that has blessed millions of readers for over three centuries. Would Bunyan have written this masterpiece if he had never suffered? Almost certainly not. God brought one of the greatest works of Christian literature out of the furnace of affliction. This does not justify the injustice of Bunyan's imprisonment. But it demonstrates that God is able to bring extraordinary good out of genuine evil.

Hebrews 12:11 teaches this principle clearly: "Now no chastening for the present seemeth to be joyous, but grievous: nevertheless afterward it yieldeth the peaceable fruit of righteousness unto them which are exercised thereby." The suffering is real. The grief is genuine. But the fruit — the peaceable fruit of righteousness — is worth more than the suffering costs. And the God who allows the suffering is the same God who produces the fruit.

The Puritan pastor William Bridge, born in 1600 in Cambridgeshire, England, who served as one of the Dissenting Brethren at the Westminster Assembly, wrote a remarkable work titled A Lifting Up for the Downcast, published in 1648. In it, Bridge addressed the various discouragements that afflict believers — doubt, temptation, suffering, failure — and showed from Scripture how God uses these trials to deepen faith, strengthen character, and draw His people closer to Himself. Bridge wrote, "The same God who promises us heaven also promises us tribulation on the way there." Suffering is not evidence that God has abandoned us. It is often evidence that He is working in us, shaping us, refining us for the glory that is to come.

It is also worth noting — and this is a point of great importance — that the problem of evil is actually a greater difficulty for atheism than for Christianity. This may seem counterintuitive, but consider the following.

If there is no God, then there is no objective standard of good and evil. If the universe is nothing but matter and energy, governed by blind, impersonal forces, then there is no moral dimension to reality. Things simply are what they are. A hurricane that kills thousands is not "evil" — it is merely a weather event. A dictator who slaughters millions is not "wicked" — he is merely a biological organism acting on his impulses. Without God, the very concepts of "good" and "evil" lose their meaning.

But the person who raises the problem of evil is presupposing that evil is real — that some things are genuinely, objectively wrong. And that presupposition only makes sense if there is an objective moral standard — a standard that transcends human opinion, cultural convention, and evolutionary instinct. And as we learned in our lesson on the moral argument, such an objective moral standard requires a moral Lawgiver — God.

The philosopher Greg Bahnsen made this point with devastating clarity in his famous debate with the atheist philosopher Gordon Stein in 1985 at the University of California, Irvine. Bahnsen argued that the very concept of evil presupposes the existence of God, because without God, there is no foundation for calling anything evil. The atheist who complains about evil in the world is borrowing from the Christian worldview to make his argument — using a concept (objective evil) that only makes sense if God exists. The problem of evil, far from disproving God, actually presupposes His existence.

C.S. Lewis recounted his own experience with this argument in Mere Christianity. Lewis had been an atheist who rejected God partly because of the cruelty and injustice of the world. But then he realized the inconsistency of his position. He wrote that his argument against God depended on the existence of a standard of justice by which the world could be judged unjust — and where did that standard come from? Lewis concluded that his very sense of injustice pointed to a just God behind the universe. His atheism, he realized, was "too simple."

The Puritan theologian John Flavel, born around 1627 in Bromsgrove, Worcestershire, England, who served as minister at Dartmouth until he was ejected from his living by the Act of Uniformity in 1662, wrote about the mystery of providence — God's sovereign governance of all events, including painful ones — in his work The Mystery of Providence, published in 1678. Flavel argued that God's providence is like a tapestry: from the back, it appears to be a tangle of random, disordered threads; but from the front, it reveals a beautiful and purposeful design. We see the back of the tapestry now. One day, we will see the front. And when we do, we will marvel at the wisdom and love that guided every thread.

Flavel wrote, "Some providences, like Hebrew letters, must be read backwards." What he meant was that many painful events in our lives will only make sense in retrospect — when we see how God used them for purposes we could not have imagined at the time. Joseph, sold into slavery by his brothers, could not have understood at the time why God was allowing such cruelty. But years later, looking back, he could say to his brothers, "Ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save much people alive" (Genesis 50:20). God's purposes are often hidden in the moment of suffering but revealed in the fullness of time.

The philosopher Peter Kreeft, who has written extensively on the problem of evil, has pointed out that every worldview must account for the reality of evil and suffering. Atheism says there is no God and no ultimate meaning to suffering — it is simply part of the blind, indifferent machinery of nature. Buddhism says that suffering is caused by desire and can be overcome by extinguishing desire — essentially by stopping caring. Hinduism says that suffering is the result of karma from previous lives and must be endured as part of the cycle of reincarnation. Islam says that suffering is the will of Allah and must be accepted with submission.

Only Christianity says that God entered into suffering Himself, that He bore the weight of evil on the cross, that He conquered death by dying, and that He will one day make all things new. Only Christianity offers a God who weeps with those who weep (John 11:35), who is "touched with the feeling of our infirmities" (Hebrews 4:15), who has "borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows" (Isaiah 53:4). Only Christianity offers both an explanation for suffering (the fall) and a solution to suffering (the cross and the resurrection). Only Christianity offers a hope that is both cosmic in scope (the new heaven and new earth) and personal in application (God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes).

This is why, throughout the history of the church, it has been Christians who have most consistently served those who suffer. Christians founded the first hospitals in the Roman Empire. Christians established orphanages, caring for children that pagan societies abandoned. Christians built leprosariums to care for those that society rejected. Christians pioneered the hospice movement to care for the dying with dignity. Christians have been at the forefront of disaster relief, famine aid, and refugee assistance around the world. The Christian response to suffering is not merely theoretical. It is practical, sacrificial, and concrete — because the Christian faith teaches that every suffering person is made in the image of God and deserves to be treated with love and dignity.

The Puritan Matthew Poole, born in 1624 in York, England, who compiled the Synopsis Criticorum and a well-known commentary on the Bible, wrote on Romans 8:28 that God's promise to work all things together for good is "the Christian's great charter" — a royal guarantee from the King of the universe that no suffering will be wasted, no tear will be forgotten, and no affliction will be without purpose. This is the confidence that carries believers through the darkest valleys and the deepest sorrows. It is the confidence that the problem of evil, however painful, is not the last word. The last word belongs to God. And His last word is glory.

Let us close with a practical word. When you encounter someone who is struggling with the problem of evil — whether it is a friend, a classmate, a family member, or even yourself — remember that this is not primarily an academic question. It is a human question, rooted in real pain. Before you offer arguments, offer compassion. Before you explain theology, express sympathy. Weep with those who weep (Romans 12:15). Sit with them in their pain. Listen to their story. Let them know that you care about them, not just about winning a debate.

And then, gently and humbly, point them to the cross — to the God who did not stand at a distance from our suffering but entered into it, bore it in His own body, and conquered it by His resurrection. Point them to the promise that this suffering is not the end of their story. Point them to the hope that one day, every wrong will be made right, every tear will be wiped away, and every broken thing will be restored.

Remember the words of the apostle Paul in 2 Corinthians 1:3-4: "Blessed be God, even the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of mercies, and the God of all comfort; Who comforteth us in all our tribulation, that we may be able to comfort them which are in any trouble, by the comfort wherewith we ourselves are comforted of God." God comforts us in our suffering so that we may comfort others. Your own experiences of suffering — and the comfort you have received from God in the midst of them — qualify you to minister to others who are hurting.

The great Puritan pastor John Owen wrote, "God is to be trusted when His providences seem to run contrary to His promises." This is the faith that sustains us in the face of evil and suffering — the faith that God is trustworthy even when His ways are mysterious, that His love is constant even when His purposes are hidden, and that His final word is not suffering but glory.

Your letter should acknowledge their pain genuinely and without dismissing it, then gently share two or three of the Christian resources for addressing the problem of evil discussed in today's lesson. Focus on being both truthful and kind. Remember that your friend needs compassion before they need arguments, and hope before they need explanations. The problem of evil is not a question to be answered glibly. It requires intellectual honesty, pastoral sensitivity, and a deep, unwavering trust in the goodness of God. The cross of Christ is the ultimate answer — God's demonstration that He has not abandoned us in our suffering but has entered into it, borne it in His own body, and overcome it by His resurrection. And the promise of future glory assures us that the suffering of this present time is not the final word. One day, every tear will be wiped away, and we will see clearly that every thread of God's providence was woven with infinite wisdom and infinite love. That day is surely coming. Hold fast to that blessed hope, and let it sustain you through every trial. Until next time, students.
