PURITAN GOLD — Episode 20
"The Way of the Righteous"
Week 3, Day 6 (Saturday) — Proverbs 12

---

"Whoso loveth instruction loveth knowledge: but he that hateth reproof is brutish."

That's Proverbs 12, verse 1. And it's one of the most uncomfortably honest verses in the entire Bible.

The person who loves instruction loves knowledge. That makes sense. We can nod along to that. But then comes the kicker — the person who hates reproof is brutish. The word in Hebrew is ba'ar — it means stupid, thick-headed, like an unreasoning animal. That's... blunt. Solomon doesn't soften it. He doesn't say, "The person who dislikes correction has some room for growth." He says you're acting like an animal that can't learn.

Why is this verse so important? Because it sets the tone for everything that follows in Proverbs 12. This chapter is a portrait of the righteous person — their character, their speech, their work ethic, their response to difficulty. And the very first stroke of that portrait is this: the righteous person loves correction.

This is Puritan Gold, and today we're walking through Proverbs 12 together. And I want us to see three dimensions of the righteous life as Solomon paints them here: how the righteous person receives correction, how the righteous person speaks, and how the righteous person works.

Let's stay with this first point for a moment, because I think it's the one we most want to skip past.

Do you love correction? Honestly? When someone points out a flaw in your thinking, a weakness in your character, a mistake in your work — is your first reaction gratitude... or defensiveness?

If you're like most of us, it's defensiveness. Something in us immediately bristles. We want to explain, to justify, to counter-argue. We want to protect our ego. We want to be right.

But the wise person — the righteous person — has learned to override that instinct. They've learned to receive correction as a gift rather than an attack. Not because they enjoy being told they're wrong. Nobody enjoys that. But because they understand that correction is one of the primary means by which God shapes their character.

John Owen wrote that "the greatest enemy of spiritual growth is the refusal to be corrected." He said that many Christians stall in their sanctification not because they face great temptations, but because they refuse small corrections. They won't listen to a friend's rebuke. They won't receive a spouse's observation. They won't accept a pastor's counsel. And so they remain stuck — spiritually immature, repeating the same mistakes, unable to grow because they've walled off the very input that growth requires.

Thomas Watson agreed. He said that "reproofs are the lancing of a wound — painful, but healing. He who refuses the lance will die of the infection." What a vivid picture. Correction hurts. Of course it does. It's a knife pressed against a tender place. But the purpose of that knife is not to harm you — it's to heal you. It's to cut away what is diseased so that what is healthy can flourish.

So here's a practical challenge, friend. The next time someone corrects you — a friend, a spouse, a colleague, a fellow believer — before you respond, pause. Take a breath. And instead of defending yourself, ask: "Is there truth in what they're saying?" You don't have to agree with everything. Not every correction is perfectly delivered or entirely accurate. But if there's even a kernel of truth... receive it. Thank the person for it. And let it do its work.

That's what the righteous person does. That's the first mark of wisdom in Proverbs 12.

Now, let's talk about speech. Because once again, as we've seen throughout Proverbs, the tongue is a central concern.

Proverbs 12:17-19: "He that speaketh truth sheweth forth righteousness: but a false witness deceit. There is that speaketh like the piercings of a sword: but the tongue of the wise is health. The lip of truth shall be established for ever: but a lying tongue is but for a moment."

Three verses. Three piercing truths.

First — truth reveals righteousness. When you speak the truth, you display what's really inside you. Your words are a window into your soul. A truthful person is a righteous person, because truth-telling requires courage, humility, and a fear of God that is greater than the fear of man.

We live in an age that has a complicated relationship with truth. We're told that everyone has "their truth." We're told that honesty is less important than diplomacy. We're told that it's better to be kind than to be truthful, as if the two were always in conflict. But Proverbs doesn't see it that way. Proverbs says that speaking truth is itself an act of righteousness. It's not optional. It's not negotiable. It's foundational.

Richard Baxter, in his pastoral writings, emphasized that Christians must be "people of the truth" — not just in the sense of believing true doctrines, but in the sense of speaking truly in every interaction. In business dealings. In personal relationships. In casual conversations. Baxter said that a Christian's word should be so reliable that an oath is unnecessary — because everyone who knows you knows that you simply do not lie.

That's a high standard. And I confess I fall short of it regularly. The little exaggerations. The selective omissions. The half-truths told to make myself look better or to avoid an uncomfortable conversation. These are all forms of deception, and Proverbs names them for what they are.

Second — reckless words wound like a sword, but wise words heal. Verse 18 is so vivid — "there is that speaketh like the piercings of a sword." Haven't you experienced that? Words that cut. Words that stab. Words spoken in anger, or carelessly, or with the intent to harm — and they leave scars that last for years. Decades, even. Some of you are still carrying wounds from words spoken to you in childhood. Words from a parent. A teacher. A peer. Words that pierced like a sword and never fully healed.

But then — the tongue of the wise is health. Healing words. Restorative words. Words that bind up what has been broken, that encourage what has been crushed, that speak life into what has been dying. Your words have that power. Every day, you have the choice — will your tongue be a sword or a salve?

John Bunyan created a scene in The Pilgrim's Progress where Christian enters the Interpreter's House and sees a fire burning against a wall, with a man pouring water on it to extinguish it. But the fire keeps burning. Christian is puzzled until he's taken behind the wall and sees another figure secretly pouring oil on the fire, keeping it alive. The Interpreter explains that the fire is the work of grace in the heart, the water is the devil's attempts to extinguish it, and the oil is the sustaining work of Christ.

I think our words can be either the water or the oil. We can pour cold water on the faith, the hope, and the courage of those around us. Or we can pour the oil of encouragement, truth, and grace — keeping the fire alive in souls that are struggling to burn.

Which are you pouring?

Third — truth endures, but lies are temporary. "The lip of truth shall be established for ever: but a lying tongue is but for a moment." Lies have an expiration date. They always collapse eventually. The truth may be slow — it may be ignored, suppressed, shouted down — but it endures. It outlasts every lie. Because truth is rooted in the nature of God Himself, who is Truth. And nothing rooted in God can be permanently destroyed.

Now, let's turn to our third theme: diligence and work. We touched on this in our study of Proverbs 10, but chapter 12 adds a new dimension.

Proverbs 12:24: "The hand of the diligent shall bear rule: but the slothful shall be under tribute."

The diligent person rises to positions of responsibility and influence — not through ambition or politicking, but through faithful work. And the slothful person ends up in servitude — not because someone enslaved them, but because their own laziness put them there.

And then verse 27: "The slothful man roasteth not that which he took in hunting: but the substance of a diligent man is precious."

Here's a picture of waste versus stewardship. The sluggard catches something in the hunt but can't be bothered to cook it. He lets it go to waste. But the diligent person treats what they have as precious. They don't squander their resources. They don't waste their opportunities. They steward everything — time, talent, money, relationships — with care and gratitude.

The Puritans elevated daily work to the level of divine calling. They spoke of the "vocatio" — the calling — and they meant not just the call to ministry, but the call to any honest labor. William Perkins, a Puritan theologian, wrote that "the action of a shepherd in keeping sheep is as pure a worship of God as the action of a pastor in preaching." Every lawful vocation is a place of worship when performed with diligence and devotion to God.

This means that your Monday morning matters as much as your Sunday morning. Your work at the office, the shop, the school, the kitchen — it's all done before the face of God. And the question is not, "Is my work glamorous?" but "Am I faithful in it?"

Let me weave these three threads together now — correction, speech, and work — because they paint a unified portrait of what the righteous life looks like.

The righteous person is teachable. They receive correction with humility because they know they're not finished yet. God is still working on them, and one of His primary tools is the honest feedback of others. They don't have to be right all the time. They don't have to protect their ego. They can say, "I was wrong. Thank you for telling me."

The righteous person is truthful. They speak with honesty, with kindness, and with purpose. They don't waste words on gossip, flattery, or manipulation. They use their tongue to heal, to encourage, and to build up. And when truth is costly — when speaking honestly might bring opposition or discomfort — they speak it anyway, because they fear God more than they fear man.

The righteous person is diligent. They work hard — not for the approval of others, not out of anxiety, not to prove their worth — but because they understand that every day is a gift from God and every task is an opportunity to glorify Him. They steward their resources well. They don't waste what they've been given. And over time, their faithfulness bears fruit — sometimes quietly, sometimes abundantly, but always genuinely.

This is the way of the righteous. It's not a spectacular way. It's not a way that makes the news or goes viral. It's a quiet, steady, faithful way — the way of a person who has learned to fear the Lord and to walk in His paths.

And the promise of Proverbs, repeated again and again, is that this way leads to life. Real life. Not the artificial life of worldly success, which can be taken away in a moment. But the deep, rooted, enduring life of a soul that is anchored in God.

Richard Baxter's prayer comes to mind: "Lord, let me live the life of the righteous, and let my last end be like his." May that be our prayer too.

Let us pray.

Father, shape us into the people described in this chapter. Make us teachable — quick to listen, slow to defend, humble enough to receive correction. Make us truthful — let our words be honest, healing, and enduring. Make us diligent — faithful in the small things, careful with what You've entrusted to us, working not for ourselves but for Your glory. We are not yet what we ought to be. But by Your grace, we are not what we once were. Continue Your good work in us until the day of Christ Jesus. In His name we pray. Amen.
