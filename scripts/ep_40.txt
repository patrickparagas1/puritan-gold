PURITAN GOLD -- Study Devotional, Day 9
Series: Romans
"Peace with God"
The blessings of justification: peace, access, hope, and love


Welcome, friend. Tonight we open our Bibles again for another session of deep, prayerful study. The Puritans believed that the richest treasures of Scripture are found not on the surface but beneath it, by those willing to dig.

We are studying the book of Romans together. Tonight's lesson is titled "Peace with God." Each session builds on what came before, so if you have been following along, you will see how tonight's passage connects to the larger argument of the book.


Let us read our passage for tonight. It comes from Romans 5:1-11:

"Therefore being justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ: By whom also we have access by faith into this grace wherein we stand, and rejoice in hope of the glory of God. And not only so, but we glory in tribulations also: knowing that tribulation worketh patience; And patience, experience; and experience, hope: And hope maketh not ashamed; because the love of God is shed abroad in our hearts by the Holy Ghost which is given unto us. For when we were yet without strength, in due time Christ died for the ungodly. For scarcely for a righteous man will one die: yet peradventure for a good man some would even dare to die. But God commendeth his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us. Much more then, being now justified by his blood, we shall be saved from wrath through him."

Read that passage again to yourself. Mark any words or phrases that stand out. The Puritans read with a pen in hand, always ready to note what God was showing them.


Within tonight's passage, one verse stands out as a key that unlocks the rest.

"But God commendeth his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us." -- Romans 5:8

This verse is the lens through which we will examine the rest of tonight's passage. Keep it in the front of your mind as we proceed.


With our key verse before us, let us examine the broader teaching.

Martyn Lloyd-Jones offers valuable commentary on this passage. The Puritans were expositors of the highest order, and their insights remain remarkably fresh.

Martyn Lloyd-Jones considered Romans 5:1-11 to be one of the most pastorally rich passages in all of Scripture, and he devoted multiple sermons to its exposition at Westminster Chapel. Lloyd-Jones was emphatic that verse 1 describes an objective reality, not a subjective feeling: 'We have peace with God.' This is not the peace of God that guards our hearts (Philippians 4:7), though that follows; this is peace with God — the cessation of hostility between the holy God and the guilty sinner. Before justification, we were enemies of God (verse 10). Through justification, the war is over. The enmity is abolished. We are reconciled.

Lloyd-Jones gave extended treatment to the chain of verses 3-5, which he called 'the logic of Christian experience.' He argued that the world sees tribulation as the enemy of hope, but Paul teaches the opposite: tribulation produces patience, patience produces experience (or proven character), experience produces hope, and hope does not disappoint. Lloyd-Jones insisted that this is not natural optimism but supernatural transformation. The Holy Spirit, poured into the believer's heart, enables them to find in suffering not despair but deepening assurance. This is why the greatest Christians in history have often been those who suffered most.

Lloyd-Jones also gave careful attention to verses 6-8, which he called 'the supreme demonstration of divine love.' He distinguished three categories: the righteous, the good, and the ungodly. For a righteous man, scarcely anyone would die. For a good man, perhaps someone might dare to die. But God demonstrates His love by dying for the ungodly — for His enemies, for those who hated Him and rebelled against Him. Lloyd-Jones argued that this is the distinguishing mark of divine love: it is not drawn out by anything lovely in the object but poured out freely upon the undeserving. This is the love that can never fail, because it does not depend on the worthiness of its objects.

Romans 5:1 opens with one of the greatest words in the Bible: 'Therefore.' This word connects everything that follows to everything that precedes. Because of justification by faith (chapters 3-4), certain glorious realities are now true. Paul is not introducing new doctrine but drawing out the implications of what he has already established. If we are justified by faith, then specific blessings necessarily follow.

The first blessing is peace: 'Therefore being justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ.' The word 'have' is in the present tense — we currently possess this peace. It is not something we hope for but something we enjoy right now. And it is peace with God, not merely peace about God. Before justification, we were at war with God. His wrath was upon us (John 3:36). We were 'enemies' (verse 10), 'without strength' (verse 6), 'ungodly' (verse 6), 'sinners' (verse 8). The cross of Christ ended the war. The blood of the Prince of Peace has established a permanent ceasefire between heaven and the believing soul.

The second blessing is access: 'By whom also we have access by faith into this grace wherein we stand' (verse 2a). The word 'access' (prosagoge) was used of the introduction of a person into the presence of a king. Through Christ, we have been ushered into the very throne room of God. We stand in grace — not occasionally visit it but permanently dwell in it. This access was pictured in the Old Testament by the veil of the temple, which separated humanity from God's presence. When Christ died, that veil was torn from top to bottom (Matthew 27:51), signifying that the way into God's presence was now open forever.

The third blessing is hope: 'and rejoice in hope of the glory of God' (verse 2b). The justified believer looks forward with confident expectation to sharing in God's glory. We who 'come short of the glory of God' (3:23) will one day be fully conformed to the image of His Son (8:29) and will behold and share in that glory (John 17:22-24). This is not wishful thinking but certain hope, grounded in the unchangeable purposes of God.

But Paul does not stop with future glory. In verses 3-5, he makes a startling claim: 'And not only so, but we glory in tribulations also.' The justified believer can rejoice not only in spite of tribulations but in tribulations themselves. This is not masochism; it is spiritual logic. Paul traces a chain of cause and effect: tribulation produces patience (or endurance — the Greek hupomone means steadfast perseverance under pressure). Patience produces experience (or proven character — dokime, the quality of something that has been tested and found genuine, like gold refined in fire). Experience produces hope — not naive optimism but battle-tested confidence that God is faithful.

And this hope 'maketh not ashamed; because the love of God is shed abroad in our hearts by the Holy Ghost which is given unto us.' The Holy Spirit, dwelling within the believer, pours out an experiential awareness of God's love that sustains us through every trial. This is the first mention of the Holy Spirit in Romans, and it connects the objective work of justification to the subjective experience of assurance. We know God loves us not merely because the Bible says so (though it does) but because the Spirit witnesses to our hearts that we are His children.

Verses 6-8 prove God's love with an argument from the greater to the lesser. Paul presents three descriptions of our condition when Christ died for us: 'without strength' (helpless), 'ungodly' (wicked), and 'sinners' (missing the mark). We were not attractive, deserving, or even neutral. We were actively hostile. Yet 'in due time Christ died for the ungodly.' Human love occasionally rises to great sacrifice — 'for a good man some would even dare to die.' But God's love surpasses all human love infinitely: 'God commendeth his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us.' The word 'commendeth' means to demonstrate, to prove, to put on display. The cross is not evidence of God's love; it is the supreme demonstration of God's love — proof beyond all argument.

Verses 9-10 argue from the greater to the lesser again, establishing the security of the believer. 'Much more then, being now justified by his blood, we shall be saved from wrath through him.' If God did the harder thing (justifying us while we were enemies through the death of His Son), He will certainly do the easier thing (keeping us saved now that we are reconciled). This is one of the strongest arguments for eternal security in the Bible. If Christ's death was sufficient to reconcile us when we were enemies, how much more will His resurrection life preserve us now that we are friends?

Verse 11 crowns the passage: 'And not only so, but we also joy in God through our Lord Jesus Christ, by whom we have now received the atonement.' The word 'atonement' here is better translated 'reconciliation' (katallage). We do not merely endure our relationship with God; we joy in God Himself. He is not only our Savior but our supreme delight. The Westminster Shorter Catechism asks, 'What is the chief end of man?' The answer: 'To glorify God, and to enjoy Him forever.' Romans 5:11 says we can begin that enjoyment now.


Application is where truth meets life. Let us consider what this passage demands of us.

The blessings of justification described in Romans 5:1-11 should transform your daily experience. Here are specific applications.

First, live in the reality of peace with God. Many Christians live as though they are still at war with God — constantly anxious, perpetually trying to earn His approval, afraid that one failure will bring condemnation. If you are justified by faith, the war is over. Begin each day by declaring this truth: 'I have peace with God through my Lord Jesus Christ.' Let that reality calm your anxieties and stabilize your emotions.

Second, reframe your suffering. The world tells you that suffering is meaningless, random, and to be avoided at all costs. Paul says tribulation produces patience, patience produces character, character produces hope. The next time you face a trial — whether it is a difficult relationship, a health crisis, financial pressure, or spiritual attack — remind yourself of this chain. Ask God: 'What are You building in me through this?' Look for the patience, the character, and the hope that God is producing.

Third, meditate on the cross as proof of God's love. When you doubt whether God truly loves you, do not look at your circumstances; look at the cross. 'While we were yet sinners, Christ died for us.' You cannot measure God's love by your comfort level. You measure it by the blood of His Son. Spend fifteen minutes this week simply contemplating Calvary and letting the love of God flood your heart.

Fourth, rest in your security. If God reconciled you while you were an enemy, He will certainly preserve you now that you are His child. Your salvation does not depend on your ability to hold on to God but on God's ability to hold on to you. 'Much more, being reconciled, we shall be saved by his life.' Let this assurance give you boldness, freedom, and unshakeable confidence.

Write down one concrete action you will take this week in response to tonight's study. Do not let this truth remain theoretical. The Puritans measured the success of a sermon not by how much the hearer learned but by how much the hearer changed.


God of peace, we thank You that the war is over. Through the blood of Christ, we have been reconciled to You — not because we sought You, but because You loved us while we were yet sinners. Pour Your love afresh into our hearts by the Holy Spirit. Teach us to glory in tribulations, knowing that You are working patience, character, and hope in us. When doubts assail and trials press, bring us back to the cross — the unchanging proof of Your unchanging love. We joy in You, O God, through our Lord Jesus Christ, by whom we have received reconciliation. Amen.

Goodnight. May the truths you have studied tonight take deep root in your heart.
