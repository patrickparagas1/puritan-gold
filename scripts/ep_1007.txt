PURITAN GOLD -- Study Topic: The Book of Sirach, Part 7 of 10
"The Grace of the Table"
Sirach Chapters 31–32 — Moderation, Generosity, and the Art of Hospitality

---

Good evening, and welcome back to Puritan Gold. Tonight we come to one of the most unexpectedly delightful passages in the entire Book of Sirach — chapters 31 and 32, where Ben Sira turns his attention to the table. Here is wisdom about eating, drinking, hosting, and being a guest. It may surprise you that a book of sacred wisdom would devote such careful attention to table manners and hospitality. But the Puritans understood what Ben Sira knew: that how we eat and drink reveals the condition of our souls.

Let us read from Sirach chapter 31, verses 12 through 18, and chapter 31, verses 25 through 28:

"When thou sittest among many, reach not thine hand out first of all. A very little is sufficient for a man well nurtured, and he fetcheth not his wind short upon his bed. Sound sleep cometh of moderate eating: he riseth early, and his wits are with him: but the pain of watching, and choler, and pangs of the belly, are with an unsatiable man. If thou hast been forced to eat, arise, go forth, vomit, and thou shalt have rest."

"Wine is as good as life to a man, if it be drunk moderately: what life is then to a man that is without wine? for it was made to make men glad. Wine measurably drunk and in season bringeth gladness of the heart, and cheerfulness of the mind: But wine drunken with excess maketh bitterness of the mind, with brawling and quarrelling."

--- Teaching ---

There is a beauty in the ordinary that the world frequently overlooks. The table — where family and friends gather to break bread — is one of the most sacred spaces in human life. Jesus Himself consecrated the table at the Last Supper, and throughout His ministry He ate and drank with sinners, Pharisees, and disciples alike. Ben Sira, writing centuries before Christ, already understood the spiritual significance of the meal.

The first great lesson of these chapters is moderation. "A very little is sufficient for a man well nurtured." In a culture obsessed with excess — whether in food, drink, entertainment, or consumption of every kind — this counsel strikes with prophetic force. Moderation is not deprivation; it is the art of enjoying God's gifts without being enslaved by them. The moderate person sleeps well, wakes refreshed, and possesses a clear mind. The glutton suffers "the pain of watching, and choler, and pangs of the belly." Anyone who has overeaten at a holiday feast knows exactly what Ben Sira means.

The Puritans had a robust theology of enjoyment rooted in moderation. They were not the dour, pleasure-hating killjoys of popular imagination. Richard Baxter wrote that "God has given us the creatures for our moderate use and delight, and it is ingratitude to refuse His gifts — but it is idolatry to worship them." Thomas Watson argued that "the world is a looking-glass to behold God in, and its comforts are the dishes at His table to nourish us on our journey." The key word in both cases is moderation. Enjoy the gift; worship the Giver.

Ben Sira's counsel on wine is remarkably balanced and deserves careful attention. "Wine is as good as life to a man, if it be drunk moderately." He does not forbid wine. He celebrates it as one of God's gifts: "It was made to make men glad." This echoes Psalm 104:15: "Wine that maketh glad the heart of man." But the sage immediately adds the warning: "Wine drunken with excess maketh bitterness of the mind, with brawling and quarrelling." The same gift that gladdens in moderation destroys in excess. Proverbs 20:1 puts it plainly: "Wine is a mocker, strong drink is raging: and whosoever is deceived thereby is not wise."

The second great theme is hospitality. Chapter 32 opens with counsel for the host: "If thou be made the master of a feast, lift not thyself up, but be among them as one of the rest; take diligent care for them, and so sit down." The good host does not lord it over the guests. He serves. He puts others at ease. He makes sure every person at the table is welcome and comfortable. This is precisely the pattern of Christ, who washed His disciples' feet — the host becoming the servant.

Ben Sira also has wise counsel for the guest. Chapter 31:17-18 teaches: "Leave off first for manners' sake; and be not unsatiable, lest thou offend. When thou sittest among many, reach not thine hand out first of all." Good manners are not mere social convention; they are expressions of love. The person who reaches first, eats fastest, takes the largest portion, and leaves nothing for others reveals a heart centered on self. The person who defers, shares, and considers others reveals a heart shaped by grace.

William Gouge, in his Domestical Duties, gave extensive counsel on household hospitality. He argued that the Christian home should be a place where strangers are welcomed, the poor are fed, and the lonely find fellowship. This was not optional generosity but a sacred duty. The Apostle Paul commands: "Given to hospitality" (Romans 12:13). The writer of Hebrews warns: "Be not forgetful to entertain strangers: for thereby some have entertained angels unawares" (Hebrews 13:2).

There is also a connection between the table and spiritual discernment. Ben Sira notes that you can learn much about a person's character by watching them at the table. The greedy, the impatient, the self-centered — all reveal themselves over a meal. Jesus observed this principle too. He noticed how the Pharisees chose the best seats at banquets (Luke 14:7-11) and used this as a parable about humility: "For whosoever exalteth himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted."

The table, then, is a school of virtue. Every meal is an opportunity to practice gratitude, self-control, generosity, and love. Whether you are hosting or being hosted, cooking or cleaning up, the table is holy ground. As the Puritan practice of saying grace before meals reminds us, every bite of food is a gift from God's hand. To eat with gratitude and moderation is an act of worship.

--- Application ---

1. Practice moderation at your next meal. Eat slowly, enjoy each bite, and stop before you are stuffed. Notice how much better you feel — in body and spirit.

2. Open your home to hospitality this month. Invite someone over for a simple meal — a neighbor, a church member, a family in need. Remember that hospitality is not about impressing guests but about welcoming them.

3. Examine your behavior as a guest. Do you defer to others? Do you express genuine gratitude to your host? Do you help clean up? Small acts of courtesy are acts of love.

4. Give thanks before every meal this week — not as a rushed habit, but as a deliberate act of worship. Thank God for the food, for the hands that prepared it, and for the company that shares it.

--- Closing ---

Let us pray. Gracious Father, who spreadest a table before us in the presence of our enemies, who givest us daily bread and wine that maketh glad the heart of man — teach us to receive Thy gifts with grateful, moderate hearts. Save us from the sins of gluttony and excess. Give us the spirit of hospitality, that our homes may be places of welcome and warmth. And may every meal remind us of that great Table which Thou hast prepared for us in Thy kingdom, where we shall feast with Thee forevermore. Through Christ our Lord. Amen.
