PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 18
"Answering Moral Objections to Christianity"
Apologetics for Young Christians — Lesson 18

---

Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we address some of the most common moral objections raised against Christianity — the charge that the Bible endorses violence, that Christians are hypocrites, and that religion has done more harm than good. Let us pray and begin.

Lord, give us wisdom to answer difficult moral questions about our faith with honesty and grace. Help us to acknowledge the failings of Christians while pointing people to the perfection of Christ. In Christ's name, Amen.

--- Lesson ---

Some of the most common objections to Christianity are not intellectual but moral. People do not merely doubt whether Christianity is true — they question whether it is good. Today we will address three of the most frequently raised moral objections.

Objection one: "The Bible endorses violence and is morally outdated." This objection usually points to Old Testament passages involving divine judgment — the destruction of the Canaanites, the flood of Noah, or the plagues of Egypt. How should Christians respond?

First, we must understand these passages in their proper context. The judgments described in the Old Testament are not random acts of violence. They are acts of divine justice — God's righteous response to extreme wickedness. The Canaanite nations, for example, practiced child sacrifice, ritual prostitution, and other horrific evils. God's judgment on them was not arbitrary cruelty. It was the just response of a holy God to profound moral corruption. God is not obligated to tolerate evil indefinitely, and His judgments serve as a warning to all people of all ages.

Second, we should note that the Old Testament also contains some of the most beautiful and humane moral teaching in the ancient world. The command to love your neighbor as yourself (Leviticus 19:18), the requirement to care for the poor and the stranger (Deuteronomy 24:17-22), the institution of the Sabbath as a day of rest for all people including servants (Exodus 20:10), and the prophets' passionate advocacy for justice (Micah 6:8) — all of these predate the moral progress of other civilizations by centuries.

Third, the moral objection often rests on an inconsistency. If there is no God and no objective moral standard, on what basis can someone call anything "immoral"? The very act of moral objection presupposes a moral law — and a moral law presupposes a moral Lawgiver. The critic who charges God with immorality is borrowing from the Christian worldview to make his argument.

Objection two: "Christians are hypocrites." This objection points to the gap between what Christians profess and how they live — and there is often truth to it. Christians have sometimes been guilty of terrible hypocrisy, both individually and institutionally.

The honest response is to acknowledge this. Christians are sinners. The church is full of imperfect people who do not always practice what they preach. This is a genuine failure that should be confessed, not denied or excused. However, several important qualifications must be made.

First, hypocrisy does not disprove the truth of Christianity. A mathematics teacher who cannot balance his own checkbook does not thereby prove that mathematics is false. The truth of a belief system is independent of the behavior of its adherents. Christianity should be judged on the basis of its claims, its evidence, and its internal consistency — not on the behavior of every person who claims to be a Christian.

Second, Christianity itself condemns hypocrisy in the strongest possible terms. Jesus reserved His harshest criticism for hypocrites (Matthew 23). The fact that Christians sometimes fail to live up to their own standards is not evidence that the standards are wrong — it is evidence that the standards are high, and that we need grace.

Third, while some Christians have been hypocrites, many others have been paragons of virtue. Christians have been at the forefront of some of the greatest moral movements in history: the abolition of slavery (William Wilberforce), the founding of hospitals and universities (countless monastic orders and missionaries), the civil rights movement (Martin Luther King Jr.), and the care of the poor and vulnerable around the world (countless Christian organizations). The fruits of genuine Christian faith are undeniable.

Objection three: "Religion has done more harm than good." This claim is often made with reference to the Crusades, the Inquisition, or religious wars. Again, honesty requires us to acknowledge that terrible things have been done in the name of Christianity. But a fuller picture reveals a very different story.

The claim that religion causes most of the world's violence is simply not supported by the historical evidence. The greatest atrocities of the twentieth century — the Holocaust, the Soviet Gulag, the killing fields of Cambodia, Mao's Cultural Revolution — were perpetrated by explicitly atheistic regimes. Secular ideologies have produced far more bloodshed than religion ever has.

Furthermore, Christianity has been an overwhelmingly positive force in human history. Christians pioneered education, established hospitals, cared for orphans and widows, championed human rights, advanced science, and created some of the greatest art, music, and literature in human civilization. The positive contributions of Christianity to the world far outweigh its negative ones.

The proper response to moral objections is not defensiveness but honesty: acknowledging failures while pointing to Christ, who is the standard by which all behavior — Christian and non-Christian — should be judged.

--- Review Questions ---

Review Question 1: How should Christians understand the violence in the Old Testament? What context helps explain these passages?

Review Question 2: How should Christians respond to the charge of hypocrisy? Why does hypocrisy not disprove the truth of Christianity?

Review Question 3: Is the claim that "religion has done more harm than good" historically accurate? What evidence supports or contradicts this claim?

--- Activity ---

Choose one of the three moral objections discussed today. Write a one-page response to someone who has raised that objection. Your response should acknowledge the legitimate concerns behind the objection, provide a thoughtful Christian answer, and do so with humility and respect. Share your response with a parent or mentor for feedback.

--- Closing ---

Moral objections to Christianity deserve honest, thoughtful, and humble responses. We must acknowledge our failures, point people to Christ, and demonstrate through our lives that the gospel produces genuine goodness. Until next time, students.
