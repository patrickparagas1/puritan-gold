PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Doctrine Deep-Dive, Day 10
"The Atonement: Penal Substitution"
Christ Died for Us — The Heart of the Gospel

---

Good evening, friend. Tonight we come to the very center of the Christian faith: the cross of Jesus Christ. Specifically, we will explore the doctrine of penal substitutionary atonement — the teaching that Christ bore the penalty of our sin in our place. The Puritans considered this the most glorious truth in all of Scripture. Let us consider it together.

--- Scripture ---

Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Isaiah 53:5-6:

"But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed. All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned every one to his own way; and the LORD hath laid on him the iniquity of us all."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.

--- Doctrine Study ---

The doctrine of penal substitutionary atonement teaches that on the cross, Jesus Christ bore the penalty that was due to sinners. He stood in our place. He received our punishment. He absorbed the wrath of God that we deserved. And because He did, all who are united to Him by faith are forever free from condemnation. This is the heart of the gospel. Without it, there is no Christianity.

The word "penal" refers to punishment. Sin is not merely a mistake or a moral failing. It is a crime against the infinite holiness of God, and as such, it deserves punishment. The justice of God requires that sin be dealt with. It cannot be overlooked, excused, or swept under the carpet. Either the sinner bears his own punishment, or a substitute bears it for him. The gospel declares that God Himself has provided the substitute: His own Son.

The word "substitutionary" means that Christ acted in our place. He did not merely die as an example of sacrificial love, though He is that. He did not merely die to demonstrate the seriousness of sin, though He did that too. He died as our substitute, taking upon Himself the guilt and punishment that belonged to us. The Westminster Larger Catechism declares that Christ, "by his perfect obedience, and sacrifice of himself, which he through the eternal Spirit once offered up unto God, hath fully satisfied the justice of his Father."

John Owen defends this doctrine with meticulous care in his great work "The Death of Death in the Death of Christ." Owen argues that the atonement was definite in its intent and effectual in its accomplishment. Christ did not merely make salvation possible; He actually saved His people. He bore their specific sins. He purchased their specific redemption. And the benefits of His death are applied to every person for whom He died, without exception. Owen writes that if Christ bore the penalty for the sins of a particular person, that person cannot also bear the penalty. Justice does not demand payment twice: once from the Surety and again from the debtor.

Thomas Watson explains the atonement with characteristic clarity and warmth. Watson writes that on the cross, a great exchange took place. Our sin was laid upon Christ, and His righteousness was imputed to us. Watson calls this "the blessed exchange": Christ took what was ours — guilt, shame, condemnation, death — and gave us what was His — innocence, honor, justification, life. This exchange is not earned by the sinner but received by faith as a free gift of grace.

Watson further explains why the atonement had to be penal. God's justice is not an attribute He can set aside at will. It is essential to His nature. A God who ignored sin would not be just. A God who was not just would not be God. Therefore, if sinners are to be saved, justice must be satisfied. And justice is satisfied only by the full payment of the penalty. On the cross, Christ paid that penalty in full. "It is finished," He cried (John 19:30). The debt is paid. The wrath is propitiated. The law is satisfied. Nothing remains to be done.

The Puritans also emphasized the voluntary nature of Christ's sacrifice. Christ was not a victim but a willing substitute. He said, "No man taketh it from me, but I lay it down of myself" (John 10:18). He went to the cross not under compulsion but out of love — love for the Father whose will He came to fulfill, and love for the people He came to save. Stephen Charnock writes that the cross reveals both the justice and the love of God in their fullest expression. Justice, because sin is punished with the severity it deserves. Love, because God Himself bears the punishment so that sinners might go free.

The practical implications of this doctrine are immense. First, it provides the only solid ground for the assurance of salvation. If Christ has borne my punishment, then there is no punishment left for me. "There is therefore now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1). This is not wishful thinking; it is the logical consequence of a completed atonement. If the debt has been paid, the debtor is free.

Second, the atonement produces gratitude and love for God. When you understand what the cross cost the Son of God — when you see the infinite weight of sin that He bore, the unimaginable agony He endured, the separation from the Father He suffered — your heart cannot help but overflow with gratitude. This gratitude, the Puritans taught, is the most powerful motivation for holiness. We obey not to earn God's favor but because we have already received it, at an incalculable price.

Third, the atonement puts to death all self-righteousness. If Christ had to die for my sins, then my sins are far worse than I imagined. And if Christ had to die for my sins, then my own efforts to atone for them are utterly futile. The cross strips away every pretension of human merit and leaves the sinner with nothing to boast of except the grace of God.

Let the cross be your meditation tonight. There is no deeper well of comfort, no firmer foundation for faith, and no greater motive for worship than the truth that Christ died for you.

--- Reflection ---

Here are some questions to carry with you into the coming days.

Question 1: Do I rest my assurance of salvation on the finished work of Christ, or do I unconsciously add my own works to the equation?

Question 2: When I contemplate the cross, does it produce in me a deeper hatred of sin and a deeper love for God?

Question 3: How does the doctrine of penal substitution comfort me when I feel the weight of my guilt?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.

--- Closing Prayer ---

Lord Jesus, we stand in awe of Thy cross. Thou wast wounded for our transgressions. Thou wast bruised for our iniquities. The chastisement of our peace was upon Thee. We deserve nothing but judgment; Thou hast given us everything in grace. Let us never cease to wonder at the cost of our redemption. Amen.

Rest tonight in the finished work of Christ. It is finished, indeed.
