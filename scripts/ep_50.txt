PURITAN GOLD -- Study Devotional, Day 19
Series: Romans
"Christ the End of the Law"
Righteousness by faith is near — in your mouth and in your heart


Good evening, student of the Word. Every time we open the Scriptures, we stand on holy ground. The God of the universe has spoken, and He invites us to listen, to understand, and to respond. Let us listen well tonight.

We are studying the book of Romans together. Tonight's lesson is titled "Christ the End of the Law." Each session builds on what came before, so if you have been following along, you will see how tonight's passage connects to the larger argument of the book.


Let us read our passage for tonight. It comes from Romans 10:1-13:

"Brethren, my heart's desire and prayer to God for Israel is, that they might be saved. For I bear them record that they have a zeal of God, but not according to knowledge. For they being ignorant of God's righteousness, and going about to establish their own righteousness, have not submitted themselves unto the righteousness of God. For Christ is the end of the law for righteousness to every one that believeth... But the righteousness which is of faith speaketh on this wise, Say not in thine heart, Who shall ascend into heaven? (that is, to bring Christ down from above:) Or, Who shall descend into the deep? (that is, to bring up Christ again from the dead.) But what saith it? The word is nigh thee, even in thy mouth, and in thy heart: that is, the word of faith, which we preach; That if thou shalt confess with thy mouth the Lord Jesus, and shalt believe in thine heart that God hath raised him from the dead, thou shalt be saved... For whosoever shall call upon the name of the Lord shall be saved."

Read that passage again to yourself. Mark any words or phrases that stand out. The Puritans read with a pen in hand, always ready to note what God was showing them.


Here is the key verse for tonight's study. Let it anchor everything else we discuss.

"That if thou shalt confess with thy mouth the Lord Jesus, and shalt believe in thine heart that God hath raised him from the dead, thou shalt be saved." -- Romans 10:9

This verse is the lens through which we will examine the rest of tonight's passage. Keep it in the front of your mind as we proceed.


Having read the text, let us explore its meaning in depth.

Robert Haldane offers valuable commentary on this passage. The Puritans were expositors of the highest order, and their insights remain remarkably fresh.

Robert Haldane, in his commentary on Romans, gave particular attention to the critical verse 4: 'For Christ is the end of the law for righteousness to every one that believeth.' Haldane argued that 'end' (telos) carries a double meaning that Paul intends us to see. Christ is the termination of the law as a way of achieving righteousness — the law's demand for perfect obedience can no longer be used as a path to justification, because Christ has fulfilled it. But Christ is also the goal or fulfillment of the law — everything the law pointed toward, foreshadowed, and promised finds its completion in Him. The entire sacrificial system, the moral code, the prophetic trajectory — all of it terminates in Christ.

Haldane gave careful treatment to Paul's use of Deuteronomy 30:12-14 in verses 6-8. He argued that Paul is not taking Moses out of context but showing the deeper meaning of Moses' words. Even in the old covenant, the word of God was not impossibly distant — it was near, in the mouth and heart. How much more so now that Christ has come. The incarnation (He descended from heaven) and the resurrection (He ascended from the dead) have already been accomplished. Salvation does not require us to perform these miraculous feats; it requires us to believe in the One who has already performed them.

Haldane found in verses 9-13 the simplest and most accessible statement of the gospel in all of Scripture. Confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord; believe in your heart that God raised Him from the dead; and you will be saved. Haldane stressed that confession and belief are not two separate conditions but two aspects of a single reality. True heart-belief inevitably produces mouth-confession, and genuine confession flows from heart-belief. And the promise is universal: 'Whosoever shall call upon the name of the Lord shall be saved.' Haldane rejoiced that 'whosoever' removes every barrier of race, class, education, and moral background. The door of salvation stands open to every human being who calls.

Romans 10 is one of the most evangelistic chapters in Paul's writings. While chapters 9 and 11 focus on the sovereignty of God in election, chapter 10 emphasizes the responsibility of human beings to believe. These are not contradictions but complementary truths held in tension throughout Scripture.

Paul begins again with his heart for Israel (verse 1): 'Brethren, my heart's desire and prayer to God for Israel is, that they might be saved.' The apostle of election is also the apostle of prayer for the lost. He does not say, 'God's sovereign purpose will work itself out, so why bother praying?' He prays fervently for Israel's salvation. Sovereignty and prayer work together in God's economy.

Verse 2 acknowledges something genuine about Israel: 'they have a zeal of God, but not according to knowledge.' Zeal is admirable, but zeal without knowledge is dangerous. Israel was passionately committed to God — but committed in the wrong way. They were zealous for the law but ignorant of the gospel. They were devoted to religious observance but blind to the righteousness of God. Sincerity is not enough. A person can be sincerely wrong, passionately pursuing a path that leads to destruction.

Verse 3 diagnoses the problem: 'For they being ignorant of God's righteousness, and going about to establish their own righteousness, have not submitted themselves unto the righteousness of God.' This is the fundamental human religious error: establishing our own righteousness instead of receiving God's. The word 'submitted' reveals the heart of the issue. Receiving God's righteousness requires submission — the surrender of self-effort, the abandonment of self-trust, the humiliation of admitting that we cannot save ourselves. Human pride resists this with every fiber of its being.

Verse 4 is one of the most important verses in the New Testament: 'For Christ is the end of the law for righteousness to every one that believeth.' Christ is the telos — the goal, the fulfillment, the termination — of the law as a means of righteousness. This does not mean the law is abolished (Matthew 5:17), but it means the law's function of driving people toward righteousness has found its fulfillment in Christ. He kept the law perfectly in His life and bore its curse perfectly in His death (Galatians 3:13). The law's demands are met. The law's penalties are paid. The one who believes in Christ has, in Christ, everything the law requires.

Verses 5-8 contrast two kinds of righteousness using Moses' own words. The righteousness of the law says: 'The man which doeth those things shall live by them' (Leviticus 18:5). This is the principle of works — do and live. It is theoretically possible but practically impossible for fallen sinners. No one has ever kept the law perfectly except Christ.

The righteousness of faith speaks differently. Paul quotes Deuteronomy 30:12-14, applying it to Christ. 'Say not in thine heart, Who shall ascend into heaven? (that is, to bring Christ down from above:) Or, Who shall descend into the deep? (that is, to bring up Christ again from the dead.)' You do not need to perform impossible feats to find salvation. Christ has already descended from heaven in the incarnation. Christ has already ascended from the dead in the resurrection. The work is done. 'But what saith it? The word is nigh thee, even in thy mouth, and in thy heart: that is, the word of faith, which we preach.' The gospel is near — as near as your mouth and your heart.

Verses 9-10 spell out the gospel response in its simplest form: 'That if thou shalt confess with thy mouth the Lord Jesus, and shalt believe in thine heart that God hath raised him from the dead, thou shalt be saved. For with the heart man believeth unto righteousness; and with the mouth confession is made unto salvation.' Two elements: heart-belief and mouth-confession. Believing involves the heart — not merely intellectual assent but deep, personal trust. Confessing involves the mouth — public declaration that Jesus is Lord. In the Roman world, to confess 'Jesus is Lord' was a political act of treason against Caesar's claim to lordship. It cost something. Genuine faith always results in public confession.

The content of belief is specific: 'that God hath raised him from the dead.' The resurrection is the foundation of saving faith. It validates everything Jesus claimed. It proves that His sacrifice was accepted. It demonstrates that death has been conquered. Without the resurrection, there is no gospel (1 Corinthians 15:14).

Verses 11-13 expand the promise to universal scope. 'For the scripture saith, Whosoever believeth on him shall not be ashamed. For there is no difference between the Jew and the Greek: for the same Lord over all is rich unto all that call upon him. For whosoever shall call upon the name of the Lord shall be saved.' The word 'whosoever' appears twice, obliterating every racial, ethnic, and social barrier. There is one Lord, rich in mercy to all who call. And the condition is beautifully simple: call upon His name. Joel 2:32, which Paul quotes, was originally a promise of deliverance from the Day of the Lord. Paul applies it to the ultimate deliverance — salvation from sin, death, and hell through Jesus Christ.

The simplicity of the gospel is intentional. God did not make salvation complex so that only the educated or the sophisticated could find it. He made it as simple as a cry, as accessible as a prayer, as near as your own heart and mouth. The great barrier to salvation is not intellectual difficulty but moral pride — the unwillingness to submit to God's righteousness and receive it as a gift.


Knowledge puffs up, Paul warns, but love builds up. Let us ensure that what we have studied tonight leads not to pride but to faithful, loving obedience.

Romans 10:1-13 calls us both to receive the gospel and to share it. Here are specific applications.

First, examine whether you have truly submitted to God's righteousness or are still trying to establish your own. Are you resting in Christ's finished work, or are you still adding your own efforts to the equation? Do you believe that His obedience is sufficient, or do you secretly think your good works contribute to your standing before God? Submission to God's righteousness means letting go of your own. Do it today.

Second, confess Christ publicly. If you believe in your heart, let it come out of your mouth. Tell someone this week that Jesus is your Lord. Speak His name at work, in your family, among your friends. In our culture, public confession may bring ridicule rather than persecution, but the principle remains: genuine faith is never purely private.

Third, pray for the lost with Paul's passion. Verse 1 shows an apostle on his knees for unbelievers. Do you pray for specific lost people by name? Adopt three people who do not know Christ and commit to praying for their salvation daily. Write their names somewhere visible. Intercede for them with the passion and persistence Paul showed for Israel.

Fourth, simplify your gospel presentation. The gospel of Romans 10:9 is not complicated. You do not need a seminary degree to share it. Practice sharing the gospel in one or two minutes: everyone has sinned, Christ died and rose, whoever believes and confesses is saved. Keep it simple. Keep it clear. Keep it Christ-centered. And remember: whosoever shall call upon the name of the Lord shall be saved.

Write down one concrete action you will take this week in response to tonight's study. Do not let this truth remain theoretical. The Puritans measured the success of a sermon not by how much the hearer learned but by how much the hearer changed.


Lord Jesus, You are the end of the law for righteousness to everyone who believes. We submit to Your righteousness and abandon our own. We confess with our mouths that You are Lord and believe in our hearts that God raised You from the dead. Thank You that the word of faith is near — in our mouths and in our hearts. Give us Paul's passion for the lost. Burden us for those who have zeal without knowledge, religion without Christ. Open doors for gospel witness this week. And we claim Your promise: whosoever shall call upon Your name shall be saved. Amen.

Until our next study, walk in the truth you have received.
