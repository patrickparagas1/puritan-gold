"The Puritans: Reforming the Reformation"
The Reformation -- Lesson 8

---

Welcome back, students. Today we arrive at the heart of our entire course -- the Puritans. These were the men and women who believed that the English Reformation, for all its achievements, had not gone far enough. They sought to purify the Church of England of every last vestige of Roman Catholic practice and to build a church, a society, and a pattern of daily life that was ordered entirely by the Word of God. The Puritans were, in the truest sense, reformers of the Reformation -- and their story is the story of Puritan Gold. Today we will trace the origins, the convictions, the struggles, and the early achievements of the Puritan movement, from the reign of Elizabeth I through the early years of the seventeenth century, and we will meet some of the great men and women who gave this movement its character, its theology, and its enduring power.

--- Lesson ---

To understand where the Puritans came from, we must go back to the Elizabethan Settlement of 1559. When Elizabeth I came to the English throne in November 1558, she inherited a nation that had been violently yanked back and forth between Protestantism and Catholicism for over a quarter century. Her father Henry VIII had broken with Rome but remained largely Catholic in his theology. Her half-brother Edward VI had pursued a vigorous Protestant reformation. Her half-sister Mary I had violently restored Roman Catholicism and burned nearly three hundred Protestants at the stake. The English people were exhausted, confused, and divided. Elizabeth's task was to find a religious settlement that would unite the nation -- or at least prevent it from tearing itself apart.

The result was the Elizabethan Settlement, enacted by Parliament in 1559 through the Act of Supremacy (which established Elizabeth as the "Supreme Governor" of the Church of England -- a slightly softer title than her father's "Supreme Head") and the Act of Uniformity (which imposed a revised Book of Common Prayer as the standard for English worship). The Thirty-Nine Articles of Religion, finalized in 1563, defined the Church of England's doctrinal position in broadly Reformed terms -- the articles affirmed justification by faith, the authority of Scripture, and the two sacraments of baptism and the Lord's Supper, while rejecting the Catholic doctrines of purgatory, transubstantiation, and papal supremacy.

In many respects, the Elizabethan Settlement was a Protestant settlement. Its theology was genuinely Reformed. Its bishops -- men like Matthew Parker, Edmund Grindal, and John Jewel -- were committed Protestants who had suffered exile during Mary's reign. The homilies read in the parish churches taught Protestant doctrine. The Bible was available in English in every church. The gospel was preached.

But for those who had experienced the thoroughgoing Reformation of Geneva, Zurich, or Strasbourg during their Marian exile, the Settlement did not go far enough. The Church of England retained bishops and archbishops in a hierarchical structure that seemed more papal than apostolic. It retained vestments -- the surplice, the cope, the cap -- that seemed to be remnants of the Mass and of priestly pretension. It retained ceremonies -- kneeling at communion, the sign of the cross in baptism, the use of the ring in marriage -- that had no warrant in Scripture. It retained church courts and a system of ecclesiastical discipline that was cumbersome, corrupt, and ineffective. And it tolerated a parish clergy that was in many places poorly educated, negligent in preaching, and barely distinguishable from the Catholic priests they had replaced.

These critics, who wanted to purify the Church of England of its remaining Catholic elements, became known as the Puritans. The name was first used in the early 1560s as a term of mockery -- their opponents derided them as self-righteous precisians who wanted to impose an impossibly strict standard of worship and morality on the English church. But the Puritans accepted the label and transformed it into a badge of honor. They were, they believed, simply following the principle of sola Scriptura -- Scripture alone -- to its logical conclusion. If the Bible was the supreme authority for faith and practice, then everything in the church's worship, government, and discipline must be tested by Scripture, and anything that could not be justified from Scripture must be removed.

The earliest phase of the Puritan movement focused on the so-called "Vestments Controversy" of the 1560s. In 1566, Archbishop Matthew Parker issued his "Advertisements," requiring all clergy to wear the surplice during worship services. Many Puritan-minded ministers refused, arguing that the surplice was a relic of the Mass and that wearing it implied approval of the Catholic doctrines it represented. The controversy seems trivial to modern ears -- why would anyone risk his career and his livelihood over a piece of clothing? But for the Puritans, the principle at stake was enormous. The question was not really about vestments -- it was about the authority of Scripture over human traditions, the purity of worship, and the right of the church to impose requirements that went beyond the Word of God. As the Puritan divine Thomas Cartwright would later argue, the church has no authority to require anything in worship that God has not commanded: "The Word of God must be the rule of all our doings, in public as well as in private."

Thomas Cartwright (1535-1603) was one of the earliest and most important Puritan leaders. He was Lady Margaret Professor of Divinity at Cambridge -- one of the most prestigious academic positions in England -- and his lectures on the Book of Acts in 1570 created a sensation. Cartwright argued from the text of Acts that the New Testament church was governed not by bishops but by pastors and elders, that the offices of archbishop, archdeacon, and dean had no scriptural basis, and that the Church of England should be reformed along the lines of the Genevan model. His lectures attracted large crowds and provoked a fierce reaction from the university authorities. Cartwright was stripped of his professorship and forced into exile, but his ideas spread rapidly among the younger generation of Cambridge-educated ministers who would become the backbone of the Puritan movement.

The debate between Cartwright and his principal opponent, John Whitgift (who would later become Archbishop of Canterbury), established the fundamental terms of the Puritan controversy. Whitgift argued that in matters of church government and ceremony, the church had authority to determine its own practices, provided they did not contradict Scripture. Scripture, he maintained, prescribed certain essential doctrines but left the details of worship and government to the discretion of the church. This principle was known as the "normative" principle of worship -- what Scripture does not forbid, the church may permit.

Cartwright argued the opposite. He maintained that in matters of worship and church government, the church must follow the pattern laid down in Scripture and must not add anything that Scripture does not authorize. This principle -- the "regulative" principle of worship -- became one of the defining convictions of the Puritan movement. It meant that every aspect of church life must be tested by one question: "Where does the Bible command this?" If Scripture did not command a practice, the church had no right to impose it. This principle would shape Puritan worship, Puritan church government, and Puritan life for the next century and beyond.

The Puritan movement was never a single, monolithic party. From the beginning, it included a range of positions on church government and the proper strategy for reform. Some Puritans were Presbyterians, who believed that the Church of England should be restructured along the lines of the Genevan and Scottish models, with a system of congregational sessions, regional presbyteries, and national assemblies. Thomas Cartwright and Walter Travers were the leading advocates of this position. Travers produced "A Full and Plain Declaration of Ecclesiastical Discipline" (1574), sometimes called the first textbook of English Presbyterianism, which laid out a detailed blueprint for a Presbyterian Church of England.

Other Puritans were Congregationalists (or Independents), who believed that each local congregation was a complete church in itself, governed by its own members under Christ, and that no external authority -- whether bishop, presbytery, or national assembly -- had the right to govern the local church. Robert Browne, whose "Reformation Without Tarrying for Any" (1582) argued that true reformation must begin at the congregational level without waiting for the approval of bishops or magistrates, was an early advocate of this position. Henry Barrow and John Greenwood, who were executed in 1593 for their separatist convictions, carried the Congregationalist principle to its logical conclusion: if the Church of England was hopelessly corrupt, true believers must separate from it entirely and form their own gathered churches.

Still other Puritans remained within the Church of England and worked for reform from within, accepting the episcopal structure while pressing for changes in worship, preaching, and pastoral care. These conforming Puritans, as they are sometimes called, included some of the greatest preachers and theologians of the Elizabethan and early Stuart church. They did not seek to overthrow the established order but to transform it from within through faithful preaching, diligent pastoral work, and the power of personal example.

Despite these differences in strategy, the Puritans shared a core set of convictions that united them across party lines. First and foremost, they believed in the supreme authority of Scripture. The Bible was not merely a collection of inspiring stories or a guide to morals -- it was the very Word of the living God, "given by inspiration of God, and profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness: that the man of God may be perfect, throughly furnished unto all good works" (2 Timothy 3:16-17). The Puritans read the Bible with a seriousness, an intensity, and a comprehensiveness that has rarely been equaled. They did not merely read it -- they studied it, memorized it, meditated upon it, and sought to obey it in every particular of their daily lives.

Second, the Puritans believed in the necessity of conversion -- of a genuine, personal experience of saving grace. It was not enough to be baptized, to attend church, to receive the sacraments, and to live a respectable life. A person must be born again -- must experience the work of the Holy Spirit in conviction of sin, repentance, faith in Christ, and the assurance of salvation. This emphasis on the converted life was central to Puritan piety and gave it an intensity and earnestness that distinguished the Puritans from nominal Christians. As our Lord declared in John 3:3: "Verily, verily, I say unto thee, Except a man be born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God." And again in John 3:7: "Marvel not that I said unto thee, Ye must be born again."

Third, the Puritans believed in the centrality of preaching. The sermon was the heart of Puritan worship -- not the sacraments, not the liturgy, not the ceremonies, but the exposition of the Word of God by a learned, godly, and Spirit-filled minister. The Puritans called preaching "the ordinary means of salvation" -- the chief instrument by which God calls sinners to repentance and builds up His saints in faith and holiness. They demanded a learned ministry -- ministers who could read the Scriptures in the original languages, who had been trained in theology and church history, and who could open the meaning of a biblical text with clarity, depth, and practical application. The Puritan sermon was a work of art and a work of theology -- it was carefully structured, richly illustrated, deeply scriptural, and intensely practical. The great Puritan preacher William Perkins (1558-1602) laid the foundations of Puritan homiletics in his influential manual "The Art of Prophesying" (1592), which taught ministers how to interpret the Scriptures, how to derive doctrines from the text, and how to apply those doctrines to the consciences of their hearers.

William Perkins deserves extended attention, for he was, in many ways, the father of the Puritan movement as it matured in the late sixteenth century. Perkins was a Fellow of Christ's College, Cambridge, and his influence on the generation of Puritan ministers who would carry the movement into the seventeenth century was enormous. His students included William Ames, who became one of the most important Puritan theologians; Paul Baynes, who succeeded Perkins at Cambridge; and Richard Sibbes, whose warm, pastoral preaching earned him the title "the heavenly Doctor Sibbes." Perkins was a Calvinist of the deepest dye -- he affirmed the sovereignty of God in election and reprobation, the total depravity of human nature, the sufficiency of Christ's atoning work, and the perseverance of the saints. But he was also a pastor of extraordinary tenderness, particularly sensitive to the spiritual struggles of those who feared they might not be among the elect. His most famous work, "A Golden Chain" (1590), traced the entire order of salvation from God's eternal decree of election through calling, justification, sanctification, and glorification, providing anxious souls with a map of the spiritual life that they could use to examine their own hearts and find assurance of their salvation.

Perkins was also a pioneer of what we might call "practical divinity" -- the application of theology to the daily lives of ordinary Christians. He wrote treatises on the Christian's calling or vocation, on cases of conscience (the difficult moral decisions that Christians face in daily life), on the right ordering of the household, on the duties of parents and children, on the proper use of wealth, and on preparation for death. He taught that every lawful occupation is a calling from God -- that the farmer, the merchant, the housewife, and the servant serve God in their daily work just as truly as the minister serves God in the pulpit. This "doctrine of the calling" was one of the most distinctive and influential contributions of Puritan theology, and it shaped the work ethic and the social vision of the English-speaking world for centuries.

Fourth, the Puritans believed in the sanctity of the Christian household. The family was, in Puritan theology, a "little church" -- a domestic congregation with the father as its pastor, responsible for the spiritual instruction, moral discipline, and devotional life of every member. The Puritans established the practice of family worship -- daily Bible reading, prayer, psalm-singing, and catechetical instruction in the home. They wrote catechisms, devotional guides, and household manuals to equip parents for this sacred responsibility. They took seriously the commandment of Deuteronomy 6:6-7: "And these words, which I command thee this day, shall be in thine heart: And thou shalt teach them diligently unto thy children, and shalt talk of them when thou sittest in thine house, and when thou walkest by the way, and when thou liest down, and when thou risest up." The Puritan home was meant to be a school of godliness, where children learned the Scriptures, the catechism, and the habits of prayer from their earliest years.

Fifth, the Puritans believed in the importance of the Sabbath. The Lord's Day was to be kept holy -- set apart from ordinary work and devoted to public worship, private devotion, works of mercy, and spiritual conversation. The Puritan Sabbath was not a day of gloom and repression, as later caricatures would have it, but a day of rest, worship, and joy -- a foretaste of the eternal Sabbath rest that awaits the people of God in heaven. As the Westminster Shorter Catechism would later summarize: "The sabbath is to be sanctified by a holy resting all that day, even from such worldly employments and recreations as are lawful on other days; and spending the whole time in the public and private exercises of God's worship, except so much as is to be taken up in the works of necessity and mercy."

Sixth, the Puritans believed in covenant theology -- the understanding that God relates to His people through covenants, or solemn agreements, in which He makes promises and imposes obligations. The Puritans saw the entire story of the Bible as the unfolding of God's covenant purposes, from the covenant of works made with Adam before the Fall, through the covenant of grace established after the Fall and progressively revealed through Abraham, Moses, David, and the prophets, to the new covenant in Christ, sealed by His blood on the cross. Covenant theology gave the Puritans a unified framework for understanding all of Scripture and all of human history. It also shaped their understanding of the church (as a covenant community), of baptism (as the sign of entry into the covenant community), of the family (as a covenanted household), and of the nation (as a people in covenant with God, accountable for national faithfulness or unfaithfulness). The words of Deuteronomy 29:9 were foundational to Puritan thinking: "Keep therefore the words of this covenant, and do them, that ye may prosper in all that ye do."

Seventh, the Puritans had a profound sense of divine providence -- the conviction that God governs all events, great and small, according to His eternal purpose. Nothing happens by chance. Every circumstance of life -- prosperity and adversity, health and sickness, success and failure -- is ordered by the sovereign hand of God for His glory and for the good of His people. The Puritans kept diaries and journals in which they recorded the providences of God in their daily lives, tracing His hand in the smallest details and finding spiritual lessons in ordinary events. This habit of mind -- this constant awareness of the divine presence and purpose in all things -- gave Puritan life a depth and gravity that distinguished it from the casual religion of their contemporaries. As the Westminster Shorter Catechism would teach: "God's works of providence are, his most holy, wise, and powerful preserving and governing all his creatures, and all their actions." The Puritans lived under the eye of God, and they knew it.

The Puritans were also deeply concerned with what they called "mortification of sin" -- the daily, lifelong battle against the remaining corruption of the sinful nature. They did not believe that conversion made a person sinless; rather, they taught that the Christian life is a warfare between the new nature implanted by the Holy Spirit and the old nature inherited from Adam. The apostle Paul's words in Romans 7:24-25 resonated deeply with Puritan experience: "O wretched man that I am! who shall deliver me from the body of this death? I thank God through Jesus Christ our Lord." John Owen, the greatest of the Puritan theologians, devoted an entire treatise to this subject -- "Of the Mortification of Sin in Believers" (1656) -- in which he argued that the duty of every believer is to be "always at it whilst we live; cease not a day from this work; be killing sin or it will be killing you." This unflinching realism about the continuing power of sin, combined with an equally strong confidence in the grace of God to overcome sin, gave Puritan spirituality its distinctive tension and its remarkable depth.

The Puritans' relationship with the English crown during the reign of Elizabeth was complex and often tense. Elizabeth was determined to maintain the Settlement of 1559, and she regarded the Puritans' demands for further reform as a threat to her royal authority and to the stability of the realm. She appointed John Whitgift as Archbishop of Canterbury in 1583, and Whitgift launched a campaign of repression against Puritan ministers, requiring them to subscribe to the Prayer Book and the episcopal structure under penalty of suspension or deprivation. Many Puritan ministers were silenced, deprived of their livings, or driven into exile.

Yet the Puritan movement continued to grow, nourished by the presses that produced a flood of Puritan books, by the universities (especially Cambridge) that trained a new generation of Puritan ministers, and by the network of godly households that practiced the Puritan way of life in their homes. The great Puritan preacher Richard Greenham (c. 1535-1594), who served as rector of Dry Drayton near Cambridge for over twenty years, established a model of Puritan pastoral ministry that combined faithful preaching, diligent catechizing, personal counseling, and the care of souls in a way that transformed his small rural parish into a center of godliness. His example inspired a generation of Puritan pastors who sought to replicate his methods in their own parishes.

The accession of King James I (James VI of Scotland) to the English throne in 1603 raised Puritan hopes. James had been raised as a Presbyterian in Scotland, and the Puritans expected him to be sympathetic to their cause. They presented him with the Millenary Petition -- so called because it claimed to have the signatures of a thousand ministers -- which requested modest reforms: the elimination of certain ceremonies, the improvement of clerical education, the provision for a preaching ministry in every parish, and other changes. James responded by calling the Hampton Court Conference in January 1604, where representatives of the Puritan party met with the bishops in the presence of the king.

The conference produced little in the way of practical reform -- James sided with the bishops on most issues, and his famous declaration "No bishop, no king" made clear his conviction that the episcopal structure of the Church of England was essential to the stability of the monarchy. But the conference did produce one result of incalculable significance: it authorized the new translation of the Bible that would become the King James Version (1611). James himself proposed the project, and the Puritan scholar John Reynolds (or Rainolds), the president of Corpus Christi College, Oxford, enthusiastically supported it. The resulting translation -- produced by forty-seven scholars working in six committees over seven years -- became the Bible of the English-speaking world and one of the supreme achievements of English literature. It was also, as we noted in our lesson on William Tyndale, deeply indebted to Tyndale's pioneer translation work.

Under James I, the Puritan movement continued to develop in several important directions. The most significant development was the emergence of what historians call "experimental Calvinism" or "experimental predestinarianism" -- a distinctive approach to the Christian life that combined rigorous Calvinist theology with intense attention to the inner workings of the soul. The Puritan preachers and writers of this period -- men like Richard Sibbes (1577-1635), John Preston (1587-1628), and John Cotton (1585-1652) -- developed a sophisticated analysis of the conversion experience, tracing the soul's journey from conviction of sin through repentance, faith, assurance, and progressive sanctification. They wrote with extraordinary psychological insight about the doubts, fears, temptations, and consolations that believers experience in their walk with God. Richard Sibbes, in his beautiful work "The Bruised Reed and Smoking Flax" (1630), offered comfort to struggling believers with the words of Isaiah 42:3, applied to Christ: "A bruised reed shall he not break, and the smoking flax shall he not quench: he shall bring forth judgment unto truth." Sibbes taught that Christ is tender toward weak and struggling believers -- that He does not despise the smallest spark of genuine faith but nurtures it into a flame.

The Puritans also developed a rich tradition of devotional writing -- works designed to guide the believer in prayer, meditation, self-examination, and the practice of the Christian virtues. Arthur Dent's "The Plain Man's Pathway to Heaven" (1601) was one of the most popular books in Elizabethan and Jacobean England, going through dozens of editions and read by people of all classes. It presented the Christian life as a journey toward heaven, with clear signposts marking the way and practical advice for avoiding the pitfalls of sin and worldliness. Lewis Bayly's "The Practice of Piety" (1611) was even more popular, becoming one of the bestselling books of the seventeenth century and being translated into numerous languages. It guided the reader through the daily practice of Christian devotion -- morning and evening prayers, meditation on Scripture, preparation for the Lord's Supper, preparation for death, and the right use of the Sabbath.

Another crucial development during the Jacobean period was the growth of what historians call "lectureships" -- preaching positions funded by private donations, town corporations, or merchant guilds rather than by the established church. These lectureships allowed Puritan ministers who had been silenced or deprived by the bishops to continue preaching, and they provided additional preaching opportunities in towns and cities where the parish minister was inadequate. The lectureships were a brilliant strategy for advancing the Puritan cause within the established system. A town might have a conforming vicar who read the Prayer Book services dutifully but preached poorly, while a Puritan lecturer, funded by godly merchants and aldermen, delivered the powerful, searching, applicatory sermons that drew the crowds and transformed lives. The bishops were frustrated by the lectureships, which they rightly saw as a vehicle for Puritan influence outside their control, but they were unable to suppress them entirely because they were privately funded and met a genuine spiritual need.

It was during this period that the Puritan concept of the "gathered church" began to take more definite shape. Even within the established Church of England, Puritan ministers sought to create what amounted to inner congregations of the truly converted -- groups of committed believers who met for additional prayer, Bible study, and mutual encouragement beyond the regular parish services. These "conventicles," as they were sometimes called, were the seedbeds of Puritan piety. They fostered intimate Christian fellowship, accountability, and spiritual growth in a way that the large, mixed parish congregations could not. They also raised difficult questions about the nature of the church: Was the true church the national establishment, which included everyone in the parish whether converted or not? Or was it the gathered company of believers who had experienced genuine saving grace? This tension between the inclusive parish model and the exclusive gathered-church model would run through the entire history of Puritanism and would eventually contribute to the division between Presbyterians and Independents during the Civil War period.

We should also note the important role of women in the Puritan movement, though they were excluded from formal positions of church leadership. Puritan women were expected to be active partners with their husbands in the spiritual life of the household -- teaching children the catechism, leading family devotions in their husbands' absence, practicing hospitality to ministers and fellow believers, and exercising a quiet but powerful influence on the spiritual tone of the home. Some Puritan women became significant in their own right. Lady Brilliana Harley defended her family's castle against Royalist forces during the Civil War. The diary of Lady Margaret Hoby, written in the early seventeenth century, provides a remarkable window into the daily devotional practices of a Puritan gentlewoman. And the mothers and wives of countless Puritan ministers sustained their families through poverty, persecution, and exile with a faith and courage that deserve to be remembered. The Proverbs 31 woman was the Puritan ideal: "She openeth her mouth with wisdom; and in her tongue is the law of kindness. She looketh well to the ways of her household, and eateth not the bread of idleness" (Proverbs 31:26-27).

But the most challenging period for the Puritans was about to begin. When Charles I came to the throne in 1625, he quickly aligned himself with the high-church party within the Church of England, led by William Laud, whom Charles appointed as Bishop of London in 1628 and then Archbishop of Canterbury in 1633. Laud was not a Catholic, but he favored a ceremonial, sacramental style of worship that was anathema to the Puritans. He insisted on the "beauty of holiness" -- richly decorated churches, elaborate vestments, altars railed off at the east end of the church (rather than communion tables placed in the nave among the people), and strict conformity to the ceremonies of the Prayer Book. He used the ecclesiastical courts to silence, suspend, and deprive Puritan ministers who refused to comply.

The Laudian persecution drove many Puritans to despair of ever reforming the Church of England from within. Some chose to emigrate. The Pilgrims -- Separatist Puritans from the congregation of John Robinson in Leiden -- had already sailed to Plymouth on the Mayflower in 1620. Now a much larger migration began. In 1630, under the leadership of John Winthrop, a fleet of eleven ships carrying over a thousand Puritan settlers sailed for Massachusetts Bay. Winthrop delivered his famous sermon "A Model of Christian Charity" aboard the ship Arbella, declaring that their colony would be "a city upon a hill" -- a model of godly community that the whole world would watch. "The eyes of all people are upon us," Winthrop warned. "So that if we shall deal falsely with our God in this work we have undertaken, and so cause Him to withdraw His present help from us, we shall be made a story and a byword through the world." Over the next decade, approximately twenty thousand Puritans emigrated to New England in what historians call the Great Migration. They carried with them their Bibles, their catechisms, their conviction of divine calling, and their dream of building a New Jerusalem in the American wilderness.

But many Puritans remained in England, and the conflict between the Puritan movement and the Stuart monarchy would soon explode into civil war. The Long Parliament, which met in November 1640, was dominated by Puritan sympathizers. It abolished the hated Star Chamber and High Commission courts that Laud had used to persecute dissenters. It impeached and imprisoned Laud himself (he was eventually executed in 1645). And in June 1643, Parliament convened the Westminster Assembly of Divines -- an assembly of 121 ministers, plus members of Parliament and Scottish commissioners -- to advise on the reformation of the church's worship, government, and doctrine.

The Westminster Assembly, which met in the Jerusalem Chamber of Westminster Abbey from 1643 to 1652, produced the greatest body of Reformed confessional literature in the English language: the Westminster Confession of Faith, the Westminster Larger Catechism, and the Westminster Shorter Catechism. These documents became the doctrinal standards of Presbyterian and Reformed churches throughout the English-speaking world, and they remain in use to this day. The Assembly also produced a Directory for Public Worship, which replaced the Book of Common Prayer, and a Form of Presbyterial Church-Government. The theological depth, scriptural thoroughness, and pastoral wisdom of the Westminster Standards are a lasting monument to the Puritan commitment to systematic theology grounded in the Word of God.

The English Civil War erupted in August 1642, when King Charles I raised his standard at Nottingham and called his loyal subjects to arms against the rebellious Parliament. The causes of the war were complex -- political, constitutional, economic, and religious grievances had accumulated over decades -- but religion was at the very heart of the conflict. The Parliamentary cause drew its most passionate support from the Puritan heartlands of eastern and southeastern England, from the merchant communities of London, and from the godly ministers who saw the war as a providential opportunity to complete the reformation of the church. The Royalist cause drew its strength from the conservative nobility, the cathedral clergy, and the regions of western and northern England where traditional religion and deference to the crown remained strong.

The Parliamentary army, reorganized in 1645 as the New Model Army under the command of Sir Thomas Fairfax and Oliver Cromwell, was one of the most remarkable military forces in history. It was an army of praying, psalm-singing, Bible-reading soldiers, many of whom saw themselves as instruments of divine judgment against a tyrannical king and a corrupt church. Cromwell, who rose from colonel to lieutenant-general to Lord Protector of England, was himself a deeply devout Puritan who believed that God had called him to the work of national reformation. After the decisive Battle of Naseby on June 14, 1645, Cromwell wrote to the Speaker of the House of Commons: "Sir, this is none other but the hand of God; and to Him alone belongs the glory, wherein none are to share with Him." His letters and speeches are saturated with scriptural language and with a profound sense of divine providence guiding the events of history.

The execution of King Charles I on January 30, 1649 -- an event that shocked Europe and divided even the Puritan movement -- led to the establishment of the Commonwealth, an English republic governed by Parliament and, increasingly, by Cromwell himself. The Commonwealth and the Protectorate that followed (1653-1658) represented the high-water mark of Puritan political power. During this period, Cromwell pursued a policy of broad religious toleration for Protestants -- Presbyterians, Independents, Baptists, and even Quakers were given considerable freedom of worship, though Catholics and Anglicans were restricted. Cromwell readmitted the Jews to England in 1656, after their expulsion in 1290, partly on grounds of religious liberty and partly in the hope that their conversion to Christianity would hasten the Second Coming of Christ. These dramatic events form the political backdrop against which the greatest generation of Puritan writers and preachers did their work. John Owen (1616-1683), widely regarded as the greatest Puritan theologian and sometimes called "the Calvin of England," served as Cromwell's chaplain and as Vice-Chancellor of the University of Oxford. Owen was born in Stadham, Oxfordshire, the son of a Puritan minister, and he entered Queen's College, Oxford, at the age of twelve. He was a prodigious student, sleeping only four hours a night and devouring the works of the Church Fathers, the Schoolmen, and the Continental reformers. His conversion experience came after hearing an unknown preacher deliver a sermon on Matthew 8:26 -- "Why are ye fearful, O ye of little faith?" -- and the assurance that flooded his soul that day became the foundation of his extraordinary theological career.

Owen's literary output was staggering. His collected works fill twenty-four volumes and cover virtually every major topic in Christian theology. His treatise "The Death of Death in the Death of Christ" (1647) is the classic defense of particular redemption -- the doctrine that Christ died specifically for the elect, not merely to make salvation possible for all. His "Discourse on the Holy Spirit" (1674) remains the most comprehensive treatment of the person and work of the Holy Spirit in the English language. His "Communion with God" (1657) explored the believer's intimate fellowship with each person of the Trinity -- the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit -- and offered practical guidance for cultivating that fellowship in daily life. Owen wrote with a precision and depth that can make his prose challenging for modern readers, but those who persevere are rewarded with theological insights of extraordinary richness. As Charles Spurgeon, the great Victorian Baptist preacher, later said of Owen: "To master his works is to be a profound theologian." Thomas Goodwin (1600-1680) served as president of Magdalen College, Oxford, and as one of Cromwell's spiritual advisors. John Milton (1608-1674) served as Latin Secretary to the Council of State and wrote "Paradise Lost," the greatest epic poem in the English language. Richard Baxter (1615-1691) served as chaplain to a Parliamentary regiment and then as minister at Kidderminster in Worcestershire, where his pastoral labors transformed the town from a place of ignorance and vice into a model of godliness. Baxter preached twice on Sundays and once on Thursdays, catechized families in their homes on a systematic rotation, established schools for the children, and devoted himself to personal evangelism and counseling with an energy that was legendary. By the end of his ministry, he reported that of the approximately eight hundred households in Kidderminster, there were barely a dozen that did not regularly attend family worship. His "Reformed Pastor" (1656) -- written as a charge to the ministers of Worcestershire, urging them to follow his example of diligent pastoral care -- remains one of the most powerful appeals for ministerial faithfulness ever written. Baxter wrote with searing honesty: "I preached as never sure to preach again, and as a dying man to dying men." His "Saints' Everlasting Rest" (1650) -- written while he was gravely ill with what he believed to be a terminal disease -- is a magnificent meditation on the heavenly rest that awaits the people of God, drawing on the words of Hebrews 4:9: "There remaineth therefore a rest to the people of God." The book was an immediate and enduring bestseller, comforting generations of believers in their afflictions and directing their hearts toward the glory that awaits them.

We must also mention John Bunyan (1628-1688), the Bedford tinker who became one of the most beloved writers in the English language. Bunyan was not a university-educated divine like Owen or Baxter -- he was a poor man, the son of a brazier, with little formal education. But he was converted through the reading of two Puritan devotional works (Arthur Dent's "Plain Man's Pathway to Heaven" and Lewis Bayly's "Practice of Piety") and through the influence of a group of godly women in Bedford whose conversation about the things of God convicted him deeply. Bunyan became a powerful preacher and was imprisoned for twelve years (1660-1672) for preaching without a license after the Restoration. It was during his imprisonment that he wrote "Grace Abounding to the Chief of Sinners" (1666), his spiritual autobiography, and began work on "The Pilgrim's Progress" (1678), the allegorical masterpiece that became the most widely read book in the English language after the Bible. Bunyan's prose, shaped by years of immersion in the King James Bible, has a clarity, a vividness, and a dramatic power that have made "The Pilgrim's Progress" beloved by readers of every age and every culture. The journey of Christian from the City of Destruction to the Celestial City is the journey of every believer, and Bunyan tells it with the artistry of a genius and the compassion of a pastor.

The Puritan vision was comprehensive. It encompassed not just the church but the whole of life -- the home, the school, the marketplace, the court, the nation. The Puritans sought to bring every sphere of human existence under the lordship of Christ and the authority of Scripture. They were not perfect -- they could be rigid, censorious, intolerant of dissent, and harsh in their treatment of those who disagreed with them. But they were also men and women of extraordinary faith, learning, courage, and devotion. They produced a body of theological, devotional, and literary work that is without parallel in the English-speaking world. And they bequeathed to their spiritual descendants -- to us, the students of Puritan Gold -- a heritage of faith and godliness that is worth studying, worth cherishing, and worth passing on to the generation that comes after us.

Let us close with the words of the apostle Paul, which the Puritans loved and lived by: "I press toward the mark for the prize of the high calling of God in Christ Jesus" (Philippians 3:14). The Puritans pressed forward. They did not look back. They did not give up. They did not compromise. They pressed toward the mark -- the mark of a purified church, a godly nation, and a life lived wholly for the glory of God. May we press forward with the same resolve.

--- Closing ---

The Puritans were not content with a half-reformed church. They believed that the Word of God demands a whole-hearted response -- in our worship, in our families, in our work, and in our daily walk with the Lord. They were reformers of the Reformation, and their unfinished work continues wherever Christians take the Bible seriously as the supreme rule of faith and life. As the Puritan Richard Baxter wrote: "I preached as never sure to preach again, and as a dying man to dying men." May we live with the same urgency. Until next time, students.
