PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Doctrine Deep-Dive, Day 21
"Adoption — The Privilege of Sons"
The Doctrine of Adoption Explored Through the Puritans

---

Good evening. There is a reason the church has always valued catechesis, the systematic teaching of Christian truth. Without it, faith becomes shallow and vulnerable. Tonight we go deeper.

Tonight we draw from the work of Thomas Watson, specifically from "A Body of Divinity." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.

--- Scripture ---

Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Galatians 4:4-7:

"But when the fulness of the time was come, God sent forth his Son, made of a woman, made under the law, To redeem them that were under the law, that we might receive the adoption of sons. And because ye are sons, God hath sent forth the Spirit of his Son into your hearts, crying, Abba, Father. Wherefore thou art no more a servant, but a son; and if a son, then an heir of God through Christ."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.

--- Doctrine Study ---

Of all the benefits that flow from our union with Christ, adoption is perhaps the most astonishing and the most neglected. Justification is glorious: it declares the guilty sinner righteous in the sight of God. Sanctification is transformative: it progressively conforms the believer to the image of Christ. But adoption goes beyond both. It changes not merely our legal standing or our moral character but our relationship to God. Through adoption, the sinner who was once an enemy, a stranger, and a rebel becomes a son — a child of the living God, with all the privileges, intimacy, and inheritance that sonship implies.

Thomas Watson, in 'A Body of Divinity,' gives one of the most accessible and pastorally rich treatments of adoption in Puritan literature. Watson begins by distinguishing adoption from the other benefits of salvation. In justification, God acts as Judge, declaring us righteous. In adoption, God acts as Father, receiving us into His family. Justification removes the penalty of sin; adoption bestows the privilege of sonship. Justification gives us a right to heaven; adoption gives us the spirit of heaven — the Spirit of the Son, crying 'Abba, Father.'

Watson identifies several privileges of adoption. First, the adopted child has access to the Father. A subject may petition a king, but a son can climb into his father's lap. The adopted believer does not merely pray to a sovereign Ruler; he cries out to a loving Father. Watson writes that the word 'Abba' is the babbling of an infant — the most intimate, unguarded, trusting address imaginable. God invites His children to approach Him not with the formality of a courtier but with the freedom of a beloved child.

Second, the adopted child receives the Father's protection. The enemies of God's children are powerful: sin, Satan, the world, and death itself. But the Father guards His children with almighty power. Watson writes that the child of God is as safe as God is strong. No enemy can pluck him from the Father's hand. No affliction can separate him from the Father's love. The protection of God is not a general umbrella over humanity; it is the personal, particular, vigilant care of a Father for His own child.

Third, the adopted child is disciplined by the Father. Watson is careful to distinguish the Father's discipline from His wrath. Wrath is punitive; it aims at the destruction of the offender. Discipline is corrective; it aims at the restoration and growth of the child. The Father chastens His children not because He has ceased to love them but because He loves them too much to leave them as they are. Watson writes that the rod of discipline is in the hand of a Father, not a tyrant, and it is wielded in love, not in anger.

Fourth, and most gloriously, the adopted child receives an inheritance. Watson unfolds this privilege with evident delight. The inheritance of God's children is nothing less than God Himself. 'If a son, then an heir of God through Christ.' Earthly inheritances are divided among many heirs, and each portion is diminished by the sharing. But the divine inheritance is infinite and undivided. Each child of God inherits all of God — His love, His presence, His glory — without any diminishment. The inheritance is 'incorruptible, and undefiled, and that fadeth not away' (1 Peter 1:4).

The Puritan John Owen adds to Watson's treatment by grounding adoption in the eternal purpose of God. Adoption is not an afterthought or an addendum to the plan of salvation; it is the very purpose of redemption. Ephesians 1:5 says that God 'predestinated us unto the adoption of children by Jesus Christ to himself, according to the good pleasure of his will.' Owen argues that the Father redeemed us in order to adopt us — that the whole work of Christ on the cross was aimed at bringing many sons to glory.

Watson also addresses the evidences of adoption. How can a person know that he is truly a child of God? Watson offers several marks: a family likeness (growing conformity to the character of the Father), a family affection (love for the Father, love for the brethren), and a family longing (desire for the Father's house — heaven). These marks are not the ground of adoption but the evidence of it.

Patrick, do you live as a son or as a slave? Do you approach God with the freedom and confidence of a beloved child, or with the anxiety and distance of a servant who is never sure of his standing? The gospel declares that you are no longer a servant but a son — and if a son, then an heir of God through Christ. Live in that identity today. Let it change the way you pray, the way you obey, the way you face suffering, and the way you think about your future. You are a son of the Most High God. Act like it — not with presumption, but with the joyful confidence of one who knows his Father's heart.

--- Reflection ---

Now let us turn the lens inward. What is God saying to you through tonight's study?

Question 1: Do I relate to God primarily as a Judge before whom I must perform, or as a Father whose love is unconditional and whose home is always open?

Question 2: How does the doctrine of adoption change the way I pray — do I approach God with the intimacy of a child or the formality of a stranger?

Question 3: Can I identify the marks of adoption in my own life — a growing family likeness, a love for the brethren, a longing for the Father's house?

Question 4: In what practical ways can I live more fully in the identity of a beloved son of God this week?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.

--- Closing Prayer ---

Abba, Father — I can scarcely believe that You have given me the right to call You by that name. I, who was an enemy and a stranger, have been adopted into Your family through the blood of Your Son. Help me to live as a son, not a slave — with confidence, not anxiety, with intimacy, not distance, with gratitude, not performance. Thank You for the inheritance that awaits me — incorruptible, undefiled, and unfading. In the name of Jesus, my Elder Brother, Amen.

Press on, dear saint. The race is worth running, and the prize is Christ Himself.
