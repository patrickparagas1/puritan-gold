PURITAN GOLD -- Melchizedek: A Complete Biblical Study, Part 6 of 8
Hebrews 7:18-28 — The Unchangeable Priesthood


Welcome back to Puritan Gold. We are deep in the heart of our complete biblical study of Melchizedek, and today we come to the summit of the argument in Hebrews chapter 7. In our last episode, the writer proved that the Melchizedekian priesthood is greater than the Levitical. Now, in verses 18 through 28, he draws out the full implications of this superiority. Here we find some of the most comforting words in all of Scripture for the troubled conscience — the better hope, the better covenant, the unchangeable priesthood, and the glorious truth that Christ is able to save them to the uttermost that come unto God by Him.

Let us read the full text. Hebrews 7, verses 18 through 28:

"For there is verily a disannulling of the commandment going before for the weakness and unprofitableness thereof. For the law made nothing perfect, but the bringing in of a better hope did; by the which we draw nigh unto God. And inasmuch as not without an oath he was made priest: (for those priests were made without an oath; but this with an oath by him that said unto him, The Lord sware and will not repent, Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchisedec:) by so much was Jesus made a surety of a better testament."

"And they truly were many priests, because they were not suffered to continue by reason of death: but this man, because he continueth ever, hath an unchangeable priesthood. Wherefore he is able also to save them to the uttermost that come unto God by him, seeing he ever liveth to make intercession for them."

"For such an high priest became us, who is holy, harmless, undefiled, separate from sinners, and made higher than the heavens; who needeth not daily, as those high priests, to offer up sacrifice, first for his own sins, and then for the people's: for this he did once, when he offered up himself. For the law maketh men high priests which have infirmity; but the word of the oath, which was since the law, maketh the Son, who is consecrated for evermore."


Let us work through this passage with the reverence and care it demands.

Verses 18 and 19: "For there is verily a disannulling of the commandment going before for the weakness and unprofitableness thereof. For the law made nothing perfect, but the bringing in of a better hope did; by the which we draw nigh unto God."

The writer makes an astonishing declaration. The former commandment — the entire system of Levitical law — is disannulled. It is set aside, abrogated, removed. And the reason is its weakness and unprofitableness. Now, this does not mean the law was sinful or that God erred in giving it. Paul says in Romans 7 that the law is holy, just, and good. But the law was weak in that it could not accomplish what sinners needed most — it could not perfect the conscience, it could not permanently remove guilt, it could not bring the worshiper into the immediate presence of God. The blood of bulls and goats could not take away sins.

But — and here is the gospel in a single verse — the bringing in of a better hope did. The word better appears again and again in Hebrews. A better hope, a better covenant, a better sacrifice, a better country. Everything about Christ's ministry is better than what came before. And the result of this better hope is that we draw nigh unto God. Under the old system, the people were kept at a distance. They could not enter the Holy of Holies. Only the high priest could enter, and only once a year, and only with blood and incense and the constant threat of death if he erred in the ritual. But through Christ, we draw nigh unto God — boldly, freely, at any time, in any place.

John Owen meditates extensively on the phrase "draw nigh unto God." He writes that this is the ultimate purpose of all religion — to bring sinners near to God. Every other blessing flows from this: forgiveness, adoption, sanctification, comfort, joy, peace — all are the fruit of nearness to God. And all were impossible under the Levitical system, which could only maintain a distant, fearful, provisional relationship between God and the worshiper. But Christ, by His better priesthood, brings us near.

Verses 20 through 22 return to the theme of the oath: "And inasmuch as not without an oath he was made priest: (for those priests were made without an oath; but this with an oath by him that said unto him, The Lord sware and will not repent, Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchisedec:) by so much was Jesus made a surety of a better testament."

The Levitical priests were appointed by statute, without an oath. But Christ was appointed by the sworn oath of God — Psalm 110:4 is quoted yet again. And the conclusion drawn is monumental: by so much was Jesus made a surety of a better testament — or better covenant. The word surety means guarantor. Jesus Himself personally guarantees the new covenant. Not angels, not prophets, not priests — Jesus. He stands as the surety, the pledge, the bond. If the covenant fails, He fails. If the covenant holds, it is because He holds it.

Thomas Goodwin writes that this is the strongest possible ground for the believer's assurance. The covenant of grace does not depend on our faithfulness but on Christ's. He is the surety. He guarantees not only God's promises to us but also our obligations to God. He has fulfilled every condition of the covenant on our behalf. His perfect obedience is counted as ours. His atoning death satisfies every demand of justice against us. The covenant cannot fail because the surety cannot fail.

Verses 23 and 24 draw the contrast between the many priests and the one priest: "And they truly were many priests, because they were not suffered to continue by reason of death: but this man, because he continueth ever, hath an unchangeable priesthood."

Under the old covenant, there was a constant succession of priests. The Jewish historian Josephus counted eighty-three high priests from Aaron to the destruction of the temple. Each one served for a time, then died, and was replaced. Their ministry was interrupted by the universal enemy — death. No matter how faithful a particular high priest was, he could not escape the grave. His ministry ended, and another took his place.

But this man — Jesus — because He continueth ever, because death has no more dominion over Him, has an unchangeable priesthood. The Greek word translated unchangeable is aparabatos, which means non-transferable, inviolable, permanent. It cannot be passed on to a successor because it never needs to be. Christ holds it exclusively and eternally.

Owen writes that the unchangeableness of Christ's priesthood is the ground of the unchangeableness of the believer's salvation. Our standing before God does not depend on a priest who might die and leave us without an intercessor. It depends on a Priest who lives forever. The prayers He offered for us yesterday, He offers today. The blood He presented in the heavenly sanctuary does not lose its efficacy with time. His intercession is as fresh and powerful now as it was the moment He ascended into glory.

And now comes verse 25, one of the most precious verses in all of Scripture: "Wherefore he is able also to save them to the uttermost that come unto God by him, seeing he ever liveth to make intercession for them."

To the uttermost. The Greek phrase eis to panteles means completely, entirely, perfectly, to the very end. It speaks both of the extent and the duration of salvation. Christ saves completely — there is no part of the sinner's need that His salvation does not reach. And He saves permanently — there is no point in the future when His salvation expires. He saves to the uttermost.

And the reason He is able to save to the uttermost is given: seeing he ever liveth to make intercession for them. This is the present, ongoing ministry of Christ at the right hand of God. He is not idle in heaven. He is actively interceding — pleading His own blood, applying His own merits, presenting His own righteousness — on behalf of every soul that has come to God through Him.

Matthew Henry writes that this verse should silence every fear and answer every doubt that troubles the believer's conscience. Are your sins great? He saves to the uttermost. Is your faith weak? He ever lives to intercede for you. Are your enemies strong? His intercession is stronger. Have you fallen and fallen again? Come unto God by Him, and He is able — not merely willing, but able, with all the power of His endless life — to save you completely and forever.

Verses 26 through 28 give us the character of this High Priest: "For such an high priest became us, who is holy, harmless, undefiled, separate from sinners, and made higher than the heavens."

This is the High Priest who became us — who was fitting, appropriate, exactly suited to our need. He is holy — devoted to God with absolute consecration. He is harmless — literally without evil, incapable of malice or deception. He is undefiled — free from every stain of sin. He is separate from sinners — not in the sense of being distant from us, for He drew near to sinners throughout His earthly ministry, but in the sense of being categorically different in His moral nature. And He is made higher than the heavens — exalted above all creation, enthroned at the right hand of the Majesty on high.

Verse 27 continues: "Who needeth not daily, as those high priests, to offer up sacrifice, first for his own sins, and then for the people's: for this he did once, when he offered up himself."

The Levitical high priests offered sacrifice daily, and they had to offer first for their own sins before they could offer for the people. They were sinners ministering on behalf of sinners. But Christ had no sins of His own. He needed no atonement for Himself. And He offered not the blood of animals but Himself — His own body, His own blood, His own life. And He did it once — not repeatedly, not annually, not generation after generation, but once for all time. The word once is the great word of Hebrews — ephapax — once for all, never to be repeated, permanently sufficient.

Verse 28 concludes the chapter with a magnificent summary: "For the law maketh men high priests which have infirmity; but the word of the oath, which was since the law, maketh the Son, who is consecrated for evermore."

Two priesthoods stand in final contrast. The law produced priests who had infirmity — who were weak, sinful, mortal. But the word of the oath — Psalm 110:4, spoken centuries after the law was given — produced the Son, who is consecrated, or perfected, for evermore. The finality of this statement is breathtaking. The argument is complete. The Son of God, appointed by divine oath, serving after the order of Melchizedek, holds an eternal, unchangeable priesthood. He saves to the uttermost. He ever lives. He is consecrated for evermore.

Stephen Charnock wrote that a right understanding of Christ's unchangeable priesthood is the greatest antidote to spiritual despair. When the soul is troubled, when sin presses heavily upon the conscience, when the believer feels distant from God — let him remember this: there is a Priest in heaven who is holy, harmless, undefiled, who has offered Himself once for all, who holds His office forever, and who at this very moment is interceding for that troubled soul.

Beloved, we have now completed the exposition of Hebrews 7. In our next episode, we shall step back from the New Testament and examine the broader Old Testament types and shadows — the theme of the priest-king throughout the Old Testament, from Salem to Jerusalem, from Melchizedek to Zechariah's vision of the Branch.
