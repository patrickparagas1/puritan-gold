PURITAN GOLD -- My Growth, Day 28
"The Saints' Everlasting Rest — Richard Baxter"
Reading Day — Baxter on Heavenly-Mindedness


Welcome. Today is a Reading day. We engage with Richard Baxter's magnum opus, The Saints' Everlasting Rest — a work written when Baxter was gravely ill and believed he was near death. It is a sustained meditation on heaven that has been called the finest treatise on the Christian's future hope in the English language.

Let us read Hebrews 4:9-11 (KJV):

"There remaineth therefore a rest to the people of God. For he that is entered into his rest, he also hath ceased from his own works, as God did from his. Let us labour therefore to enter into that rest, lest any man fall after the same example of unbelief."


Richard Baxter wrote The Saints' Everlasting Rest in 1650, when chronic illness convinced him that death was imminent. He survived another forty years, but the urgency of the book never faded. It is a work born from the threshold of eternity — written by a man who believed he was stepping through the door.

Baxter's central argument is that the Christian's primary meditation should be on the rest that awaits in heaven. Not rest as idleness, but rest as arrival — the end of the journey, the consummation of all hopes, the fulfillment of every longing. Baxter writes that the saints' rest includes the cessation of all suffering, the perfection of all joy, and the unmediated presence of God.

Baxter describes heaven with a combination of theological precision and emotional intensity that is unmatched. Heaven, he writes, is the full and perfect enjoyment of God by the perfected saints according to the measure of their capacity through all eternity. Every word matters. Full — nothing lacking. Perfect — no defect. Enjoyment — not merely knowledge but experience. Of God — not of rewards or blessings, but of God Himself. By perfected saints — those who have been completely freed from sin. According to the measure of their capacity — finite creatures enjoying an infinite God, forever growing in capacity. Through all eternity — without end.

Baxter then makes a practical argument that is the heart of the book: Christians should practice the discipline of heavenly meditation. He writes that the mind that dwells on heaven will be transformed by what it contemplates — just as the face of Moses shone after being in God's presence. The Christian who regularly meditates on the future rest will find that present suffering becomes more bearable, present temptation becomes less appealing, and present duties become more meaningful.

Baxter offers a detailed method for this meditation. First, set apart a specific time — ideally daily, at least weekly — for sustained meditation on heaven. Second, retire to a private place free from distraction. Third, begin by remembering the truths of Scripture about the future rest — the promises of Christ, the descriptions in Revelation, the assurances of the epistles. Fourth, engage your affections — do not merely think about heaven; feel it. Let the meditation move you to longing, to joy, to gratitude, to worship. Fifth, apply the meditation to your present circumstances — let the reality of the coming rest reshape your relationship to present pain and present pleasure.

Baxter warns against several obstacles to heavenly meditation. Worldly-mindedness — the habit of thinking only about earthly concerns — is the greatest enemy. The person whose mind is absorbed by finances, entertainment, ambition, or even daily routine has no room for heaven. Unbelief is another obstacle — the nagging doubt that heaven is real, that it is worth longing for, that it is actually coming. Guilt is a third — the sense that you are too sinful to contemplate heaven, too unworthy to claim a place there. Baxter answers all of these with the Gospel: heaven is real because God has promised it, it is worth longing for because God is there, and you are qualified to enter not by your merit but by Christ's blood.

The effect of Baxter's meditation, when practiced faithfully, is a transformation of the entire Christian life. The heavenly-minded person is not detached from earth. Rather, they are more effective on earth because they hold it loosely. They serve more freely because they are not grasping for earthly rewards. They suffer more patiently because they know the suffering is temporary. They love more generously because they have an inexhaustible source of joy that does not depend on human reciprocation.


There remains a rest for the people of God. Meditate on that rest today. Let its glory reshape your perspective on everything. Lord, lift my eyes above the horizon of this present age. Let me see the rest that awaits — the fullness, the perfection, the joy, the presence. And let that vision make me more faithful, more patient, and more joyful in the journey that remains. Amen.
