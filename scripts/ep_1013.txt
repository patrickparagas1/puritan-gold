PURITAN GOLD -- Study Topic: Female Piety, Part 3 of 10
"The Character of the Pious Woman — Virtue, Grace, and Inner Beauty"
John Angell James — Female Piety

---

Welcome back, dear listener, to Puritan Gold. We are studying Female Piety by John Angell James, and today we come to one of the richest chapters in the whole work: the character of the pious woman. What does she look like — not outwardly, but inwardly? What are the marks of genuine grace in the female soul? Let us turn to Scripture and to our author, and let us be instructed.

Let us read from 1 Peter 3:3-4, in the King James Version:

"Whose adorning let it not be that outward adorning of plaiting the hair, and of wearing of gold, or of putting on of apparel; But let it be the hidden man of the heart, in that which is not corruptible, even the ornament of a meek and quiet spirit, which is in the sight of God of great price."

And from Galatians 5:22-23:

"But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, faith, Meekness, temperance: against such there is no law."

--- Teaching ---

John Angell James was not content to merely assert that women should be pious. He wanted to paint a portrait — a detailed, living portrait — of what true female piety actually looks like when it takes root in the heart and works its way outward into every dimension of a woman's life. In doing so, he produced some of the most beautiful and searching pages in all of Victorian devotional literature.

James begins by drawing a sharp distinction between what the world calls an accomplished woman and what God calls a godly woman. The world, he observes, admires beauty of face, elegance of manner, brilliance of conversation, and mastery of the fashionable arts. These things are not sinful in themselves, but they are utterly insufficient as the foundation of true character. A woman may possess all of them and yet be destitute of the one thing needful — the grace of God in her heart. Conversely, a woman may be plain in appearance, simple in manner, and unnoticed by the world, and yet possess a beauty that angels admire and God Himself pronounces precious.

This is the beauty that Peter describes: the ornament of a meek and quiet spirit. James expounds this phrase with great care. Meekness, he explains, is not weakness. It is strength under divine control. It is the disposition that submits to the will of God without murmuring, that bears injuries without resentment, and that responds to provocation with patience and gentleness. The meek woman is not a doormat; she is a woman whose soul is anchored in God, who draws her strength not from herself but from the indwelling Spirit of Christ. She can endure what would break a merely natural spirit, because her confidence is not in her own resources but in the faithfulness of her covenant God.

Quietness of spirit, James continues, does not mean silence or passivity. It means an inward tranquility that is not easily disturbed by outward circumstances. The quiet spirit is the spirit that has learned, in the words of the apostle Paul, to be content in whatsoever state it finds itself. It is the spirit that does not fret over what it cannot control, does not clamor for attention or recognition, and does not allow the anxieties of life to overwhelm the peace that Christ gives. This is a rare and precious thing, and it is the fruit not of temperament but of grace.

Thomas Watson, in his treatise The Art of Divine Contentment, described the contented Christian as one who has learned that God's providence is wiser than our wishes. James applies this insight directly to the character of the pious woman. She is content not because her circumstances are easy — they often are not — but because she trusts the hand that orders them. She can say, with the Psalmist, "My soul, wait thou only upon God; for my expectation is from him" (Psalm 62:5).

James goes on to describe several specific marks of the pious woman's character. Let us consider the chief of these.

First, she is marked by humility. The pious woman knows herself to be a sinner saved by grace. She does not think of herself more highly than she ought to think. She is genuinely surprised when others notice her virtues, because she is far more aware of her remaining corruptions than of her attainments. This humility is not affected or false; it flows naturally from a true knowledge of God's holiness and her own unworthiness. Richard Baxter once wrote that the humble soul sees God as infinitely great and itself as infinitely small, and in that vision finds both its terror and its peace. The pious woman embodies this truth.

Second, she is marked by devotion to the Word of God. James observes that the truly pious woman loves the Scriptures. She does not read them merely out of duty or habit, but out of genuine delight. The Bible is her daily companion, her counselor, her comfort, and her guide. She reads it in the morning to prepare her soul for the day. She returns to it in the evening to examine her conscience and to find rest in the promises of God. She memorizes its words, meditates upon its truths, and applies its precepts to every situation she faces. She is, in the language of Psalm 1, like a tree planted by the rivers of water, that bringeth forth her fruit in her season.

Third, she is marked by a life of prayer. James insists that the pious woman is, above all things, a woman of prayer. She prays in the morning and in the evening. She prays for her husband, her children, her friends, her pastor, her church, and her neighbors. She prays in seasons of joy and in seasons of sorrow. She prays when she is strong and when she is weak. Prayer is the breath of her spiritual life, the channel through which grace flows into her soul and through which her soul pours itself out before God. John Flavel wrote that prayer is the soul's trading with heaven, and the godly woman carries on this holy commerce daily, with a fervency and constancy that puts many men to shame.

Fourth, she is marked by gentleness and kindness. The pious woman is not harsh, bitter, or censorious. She speaks the truth in love. She is quick to forgive and slow to take offense. She looks for the best in others and is grieved when she must acknowledge the worst. Her tongue is governed by grace, and her words carry the fragrance of Christ. "The mouth of the righteous speaketh wisdom, and his tongue talketh of judgment. The law of his God is in his heart; none of his steps shall slide" (Psalm 37:30-31). James applies these words broadly to the speech and demeanor of the godly woman, who adorns the doctrine of God in all things.

Fifth, she is marked by practical benevolence. Her piety is not confined to the closet; it issues forth in acts of mercy and compassion. She visits the sick, feeds the hungry, comforts the mourning, and cares for the orphan and the widow. She does not merely feel sympathy; she acts upon it. James notes that the Proverbs 31 woman "stretcheth out her hand to the poor; yea, she reacheth forth her hands to the needy" (Proverbs 31:20). True piety always bears fruit in practical love.

This portrait is not an impossible ideal. It is the portrait of a woman whom grace has transformed — not perfectly in this life, but truly and progressively. Every mark James describes is a fruit of the Holy Spirit's work, and every woman who belongs to Christ may grow in these graces by the power of that same Spirit. The goal is not perfection but direction — the steady, persevering pursuit of holiness, which is the calling of every believer.

--- Application ---

1. Examine your own character against the marks James describes: humility, devotion to Scripture, prayerfulness, gentleness, and practical kindness. In which area do you sense the greatest need for growth? Bring that area before the Lord in prayer this week.

2. Memorize 1 Peter 3:3-4 and meditate upon it daily. Ask the Holy Spirit to form in you the ornament of a meek and quiet spirit.

3. Consider the difference between worldly beauty and the beauty that God prizes. Are there areas of your life where you are investing more in outward adornment than in the cultivation of inward grace? Ask the Lord for a right ordering of your priorities.

4. Practice one act of practical benevolence this week — a visit to someone who is sick, a word of encouragement to someone who is discouraged, a gift to someone in need. Let your piety bear visible fruit.

--- Closing ---

Let us pray.

O Lord God, Thou who seest not as man seeth, who lookest not upon the outward appearance but upon the heart — we pray that Thou wouldest form in us the character of true piety. Grant us the humility to know ourselves, the devotion to love Thy Word, the fervency to pray without ceasing, the gentleness to speak with grace, and the compassion to serve those in need. Adorn us, we beseech Thee, with the incorruptible beauty of a meek and quiet spirit, which is in Thy sight of great price. Through Jesus Christ our Lord, who is Himself the fairest of ten thousand and the altogether lovely. Amen.

Until next time, dear listener, may the beauty of the Lord our God be upon you, and may His grace be your adornment. Walk in peace.
