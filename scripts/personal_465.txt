PURITAN GOLD -- My Growth, Day 4
"The Bruised Reed — Richard Sibbes"
Reading Day — Richard Sibbes and the Tenderness of Christ


Welcome. Today is a Reading day, and we turn to one of the most beloved Puritan works ever written: The Bruised Reed by Richard Sibbes (1577-1635). Sibbes was known as "the heavenly Doctor Sibbes" for the warmth and tenderness of his preaching. His little book on Isaiah 42:3 has comforted anxious souls for nearly four hundred years.

Let us read Isaiah 42:3 (KJV):

"A bruised reed shall he not break, and the smoking flax shall he not quench: he shall bring forth judgment unto truth."


Richard Sibbes was a preacher of uncommon gentleness. While other Puritans thundered against sin — and rightly so — Sibbes specialized in comforting the afflicted conscience. He knew that many Christians struggle not with flagrant rebellion but with crushing discouragement. They feel that their faith is too weak, their obedience too inconsistent, their love for God too cold. They look at their spiritual lives and see a bruised reed ready to snap and a wick barely smoking, about to go out.

To these struggling believers, Sibbes brought the words of Isaiah: Christ will not break the bruised reed. He will not quench the smoking flax. He will not finish off what is already dying. Instead, He will tend it, nurture it, and fan it back to flame.

Sibbes begins by identifying who the bruised reed is. It is any Christian who feels the weight of their own weakness. It is the new convert who wonders whether their faith is genuine. It is the longtime believer who has grown cold and wonders whether God has given up on them. It is the person who knows they should pray more, love more, serve more, trust more — but who finds themselves falling short again and again. If this describes you, Sibbes says, take heart. You are not disqualified. You are precisely the person Christ came to help.

The nature of a reed is to be slender, fragile, easily bent by the wind. A bruised reed is weaker still — cracked, damaged, useless for any practical purpose. In the hands of anyone other than Christ, it would be discarded. But Christ does not discard bruised reeds. He holds them gently. He binds up the crack. He supports the weakness until the reed can stand again.

Sibbes writes that Christ deals with His people according to their condition, not according to some rigid standard of perfection. He knows that you are dust. He remembers your frame. He does not demand from you a strength that you do not have. Instead, He meets you in your weakness and provides what you lack. This is grace — not grace for the strong, but grace for the broken.

The smoking flax is equally instructive. Flax was used as a wick in oil lamps. When the oil runs low, the wick smokes — giving off an unpleasant odor and barely any light. A housewife would pinch it out and replace it. But Christ does not pinch out the smoking wick. He adds oil. He replenishes what has been depleted. He restores the flame that was about to die.

Sibbes applies this to the Christian who has lost their spiritual fervor. Perhaps you once burned brightly — passionate in prayer, eager in worship, zealous in service. But the oil has run low. The demands of life, the weight of disappointment, the accumulation of small discouragements have left you barely smoking. Sibbes says: Christ sees you. He does not look at your smoking wick with disgust. He looks at it with compassion. And He adds oil.

This is the heart of Christ that the Puritans could not stop talking about. Thomas Goodwin wrote an entire treatise on the subject — The Heart of Christ in Heaven Towards Sinners on Earth — arguing that Christ's heart is not cold or indifferent toward struggling believers. It is warm, tender, moved with compassion. Christ's present ministry in heaven is not judicial severity but pastoral tenderness. He is touched with the feeling of our infirmities.

Sibbes also counsels those who minister to bruised reeds — pastors, parents, friends. He warns against harshness with struggling souls. "Do not break what Christ would heal," he writes. "Do not quench what Christ would fan to flame." Many Christians have been damaged not by the world but by the church — by harsh words from leaders, by unrealistic expectations from fellow believers, by a culture of performance that leaves no room for weakness. Sibbes calls the church to gentleness, to patience, to the same tenderness that Christ Himself displays.

If you are bruised today, hear this: Christ will not break you. If your flame is barely smoking, hear this: Christ will not quench you. He is gentle. He is patient. He is committed to your restoration. And the flame that He fans back to life will burn brighter than it ever burned before.


Are you a bruised reed? Christ holds you gently. Is your flame barely smoking? Christ adds oil. Trust His tenderness today. Lord Jesus, I am bruised. I am barely smoking. But You have promised not to break me and not to quench me. Fan the flame. Bind up the crack. Restore what sin and weariness have depleted. I trust Your gentle hands. Amen.
