PURITAN GOLD — Episode 26
"A Gentle Answer Turns Away Wrath"
Week 4, Day 5 (Friday) — Proverbs 15

---

Think back to the last argument you had. Not a major blowup, necessarily — maybe just a tense exchange with your spouse, a sharp word with a coworker, a moment of frustration with one of your kids. Now... think about how it started. What lit the fuse?

I'd be willing to bet it wasn't the issue itself that caused the explosion. It was the tone. It was how something was said, not what was said. A harsh word. A dismissive sigh. A voice raised just a fraction too high. And suddenly, what could have been a calm conversation became a battlefield.

Solomon knew this. Three thousand years ago, he put his finger on the very nerve of human conflict and wrote one of the most famous lines in all of Scripture. Proverbs 15, verse 1: "A soft answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger."

Today on Puritan Gold, we're opening up Proverbs 15 — and friends, this chapter is medicine for the soul. It speaks to the power of our words, the reach of God's eyes, the condition of our hearts, and the kind of life that truly satisfies. This is practical wisdom at its finest.

Let's begin.

---

"A soft answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger."

It sounds so simple, doesn't it? And yet how rarely we do it. When someone comes at us with anger, our natural instinct is to match their energy. Fire meets fire. Volume meets volume. Accusation meets counter-accusation. It's reflexive. It's human. And it's disastrous.

But Solomon offers us a different way. A soft answer. Not a weak answer — a soft one. There's a world of difference. Weakness retreats. Softness engages — but with gentleness, with restraint, with the kind of measured grace that refuses to let anger set the terms of the conversation.

Spurgeon reflected on this principle and observed that "a gentle word is a healing balm, while a sharp retort is a poisoned arrow." And he's right. You can defuse nearly any confrontation if you're willing to lower your voice when everything in you wants to raise it.

But here's the deeper truth: you can't give a soft answer if your heart is hard. The mouth speaks from the overflow of the heart — Jesus said that in Matthew 12:34. So this isn't just about vocal techniques or communication strategies. This is about the condition of your inner life. A heart saturated in the fear of the Lord, a heart softened by grace, a heart that has been broken and rebuilt by the gospel — that heart can produce a soft answer even in the heat of conflict.

---

Solomon doesn't stop at verse 1. He continues to explore the power of the tongue through the opening verses of the chapter. Verse 2: "The tongue of the wise commends knowledge, but the mouths of fools pour out folly." Verse 4: "A gentle tongue is a tree of life, but perverseness in it breaks the spirit."

There it is again — "a tree of life." We saw this image in Proverbs 13, and here it returns. A gentle tongue — a tongue governed by wisdom, restrained by grace, directed by love — is a source of life. It nourishes. It heals. It refreshes.

But a perverse tongue — a tongue that twists, that manipulates, that cuts — breaks the spirit. Not just annoys the spirit. Breaks it. Solomon is telling us that words have the power to shatter a human soul. And if you've ever been on the receiving end of verbal cruelty — from a parent, a spouse, a friend — you know this is true.

The Puritan pastor Jeremiah Burroughs, in his classic work The Rare Jewel of Christian Contentment, wrote about the importance of guarding not only our words to God but our words to one another. He understood that the tongue is the instrument through which the heart reveals itself — and that a contentment with God's providence naturally produces gentleness in speech. When you're at peace with God, you're less likely to be at war with others.

---

Now, let me draw your attention to verse 3, because it's one of those verses that should stop us in our tracks: "The eyes of the Lord are in every place, keeping watch on the evil and the good."

Every place. Not just the church. Not just the moments when you're on your best behavior. Every place. The office. The kitchen. The car when you're stuck in traffic. The bedroom at night when no one else is watching. The private conversations. The secret thoughts. The hidden motives.

God sees it all.

Now... this could terrify you. And in a sense, it should. The awareness that the holy God of the universe is watching everything you do should produce a reverent fear. But it should also comfort you. Because the same God who sees your sin also sees your struggle. He sees the tears you cry when nobody's looking. He sees the battles you fight against temptation that no one else knows about. He sees the quiet faithfulness that the world overlooks.

Thomas Watson wrote that "the eye of God is as much upon the believer in the valley as it is in the mountaintop. He sees you in your secret sorrow as clearly as He sees you in your public praise." And that's a precious thought. You are never unseen. You are never unnoticed. The eyes of the Lord are upon you — and they are eyes of love.

---

As we move deeper into Proverbs 15, we encounter one of the most beautiful and counter-cultural themes in all of wisdom literature: the theme of contentment.

Verse 13: "A glad heart makes a cheerful face, but by sorrow of heart the spirit is crushed."

Verse 15: "All the days of the afflicted are evil, but the cheerful of heart has a continual feast."

And then — verse 16: "Better is a little with the fear of the Lord than great treasure and trouble with it."

This is revolutionary, friends. Solomon — the wealthiest man in the ancient world — is telling us that wealth isn't the point. Treasure isn't the goal. The fear of the Lord... that's the treasure. A little with God is better than a fortune without Him.

This cuts directly against the grain of everything our culture tells us. We're told that more is better. That comfort is king. That if you just had a little more money, a little more success, a little more recognition, then you'd finally be happy.

But Solomon, who had more than anyone, says no. Happiness — true, deep, lasting happiness — comes from fearing the Lord. It comes from a glad heart. And a glad heart doesn't depend on your bank account. It depends on your relationship with God.

Verse 17 reinforces this: "Better is a dinner of herbs where love is than a fattened ox and hatred with it." A simple meal shared in love is worth more than a feast served in bitterness. The quality of your relationships matters more than the quantity of your possessions. And the atmosphere of your home — the love, the peace, the warmth — is worth more than anything money can buy.

---

Let me pause here and speak pastorally for a moment. Because I know that some of you listening don't feel cheerful. Some of you are in the "afflicted" category Solomon mentions in verse 15. All your days feel evil. The sorrow of heart in verse 13 — that's yours. You know what it's like to have a crushed spirit.

And I want you to hear this: Solomon is not dismissing your pain. He's not saying, "Just cheer up." He's acknowledging the reality of affliction. He knows that some seasons of life are relentlessly hard. And in the same breath, he's pointing you to the only source of genuine cheer — the fear of the Lord.

Not a forced cheerfulness. Not a plastic smile. But a deep, quiet gladness that comes from knowing that the God who sees everything is also the God who sustains everything. A gladness rooted not in your circumstances but in your God.

Spurgeon, who suffered terribly from depression, once wrote: "I have found that the sweetest comforts in trial come not from the removal of the trial but from the presence of the Lord within it." And that's the continual feast Solomon is talking about. Not the absence of pain — but the presence of God in the pain.

---

Let me also highlight verse 22: "Without counsel plans fail, but with many advisers they succeed."

This is practical wisdom for every area of life — but especially for the major decisions. Don't trust your own judgment alone. Seek counsel. Ask the wise. Listen to people who fear the Lord and have walked with Him longer than you have.

The Puritans were great practitioners of this principle. They gathered in conferences. They wrote letters to one another seeking advice. They submitted their theological writings to their peers for correction. They understood that wisdom is not a solo endeavor. It's communal. It grows in the soil of honest, humble conversation.

And verse 33 closes the chapter with this: "The fear of the Lord is instruction in wisdom, and humility comes before honor."

There it is again. The fear of the Lord. It's the thread that runs through every chapter of Proverbs, every verse of wisdom literature, every page of the Bible. Fear God. Revere Him. Take Him seriously. And from that reverent fear will flow the gentle tongue, the glad heart, the contented spirit, and the wise counsel that this chapter commends.

---

So as we close today, let me leave you with this challenge. This weekend, pay attention to your words. Not just what you say — but how you say it. When tension arises — and it will — practice the soft answer. Lower your voice when you want to raise it. Choose gentleness when your flesh demands harshness.

And remember: you can only give what you have. So fill your heart with the Word. Soak your soul in the fear of the Lord. Let the gospel make you gentle. Because the gentle tongue... is a tree of life. And this world — your family, your church, your community — desperately needs that tree.

Let's pray.

Lord, we confess that our tongues have been instruments of harm more often than we'd like to admit. We've stirred up anger with harsh words. We've crushed spirits with careless speech. Forgive us. And by Your Spirit, make us people of gentle answers. Give us glad hearts — not because our circumstances are easy, but because You are good. Help us to treasure Your fear above all earthly treasure, and to find in You the continual feast that nothing in this world can provide. Guard our words this day, O Lord, and let the meditations of our hearts be acceptable in Your sight. Through Christ, our gentle and mighty Savior, we pray. Amen.
