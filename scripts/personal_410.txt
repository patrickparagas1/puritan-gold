PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Guided Puritan Reading, Day 10
"What Mortification Is and Is Not"
John Owen — Mortification of Sin, Chapters 5-6


Welcome to our guided reading. The book before us tonight has nourished Christians for centuries. We approach it not as critics but as students, not as experts but as hungry learners. Let us read together, slowly and prayerfully.

Tonight we draw from the work of John Owen, specifically from "The Mortification of Sin." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Romans 6:11-12:

"Likewise reckon ye also yourselves to be dead indeed unto sin, but alive unto God through Jesus Christ our Lord. Let not sin therefore reign in your mortal body, that ye should obey it in the lusts thereof."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


Owen now offers a precise definition of what mortification is, and equally important, what it is not. His goal is to protect the believer from both false confidence and false despair.

Owen begins with what mortification is not. First, mortification is not the utter destruction of sin in this life. No believer achieves sinless perfection before glorification. Owen writes that 'indwelling sin will remain in us as long as we are in this body.' The man who expects to reach a point where temptation ceases entirely is pursuing a mirage. Mortification weakens sin, limits its operations, and reduces its dominion, but it does not annihilate it.

Second, mortification is not the mere dissimulation or concealment of sin. A man may hide his sin from others and even from himself, yet that sin remains unmortified. Owen warns against the common self-deception of confusing invisibility with victory. The sin that is pushed underground does not die; it merely finds new channels. Owen writes that some men think they have mortified a sin when they have merely transferred it. The man who conquers open anger may become inwardly bitter. The man who stops overeating may begin overworking. The flesh is endlessly creative in finding new expressions for old desires.

Third, mortification is not the occasional disruption of sin's activity. Owen notes that sin may be temporarily quieted by various means: a change in circumstances, a season of illness, the natural waning of particular desires with age. But these are not mortification; they are merely the ebb of a tide that will flow again. Mortification requires deliberate, Spirit-empowered engagement with the root of sin, not merely its symptoms.

Fourth, mortification is not the diversion of sinful energy into socially acceptable channels. Owen is remarkably perceptive on this point. A man may redirect his pride from self-aggrandizement into religious zeal, or his anger from domestic rage into righteous indignation, and believe that he has dealt with the sin. But Owen insists that the root of pride and anger remain intact. The fruit has changed its appearance, but the tree is the same.

Having established what mortification is not, Owen turns to what it is. He identifies three essential elements.

First, mortification involves the habitual weakening of sin. Owen uses the metaphor of a sick man gradually losing strength. As the disease of sin is treated by the Spirit, its power to influence the will, dominate the affections, and control the actions diminishes. The temptation that once overwhelmed now merely nags. The desire that once consumed now merely irritates. This weakening is not sudden but gradual, and it is the fruit of sustained, Spirit-dependent effort.

Second, mortification involves constant fighting against sin. Owen is insistent: mortification is not a passive state but an active warfare. The believer must resist the first motions of sin in the heart, fight against sinful thoughts before they become sinful affections, and oppose sinful affections before they become sinful actions. Owen writes that the man who is truly mortifying sin is in a state of perpetual vigilance, like a city with sentries posted at every gate.

Third, mortification involves frequent success against sin. Owen does not say unbroken success; the believer will sometimes fall. But the pattern of a mortifying soul is one of increasing victory. The falls become less frequent, less grievous, and more quickly repented of. The man who is truly mortifying sin can look back over months and years and see, by the grace of God, genuine progress in holiness.

Owen summarizes the nature of mortification beautifully: it is the soul's perpetual conflict with sin, carried on by the power of the Holy Spirit, resulting in the progressive weakening of sinful desires and the progressive strengthening of holy ones, all to the glory of God and the assurance of the soul.

Take stock today. Are you engaged in true mortification, or in one of the counterfeits? Are you hiding your sin, transferring it, or genuinely weakening it by the Spirit's power? The honest answer to this question determines the trajectory of your spiritual life.


Here are some questions to carry with you into the coming days.

Question 1: Which of Owen's counterfeits of mortification do I most recognize in my own life — concealment, transfer, or diversion?

Question 2: Can I honestly see evidence of the habitual weakening of a particular sin over the past year?

Question 3: Am I in a state of 'perpetual vigilance' against sin, or have I let down my guard in certain areas?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


O God, give me discernment to distinguish true mortification from its counterfeits. I do not want to deceive myself. Show me where I have been hiding sin rather than killing it. Grant me the perseverance to fight daily and the assurance that by Your Spirit, genuine progress is possible. In Jesus' name, Amen.

Rest in His grace tonight. Tomorrow is a new day of growth.
