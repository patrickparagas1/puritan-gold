PART ONE: WHY SCRIPTURE COMES FIRST

The Westminster Confession of Faith begins, fittingly, with the doctrine of Scripture. This was not an arbitrary choice. The divines understood that before you can say anything authoritative about God, creation, salvation, or the church, you must first establish the authority of the source from which those truths are drawn. If the Bible is not trustworthy, then nothing built upon it is secure. But if the Bible is what it claims to be — the inspired, infallible Word of God — then its teachings carry the weight of divine authority. Every other chapter of the Confession depends upon this first chapter. The doctrine of the Trinity, the doctrine of salvation, the doctrine of the church — all of these rest upon the conviction that the Bible is the reliable Word of God.

This ordering reflects a principle that the Protestant Reformers established a century before the Westminster Assembly. Martin Luther, at the Diet of Worms in 1521, declared: "My conscience is captive to the Word of God. I cannot and I will not retract anything, since it is neither safe nor right to go against conscience. Here I stand. I can do no other. God help me." Luther was asserting that Scripture, not popes, not councils, not tradition, is the final authority for the Christian. John Calvin, in his Institutes of the Christian Religion, placed his discussion of Scripture at the very beginning, writing: "For as the aged, or those whose sight is defective, when any book, however fair, is set before them, though they perceive that there is something written, are scarcely able to make out two consecutive words, but, when aided by glasses, begin to read distinctly, so Scripture, gathering together the impressions of Deity, which, till then, lay confused in our minds, dissipates the darkness, and shows us the true God clearly."

The Westminster divines stood firmly in this Reformation tradition. They knew that the question "How do we know anything about God?" must be answered before any other theological question can be addressed. And their answer was: we know about God through His Word — the Holy Scriptures.

PART TWO: THE NECESSITY OF SCRIPTURE — SECTION 1

The Confession opens with a remarkable statement about the necessity of Scripture. Section 1 of Chapter 1 reads:

"Although the light of nature, and the works of creation and providence, do so far manifest the goodness, wisdom, and power of God, as to leave men inexcusable; yet are they not sufficient to give that knowledge of God, and of his will, which is necessary unto salvation; therefore it pleased the Lord, at sundry times, and in divers manners, to reveal himself, and to declare that his will unto his church; and afterwards, for the better preserving and propagating of the truth, and for the more sure establishment and comfort of the church against the corruption of the flesh, and the malice of Satan and of the world, to commit the same wholly unto writing; which maketh the holy Scripture to be most necessary; those former ways of God's revealing his will unto his people being now ceased."

This dense paragraph contains several crucial truths. Let us unpack them carefully.

First, the Confession acknowledges what theologians call "general revelation" — the knowledge of God that is available through nature, conscience, and the providential ordering of the world. The Psalmist declared this truth magnificently: "The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork. Day unto day uttereth speech, and night unto night sheweth knowledge. There is no speech nor language, where their voice is not heard" (Psalm 19:1-3, KJV). The Apostle Paul made the same point in his letter to the Romans: "For the invisible things of him from the creation of the world are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made, even his eternal power and Godhead; so that they are without excuse" (Romans 1:20, KJV).

General revelation is real and significant. The beauty of a sunset, the vastness of the starry heavens, the intricate design of a living cell, the voice of conscience within the human heart — all of these point to the existence and character of God. They reveal something of His "goodness, wisdom, and power," as the Confession says. And they leave human beings "without excuse" for their failure to acknowledge Him.

But — and this is the crucial point — general revelation is not sufficient for salvation. The beauty of creation can point us toward God, but it cannot tell us the gospel. The voice of conscience can tell us something about right and wrong, but it cannot tell us how to be forgiven. The ordering of providence can show us that God governs the world, but it cannot reveal the way of salvation through Jesus Christ. For this saving knowledge, we need something more. We need special revelation — God speaking directly to His people.

The Puritan theologian Stephen Charnock, in his monumental work The Existence and Attributes of God, explained this distinction with characteristic depth: "The works of creation declare the being of God, but do not declare the being of the Trinity or the method of salvation. Nature can show us that God is, but only Scripture can show us who God is and what He has done for us in Christ. The heavens declare the glory of God's power, but the Scriptures declare the glory of God's grace."

The Confession then traces the history of divine revelation. God revealed Himself "at sundry times, and in divers manners" — echoing the language of Hebrews 1:1, which declares, "God, who at sundry times and in divers manners spake in time past unto the fathers by the prophets" (KJV). This revelation came through dreams and visions, through theophanies and angelic messengers, through prophets and priests and kings. But over time, God committed this revelation to writing. The oral traditions of the patriarchs were inscribed by Moses. The words of the prophets were recorded on scrolls. The teachings of the apostles were preserved in letters and gospels. And the result is the Holy Scriptures — the written Word of God.

Why was it necessary for God to commit His revelation to writing? The Confession gives three reasons. First, "for the better preserving" of the truth — written documents endure in a way that oral traditions do not. Second, "for the more sure establishment and comfort of the church against the corruption of the flesh, and the malice of Satan and of the world" — a written standard provides certainty and stability in the face of human weakness and Satanic opposition. Third, because "those former ways of God's revealing his will unto his people" have "now ceased" — the era of new prophetic revelation ended with the apostles. We do not need new revelations because we have the completed Scriptures, which contain everything necessary for faith and life.

William Perkins, the great Puritan theologian of Cambridge who died in 1602, just four decades before the Assembly convened, wrote extensively on the necessity of Scripture. He taught that "the Word of God is the only foundation of faith, the only rule of life, and the only judge of controversies." Perkins's influence on the Westminster divines was immense. Many of them had been trained in the Puritan tradition he helped to establish, and his emphasis on the centrality of Scripture permeated their work.

PART THREE: THE CANON OF SCRIPTURE — SECTION 2

The Confession then addresses the canon of Scripture — the list of books that belong in the Bible. Section 2 enumerates all sixty-six books of the Old and New Testaments by name. For the Old Testament, it lists the five books of Moses (Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy), the historical books (Joshua, Judges, Ruth, First and Second Samuel, First and Second Kings, First and Second Chronicles, Ezra, Nehemiah, Esther), the poetic books (Job, Psalms, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Song of Solomon), and the prophetic books (Isaiah, Jeremiah, Lamentations, Ezekiel, Daniel, Hosea, Joel, Amos, Obadiah, Jonah, Micah, Nahum, Habakkuk, Zephaniah, Haggai, Zechariah, Malachi). For the New Testament, it lists the four Gospels, the Acts of the Apostles, the epistles of Paul, the general epistles, and the Revelation of John.

The Confession affirms that these books, "all of which are given by inspiration of God, to be the rule of faith and life." The word "inspiration" here translates the Greek word theopneustos, used by the Apostle Paul in 2 Timothy 3:16: "All scripture is given by inspiration of God" — literally, "God-breathed." The Scriptures are not merely human documents that contain some divine truth. They are the very breath of God — His words communicated through human authors.

The Confession also addresses the books commonly called the Apocrypha — books such as Tobit, Judith, Wisdom of Solomon, Ecclesiasticus, Baruch, and First and Second Maccabees. Section 3 states that these books, "not being of divine inspiration, are no part of the Canon of the Scripture; and therefore are of no authority in the Church of God, nor to be any otherwise approved, or made use of, than other human writings." This was an important distinction in the seventeenth century, as the Roman Catholic Church had officially declared the Apocrypha to be canonical at the Council of Trent in 1546. The Westminster divines rejected this claim, following the consensus of the Protestant Reformers and the testimony of the early church, which had generally distinguished between the canonical and apocryphal books.

The Puritan scholar John Lightfoot, one of the most learned members of the Assembly and an expert in rabbinic literature, argued persuasively that the Jewish canon — the twenty-four books of the Hebrew Bible, which correspond to our thirty-nine Old Testament books — was the canon recognized by Jesus and the apostles. He pointed out that Jesus, in Luke 24:44, referred to "the law of Moses, and the prophets, and the psalms" — the three divisions of the Hebrew Bible — as the Scriptures that testified about Him. The apocryphal books were not included in this canon.

PART FOUR: THE INSPIRATION OF SCRIPTURE

Before we examine the Confession's teaching on the authority of Scripture, we must dwell on the doctrine of inspiration itself, for this is the foundation upon which the authority, sufficiency, and clarity of Scripture all rest.

The word "inspiration" as applied to Scripture comes from the Apostle Paul's declaration in 2 Timothy 3:16-17: "All scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness: that the man of God may be perfect, throughly furnished unto all good works" (KJV). The Greek word theopneustos, translated "given by inspiration of God," literally means "God-breathed." The Scriptures did not originate in the minds of men; they originated in the mind of God and were breathed out by Him through human authors.

The Apostle Peter further explained the mechanism of inspiration: "For the prophecy came not in old time by the will of man: but holy men of God spake as they were moved by the Holy Ghost" (2 Peter 1:21, KJV). The word "moved" here is the Greek word pheromenoi, which means to be carried along, as a ship is carried along by the wind. The human authors of Scripture were not passive instruments, like pens in the hand of a scribe. They retained their individual personalities, vocabularies, writing styles, and perspectives. But they were carried along by the Holy Spirit in such a way that what they wrote was exactly what God intended them to write. The result is a book that is fully human and fully divine — written by men, but authored by God.

The Westminster divines understood this doctrine of inspiration as extending to every word of the original text — what theologians call "verbal plenary inspiration." "Verbal" means that the inspiration extends to the very words, not merely to the ideas or concepts. "Plenary" means that every part of Scripture is equally inspired — not just the theological sections but the historical sections, the genealogies, the poetry, the narratives. The Lord Jesus Himself affirmed the verbal inspiration of Scripture when He said, "For verily I say unto you, Till heaven and earth pass, one jot or one tittle shall in no wise pass from the law, till all be fulfilled" (Matthew 5:18, KJV). A "jot" is the smallest letter in the Hebrew alphabet, and a "tittle" is a small distinguishing mark on a Hebrew letter. Jesus was saying that not even the smallest detail of Scripture will fail to be fulfilled. That is how precise divine inspiration is.

The Puritan John Owen, in his treatise on the divine original of Scripture, wrote extensively on this subject. Owen argued that the Holy Spirit so guided the human authors that "every word, every syllable, every letter of the sacred text was precisely what God intended to be written." Owen was careful to distinguish this from the idea of mechanical dictation — the authors were not mindless scribes taking down dictation. Rather, the Spirit so worked in and through their personalities, experiences, and abilities that the resulting text was simultaneously their own and God's.

This doctrine of inspiration has practical implications that we must not overlook. If the Bible is God-breathed, then it is infallible — it cannot err. If it is God-breathed, then it is inerrant — it contains no errors of fact. If it is God-breathed, then it is authoritative — it carries the weight of God's own authority. These are not abstract theological concepts. They determine how you approach your Bible every time you open it.

PART FIVE: THE AUTHORITY OF SCRIPTURE — SECTIONS 4 AND 5

With the doctrine of inspiration firmly established, the Confession makes a crucial affirmation about the authority of Scripture. Section 4 states:

"The authority of the Holy Scripture, for which it ought to be believed and obeyed, dependeth not upon the testimony of any man or church, but wholly upon God (who is truth itself), the author thereof; and therefore it is to be received, because it is the Word of God."

This is one of the most important statements in the entire Confession. The Bible's authority does not depend on the testimony of any man or church. The authority of Scripture rests on God alone, who is its author. The church recognizes and receives the Bible as authoritative, but it does not make the Bible authoritative. This is a vital distinction. The Roman Catholic Church at the time of the Reformation claimed that the Bible's authority derived from the church's endorsement — that the church gave the Bible its authority by recognizing it as canonical. The Westminster divines rejected this claim emphatically. The Bible is authoritative because God spoke it, period. The church is the servant of the Word, not the master of it.

John Calvin had made this point with his characteristic precision: "A most pernicious error widely prevails that Scripture has only so much weight as is conceded to it by the consent of the church. As if the eternal and inviolable truth of God depended upon the decision of men!" The Westminster divines echoed Calvin's conviction. The church did not create the canon; it recognized it. The church did not give the Bible its authority; it submitted to it.

But how do we know that the Bible is truly from God? Section 5 addresses this question with characteristic care:

"We may be moved and induced by the testimony of the Church to an high and reverend esteem of the holy Scripture; and the heavenliness of the matter, the efficacy of the doctrine, the majesty of the style, the consent of all the parts, the scope of the whole (which is to give all glory to God), the full discovery it makes of the only way of man's salvation, the many other incomparable excellencies, and the entire perfection thereof, are arguments whereby it doth abundantly evidence itself to be the Word of God; yet, notwithstanding, our full persuasion and assurance of the infallible truth and divine authority thereof, is from the inward work of the Holy Spirit, bearing witness by and with the Word in our hearts."

This section presents a two-layered approach to the assurance that the Bible is God's Word. The first layer consists of external evidences — rational arguments that point toward the divine origin of Scripture. The Confession lists several of these: the heavenliness of its content, the power of its teaching, the majesty of its style, the harmony of its parts, the God-centered scope of the whole, the clarity of its revelation of salvation, and its many other excellencies. These are genuine and weighty evidences. The unity of the Bible — sixty-six books written by over forty authors across fifteen centuries, yet telling one coherent story — is remarkable. The fulfilled prophecies of Scripture — hundreds of specific predictions about Christ, all fulfilled in precise detail — are compelling. The transforming power of Scripture — its ability to convert sinners, comfort the afflicted, and sanctify believers — is undeniable.

But the Confession recognizes that external evidence alone is not enough to produce "full persuasion and assurance." For that, we need the internal testimony of the Holy Spirit. The Spirit who inspired the Scriptures also testifies in the hearts of believers that these writings are indeed divine. This is not a subjective feeling divorced from evidence but the Spirit's work of opening our eyes to see what is objectively true. Just as the sun provides both the light that illuminates objects and the light that enables our eyes to see them, so the Spirit both inspired the Scriptures and enables us to recognize them for what they are.

The Puritan John Owen, in his massive work The Reason of Faith, developed this doctrine of the internal testimony of the Spirit at great length. Owen argued that the Holy Spirit's witness is not an additional revelation beyond Scripture but a divine illumination that enables us to perceive the self-authenticating glory of Scripture. He wrote: "The Scripture hath that in itself which will evidence its own divine original to them that are not blinded by the god of this world. It hath that glory shining in it, or through it, which manifesteth the majesty of its Author."

This teaching has immense practical significance. It means that our confidence in the Bible does not ultimately rest on our ability to win intellectual arguments about its reliability — though such arguments are valuable and important. Our confidence rests on the Spirit's witness within us, confirming what the external evidence already indicates. This is why even a child or an uneducated believer can have a deep and unshakeable confidence that the Bible is God's Word — not because they have mastered the arguments of apologetics, but because the Spirit has borne witness in their hearts.

PART FIVE: THE SUFFICIENCY OF SCRIPTURE — SECTION 6

The Confession next affirms the sufficiency of Scripture. Section 6 states:

"The whole counsel of God, concerning all things necessary for his own glory, man's salvation, faith, and life, is either expressly set down in Scripture, or by good and necessary consequence may be deduced from Scripture: unto which nothing at any time is to be added, whether by new revelations of the Spirit, or traditions of men."

This is a sweeping and important claim. The Bible contains "the whole counsel of God" concerning everything necessary for God's glory and human salvation, faith, and life. We do not need additional revelations, visions, traditions, or pronouncements from any human authority to know what God requires of us. The Bible is sufficient — it is enough.

The Confession recognizes two ways that biblical truth may be known. Some truths are "expressly set down in Scripture" — stated explicitly in the text. For example, the command "Thou shalt not steal" (Exodus 20:15, KJV) is expressly stated. Other truths may be deduced "by good and necessary consequence" from what Scripture states. For example, the doctrine of the Trinity is not stated in a single verse but is deduced from the combined testimony of many passages that affirm the deity of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, and the unity of the one God. Both kinds of truth — explicit statements and necessary deductions — carry the authority of Scripture.

The practical implications of this doctrine are enormous. If the Bible is sufficient, then we do not need to look to popes, prophets, or gurus for additional revelation. We do not need to wait for new visions or special words from God to know His will. We have His will — it is written in the sixty-six books of the Bible. The Psalmist expressed this sufficiency beautifully: "The law of the LORD is perfect, converting the soul: the testimony of the LORD is sure, making wise the simple. The statutes of the LORD are right, rejoicing the heart: the commandment of the LORD is pure, enlightening the eyes" (Psalm 19:7-8, KJV).

The Puritan pastor Thomas Brooks, in his work Precious Remedies Against Satan's Devices, warned against those who would add to God's Word: "Satan knows that if he can get Christians to look beyond the Scripture for guidance, he has them in his snare. The sufficiency of Scripture is the Christian's shield against every device of the devil. When Satan tempted Christ in the wilderness, our Lord answered every temptation with Scripture: 'It is written.' He did not appeal to visions. He did not appeal to tradition. He appealed to the written Word of God. And that Word was sufficient to defeat the tempter."

The Confession also adds a cautionary note: "Nevertheless, we acknowledge the inward illumination of the Spirit of God to be necessary for the saving understanding of such things as are revealed in the Word." The Spirit's illumination is necessary not because the Bible is unclear but because we are sinful. Sin has darkened our understanding, and we need the Spirit to open our eyes to comprehend what the Bible teaches. The Apostle Paul explained this in his first letter to the Corinthians: "But the natural man receiveth not the things of the Spirit of God: for they are foolishness unto him: neither can he know them, because they are spiritually discerned" (1 Corinthians 2:14, KJV).

PART SIX: THE CLARITY OF SCRIPTURE — SECTION 7

The Confession teaches the clarity — or perspicuity — of Scripture. Section 7 states:

"All things in Scripture are not alike plain in themselves, nor alike clear unto all; yet those things which are necessary to be known, believed, and observed, for salvation, are so clearly propounded and opened in some place of Scripture or other, that not only the learned, but the unlearned, in a due use of the ordinary means, may attain unto a sufficient understanding of them."

This is a balanced and wise statement. The Confession does not claim that every passage of the Bible is easy to understand. Some passages are genuinely difficult. The Apostle Peter acknowledged this when he wrote about the letters of Paul: "in which are some things hard to be understood, which they that are unlearned and unstable wrest, as they do also the other scriptures, unto their own destruction" (2 Peter 3:16, KJV). The book of Revelation contains symbols that have puzzled interpreters for two millennia. Some of the prophetic literature of the Old Testament is extraordinarily complex. The Westminster divines were honest about this.

But — and this is the crucial point — the things necessary for salvation are clearly taught. The gospel is not hidden in obscure passages. The way of salvation through faith in Christ is plainly set forth throughout the Bible. The Ten Commandments are clearly stated. The Lord's Prayer is straightforward. The great truths of the faith — that God is the Creator, that man is a sinner, that Christ is the Savior, that salvation is by grace through faith — are taught so clearly and in so many places that anyone, "not only the learned, but the unlearned," can understand them.

This doctrine was revolutionary in the context of the Reformation. The Roman Catholic Church had argued that the Bible was too difficult for ordinary people to understand and that the church's magisterium (teaching authority) was needed to interpret it correctly. This was used to justify keeping the Bible in Latin and discouraging its translation into the common language of the people. The Reformers insisted, on the contrary, that the Bible belongs to the people and is accessible to the people. William Tyndale, the great English Bible translator who was martyred in 1536, declared to a learned opponent: "If God spare my life, ere many years I will cause a boy that driveth the plough shall know more of the Scripture than thou doest."

The Puritan William Bridge wrote: "God hath so ordered His Word that the fundamental and saving truths thereof lie upon the surface, like gold upon the ground, that every passenger may take them up. And yet there are deeper mines of truth for the learned to dig into." This captures the Confession's teaching perfectly. The Bible is both accessible to the simplest believer and inexhaustible for the profoundest scholar.

PART SEVEN: THE INTERPRETATION OF SCRIPTURE — SECTIONS 9 AND 10

The Confession addresses the original languages of Scripture in Section 8, affirming that the Old Testament in Hebrew and the New Testament in Greek, "being immediately inspired by God, and by his singular care and providence kept pure in all ages, are therefore authentical." It also affirms the necessity of translating the Scriptures into the common language of every nation, "so that the Word of God dwelling plentifully in all, they may worship him in an acceptable manner, and, through patience and comfort of the Scriptures, may have hope" (citing Romans 15:4).

Section 9 provides the foundational hermeneutical principle — the rule of interpretation. The Confession states that "the infallible rule of interpretation of Scripture, is the Scripture itself; and therefore, when there is a question about the true and full sense of any Scripture, (which is not manifold, but one,) it must be searched and known by other places that speak more clearly."

This principle — that Scripture interprets Scripture — is one of the most important principles in the history of biblical interpretation. It is known by its Latin name, analogia fidei — the analogy of faith. When a passage is difficult to understand, we should look to clearer passages on the same subject for guidance. We should not impose our own meanings on the text. We should not read the Bible through the lens of our own cultural assumptions or personal preferences. We should let the Bible speak for itself, comparing Scripture with Scripture to arrive at the true meaning.

The Puritan theologian Thomas Manton, who served as clerk to the Westminster Assembly and later wrote an extensive commentary on the Epistle of James, exemplified this principle in his own preaching and writing. Manton insisted that "no doctrine should be established from obscure texts when plain texts on the same subject are available. The Spirit of God doth not speak one thing in one place and a contrary thing in another. When we find a hard saying, we must seek the sense from plainer passages."

The Lord Jesus Himself modeled this principle. When Satan tempted Him in the wilderness by quoting Psalm 91 out of context, Jesus responded by citing Deuteronomy 6:16: "It is written again, Thou shalt not tempt the Lord thy God" (Matthew 4:7, KJV). Jesus used a clearer passage to correct a misinterpretation of a less clear passage. Scripture was interpreted by Scripture.

Finally, Section 10 establishes the supreme authority of Scripture as the final judge of all theological controversies: "The Supreme Judge, by which all controversies of religion are to be determined, and all decrees of councils, opinions of ancient writers, doctrines of men, and private spirits, are to be examined, and in whose sentence we are to rest, can be no other but the Holy Spirit speaking in the Scripture."

This is a magnificent conclusion to the chapter. The final authority in all matters of faith and practice is not the church, not tradition, not human reason, not personal experience — but the Holy Spirit speaking in the Scripture. Councils may err. Theologians may be wrong. Traditions may be misguided. But God's Word stands forever. As the prophet Isaiah declared, "The grass withereth, the flower fadeth: but the word of our God shall stand for ever" (Isaiah 40:8, KJV).

PART EIGHT: THE BIBLE IN THE HISTORY OF THE CHURCH

The Westminster Confession's chapter on Scripture did not arise in a vacuum. It was the culmination of centuries of Christian reflection on the nature and authority of the Bible.

In the early church, the fathers wrestled with the question of which books belonged in the canon. Athanasius, Bishop of Alexandria, in his Festal Letter of 367 AD, provided the first known list that exactly matches our present New Testament canon of twenty-seven books. The Council of Carthage in 397 AD confirmed this same list. Throughout this process, the church was not creating the canon but recognizing the books that the Holy Spirit had already authenticated through their apostolic origin, their consistency with the received faith, and their acceptance by the universal church.

The medieval church, unfortunately, increasingly obscured the central truths of Scripture beneath layers of tradition, allegory, and clerical authority. The Bible was chained to pulpits — literally — and the common people were discouraged from reading it for themselves. The Fourth Lateran Council of 1215 moved to restrict Bible reading to the clergy, and the Council of Toulouse in 1229 explicitly forbade lay people from possessing copies of the Scriptures in the common tongue.

The Reformation reversed this tragic development. Martin Luther translated the Bible into German. William Tyndale translated it into English, at the cost of his life — he was strangled and burned at the stake in Vilvoorde, Belgium, in October 1536. His dying prayer, according to the chronicler John Foxe, was: "Lord, open the King of England's eyes." Within three years of Tyndale's death, King Henry the Eighth authorized the Great Bible for use in every English church — a Bible that drew heavily upon Tyndale's translation.

The Geneva Bible of 1560, produced by English Reformers in exile during Mary's reign, became the Bible of the Puritans. It was the first English Bible to use verse numbers, and its extensive marginal notes provided a running Reformed commentary on the text. This was the Bible that the Pilgrims brought to America. The King James Version of 1611, authorized by James the First, drew upon all previous English translations, including Tyndale's and the Geneva Bible's, and produced what many consider the most magnificent English prose ever written. The Westminster divines worked with the King James Version before them, and its cadences echo throughout the Confession and Catechisms.

The Westminster Confession's chapter on Scripture, then, was the mature fruit of over a century of Protestant reflection on the nature and authority of the Bible. It drew upon the insights of Luther, Calvin, Tyndale, Cranmer, and Perkins, and it synthesized them into the most comprehensive and carefully worded statement about Scripture that the Reformed tradition has ever produced.

PART NINE: PURITAN DEVOTION TO SCRIPTURE

The Westminster divines did not merely write about Scripture in the abstract. They lived lives saturated with the Word of God. Their devotion to the Bible was legendary, and their example continues to challenge and inspire us.

Consider Thomas Goodwin, one of the Independent divines at the Assembly. Goodwin described his own conversion as being overwhelmed by the glory of Christ as revealed in Scripture. He spent hours each day in private Bible study and meditation, and his voluminous works are filled with exegetical insight drawn from a lifetime of careful biblical study.

Consider also William Gouge, who pastored the congregation at St. Ann Blackfriars for over forty years. Gouge was known for his morning devotions, which began at five o'clock each day with extended Bible reading and prayer. His commentary on the Epistle to the Hebrews, produced after years of preaching through that epistle, runs to over a thousand pages and demonstrates the kind of thorough, patient, Scripture-saturated scholarship that characterized the Westminster divines.

The Puritan Richard Greenham, an earlier generation's pastor whose influence extended to many of the Westminster divines, gave this counsel regarding Bible reading: "Read the Scriptures with diligence. Come to them with a humble heart and a sincere desire to know the truth. Read them not to fill your head with knowledge but to fill your heart with grace. And when you have read, meditate upon what you have read, that the truth may sink down into your soul."

The Psalmist captured this devotion to the Word of God with unforgettable beauty: "O how love I thy law! it is my meditation all the day. Thou through thy commandments hast made me wiser than mine enemies: for they are ever with me. I have more understanding than all my teachers: for thy testimonies are my meditation. I understand more than the ancients, because I keep thy precepts. I have refrained my feet from every evil way, that I might keep thy word. I have not departed from thy judgments: for thou hast taught me. How sweet are thy words unto my taste! yea, sweeter than honey to my mouth!" (Psalm 119:97-103, KJV).

PART NINE: PRACTICAL APPLICATION

These truths about Scripture are not merely academic. They are the foundation of your entire education, your faith, and your life. The Bible you hold in your hands is the inspired, authoritative, sufficient, clear Word of God. Let me draw out several practical applications.

First, read your Bible daily. If the Westminster divines, who were among the most learned men of their age, devoted themselves to daily Bible reading, how much more should we? Establish a regular habit of Bible reading. Read systematically — work through entire books rather than picking verses at random. Let the Word of God dwell in you richly, as the Apostle Paul urged: "Let the word of Christ dwell in you richly in all wisdom" (Colossians 3:16, KJV).

Second, trust your Bible completely. Because the Bible is God's inspired, infallible Word, you can trust it without reservation. When the Bible makes a historical claim, it is accurate. When it makes a moral claim, it is binding. When it makes a promise, it is certain. The Lord Jesus Himself affirmed the total reliability of Scripture: "The scripture cannot be broken" (John 10:35, KJV). And again: "Till heaven and earth pass, one jot or one tittle shall in no wise pass from the law, till all be fulfilled" (Matthew 5:18, KJV).

Third, submit to your Bible humbly. The Bible is not a book to be mastered but a master to be obeyed. When Scripture challenges your assumptions, correct your assumptions. When Scripture confronts your sin, repent. When Scripture calls you to action, obey. As the Apostle James wrote, "But be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving your own selves" (James 1:22, KJV).

Fourth, study your Bible carefully. Use the tools available to you — commentaries, lexicons, concordances, the original languages when possible. Compare Scripture with Scripture. Seek the guidance of faithful teachers and pastors. The Berean Christians were commended because they "received the word with all readiness of mind, and searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so" (Acts 17:11, KJV). Be a Berean.

Fifth, love your Bible deeply. The Psalmist said, "O how love I thy law!" (Psalm 119:97, KJV). The Bible is not a burden to be endured but a treasure to be cherished. It contains the words of eternal life. It reveals the character of the God who made you and the Savior who redeemed you. It is, as the Puritan Thomas Watson described it, "the storehouse of divine treasures, the precious mine of heavenly jewels."

Sixth, defend the Bible courageously. There will come times in your life when the truth and authority of Scripture are challenged — by skeptics, by false teachers, by the spirit of the age. When those challenges come, stand firm. The Apostle Peter exhorted believers to "be ready always to give an answer to every man that asketh you a reason of the hope that is in you with meekness and fear" (1 Peter 3:15, KJV). Know why you believe the Bible is God's Word. Know the evidence for its reliability. But above all, know the Bible itself — its teachings, its promises, its power. The best defense of the Bible is a life transformed by the Bible.

The Puritan pastor John Flavel gave this stirring counsel: "The Scriptures teach us the best way of living, the noblest way of suffering, and the most comfortable way of dying. Reader, if thou art wise, thou wilt study them more and love them better. Set them before thine eyes, bind them upon thy fingers, write them upon the table of thine heart. They are thy life, for in them thou findest the way to the Author of life."

Let me close this section with the words of the prophet Jeremiah, which capture the experience of every believer who has tasted the sweetness of God's Word: "Thy words were found, and I did eat them; and thy word was unto me the joy and rejoicing of mine heart: for I am called by thy name, O LORD God of hosts" (Jeremiah 15:16, KJV).

--- Closing ---

The doctrine of Scripture is the bedrock of the Christian faith. Everything else we will study in this unit rests upon the conviction that the Bible is the trustworthy, inspired, sufficient, clear Word of God. The Puritan Samuel Rutherford, writing from his prison cell in Aberdeen, expressed his love for Scripture in words that have echoed through the centuries: "The Bible is the breathed-out Word of the living God, and there is not a page in it that doth not carry the stamp of divine majesty." Hold fast to this truth. Treasure your Bible. Study it with diligence. Trust it without reservation. And let its truths transform your life from the inside out. Until next time, students.
