PURITAN GOLD -- Study Devotional, Day 10
Series: Romans
"Adam and Christ"
Where sin abounded, grace did much more abound


Good evening. There is no shortcut to spiritual depth. It comes through sustained, careful engagement with God's Word. That is what we are doing together in this study series, and tonight we take the next step.

We are studying the book of Romans together. Tonight's lesson is titled "Adam and Christ." Each session builds on what came before, so if you have been following along, you will see how tonight's passage connects to the larger argument of the book.


Let us read our passage for tonight. It comes from Romans 5:12-21:

"Wherefore, as by one man sin entered into the world, and death by sin; and so death passed upon all men, for that all have sinned... But not as the offence, so also is the free gift. For if through the offence of one many be dead, much more the grace of God, and the gift by grace, which is by one man, Jesus Christ, hath abounded unto many... For as by one man's disobedience many were made sinners, so by the obedience of one shall many be made righteous. Moreover the law entered, that the offence might abound. But where sin abounded, grace did much more abound: That as sin hath reigned unto death, even so might grace reign through righteousness unto eternal life by Jesus Christ our Lord."

Read that passage again to yourself. Mark any words or phrases that stand out. The Puritans read with a pen in hand, always ready to note what God was showing them.


The Puritans often identified a single verse as the hinge of a passage. Tonight, that verse is this:

"But where sin abounded, grace did much more abound." -- Romans 5:20

This verse is the lens through which we will examine the rest of tonight's passage. Keep it in the front of your mind as we proceed.


Let us unfold the meaning of this passage together.

Thomas Goodwin offers valuable commentary on this passage. The Puritans were expositors of the highest order, and their insights remain remarkably fresh.

Thomas Goodwin considered Romans 5:12-21 to be one of the most profound passages in all of divine revelation, and he devoted extensive treatment to it in his work on Christ as Mediator. Goodwin argued that Paul here reveals the deepest structure of God's redemptive plan — the principle of federal headship, whereby one man represents many. Adam was not merely the first man but the covenant head of the entire human race. When he sinned, he sinned not merely for himself but for all whom he represented. Likewise, Christ is the covenant head of the new humanity. When He obeyed, He obeyed not merely for Himself but for all whom He represented.

Goodwin found in this passage the most powerful demonstration of the surpassing nature of grace. He cataloged the ways in which the free gift exceeds the offense: Adam's sin brought condemnation, but Christ's obedience brings justification. Adam's one sin brought death, but Christ's righteousness overcomes many offenses. Death reigned through Adam, but those who receive the gift reign in life through Christ. Sin reigned unto death, but grace reigns through righteousness unto eternal life. At every point, grace overflows, superabounds, and overwhelms the damage done by sin.

Goodwin was particularly moved by the phrase 'much more' (Greek: pollo mallon), which appears five times in this section. He argued that these two words contain the whole comfort of the Christian religion. Whatever sin has done, grace has done much more. Whatever death has destroyed, life has restored much more. Whatever the fall has ruined, redemption has rebuilt much more. The scales are not even — they are tipped overwhelmingly in favor of grace. Goodwin saw in this not only comfort for troubled souls but a vision of the cosmic scope of redemption: Christ has not merely reversed the fall but has elevated redeemed humanity to heights that unfallen Adam never knew.

Romans 5:12-21 is one of the most theologically dense passages in the entire Bible. It establishes the doctrine of federal headship — the principle that one person can act as the representative of many — and it uses this principle to explain both how humanity fell in Adam and how believers are saved in Christ.

Verse 12 introduces the comparison: 'Wherefore, as by one man sin entered into the world, and death by sin; and so death passed upon all men, for that all have sinned.' Paul traces the origin of sin and death to one man — Adam. Sin did not evolve gradually; it entered through a single act of disobedience in the Garden of Eden (Genesis 3). And death came 'by sin' — it is not natural, not part of God's original design, but the penalty for transgression. 'The wages of sin is death' (Romans 6:23). And death 'passed upon all men, for that all have sinned.' The universal reign of death proves the universal reality of sin.

The phrase 'for that all have sinned' has been interpreted in two primary ways. Some take it to mean that all humans are counted as having sinned in Adam because he was their representative head. Others take it to mean that all humans have personally sinned and therefore incur death. Both are true, but the context favors the first reading. Paul's whole argument depends on the parallel between Adam and Christ: just as we sinned in Adam (by representation), so we are made righteous in Christ (by representation). If 'all have sinned' means only personal sin, the parallel with Christ's imputed righteousness breaks down.

Verses 13-14 confirm this reading: 'For until the law sin was in the world: but sin is not imputed where there is no law. Nevertheless death reigned from Adam to Moses, even over them that had not sinned after the similitude of Adam's transgression.' Between Adam and Moses, there was no written law. Yet people still died — including infants who had never personally transgressed. Why? Because they were constituted sinners in Adam. Death reigned not because each individual earned it through personal lawbreaking but because Adam's sin was imputed to the entire race.

Adam is described as 'the figure of him that was to come' — a type of Christ. Just as Adam was the representative head of the old humanity, Christ is the representative head of the new humanity. This typological parallel is the backbone of the entire passage.

But verses 15-17 emphasize that the parallel is not exact — the free gift far exceeds the offense. 'But not as the offence, so also is the free gift.' The differences all favor grace. 'For if through the offence of one many be dead, much more the grace of God, and the gift by grace, which is by one man, Jesus Christ, hath abounded unto many.' If one sin by one man could plunge the whole race into death, how much more can the grace of one Man — the God-Man Jesus Christ — bring life and righteousness?

Verse 16 adds another dimension: 'And not as it was by one that sinned, so is the gift: for the judgment was by one to condemnation, but the free gift is of many offences unto justification.' Adam's single sin brought condemnation. But Christ's single act of righteousness dealt with not just one sin but 'many offences' — the accumulated sins of all His people across all of history. The scope of grace exceeds the scope of sin by an immeasurable margin.

Verse 17 reaches a crescendo: 'For if by one man's offence death reigned by one; much more they which receive abundance of grace and of the gift of righteousness shall reign in life by one, Jesus Christ.' In Adam, death reigns over us. In Christ, we reign over death. The shift is from passive subjugation to active dominion. We are not merely rescued from death's kingdom; we are enthroned in life's kingdom. We reign — present and future — through Christ.

Verses 18-19 present the comparison in its most concentrated form. 'Therefore as by the offence of one judgment came upon all men to condemnation; even so by the righteousness of one the free gift came upon all men unto justification of life.' One man's offense — condemnation for all. One man's righteousness — justification for all who are in Him. 'For as by one man's disobedience many were made sinners, so by the obedience of one shall many be made righteous.' Adam's disobedience constituted us sinners; Christ's obedience constitutes us righteous. The verb 'made' (kathistemi) means to appoint, to constitute, to place in a category. We were placed in the category of sinners through Adam; we are placed in the category of the righteous through Christ.

Verse 20 addresses the role of the law: 'Moreover the law entered, that the offence might abound.' The law was not the solution to sin; it was the spotlight on sin. By revealing God's holy standard, the law made sin more visible and more culpable. 'But where sin abounded, grace did much more abound.' The Greek huperperisseuo means to super-abound, to overflow beyond all measure. However great the flood of sin, the flood of grace is immeasurably greater. This is not a license to sin (Paul will address that in chapter 6) but a declaration of the inexhaustible sufficiency of divine grace.

Verse 21 brings the section to its magnificent conclusion: 'That as sin hath reigned unto death, even so might grace reign through righteousness unto eternal life by Jesus Christ our Lord.' Two kingdoms, two reigns, two destinies. In Adam, sin reigns through death. In Christ, grace reigns through righteousness unto eternal life. Notice that grace reigns 'through righteousness' — not by ignoring sin but by satisfying justice through the cross. Grace is not lawless; it is the triumph of righteousness over sin through the substitutionary death of Christ.

This passage establishes one of the most comforting truths in all of theology: whatever damage Adam's fall inflicted, Christ's redemption has more than repaired. We who are in Christ are not merely restored to Adam's pre-fall condition; we are elevated far above it. Adam was innocent but fallible; we are justified and will be glorified. Adam walked with God in a garden; we will behold God's face in the new creation. Where sin abounded, grace did much more abound.


Study without application is like a seed without soil. Let us now plant what we have learned in the ground of daily life.

The Adam-Christ parallel in Romans 5:12-21 carries profound implications for daily life.

First, understand your identity in Christ. You are either in Adam or in Christ — there is no third category. In Adam, you are condemned, enslaved to sin, and destined for death. In Christ, you are justified, freed from sin's dominion, and destined for glory. If you have placed your faith in Christ, your identity is defined by His obedience, not Adam's disobedience. When you fail, you do not revert to Adam. You remain in Christ. Let this truth stabilize your sense of self.

Second, let grace overwhelm your shame. 'Where sin abounded, grace did much more abound.' Whatever sins haunt your past, whatever failures burden your present, grace is greater. This is not an excuse to be casual about sin, but it is a reason to refuse despair. The flood of grace is deeper and wider than the flood of sin. Bring your worst memories to the cross and let grace super-abound over them.

Third, reign in life. Verse 17 says those who receive grace shall 'reign in life' through Christ. You are not a victim of your circumstances, your past, or your fallen nature. You are royalty — a child of the King, destined to reign. Live with the dignity and authority of someone who is reigning in life, not cowering under death. Make decisions today not from a posture of defeat but from a posture of victory.

Fourth, worship the God of 'much more.' The repeated refrain of this passage is 'much more' — grace exceeds judgment at every point. Let this produce overwhelming gratitude. Christ has not merely fixed what was broken; He has given us more than Adam ever had. Spend time this week praising God specifically for the ways His grace has super-abounded in your life.

Write down one concrete action you will take this week in response to tonight's study. Do not let this truth remain theoretical. The Puritans measured the success of a sermon not by how much the hearer learned but by how much the hearer changed.


Sovereign God, we marvel at the depth of Your redemptive plan. In Adam we fell, but in Christ we rise to heights Adam never knew. Where our sin abounded, Your grace has much more abounded. Thank You that Christ's obedience is greater than Adam's disobedience, that the free gift overwhelms the offense, that grace reigns through righteousness unto eternal life. Teach us to live as those who reign in life through Jesus Christ our Lord. Free us from shame and fill us with the joy of Your super-abounding grace. Amen.

Continue to meditate on tonight's passage. The more you return to it, the more it will reveal.
