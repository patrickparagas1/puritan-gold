Good morning, students. Welcome to the Puritan Academy. Let us begin this day as the Puritans did, by acknowledging God as the source of all knowledge and wisdom. Before we open our textbooks, let us open our hearts.

Almighty God, the fountain of all wisdom, we ask You to bless our studies today. Open our minds to understand, our hearts to receive, and our wills to obey the truth. Grant us diligence, humility, and a spirit of inquiry that honors You. Through Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.

Let us meditate on a portion of Scripture before we begin our main lesson.

Our Scripture for today is Romans 3:10-18; Ephesians 2:1-3; Jeremiah 17:9; Genesis 6:5:

"As it is written, There is none righteous, no, not one: There is none that understandeth, there is none that seeketh after God. They are all gone out of the way, they are together become unprofitable; there is none that doeth good, no, not one. Their throat is an open sepulchre; with their tongues they have used deceit; the poison of asps is under their lips: Whose mouth is full of cursing and bitterness: Their feet are swift to shed blood: Destruction and misery are in their ways: And the way of peace have they not known: There is no fear of God before their eyes. And you hath he quickened, who were dead in trespasses and sins; Wherein in time past ye walked according to the course of this world, according to the prince of the power of the air, the spirit that now worketh in the children of disobedience: Among whom also we all had our conversation in times past in the lusts of our flesh, fulfilling the desires of the flesh and of the mind; and were by nature the children of wrath, even as others. The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately wicked: who can know it? And GOD saw that the wickedness of man was great in the earth, and that every imagination of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually."

Consider today the depths from which God has rescued you. Apart from His grace, your mind would remain blind to truth, your will enslaved to sin, your affections disordered, and your whole being under the sentence of divine wrath. You did not choose God — He chose you. You did not raise yourself from death — He quickened you. Let this truth humble you to the dust and then lift your eyes to the cross, where infinite love met infinite holiness to redeem the utterly helpless. You are not saved because you are better than others, but because God is gracious beyond all comprehension. Let gratitude be the song of your heart today.

As we prepare for today's lesson, remember that everything we study flows from this one fountain: the Word of God. Our lesson today is part of The Doctrines of Grace — Westminster Standards, and it is titled: Total Depravity — Our Great Need. Let us proceed with minds engaged and hearts open.


Now let us turn to our main lesson for today.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes in Romans 3:10-18; Ephesians 2:1-3; Jeremiah 17:9; Genesis 6:5.

The doctrine of total depravity is not the claim that every human being is as wicked as they could possibly be, but rather the biblical teaching that every part of human nature — mind, will, affections, body — has been corrupted by the fall of Adam, so that no person in their natural state is able to please God or even to seek Him. This doctrine is the necessary starting point for understanding the gospel, for if we do not understand how desperate our condition is, we will never appreciate how glorious our salvation is.

The apostle Paul, writing to the church at Rome, compiles a devastating catalogue of human sinfulness drawn from the Old Testament Psalms and Prophets. In Romans 3:10-12 he declares, "There is none righteous, no, not one: There is none that understandeth, there is none that seeketh after God. They are all gone out of the way, they are together become unprofitable; there is none that doeth good, no, not one." Notice the absolute, universal language: "none," "not one," "all," "none." Paul is not speaking of a particular class of especially wicked people. He is describing the entire human race in its natural, fallen condition. This is the verdict of God Himself upon the children of Adam.

The passage continues with a shocking anatomy of sin. The throat is "an open sepulchre" — a gaping grave that breathes out the stench of death. The tongues "have used deceit" — language, that uniquely human gift, has been turned into an instrument of falsehood. "The poison of asps is under their lips" — speech that was designed to praise God and bless others has become venomous and deadly. The mouth is "full of cursing and bitterness." The feet are "swift to shed blood." "Destruction and misery are in their ways." And the summary verdict: "There is no fear of God before their eyes."

This last phrase is perhaps the most revealing. The root of all human sin is the absence of the fear of God. When men and women do not revere God, do not tremble before His holiness, do not regard His commandments as binding — then every form of wickedness follows naturally. The fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom (Proverbs 9:10), and where that beginning is absent, only folly and destruction can follow.

Paul's indictment in Romans 3 is not an isolated text. It echoes and confirms the testimony of the entire Old Testament. In Genesis 6:5, before the great flood, God surveyed the earth He had made and saw "that the wickedness of man was great in the earth, and that every imagination of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually." Consider the totality of that statement. Not merely the actions but the imaginations. Not merely some thoughts but every thought. Not merely occasionally but continually. This is the divine diagnosis of the human heart apart from grace.

The prophet Jeremiah confirms this with one of the most penetrating statements in all of Scripture: "The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately wicked: who can know it?" (Jeremiah 17:9). The word translated "desperately wicked" in Hebrew is 'anush,' which carries the meaning of incurable, terminally ill, beyond remedy by any human means. Jeremiah is saying that the human heart is not merely sick — it is dying, and no human physician can heal it. Only God can perform the surgery that is needed. This is why the new birth is not a cooperative effort between God and man but a sovereign act of divine power.

In Ephesians 2:1-3, Paul describes the pre-conversion state of every believer with devastating clarity: "And you hath he quickened, who were dead in trespasses and sins." The key word is "dead." Not sick, not weak, not struggling — dead. A dead man cannot help himself. A dead man cannot reach up for a lifeline. A dead man cannot decide to come back to life. He is utterly dependent on someone else to raise him. This is the condition of every person apart from the regenerating work of the Holy Spirit.

Paul further describes the three great enemies that held us captive: the world ("ye walked according to the course of this world"), the devil ("the prince of the power of the air, the spirit that now worketh in the children of disobedience"), and the flesh ("we all had our conversation in times past in the lusts of our flesh, fulfilling the desires of the flesh and of the mind"). Every unconverted person is under the dominion of all three. They follow the patterns of a fallen world, they are influenced by the god of this age, and they are enslaved to their own sinful desires.

The crowning phrase is "and were by nature the children of wrath, even as others." This is not wrath earned by particularly bad behavior but wrath that belongs to us by nature — by virtue of being children of Adam. We are not sinners because we sin; we sin because we are sinners. The corruption is not merely behavioral but constitutional. It runs to the very root of what we are.

The Westminster Confession of Faith, Chapter 6, Section 2 states it clearly: "By this sin they fell from their original righteousness and communion with God, and so became dead in sin, and wholly defiled in all the parts and faculties of soul and body." And Section 4 adds: "From this original corruption, whereby we are utterly indisposed, disabled, and made opposite to all good, and wholly inclined to all evil, do proceed all actual transgressions." This is total depravity rightly understood — not that people are as bad as they could be, but that they are as bad off as they could be, unable to change their own nature or merit God's favor by any effort of their own.

This doctrine, rightly grasped, accomplishes two vital things in the soul. First, it destroys all pride and self-righteousness. If we are truly dead in sin, then we have nothing to boast of. Salvation cannot be a reward for our goodness because we have no goodness to offer. Second, it magnifies the grace of God to an infinite degree. If God saves those who are dead, hostile, enslaved, and under wrath — then salvation is entirely of grace, entirely of mercy, entirely the work of a sovereign and compassionate God. The darker we understand our condition to be, the brighter the light of the gospel shines.

This biblical foundation is essential. Without it, everything else we study today would be like a house built on sand. But with it, we stand on solid rock.


Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. Thomas Boston wrote extensively on this subject in "Human Nature in its Fourfold State." The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life.

Thomas Boston (1676-1732) was a Scottish pastor and theologian whose work "Human Nature in its Fourfold State" became one of the most widely read books in Scotland for generations. In this masterful work, Boston traces the history of human nature through four stages: the state of innocence (creation), the state of nature (the fall), the state of grace (redemption), and the state of glory (heaven). It is in his treatment of the second state — the state of nature, or fallen humanity — that Boston provides one of the most thorough and compelling expositions of total depravity in all of Puritan literature.

Boston begins by examining the understanding or mind of fallen man. He writes that the natural mind is shrouded in darkness concerning spiritual things. Citing 1 Corinthians 2:14, "The natural man receiveth not the things of the Spirit of God: for they are foolishness unto him: neither can he know them, because they are spiritually discerned," Boston argues that the fall did not merely weaken man's intellectual capacity for spiritual truth but fundamentally disabled it. A man may be brilliant in worldly affairs — in science, commerce, philosophy — and yet be utterly blind to the realities of God, sin, and salvation. This is not an intellectual problem that can be solved by better arguments or more persuasive preaching alone. It is a spiritual blindness that only the Holy Spirit can cure.

Boston then turns to the will, which he describes as being in complete bondage to sin. The Confession of Faith teaches that man, by his fall, "hath wholly lost all ability of will to any spiritual good accompanying salvation" (WCF 9.3). Boston illustrates this by observing that the will always follows the last dictate of the understanding. Since the understanding is darkened and cannot perceive spiritual good as truly good, the will cannot choose it. The unconverted person does not fail to choose God because of some external constraint — they fail because they do not want God. Their will is free in the sense that they do what they want, but what they want is always sin. As our Lord Himself declared, "Ye will not come to me, that ye might have life" (John 5:40).

Boston addresses the affections with equal penetration. He writes that in fallen man, the affections are completely disordered. What should be loved is hated, and what should be hated is loved. The natural man loves sin and hates holiness. He loves the world and hates the things of God. He loves self and is indifferent to the glory of God. Boston observes that this disorder of affection is not merely a bad habit that can be corrected with discipline — it is a disease of the soul that only divine grace can heal. He compares the fallen heart to a garden overrun with weeds: you may cut the weeds down, but they will grow back because the roots are still there. Only a new nature can produce new affections.

One of Boston's most powerful contributions is his treatment of the conscience in fallen man. He argues that even the conscience — that faculty which is supposed to be the voice of God in the soul — has been corrupted by the fall. The conscience of the natural man may accuse him of some sins, but it does not accuse him of all sins. It may trouble him about outward transgressions while leaving him perfectly comfortable with inward sins like pride, covetousness, and unbelief. Boston writes that the fallen conscience is like a clock that has been damaged — it still ticks, but it no longer tells the right time. It gives a semblance of moral awareness without true spiritual discernment.

Boston is also careful to show that total depravity extends to the body as well as the soul. The members of the body, he writes, have become "instruments of unrighteousness" (Romans 6:13). The eyes that were made to behold the beauty of God's creation are used for lust. The tongue that was made to praise God is used for gossip, slander, and blasphemy. The hands that were made for honest labor are used for violence and theft. Every part of the human constitution has been enlisted in the service of sin.

The Westminster Confession of Faith, Chapter 6, provides the doctrinal framework that Boston expounds. It teaches that our first parents, "being seduced by the subtlety and temptation of Satan, sinned in eating the forbidden fruit" and that "by this sin they fell from their original righteousness and communion with God, and so became dead in sin, and wholly defiled in all the parts and faculties of soul and body." The Confession further teaches that this corruption is conveyed to all their posterity "by ordinary generation," so that "we are utterly indisposed, disabled, and made opposite to all good, and wholly inclined to all evil."

Boston's pastoral genius lies in his ability to press this doctrine upon the conscience without driving souls to despair. He always points from the diagnosis to the cure. Having painted the darkest possible picture of human depravity, he then holds up the gospel of Jesus Christ as the only remedy. He writes that the same God who pronounces the sentence of death upon the natural man is the God who raises the dead. The same Christ who said "without me ye can do nothing" (John 15:5) also said "Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest" (Matthew 11:28).

Boston reminds his readers that the doctrine of total depravity is not meant to paralyze us but to humble us, and humility is the soil in which grace grows. When we truly understand that we are dead in sin, we stop trying to save ourselves and cast ourselves wholly upon Christ. When we truly understand that our will is in bondage, we cry out for the Holy Spirit to free us. When we truly understand that our affections are disordered, we plead for a new heart.

The practical application of this doctrine for the Christian life is immense. It teaches us to depend entirely on grace for every spiritual act — for faith, for repentance, for perseverance, for growth in holiness. It teaches us to be humble, knowing that whatever good is in us is there only because God put it there. It teaches us to be compassionate toward unbelievers, knowing that but for the grace of God, we would be in the same condition. And it teaches us to give all the glory for our salvation to God alone, for we contributed nothing to it but the sin from which we needed to be saved.

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.


Let us now press deeper into the theological implications of total depravity, examining additional Scripture passages and drawing from further Puritan sources to enrich our understanding of this foundational doctrine.

The prophet Isaiah provides one of the most vivid portrayals of the universality of human corruption. In Isaiah 64:6, the prophet declares on behalf of the entire nation: "But we are all as an unclean thing, and all our righteousnesses are as filthy rags; and we all do fade as a leaf; and our iniquities, like the wind, have taken us away." Notice that Isaiah does not merely say our sins are like filthy rags — he says our righteousnesses are. The very best that fallen humanity can produce — its finest moral achievements, its noblest deeds, its most impressive religious performances — are, in the sight of a perfectly holy God, no better than filthy, polluted garments. If even our righteousness is corrupt, how much more our unrighteousness! This text demolishes every attempt to stand before God on the basis of human merit.

The apostle Paul reinforces this truth in Romans 8:7-8: "Because the carnal mind is enmity against God: for it is not subject to the law of God, neither indeed can be. So then they that are in the flesh cannot please God." The carnal mind — the mind of the unregenerate person — is not merely indifferent to God but actively hostile toward Him. It is enmity against God. And Paul adds the devastating words "neither indeed can be." The natural mind is not merely unwilling to submit to God's law — it is unable. This is not a deficiency that education, culture, or moral effort can remedy. It is a constitutional incapacity that only the regenerating work of the Holy Spirit can overcome.

Consider also the testimony of Psalm 51, David's great psalm of repentance after his sin with Bathsheba. In verse 5, David traces his sinfulness back to its very root: "Behold, I was shapen in iniquity; and in sin did my mother conceive me." David is not accusing his mother of immorality. He is confessing that sin is woven into the very fabric of his nature from the moment of his conception. He did not become a sinner at some point during his life — he was a sinner from the very beginning. This is the doctrine of original sin, the teaching that the guilt and corruption of Adam's first transgression are transmitted to all his descendants by natural generation.

The Puritan divine Stephen Charnock (1628-1680), in his monumental work "The Existence and Attributes of God," provides further insight into the effects of total depravity upon the human understanding of God Himself. Charnock argues that fallen man does not merely fail to know God — he actively constructs false images of God that are more congenial to his sinful nature. The natural man fashions a god who is tolerant of sin, indifferent to holiness, and unthreatening to the sinner's autonomy. Charnock writes that fallen man "doth not desire to know God as He is, but frames a god agreeable to his own humor." This idolatry of the mind is the deepest form of depravity — the corruption not merely of our morals but of our theology. We do not merely behave badly; we think wrongly about the most important subject in the universe.

Charnock further observes that total depravity manifests itself in the way fallen man treats the very means God has provided for his recovery. The preaching of the gospel, which should be received with joy and gratitude, is despised as foolishness (1 Corinthians 1:18). The offer of free grace, which should humble the sinner and draw him to repentance, is either presumed upon or resented. The commandments of God, which are designed for our good and His glory, are treated as burdensome restrictions on our liberty. The fallen heart turns every gift of God into an occasion for further sin. This is the depth of our corruption — we cannot even receive the remedy without corrupting it.

Another Puritan who illuminated the doctrine of total depravity with remarkable clarity was Jeremiah Burroughs (1599-1646), known for his irenic spirit and pastoral wisdom. In his work "The Evil of Evils," Burroughs argues that sin is the greatest evil in the universe — worse than suffering, worse than death, worse than hell itself — because sin is the only thing that is directly contrary to the nature of God. Burroughs reasons that suffering and affliction may serve God's purposes and even bring glory to Him, but sin is never God's purpose and always dishonors Him. Total depravity means that this greatest of all evils — sin — pervades every dimension of human existence. Every thought we think, every word we speak, every deed we perform is tainted by it.

Burroughs also draws attention to the subtlety of depravity. It is not always manifest in gross outward sins. Sometimes it hides behind a mask of respectability, morality, and even religious devotion. The Pharisees were the most morally upright people of their day, yet Jesus reserved His most severe condemnations for them. Their outward righteousness concealed an inward corruption of pride, self-righteousness, and lovelessness that was, in God's sight, more offensive than the open sins of tax collectors and prostitutes. Burroughs warns that total depravity includes what he calls "refined wickedness" — the ability of the human heart to turn even virtue into an instrument of sin by making it a ground of self-trust rather than a response of gratitude to God.

The Westminster Larger Catechism, Question 25, asks: "Wherein consisteth the sinfulness of that estate whereinto man fell?" The answer is comprehensive: "The sinfulness of that estate whereinto man fell, consisteth in the guilt of Adam's first sin, the want of that righteousness wherein he was created, and the corruption of his nature, whereby he is utterly indisposed, disabled, and made opposite to all that is spiritually good, and wholly inclined to all evil, and that continually." Three elements compose our sinfulness: guilt (we bear the legal liability of Adam's transgression), want (we lack the original righteousness with which Adam was created), and corruption (our nature is actively inclined toward evil). All three must be addressed for salvation to be complete, and all three are addressed by Christ — His righteousness covers our guilt, His imputed holiness supplies our want, and His Spirit overcomes our corruption.

Let us also consider the practical evidence of total depravity that confronts us daily. Every newspaper, every history book, every honest examination of our own hearts confirms the biblical diagnosis. Wars, murders, thefts, lies, betrayals, addictions, exploitation — the catalogue of human wickedness is endless and universal. No culture, no era, no social class is exempt. The twentieth century, with all its advances in education, science, and technology, produced the Holocaust, the Gulag, two world wars, and genocides on every continent. Education does not cure depravity. Technology does not cure depravity. Political systems do not cure depravity. Only the grace of God, applied by the Holy Spirit through the gospel of Jesus Christ, can transform the human heart.

The Puritan pastor Richard Sibbes (1577-1635), known as "the heavenly Doctor Sibbes" for the sweetness of his preaching, brought a tender pastoral touch to the doctrine of total depravity. In his beloved work "The Bruised Reed," Sibbes shows how the awareness of our own sinfulness, far from being a barrier to Christ, is actually the very thing that qualifies us to receive Him. Christ came not to call the righteous but sinners to repentance (Matthew 9:13). The Great Physician seeks the sick, not the well. Sibbes writes that Christ "will not break the bruised reed, nor quench the smoking flax" (Isaiah 42:3) — meaning that even the faintest spark of spiritual life, even the weakest desire for God, will not be despised or extinguished by the Savior. The soul that is most deeply convinced of its depravity is the soul that is most ready to receive the grace of Christ.

Sibbes further teaches that the doctrine of total depravity, when rightly understood, produces not despair but a peculiar kind of hope. For if we are utterly helpless in ourselves, then our only hope is in God — and God is an infinitely sufficient hope. The sinner who has abandoned all confidence in his own ability to save himself is precisely the sinner who is ready to cast himself wholly upon the mercy of Christ. Self-despair, Sibbes argues, is the doorway to divine grace. The emptier we are of self, the fuller we can be of Christ. Total depravity does not leave us without hope — it directs our hope away from ourselves and toward the only One who can help us.

This deeper analysis reveals that total depravity is not a doctrine designed to crush the human spirit but to redirect it. It strips away every false foundation — every reliance on human goodness, human effort, human wisdom — and points the soul to the only true foundation: the grace of God in Jesus Christ. The deeper we understand our need, the more glorious the gospel becomes. The blacker the night, the brighter the stars. And the stars of God's grace — election, redemption, regeneration, justification, sanctification, glorification — shine with a brilliance that can only be appreciated against the backdrop of our total depravity.


Before we move to our discussion questions, let us consider the practical application of today's doctrine. The Puritans never studied a truth without asking: how does this change the way I live?

First, the doctrine of total depravity should produce deep humility in the believer. If everything good in us is there only because God put it there, then we have absolutely nothing to boast of. Our faith is a gift (Ephesians 2:8). Our repentance is a gift (Acts 11:18). Our ability to do any good work is a gift (Philippians 2:13). The Christian who truly grasps total depravity will never look down on an unbeliever with contempt, because he knows that apart from the grace of God, he would be in the same condition — or worse.

Second, this doctrine should produce fervent and dependent prayer. If we cannot think a right thought, will a right action, or feel a right affection apart from the enabling grace of God, then prayer is not optional — it is essential. Every morning, we should come before God confessing our utter dependence upon Him for everything: for the ability to understand His Word, for the desire to obey His commandments, for the strength to resist temptation, for the love to serve others. The Puritan practice of beginning every day with extended prayer was rooted in their conviction of total depravity — they knew that without God's continual aid, they could do nothing.

Third, this doctrine should transform our approach to evangelism and missions. If the unconverted are truly dead in sin, then clever arguments and persuasive techniques alone will never convert a single soul. Only the power of God can raise the dead. This means that our evangelism must be saturated with prayer, dependent upon the Holy Spirit, and faithful to the gospel of grace. We do not save anyone — God saves through the means of the preached Word, empowered by the Spirit. Our job is to be faithful messengers; the results belong entirely to God.

Fourth, total depravity should make us vigilant against remaining sin. Even as believers, we are not yet fully sanctified. The old nature, though dethroned, is not yet destroyed. The Puritans spoke of "indwelling sin" — the remnants of the old corrupt nature that continues to war against the new nature implanted by the Holy Spirit. John Owen wrote an entire treatise on "The Mortification of Sin," arguing that believers must engage in a lifelong battle against the remaining corruption within them. Complacency about sin is never appropriate for the Christian. We must, by the power of the Spirit, be continually putting to death the deeds of the flesh (Romans 8:13).

Fifth, and most importantly, this doctrine should drive us to Christ again and again. The gospel is not merely the door through which we enter the Christian life — it is the path on which we walk every day. Every day we need to be reminded that our standing before God rests not on our performance but on the perfect righteousness of Christ. Every day we need the assurance that our sins — past, present, and future — have been fully atoned for at the cross. Every day we need the Holy Spirit to apply the benefits of Christ's death and resurrection to our hearts. The doctrine of total depravity ensures that we never graduate from the gospel but remain perpetual students of grace, forever marveling at the love that rescued us from such depths.


Now let us pause and consider what we have learned. Discussion is where understanding deepens.

Discussion Question 1: What does it mean that total depravity affects every part of human nature? How is this different from saying that every person is as evil as possible?

Discussion Question 2: Paul says that apart from Christ we are 'dead in trespasses and sins.' What are the implications of being spiritually dead rather than merely spiritually sick?

Discussion Question 3: Thomas Boston describes the will of fallen man as being in bondage to sin. How does this challenge the common idea that people have 'free will' to choose God on their own?

Discussion Question 4: How should the doctrine of total depravity affect the way we evangelize? If people cannot come to God on their own, what role does human preaching play?

Discussion Question 5: Boston says this doctrine should humble us, not paralyze us. How does understanding our total inability actually lead us to greater hope rather than to despair?

Take time with each question. The Puritans valued what they called "holy conference," the practice of discussing spiritual truths together. Do not settle for quick, surface answers. Push deeper. Ask follow-up questions. Listen carefully to one another.

Application is where truth becomes life. It is not enough to know what Romans 3:10-18; Ephesians 2:1-3; Jeremiah 17:9; Genesis 6:5 says. We must ask: how does this change the way I live today? How does this shape my thoughts, my words, my actions? The Puritans insisted that a truth that does not transform is a truth that has not been truly understood.


Now we move to our afternoon activity, where we put truth into practice.

Students will create a 'Fourfold State' diagram based on Thomas Boston's framework. On a large sheet of poster paper divided into four quadrants, students will illustrate the four states of human nature: (1) Innocence — Adam and Eve in the Garden, with the faculties of mind, will, and affections all properly ordered toward God; (2) Nature — the fall and its effects, with arrows showing how each faculty has been corrupted; (3) Grace — the work of regeneration, with Scripture verses showing how God restores each corrupted faculty; (4) Glory — the final state, where every faculty is perfected. For each quadrant, students should include at least two Scripture references and one quote from Boston or the Westminster Confession. The visual should clearly show the contrast between the devastation of the fall and the restoration of grace.

Materials needed: Large poster paper or butcher paper, colored markers, rulers for dividing quadrants, printed copies of Westminster Confession Chapter 6, index cards for Scripture references

For advanced students, add a fifth section at the bottom of the poster that traces the specific Puritan authors studied today — Boston, Charnock, Burroughs, and Sibbes — and includes a brief quotation from each one alongside the state of human nature they most illuminated. This exercise will help you see how different Puritan pastors brought different emphases and pastoral concerns to the same foundational doctrine. Boston excelled in systematic analysis of the faculties. Charnock pierced to the heart of our idolatrous thinking about God. Burroughs revealed the exceeding sinfulness of sin itself. And Sibbes showed how the awareness of our corruption prepares us to receive the tenderness of Christ. Together, they present a full-orbed portrait of our fallen condition and the glory of God's redeeming grace.

Students should also prepare to present their completed diagrams to the class, explaining how each quadrant connects to the specific Scripture passages and Puritan quotations they have chosen. Presentations should be approximately three to five minutes in length. After all presentations, the class should discuss which aspects of Boston's fourfold framework were most striking, most convicting, and most encouraging. The goal is not merely to display artistic skill but to demonstrate theological understanding and the ability to communicate complex doctrines visually and verbally.

As you complete this activity, think about how it connects to what you studied in today's lesson. The goal is not just to do something, but to reinforce the truths you have been learning. The Puritans believed that education should engage the whole person: mind, heart, and hands.

Take your time with this activity. Quality matters more than speed. When you are finished, reflect on what you learned through the process of doing. Often we understand something far better after we have tried to put it into practice.


One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

Thomas Boston served as pastor in the remote Scottish parish of Ettrick from 1707 until his death in 1732. His discovery of an old copy of Edward Fisher's 'The Marrow of Modern Divinity' sparked the 'Marrow Controversy' in the Church of Scotland, a pivotal debate about the relationship between law and grace that shaped Scottish Presbyterianism for generations. Study the social and political context of early 18th-century Scotland and how theological controversies intersected with national identity.

Literature Connection:

Boston's writing style represents a distinctive genre of Puritan prose that combines systematic theology with vivid illustration and pastoral warmth. Compare his use of extended metaphor and analogy with other theological writers. Notice how he structures his arguments — moving from biblical exegesis to doctrinal formulation to practical application. Analyze how his four-fold framework serves as both a literary and theological organizing principle that makes complex doctrine accessible.

Science Connection:

The concept of inherited characteristics has parallels in genetics and biology. Just as physical traits are passed from parents to children through DNA, the Bible teaches that a corrupted spiritual nature is passed from Adam to all his descendants. Explore the scientific concepts of genetic inheritance, dominant and recessive traits, and how certain conditions are passed down through generations. While the theological and biological mechanisms are different, both illustrate the principle that what we inherit shapes what we are.

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.


We have reached the final segment of today's lesson. The Puritans placed great value on review and memorization. They knew that what is not reviewed is soon forgotten. Let us take time now to consolidate what we have learned.

Review Questions:

Review Question 1: Define total depravity in your own words. What does 'total' mean in this context?

Review Question 2: According to Romans 3:10-18, what are the specific effects of sin on human speech, actions, and attitudes?

Review Question 3: What does Paul mean in Ephesians 2:1 when he says we were 'dead in trespasses and sins'?

Review Question 4: How does the Westminster Confession Chapter 6 describe the effects of Adam's fall on all his posterity?

Review Question 5: According to Thomas Boston, why can the fallen will not choose spiritual good?

Review Question 6: How does Isaiah 64:6 deepen our understanding of total depravity beyond merely sinful actions to include our very best efforts?

Review Question 7: Stephen Charnock argued that fallen man constructs false images of God. How does total depravity corrupt not only our behavior but our theology — our very thinking about who God is?

Review Question 8: Jeremiah Burroughs called sin "the evil of evils." Why did he consider sin worse than suffering, affliction, or even death itself?

Review Question 9: Richard Sibbes taught that awareness of our depravity is actually the qualification for receiving grace. How does self-despair become the doorway to divine hope?

Review Question 10: Explain the three elements of sinfulness identified in the Westminster Larger Catechism Question 25: guilt, want, and corruption. How does Christ address each one?

Write out your answers to these questions. Use complete sentences and support your answers with what you learned in today's lesson. If you are unsure of an answer, go back and review that section of the lesson.

Memory Verse:

"The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately wicked: who can know it?" -- Jeremiah 17:9

Read this verse aloud five times. Then close your eyes and try to recite it from memory. Write it out by hand on a card or in your journal. The Puritans memorized vast portions of Scripture, and it was one of the great sources of their spiritual strength. Even one verse, truly hidden in the heart, can sustain you in times of trial.

Additional Memory Verse:

"But we are all as an unclean thing, and all our righteousnesses are as filthy rags; and we all do fade as a leaf; and our iniquities, like the wind, have taken us away." -- Isaiah 64:6

This second verse reinforces the lesson of total depravity from a different angle. While Jeremiah 17:9 speaks of the deceitfulness and wickedness of the heart, Isaiah 64:6 speaks of the corruption of our very best efforts. Together, these two verses paint a complete picture: our hearts are wicked, and even our attempts at righteousness are polluted. Memorize both verses this week and meditate on how they work together to drive you away from self-reliance and toward utter dependence upon the grace of God in Jesus Christ. As you go about your daily activities — your studies, your chores, your interactions with family and friends — let these verses remind you that everything good you do is not the product of your own virtue but the fruit of God's grace working in you and through you.

That concludes today's lesson at the Puritan Academy. You have studied well. May the Lord seal what you have learned in your heart and bring it to mind when you need it most. Rest well tonight, and return tomorrow ready to learn more.
