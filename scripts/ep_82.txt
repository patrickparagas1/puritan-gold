PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"Out of the Depths"
May 21, 2026

---

"Out of the depths have I cried unto thee, O Lord. Lord, hear my voice: let thine ears be attentive to the voice of my supplications. If thou, Lord, shouldest mark iniquities, O Lord, who shall stand? But there is forgiveness with thee, that thou mayest be feared. I wait for the Lord, my soul doth wait, and in his word do I hope." -- Psalm 130:1-5

This is one of the seven great penitential psalms of the church, and it has been the refuge of burdened sinners for three thousand years. Martin Luther counted it among his favourite psalms and called it one of the Pauline psalms, because it sets forth with such clarity the doctrine of justification by grace through faith. Out of the depths, the psalmist cries to God, and from those depths he discovers truths about the character of God that the comfortable and the self-satisfied can never learn.

"Out of the depths have I cried unto thee, O Lord." The depths. What depths are these? They are the depths of conviction, the depths of guilt, the depths of that terrible awareness of sin that comes when the Holy Spirit shines His light into the dark chambers of the human heart. There are depths of sorrow, depths of affliction, depths of outward trouble that drive men to their knees. But the deepest depths of all are the depths of a conscience awakened to the enormity of its sin against a holy God. It is from these depths that the most desperate and the most effectual prayers have always risen.

And notice: the psalmist does not cry out of these depths to man, or to angels, or to saints departed, or to any created being. He cries unto the Lord. This is the instinct of the regenerate soul. When the depths close in and the waters rise, the believer turns not to human counsellors but to God Himself. Not because human counsel is worthless, but because in the extremity of spiritual distress, only the voice of God can bring comfort, and only the hand of God can effect deliverance.

"If thou, Lord, shouldest mark iniquities, O Lord, who shall stand?" Here is one of the most searching questions in the entire Bible. If God were to deal with us according to strict justice, if He were to call us to account for every sin of thought, word, and deed, if He were to weigh our lives in the balance of His perfect holiness and pronounce sentence accordingly, who among the sons of men could survive the verdict? The answer is devastatingly simple: no one. Not the greatest saint who ever lived. Not the most devoted servant of God. Not the apostle Paul. Not the prophet Daniel. Not Moses. Not Abraham. If God should mark iniquities, the entire human race would be condemned without exception and without remedy.

This is a truth that many find uncomfortable, but it is a truth that must be grasped before the gospel can be truly appreciated. The man who thinks he can stand before God on the basis of his own righteousness has not yet understood the nature of God's holiness. He is like a man who thinks he can swim across the Pacific Ocean because he has managed to paddle across the municipal swimming pool. The distance between human righteousness and divine holiness is not a gap that greater effort can bridge. It is an infinite chasm, and only the grace of God can span it.

"But there is forgiveness with thee, that thou mayest be feared." This single verse is a pearl of incalculable price. After the devastating question of the previous verse, we might expect the psalmist to say, "Therefore we are all without hope, and nothing remains for us but the fearful looking for of judgment." But instead he says, "But." But! That glorious, gospel conjunction that turns despair into hope and death into life. But there is forgiveness with thee. Not "there may be," not "there could be," not "there sometimes is," but "there is." Forgiveness is a fixed and permanent attribute of God. It is part of who He is. It is always available, always sufficient, always ready to be bestowed upon every soul that comes to Him in humble, penitent faith.

And then the psalmist adds a phrase that has puzzled many a reader: "That thou mayest be feared." We might have expected him to say, "That thou mayest be loved," or "That thou mayest be thanked." But he says, "feared." And this is profoundly right, for the forgiveness of God, rightly understood, does not breed presumption but reverence. The man who truly grasps the magnitude of what God has done in pardoning his sins, who understands the cost of that pardon in the blood of Christ, who realizes that the just and holy God has passed over his transgressions not by ignoring them but by bearing them in the person of His own Son, that man is filled not with a casual familiarity but with a holy and trembling awe. He fears God, not with the craven fear of a slave, but with the reverent fear of a son who knows the greatness of his Father's love.

"I wait for the Lord, my soul doth wait, and in his word do I hope." Here is the posture of the forgiven sinner: waiting and hoping. Waiting for the full experience of that forgiveness to flood his soul with peace. Hoping in the Word of God, which has declared that there is forgiveness with Him. The waiting is not passive. It is active, expectant, eager. It is the waiting of a man who knows that the dawn is coming because the Word of God has promised it, even though the night is still dark around him.

"My soul waiteth for the Lord more than they that watch for the morning: I say, more than they that watch for the morning." The watchmen on the walls of Jerusalem waited through the long night hours for the first grey light of dawn. Their waiting was not in vain, for the morning always came. And the soul that waits for the Lord is engaged in a waiting that is even more certain to be rewarded. For the God who cannot lie has said, "I will forgive their iniquity, and I will remember their sin no more."

"Let Israel hope in the Lord: for with the Lord there is mercy, and with him is plenteous redemption. And he shall redeem Israel from all his iniquities." Plenteous redemption. Not a scanty, grudging, barely sufficient redemption, but a plenteous one, an overflowing one, a redemption as vast and as deep as the ocean of God's mercy. Out of the depths we cry, and from those depths we are lifted by the plenteous redemption of our God. Amen.
