PURITAN GOLD -- Family Devotional, Day 14
"The Lord's Day — A Gift of Rest and Worship"
Matthew Henry — Commentary on Isaiah


Welcome, dear family. Another day has passed, and God has been faithful through it all. Now let us close this day together by sitting at the feet of Jesus and hearing His Word.

Let us pray. Almighty God, You are the giver of every good gift, and one of the greatest gifts You have given us is this family. We dedicate this time to You. Help us to listen well and to grow in wisdom together. Through Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.

Our passage tonight is Isaiah 58:13-14. Listen with expectant hearts:

"If thou turn away thy foot from the sabbath, from doing thy pleasure on my holy day; and call the sabbath a delight, the holy of the LORD, honourable; and shalt honour him, not doing thine own ways, nor finding thine own pleasure, nor speaking thine own words: Then shalt thou delight thyself in the LORD; and I will cause thee to ride upon the high places of the earth, and feed thee with the heritage of Jacob thy father: for the mouth of the LORD hath spoken it."

That is Isaiah 58:13-14. Let those words settle in your heart for a moment before we continue.


As we close our week with Matthew Henry, we consider a subject that was very dear to the Puritans — the Lord's Day, the Christian Sabbath. Isaiah 58:13-14 gives us God's own vision for what this day should look like.

God says through Isaiah, 'If thou turn away thy foot from the sabbath, from doing thy pleasure on my holy day; and call the sabbath a delight, the holy of the LORD, honourable.' Notice something crucial here: God calls the Sabbath 'my holy day.' It belongs to Him. It is set apart. But — and this is the part many people miss — God says to call it a delight. Not a burden. Not a restriction. A delight.

The Sabbath principle goes all the way back to creation. In Genesis 2:2-3, 'And on the seventh day God ended his work which he had made; and he rested on the seventh day from all his work which he had made. And God blessed the seventh day, and sanctified it.' God Himself modeled the rhythm of work and rest. Not because He was tired — the Creator of the universe does not get tired — but because He was establishing a pattern for His creatures.

The fourth commandment in Exodus 20:8-11 enshrines this pattern: 'Remember the sabbath day, to keep it holy. Six days shalt thou labour, and do all thy work: But the seventh day is the sabbath of the LORD thy God: in it thou shalt not do any work.' The word 'remember' suggests that God knew we would be tempted to forget. We are so driven to work, to produce, to stay busy, that we need God to command us to stop.

Jesus clarified the purpose of the Sabbath in Mark 2:27: 'The sabbath was made for man, and not man for the sabbath.' The Sabbath is a gift, not a chain. God designed it for our benefit — for our physical rest, our spiritual renewal, and our relational refreshment.

In the New Testament, Christians moved their day of worship to Sunday, the first day of the week, because that is the day Christ rose from the dead (Acts 20:7, 1 Corinthians 16:2). The early Christians called it 'the Lord's Day' (Revelation 1:10). The principle of one day in seven set apart for rest and worship continues, now centered on the resurrection.

Matthew Henry was deeply committed to keeping the Lord's Day well. He called it the market day of the soul — the day when we lay in spiritual provisions for the week ahead, just as people went to market to buy food for the week. Without this regular restocking, our spiritual pantry runs bare.

Henry wrote that the Sabbath is God's gift to man, not a burden upon him, and that those who call it a delight will find it so. He believed the key to a joyful Lord's Day is not merely what we avoid (unnecessary work, frivolous entertainment) but what we embrace — worship, prayer, Scripture reading, fellowship, acts of mercy, rest, and family time.

Henry's own Lord's Day practices were a model. He would rise early for private prayer, then lead his family in morning worship with Scripture and song. The family would attend public worship together. After the service, they would share a meal and discuss the sermon. The afternoon was for rest, spiritual reading, visiting the sick, or quiet walks. The evening closed with family worship and preparation for the week ahead.

Henry warned against two extremes. One is legalism — turning the Lord's Day into a joyless list of 'thou shalt nots.' The Pharisees made this mistake, and Jesus rebuked them for it. The other extreme is treating Sunday as merely another weekend day for errands, sports, and entertainment, with no thought for God at all. The biblical balance is a day that is genuinely different — set apart, restful, joyful, and God-centered.

For our youngest ones: the Lord's Day is God's special gift of a day for rest and worship. It is like a weekly birthday party for Jesus, where we celebrate that He is alive! For older children: in a world that never stops — always connected, always busy — the Lord's Day offers a counter-cultural rhythm of rest. Have you ever noticed how refreshed you feel after a truly restful day? For the whole family: how can we make our Lord's Day more of a delight? Not by adding rules but by adding joy, worship, and rest.


Let us sit at the feet of a Puritan teacher and learn something that will deepen our understanding.

Matthew Henry, in his work "Commentary on the Whole Bible," wrote these words:

"The sabbath is God's gift to man, not a burden upon him. Those who call it a delight shall find it so. The Lord's Day is the market day of the soul, in which we lay in provisions for the week."

What Matthew Henry is reminding us is that the truths we have read tonight in Isaiah 58:13-14 are not new. They have been treasured by faithful Christians for centuries. And they are just as true and just as needed in our family today as they were hundreds of years ago. Let us take these words to heart and let them shape the way we live together as a family.


Let us discuss what we have heard. Parents, encourage even the youngest to share what they are thinking.

Question 1: What does God call the Sabbath? (A delight!) Does that surprise you?

Question 2: Why did God create a day of rest? Do you think we need it?

Question 3: What is the difference between making the Lord's Day a burden and making it a delight?

Question 4: What are some things our family could do to make our Lord's Day more restful, joyful, and God-centered?

Take a few minutes to talk about these questions together. Let everyone share, and listen to one another with patience and love.


Now, here is our memory verse for today. Let us say it together:

"Call the sabbath a delight, the holy of the LORD, honourable." -- Isaiah 58:13b

Try to say it out loud three times right now. Then, before bed tonight, see if you can say it from memory. Tomorrow morning, try again. The more we hide God's Word in our hearts, the more it will guide us when we need it most.


Before we close, here is a family challenge for the days ahead:

Plan your next Lord's Day together as a family. Each person suggests one thing that would make it more restful or worshipful — a special meal, a nature walk, a family game, a time of singing, visiting someone who is lonely. Try to include everyone's suggestion.


Heavenly Father, we end where we began, in gratitude. Thank You for Your Word, for this family, and for the hope we have in Christ. May tonight's lesson from Isaiah 58:13-14 shape the way we live, the way we speak, and the way we love one another. Amen and amen.

Until next time, dear family. The Lord goes before you. Walk with Him.
