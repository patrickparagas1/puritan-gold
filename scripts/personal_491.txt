PURITAN GOLD -- My Growth, Day 30
"The Means of Grace"
Theological Day — The Ordinary Channels of God's Blessing

---

Welcome. Today is a Theological day. We examine a doctrine the Puritans held dear: the means of grace — the ordinary channels through which God communicates His saving and sanctifying power to believers.

Let us read Acts 2:42 (KJV):

"And they continued stedfastly in the apostles' doctrine and fellowship, and in breaking of bread, and in prayers."

--- Teaching ---

The Puritans distinguished between God's extraordinary works — miracles, visions, direct revelations — and His ordinary works, which He accomplishes through established means. While God is free to work however He chooses, He has appointed specific channels through which His grace normally flows. These channels are called the means of grace, and the Christian who neglects them should not expect spiritual growth any more than a farmer who neglects plowing should expect a harvest.

The Westminster Shorter Catechism identifies three primary means of grace: the Word, the sacraments, and prayer. Let us consider each.

First, the Word of God. The Puritans valued the Word in three forms: the Word read, the Word preached, and the Word meditated upon. They believed that the Bible is the primary instrument the Holy Spirit uses to convert sinners, sanctify believers, and sustain the church. John Owen wrote that the Spirit always works in conjunction with the Word — never in contradiction to it, and rarely apart from it. The Christian who saturates his mind with Scripture gives the Spirit abundant material to work with.

The Puritans placed special emphasis on preaching. They considered the faithful exposition of Scripture by a called and qualified minister to be the most powerful means of grace available to the church. They noted that Romans 10:17 says faith comes by hearing, and hearing by the Word of God. The sermon was not a lecture or a performance; it was a means by which the living God addressed His people. The Puritans prepared for sermons with prayer, attended them with expectation, and reviewed them afterward with meditation. They treated preaching as an encounter with God, not merely an exercise in instruction.

Second, the sacraments. Baptism and the Lord's Supper are visible signs and seals of the covenant of grace. The Puritans did not view the sacraments as magical rituals that automatically convey grace. Nor did they view them as mere symbols with no spiritual power. They held a middle position: the sacraments are genuine means of grace — instruments through which God strengthens, nourishes, and confirms the faith of believers — but they are effective only in conjunction with faith and the work of the Spirit.

The Lord's Supper was particularly precious to the Puritans. They approached the table with careful preparation — examining themselves, confessing known sin, and stirring up faith and desire for communion with Christ. Richard Baxter wrote that the Lord's Supper is the closest thing to heaven available on earth — a moment when the believer feeds on Christ by faith and is strengthened for the journey ahead. The Puritans would be grieved by the casual, infrequent, unreflective way many modern churches observe this ordinance.

Third, prayer. We have already devoted a pastoral day to prayer, but it deserves mention here as a means of grace. Prayer is not merely talking to God. It is a divinely appointed channel through which God communicates His grace, wisdom, strength, and comfort to the soul. The Puritans believed that prayer changes things — not because it manipulates God into doing our will, but because God has ordained prayer as the means by which He accomplishes many of His purposes.

Beyond these three primary means, the Puritans also recognized additional channels of grace. Christian fellowship — the mutual encouragement, accountability, and love of the body of Christ — is a means of grace. Suffering — the furnace of affliction through which God purifies and strengthens His people — is a means of grace. The providence of daily life — the ordering of circumstances by a sovereign God — is a means of grace. Even the conviction of the conscience — the inner voice that warns of sin and calls to repentance — is a means of grace.

The Puritans' practical counsel on the means of grace can be summarized in four words: attend them diligently. Do not neglect the Word. Do not skip the Lord's Table. Do not abandon prayer. Do not forsake fellowship. These are the channels through which God has promised to meet you. He will not fail to keep His appointment. The question is whether you will show up for yours.

--- Closing ---

The means of grace are ordinary — not flashy, not dramatic, not spectacular. But they are powerful. They are the channels through which the Almighty feeds His people. Do not despise them. Lord, forgive me for neglecting the means You have appointed. Draw me back to the Word, the Table, and the prayer closet. Meet me there as You have promised. And let the ordinary channels of Your grace produce extraordinary fruit in my life. Amen.
