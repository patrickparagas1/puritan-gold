PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 13
"Can We Trust the Bible?"
Apologetics for Young Christians — Lesson 3

---

Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we address one of the most common and most important challenges to the Christian faith: Can we trust the Bible? Is the Bible we hold in our hands a reliable and accurate record of what was originally written? And is it truly what it claims to be — the inspired, authoritative Word of the living God? We will examine the evidence from four angles: manuscript evidence, archaeological confirmation, internal consistency, and fulfilled prophecy. Let us pray and begin.

Lord, Thy Word is truth. Jesus prayed in His high priestly prayer, "Sanctify them through thy truth: thy word is truth." As we study the evidence for the trustworthiness of Scripture, strengthen our confidence in the Book that speaks with Thy voice. May we come to treasure it more deeply, trust it more fully, and obey it more faithfully. In Christ's name, Amen.

--- Lesson ---

The Bible is the foundation of the Christian faith. Everything we believe about God, about Christ, about salvation, about eternity, about the meaning and purpose of human life, comes to us through the pages of this extraordinary book. If the Bible is unreliable — if it has been corrupted over the centuries, if its historical claims are false, if its internal contradictions are real — then Christianity crumbles. But if the Bible is what it claims to be — the inspired, inerrant, infallible Word of God — then it deserves our complete confidence, our wholehearted obedience, and our deepest love.

The importance of this question cannot be overstated. Attacks on the Bible are not new. Throughout history, skeptics and enemies of the faith have tried to discredit, destroy, and dismiss the Scriptures. The Roman emperor Diocletian, in 303 AD, issued an imperial edict commanding that all copies of the Christian Scriptures be burned and all churches destroyed. He believed he could wipe the Bible from the face of the earth. He failed. Within a decade, the emperor Constantine was promoting Christianity, and the Bible was being copied and distributed more widely than ever before. The French philosopher Voltaire, who died in 1778, predicted that within a hundred years Christianity would cease to exist and the Bible would be found only in museums. Within fifty years of his death, the Geneva Bible Society had purchased his former house and was using it as a warehouse for distributing Bibles across Europe. The Bible has outlived every one of its enemies, and it will outlive every future enemy as well.

In our previous lessons, we studied the arguments for the existence of God and saw that the evidence from creation powerfully supports the reality of a divine Creator. But the God of creation is also the God of revelation. He has not merely created the world and left us to figure things out on our own. He has spoken. He has communicated truth about Himself, about us, and about His purposes for the world. And He has done so through the pages of Scripture.

The apostle Paul declared in Second Timothy chapter three, verses sixteen and seventeen: "All scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness: That the man of God may be perfect, throughly furnished unto all good works." The word translated "inspiration of God" is theopneustos in Greek, which literally means "God-breathed." The Scriptures are not merely the product of human effort. They are breathed out by God — the very words of the Almighty communicated through human authors.

The apostle Peter confirmed this in his second epistle, chapter one, verses twenty and twenty-one: "Knowing this first, that no prophecy of the scripture is of any private interpretation. For the prophecy came not in old time by the will of man: but holy men of God spake as they were moved by the Holy Ghost." The phrase "moved by the Holy Ghost" uses the Greek word pheromenoi, which means "carried along" — the same word used in Acts 27:15 to describe a ship driven along by the wind. The human authors of Scripture were carried along by the Holy Spirit, so that what they wrote was simultaneously their own words and God's words.

The Puritan William Ames, in his Medulla Theologiae published in 1627, wrote, "The Scriptures are not a dead letter but a living oracle, through which God speaks to His people in every age." And the great Westminster Confession of Faith, adopted in 1646 by the Westminster Assembly of Divines meeting at Westminster Abbey in London, declares in its first chapter that the Scriptures are "given by inspiration of God to be the rule of faith and life" and that their "authority dependeth not upon the testimony of any man or church, but wholly upon God, the Author thereof."

But the skeptic asks: How do we know? How do we know that the Bible we hold in our hands today is the same Bible that was originally written? How do we know that it has not been corrupted, altered, or fabricated over the centuries? These are fair questions, and they deserve careful, thorough answers.

Part One: The Manuscript Evidence

The first and perhaps most powerful line of evidence is the manuscript evidence. When skeptics claim that the Bible has been copied so many times that we cannot know what the original text said — like a game of "telephone" in which the message becomes garbled beyond recognition — they reveal an ignorance of the actual evidence that is almost breathtaking.

Let us begin with the New Testament. The New Testament is, by far, the best-attested document from the ancient world. Nothing else even comes close. We possess over 5,800 manuscripts of the New Testament in Greek — the original language. In addition, we have over 10,000 manuscripts in Latin, over 9,300 in various other ancient languages — Syriac, Coptic, Armenian, Georgian, Ethiopic, Gothic, and Slavonic — for a total of approximately 25,000 manuscripts and fragments of the New Testament. This is an embarrassment of riches that no other ancient text can match.

But the number of manuscripts is only part of the story. The date of the manuscripts is equally important. The earliest known fragment of the New Testament is a small piece of papyrus known as P52, which contains a portion of the Gospel of John, chapter eighteen. This fragment is dated to approximately 125 AD — less than forty years after the Gospel of John was written, which is generally dated to around 90 AD. Other early papyri — such as P66 and P75, which date to roughly 175 to 225 AD — contain large portions of the Gospels of Luke and John. The Chester Beatty Papyri, dating to the early third century, contain substantial portions of the Gospels, Acts, the Pauline epistles, and the book of Revelation. The Bodmer Papyri, also from the third century, contain nearly complete copies of the Gospels of Luke and John.

Now, compare this to other ancient texts that no historian questions. We have only seven manuscripts of Plato's works, the earliest dating roughly 1,200 years after Plato wrote. We have ten manuscripts of Caesar's Gallic Wars, the earliest dating about 950 years after Caesar wrote. We have only two manuscripts of Tacitus's Annals, the earliest dating about 850 years after Tacitus wrote. We have 643 manuscripts of Homer's Iliad — the closest competitor to the New Testament — with the earliest dating about 500 years after Homer composed it.

Let me put this in a chart form for you to visualize:

Homer's Iliad: 643 manuscripts, earliest copy roughly 500 years after original.
Herodotus's History: 8 manuscripts, earliest copy roughly 1,350 years after original.
Plato's Dialogues: 7 manuscripts, earliest copy roughly 1,200 years after original.
Caesar's Gallic Wars: 10 manuscripts, earliest copy roughly 950 years after original.
Tacitus's Annals: 2 manuscripts, earliest copy roughly 850 years after original.
New Testament: over 25,000 manuscripts, earliest copy roughly 25 to 40 years after original.

The contrast is staggering. No historian doubts the reliability of Plato, Caesar, or Tacitus, despite the tiny number of manuscripts and the enormous gap between the originals and the earliest copies. If the same standard were applied to the New Testament, its reliability would be beyond all possible dispute.

Furthermore, the variations between manuscripts — what scholars call "textual variants" — are overwhelmingly trivial. The vast majority involve differences in spelling, word order, or the inclusion or omission of a definite article. The renowned textual scholar Sir Frederic Kenyon, who served as Director of the British Museum, concluded that "the number of manuscripts of the New Testament, of early translations from it, and of quotations from it in the oldest writers of the Church, is so large that it is practically certain that the true reading of every doubtful passage is preserved in some one or other of these ancient authorities. This can be said of no other ancient book in the world." The New Testament scholar Bruce Metzger, who spent his career at Princeton Theological Seminary, estimated that the New Testament text is approximately 99.5 percent pure — meaning that 99.5 percent of the text is certain beyond any reasonable doubt.

No essential Christian doctrine depends on a textually disputed passage. Not one. The deity of Christ, the Trinity, the atonement, the resurrection, salvation by grace through faith — every major doctrine of the Christian faith is established by multiple passages whose text is undisputed. The textual critic Daniel Wallace, who has spent his career studying the manuscripts of the New Testament, has stated that the wealth of manuscript evidence actually works in our favor: "We have so many manuscripts that we can effectively reconstruct the original text."

Additionally, the early Church Fathers — Christian writers and leaders of the second, third, and fourth centuries — quoted the New Testament so extensively in their writings that virtually the entire New Testament could be reconstructed from their quotations alone, even if every manuscript had been lost. The scholars Johann Jakob Griesbach and later Sir David Dalrymple both observed that the quotations from the New Testament found in the writings of the Church Fathers number in the tens of thousands, providing an independent witness to the text that confirms the manuscript tradition.

Let me also mention the early translations of the New Testament. Within a few centuries of the apostolic era, the New Testament was translated into Latin (the Old Latin versions date to the second century, and Jerome's Vulgate was completed around 405 AD), into Syriac (the Peshitta dates to the early fifth century, but earlier Syriac versions existed in the second century), into Coptic (third and fourth centuries), and into numerous other languages. These translations provide additional witnesses to the original text, because they were made from Greek manuscripts that are now lost. By comparing the translations to the Greek manuscripts, scholars can trace the textual history with remarkable precision.

It is worth pausing to address a common misconception. Some people imagine that the Bible was transmitted like a game of telephone — each person whispering the message to the next, with inevitable distortions accumulating at every step. But this analogy is profoundly misleading. The transmission of the biblical text was not oral but written. It was not casual but formal, governed by strict scribal practices. And we do not have just one line of transmission but thousands of independent manuscript lines that can be compared and cross-checked against one another. If one manuscript contains an error, the error can be detected by comparing it with the hundreds or thousands of other manuscripts that preserve the correct reading. The manuscript tradition of the Bible is not a single fragile thread but a vast, interwoven web of independent witnesses.

The Old Testament is similarly well-preserved. Before 1947, the oldest known Hebrew manuscripts of the Old Testament were the Masoretic texts, dating to roughly the ninth and tenth centuries AD. Skeptics argued that the Old Testament text must have been corrupted during the many centuries between the original writings and these medieval copies. Then, in 1947, a young Bedouin shepherd named Muhammad ed-Dhib was searching for a lost goat in the caves above the western shore of the Dead Sea, near the ancient ruins of Qumran in the Judean Desert. In one cave, he discovered a collection of clay jars containing ancient scrolls wrapped in linen. These became known as the Dead Sea Scrolls — one of the greatest archaeological discoveries of the twentieth century.

Over the following years, fragments of approximately 900 manuscripts were recovered from eleven caves near Qumran. Among them were copies of every book of the Old Testament except Esther, some dating to as early as the third century BC — roughly a thousand years older than the previously known Masoretic texts. When scholars compared the Dead Sea Scrolls to the later Masoretic manuscripts, they found an astonishing degree of accuracy. The Great Isaiah Scroll, for example, a complete copy of the book of Isaiah dating to approximately 125 BC, was found to be virtually identical to the Masoretic text of Isaiah — despite a gap of more than a thousand years between them. The few variations that existed were minor — differences in spelling and occasional word substitutions that did not affect the meaning of the text.

This discovery confirmed what the Jewish scribes had always maintained: that they had transmitted the text of the Old Testament with extraordinary, painstaking care. The Masoretic scribes followed strict rules in their copying: they counted every letter of every book, identified the middle letter and the middle word, and if any errors were found, the entire copy was destroyed and the work begun again. Their reverence for the sacred text produced a level of accuracy that modern scholars have found remarkable.

The Puritan John Owen, in his treatise on the divine original and authority of the Scriptures, argued that the providential preservation of the biblical text is itself evidence of divine oversight. Owen wrote, "God, who was the Author of the Scripture, did also by His providence take care for the preservation thereof, that it might be useful to the church in all ages." The extraordinary accuracy of the biblical manuscript tradition is not merely a fortunate accident. It is the product of God's providential care for His Word.

Part Two: Archaeological Confirmation

The second line of evidence is archaeological confirmation. Over the past two hundred years, the discipline of biblical archaeology has produced discovery after discovery confirming the historical accuracy of the Bible. Each new excavation, each new inscription uncovered from the sands of the ancient Near East, each new artifact pulled from the earth adds another piece of evidence to the mounting case for biblical reliability. The pattern is unmistakable and, for the skeptic, deeply inconvenient: wherever the Bible's historical claims can be tested against the archaeological record, the Bible is consistently vindicated.

In the nineteenth century, many scholars confidently asserted that the Bible was full of historical errors. They claimed that the Hittites, mentioned frequently in the Old Testament, never existed. They said that writing did not exist in Moses's day, so the Pentateuch could not have been written by Moses. They denied the existence of King David, the historicity of the Exodus, and the accuracy of the Gospels' descriptions of first-century Palestine.

One by one, archaeology has vindicated the Bible and silenced its critics.

The Hittites, once dismissed as a biblical fiction, were dramatically confirmed in 1906 when the German archaeologist Hugo Winckler excavated the Hittite capital of Hattusa, near the modern village of Bogazkale in central Turkey. He discovered a vast archive of over ten thousand clay tablets documenting the language, laws, treaties, and history of a powerful Hittite empire that flourished from approximately 1600 to 1178 BC — exactly as the Bible had described.

The claim that writing did not exist in Moses's day was thoroughly refuted by the discovery of numerous writing systems predating Moses by centuries, including Sumerian cuneiform (dating to roughly 3400 BC), Egyptian hieroglyphics (roughly 3200 BC), and the Proto-Sinaitic script (roughly 1800 BC), which was discovered in the Sinai Peninsula — the very region through which Moses led the Israelites.

The Pool of Siloam, mentioned in John chapter nine where Jesus healed the blind man, was discovered in 2004 during construction work in the City of David in Jerusalem. Archaeologists uncovered a large, stepped pool from the Second Temple period that matched the biblical description precisely.

The existence of Pontius Pilate, the Roman governor who sentenced Jesus to crucifixion, was confirmed in 1961 when a stone inscription bearing his name and title was discovered at Caesarea Maritima by Italian archaeologists. The inscription reads, in part, "Pontius Pilatus, Prefect of Judea" — confirming not only Pilate's existence but his precise title.

The Tel Dan Stele, discovered in 1993 in northern Israel by archaeologist Avraham Biran, contains an inscription dating to the ninth century BC that refers to the "House of David" — providing the first extrabiblical confirmation of King David's existence and dynasty.

The Merneptah Stele, an Egyptian victory inscription dating to approximately 1208 BC, during the reign of Pharaoh Merneptah, contains the earliest known extrabiblical reference to Israel as a people in the land of Canaan — confirming that the Israelites were an established presence in the land by the late thirteenth century BC.

The city of Nineveh, the great Assyrian capital described in the book of Jonah and in the prophecy of Nahum, was excavated by Austen Henry Layard beginning in 1847 and confirmed to be a massive, fortified city matching the biblical descriptions.

Nelson Glueck, the renowned biblical archaeologist who served as president of Hebrew Union College, stated, "It may be stated categorically that no archaeological discovery has ever controverted a biblical reference. Scores of archaeological findings have been made which confirm in clear outline or exact detail historical statements in the Bible." This is a remarkable testimony from a scholar who spent decades conducting archaeological research in the biblical lands.

The ancient city of Jericho has been excavated repeatedly, and the evidence — including collapsed walls that fell outward rather than inward, signs of destruction by fire, and large stores of grain indicating a short siege rather than a prolonged one — is remarkably consistent with the biblical account of the fall of Jericho described in Joshua chapter six.

The Pool of Bethesda, described in John chapter five as having "five porches" — a detail once ridiculed by critics as an invention — was discovered in the nineteenth century in the northeastern quarter of the Old City of Jerusalem. The ruins revealed, exactly as John described, a pool with five covered colonnades.

Sir William Ramsay, one of the most distinguished archaeologists of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, born in 1851 in Glasgow, Scotland, and who died in 1939, began his career as a skeptic who believed the book of Acts was a late, unreliable fiction. After decades of archaeological research in Asia Minor, he completely reversed his position. Ramsay concluded that Luke, the author of Acts, was "a historian of the first rank" whose accuracy in every detail — names of officials, political titles, geographical descriptions, legal procedures — was consistently confirmed by the archaeological evidence. Ramsay's intellectual journey from skepticism to faith, driven by archaeological evidence, is itself a powerful testimony to the reliability of the biblical text.

The Puritan Matthew Henry, born on October 18, 1662, in Flintshire, Wales, and who died on June 22, 1714, wrote in his commentary on the Scriptures, "The Word of God is a tried Word. It has been tried by the fiery trial of persecution, by the cold trial of neglect, by the sharp trial of criticism, and it has come forth as gold." The archaeological record is one more trial through which the Bible has passed triumphantly.

Part Three: Internal Consistency

The third line of evidence is the internal consistency of the Bible. This is perhaps the most remarkable feature of all.

The Bible was written by approximately forty different authors over a period of roughly 1,500 years. These authors included kings and shepherds, fishermen and tax collectors, priests and prophets, scholars and soldiers. They wrote in three languages — Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek. They wrote on three continents — Asia, Africa, and Europe. They wrote in a wide variety of literary genres — history, poetry, prophecy, law, wisdom literature, gospel narrative, epistles, and apocalyptic vision. They wrote under vastly different circumstances — some in palaces, some in prisons, some in exile, some in the wilderness.

These authors did not sit down together in a conference room and coordinate their work. Most of them never met one another. Many of them were separated by centuries. Moses wrote in the wilderness of Sinai around 1400 BC. David wrote psalms in Jerusalem around 1000 BC. Isaiah prophesied in the court of Judah around 700 BC. Daniel wrote from Babylonian exile around 536 BC. Ezra wrote after the return from exile around 450 BC. The apostles wrote their Gospels and epistles in the second half of the first century AD. These men had no opportunity to collaborate.

And yet, despite this extraordinary diversity of authorship, language, culture, and circumstance, the Bible tells a single, unified story — the story of God's creation of the world, humanity's fall into sin, God's covenant of grace, and God's plan of redemption culminating in the person and work of Jesus Christ. From Genesis to Revelation, from the creation of the first Adam to the coming of the last Adam, from the Garden of Eden to the New Jerusalem, the Bible unfolds a coherent narrative with a consistent message, a progressive revelation, and a triumphant conclusion.

Consider the sheer improbability of this unity on purely human terms. Imagine gathering forty authors from twenty different countries, spanning fifteen centuries, writing in three languages, on the most controversial subjects known to humanity — the nature of God, the meaning of life, the definition of morality, the destiny of the soul — and asking them to produce a unified, coherent, harmonious work. The result would be chaos. The contradictions and disagreements would be endless.

But the Bible achieves what no committee of human authors could ever produce. Its unity is a fingerprint of divine authorship — evidence that behind the forty human authors stands one divine Author who guided the process from beginning to end.

Consider some specific examples of this unity. In Genesis chapter three, verse fifteen, immediately after the fall of Adam and Eve, God promised that the seed of the woman would crush the head of the serpent — a promise that theologians call the protoevangelium, the first gospel. This promise is progressively unfolded through the rest of Scripture: through the covenant with Abraham (Genesis 12, 15, 17), through the covenant with David (2 Samuel 7), through the prophecies of the Messiah in Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Daniel, Micah, Zechariah, and Malachi, and finally through the birth, life, death, resurrection, and ascension of Jesus Christ as recorded in the four Gospels and interpreted in the epistles. The final book of the Bible, Revelation, brings the story to its climax as the serpent — now identified as "that old serpent, called the Devil, and Satan" (Revelation 12:9) — is cast into the lake of fire, and the people of God dwell with Him forever in a restored creation. The golden thread of redemption runs from the first page to the last, unbroken and unwavering.

Or consider the theme of sacrifice. In Genesis, God clothes Adam and Eve with animal skins — the first sacrifice (Genesis 3:21). In the Mosaic law, God institutes an elaborate system of animal sacrifices pointing forward to the ultimate sacrifice. In the prophets, God declares, "I desired mercy, and not sacrifice" (Hosea 6:6), preparing His people to understand that the animal sacrifices are not an end in themselves but a shadow of something greater. In the Gospels, John the Baptist sees Jesus and cries, "Behold the Lamb of God, which taketh away the sin of the world" (John 1:29). In the epistles, Paul declares, "For even Christ our passover is sacrificed for us" (1 Corinthians 5:7). And in Revelation, the redeemed sing praises to "the Lamb that was slain" (Revelation 5:12). This theme, developed over fifteen centuries by dozens of different authors, displays a coherence and progression that is impossible to explain apart from divine authorship.

The Puritan Thomas Watson expressed this beautifully: "The Scripture is like a ring of gold — every link is joined to every other. The first promise in Genesis and the last promise in Revelation meet in Christ." Watson understood that the unity of the Bible is not accidental but intentional — the product of a divine mind that wove together sixty-six books into a single, seamless tapestry of redemptive truth.

Part Four: Fulfilled Prophecy

The fourth line of evidence is fulfilled prophecy. The Bible contains hundreds of predictive prophecies — specific, detailed predictions about future events that were written centuries before those events occurred and were fulfilled with remarkable precision. No other religious text in the world contains anything comparable.

Consider the messianic prophecies — the predictions about the coming Messiah that are found throughout the Old Testament and that were fulfilled in the life of Jesus Christ.

The prophet Micah, writing around 700 BC, predicted that the Messiah would be born in Bethlehem: "But thou, Bethlehem Ephratah, though thou be little among the thousands of Judah, yet out of thee shall he come forth unto me that is to be ruler in Israel; whose goings forth have been from of old, from everlasting" (Micah 5:2). Jesus was born in Bethlehem (Matthew 2:1, Luke 2:4-7).

The prophet Isaiah, writing around 700 BC, predicted that the Messiah would be born of a virgin: "Therefore the Lord himself shall give you a sign; Behold, a virgin shall conceive, and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel" (Isaiah 7:14). Jesus was born of the virgin Mary (Matthew 1:18-25, Luke 1:26-35).

The prophet Zechariah, writing around 520 BC, predicted that the Messiah would enter Jerusalem riding on a donkey: "Rejoice greatly, O daughter of Zion; shout, O daughter of Jerusalem: behold, thy King cometh unto thee: he is just, and having salvation; lowly, and riding upon an ass, and upon a colt the foal of an ass" (Zechariah 9:9). Jesus entered Jerusalem on Palm Sunday riding a donkey (Matthew 21:1-11, John 12:12-16).

The Psalmist David, writing around 1000 BC, described the crucifixion of the Messiah in Psalm twenty-two — a thousand years before crucifixion was even invented by the Persians and adopted by the Romans: "They pierced my hands and my feet. I may tell all my bones: they look and stare upon me. They part my garments among them, and cast lots upon my vesture" (Psalm 22:16-18). Every detail was fulfilled at the crucifixion of Jesus (Matthew 27:35, John 19:23-24, John 20:25-27).

The prophet Isaiah described the suffering of the Messiah in chapter fifty-three with astonishing specificity: "He is despised and rejected of men; a man of sorrows, and acquainted with grief... he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed... He was oppressed, and he was afflicted, yet he opened not his mouth: he is brought as a lamb to the slaughter, and as a sheep before her shearers is dumb, so he openeth not his mouth... And he made his grave with the wicked, and with the rich in his death" (Isaiah 53:3, 5, 7, 9). Every element was fulfilled in the trial, crucifixion, and burial of Jesus Christ.

The mathematician Peter Stoner calculated the probability of just eight of these messianic prophecies being fulfilled by chance in any one person. The result was one in ten to the seventeenth power — one in one hundred quadrillion. To illustrate this probability, Stoner suggested covering the entire state of Texas with silver dollars two feet deep, marking one of them, and then having a blindfolded person pick the marked coin on the first try. The odds of that happening are the same as the odds of eight prophecies being fulfilled by chance. And there are not merely eight messianic prophecies — there are over three hundred.

The prophet Daniel, writing from Babylonian exile around 536 BC, predicted in chapter nine, verses twenty-five and twenty-six, that the Messiah would come before the destruction of the Second Temple — which occurred in 70 AD when the Roman legions under Titus sacked Jerusalem. Daniel wrote, "Know therefore and understand, that from the going forth of the commandment to restore and to build Jerusalem unto the Messiah the Prince shall be seven weeks, and threescore and two weeks... And after threescore and two weeks shall Messiah be cut off, but not for himself: and the people of the prince that shall come shall destroy the city and the sanctuary." The precision of this prophecy — predicting both the timing of the Messiah's appearance and the destruction of the Temple — is extraordinary.

The prophet Zechariah predicted that the Messiah would be betrayed for thirty pieces of silver: "And I said unto them, If ye think good, give me my price; and if not, forbear. So they weighed for my price thirty pieces of silver" (Zechariah 11:12). This was fulfilled precisely in the betrayal of Jesus by Judas Iscariot (Matthew 26:14-15).

Isaiah prophesied that the Messiah would be silent before his accusers: "He was oppressed, and he was afflicted, yet he opened not his mouth" (Isaiah 53:7). The Gospels record that Jesus remained silent before Pilate and Herod (Matthew 27:12-14, Luke 23:9).

The cumulative weight of these prophecies is staggering. No human being could have arranged to be born in a specific town, of a specific lineage, in a specific manner, and then die in a specific way that was predicted centuries in advance. The fulfillment of messianic prophecy is among the most powerful evidences for both the divine inspiration of the Bible and the divine identity of Jesus Christ.

The Puritan John Flavel wrote, "The whole Scripture is a golden chain in which every link is Christ." The prophecies of the Old Testament and their fulfillment in the New form an unbreakable chain of evidence that demonstrates the divine origin of the Bible and the divine identity of Jesus Christ.

Part Five: The Testimony of Jesus Christ Himself

Before we turn to the final line of evidence, we must consider the testimony of the most important witness of all: the Lord Jesus Christ Himself. Jesus regarded the Old Testament Scriptures as the authoritative, inerrant Word of God. He quoted them constantly. He submitted to their authority. He affirmed their historical accuracy in ways that leave no room for doubt.

Jesus affirmed the creation of Adam and Eve as historical fact: "Have ye not read, that he which made them at the beginning made them male and female?" (Matthew 19:4). He affirmed the historicity of Noah and the flood: "As the days of Noe were, so shall also the coming of the Son of man be. For as in the days that were before the flood they were eating and drinking, marrying and giving in marriage, until the day that Noe entered into the ark" (Matthew 24:37-38). He affirmed the historical reality of Lot and the destruction of Sodom: "Likewise also as it was in the days of Lot... the same day that Lot went out of Sodom it rained fire and brimstone from heaven, and destroyed them all" (Luke 17:28-29). He affirmed the historicity of Jonah: "For as Jonas was three days and three nights in the whale's belly; so shall the Son of man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth" (Matthew 12:40). He affirmed the Mosaic authorship of the Pentateuch: "Had ye believed Moses, ye would have believed me: for he wrote of me" (John 5:46). He affirmed the prophetic authorship of Isaiah: "Esaias also prophesied of you hypocrites" (Mark 7:6). He affirmed the inspiration and inerrancy of every part of Scripture: "The scripture cannot be broken" (John 10:35), and "Till heaven and earth pass, one jot or one tittle shall in no wise pass from the law, till all be fulfilled" (Matthew 5:18).

If Jesus is who He claimed to be — the Son of God, the incarnate Word, the one who said "I am the way, the truth, and the life" (John 14:6) — then His testimony about the Scriptures settles the matter. If Christ affirmed the authority, historicity, and divine inspiration of the Bible, then we have the testimony of One who knows all things and cannot lie. As the Puritan Stephen Charnock wrote, "The veracity of God is the foundation of all religion." And the veracity of God is pledged to the truthfulness of His Word.

Part Six: The Internal Testimony of the Holy Spirit

Before we close, I must add one more line of evidence that the Puritans considered supreme above all others: the internal testimony of the Holy Spirit. While the external evidences — manuscripts, archaeology, consistency, and prophecy — are powerful and important, the ultimate ground of our confidence in the Bible is the witness of the Holy Spirit in our hearts.

The Westminster Confession of Faith states this with characteristic precision: "Our full persuasion and assurance of the infallible truth and divine authority [of the Scripture] is from the inward work of the Holy Spirit, bearing witness by and with the Word in our hearts." John Calvin, in his Institutes, wrote, "The same Spirit who spoke through the prophets must enter our hearts and convince us that they faithfully delivered what was divinely entrusted to them."

The Puritan John Owen developed this doctrine with great depth in his work The Reason of Faith. Owen argued that the Scriptures carry within themselves the marks of divine authorship — what he called their "self-evidencing light." Just as we recognize the light of the sun not by external proof but by the sun's own radiance, so we recognize the divine authority of Scripture not merely by external evidence but by the Scripture's own self-authenticating power, made effectual in our hearts by the Holy Spirit.

The Puritan Thomas Goodwin wrote, "The Spirit does not reveal new truths beyond Scripture, but He opens the eyes of the soul to see the truth that is already in Scripture." The Holy Spirit does not bypass our reason. He illuminates it. He does not replace the evidence. He enables us to see the evidence for what it truly is.

This does not mean that the external evidence is unimportant. It is very important. It confirms what the Spirit reveals. It removes intellectual obstacles. It equips us to defend the faith before skeptics and seekers. But the deepest, most unshakable confidence in the Bible comes not from archaeological discoveries or manuscript counts, however impressive they may be. It comes from the Spirit of God bearing witness in our hearts that the Word of God is true.

The great Puritan pastor and theologian John Bunyan, born in 1628 in Elstow, Bedfordshire, and who died in 1688 — the author of The Pilgrim's Progress, the most widely read book in the English language after the Bible itself — testified from his prison cell in Bedford Gaol, where he spent twelve years imprisoned for preaching without a license, that the Scriptures were his constant companion and unfailing comfort. Bunyan wrote, "I have found the Word of God sweeter than honey, and more precious than gold. In my darkest hours, it has been a lamp unto my feet and a light unto my path." His testimony, born from suffering, echoes the testimony of millions of believers throughout the centuries who have found in the Bible the living voice of God speaking truth, comfort, and hope to their souls.

The evidence for the reliability of the Bible is powerful and cumulative. No single argument by itself settles the question, but taken together — the manuscript evidence, the archaeological confirmation, the internal consistency, the fulfilled prophecy, and the testimony of the Holy Spirit — they create a case of overwhelming strength. The Bible is not a collection of myths and legends. It is not a flawed human book that has been corrupted beyond recognition. It is not a product of priestly conspiracy or political manipulation. It is the trustworthy, reliable, divinely inspired Word of the living God, preserved by His providence across millennia for the instruction, comfort, and salvation of His people in every age. As the Puritan Thomas Brooks wrote, "The Word of God is the only rule to direct us how we may glorify and enjoy Him." It is the rule by which we measure all things, the standard by which we judge all claims, and the foundation upon which we build our lives.

As the Psalmist declared in Psalm one hundred and nineteen, verse one hundred and sixty: "Thy word is true from the beginning: and every one of thy righteous judgments endureth for ever." And as the Lord Jesus Himself affirmed in Matthew chapter twenty-four, verse thirty-five: "Heaven and earth shall pass away, but my words shall not pass away." This promise from the lips of Christ Himself is the ultimate guarantee that the Word of God will endure forever — through every trial, every attack, and every age of human history.
