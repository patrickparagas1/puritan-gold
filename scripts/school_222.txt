Welcome to a new day of learning at the Puritan Academy. The Puritans believed that all truth is God's truth, and that the pursuit of knowledge is a form of worship. Let us worship Him through our studies today.

Heavenly Father, we dedicate this day of learning to You. We confess that apart from You we can understand nothing rightly. Illuminate our minds with the light of Your truth. Make us faithful students who study to show ourselves approved. In Christ's name. Amen.

Our Scripture for today is Ephesians 1:4-6; Romans 9:11-16; 2 Timothy 1:9:

"According as he hath chosen us in him before the foundation of the world, that we should be holy and without blame before him in love: Having predestinated us unto the adoption of children by Jesus Christ to himself, according to the good pleasure of his will, To the praise of the glory of his grace, wherein he hath made us accepted in the beloved. For the children being not yet born, neither having done any good or evil, that the purpose of God according to election might stand, not of works, but of him that calleth; It was said unto her, The elder shall serve the younger. Pause and let this truth settle deeply into your soul: before the stars were flung into space, before the mountains rose from the deep, before a single human heart had ever beaten — God knew you and chose you. Not because of your goodness, for you had none. Not because of your faith, for you did not yet exist. But according to the good pleasure of His will, for the praise of the glory of His grace. If this does not humble you, nothing will. If this does not comfort you, nothing can. Rest today in the sovereign, eternal, unchangeable love of a God who chose you before the foundation of the world.

As we prepare for today's lesson, remember that everything we study flows from this one fountain: the Word of God. Our lesson today is part of The Doctrines of Grace — Westminster Standards, and it is titled: Unconditional Election — God's Sovereign Choice. Let us proceed with minds engaged and hearts open.

SEGMENT 2: MAIN LESSON PART 1 -- BIBLICAL FOUNDATION
----------------------------------------

With our hearts prepared, let us move into the substance of today's lesson.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes in Ephesians 1:4-6; Romans 9:11-16; 2 Timothy 1:9.

The doctrine of unconditional election teaches that before the foundation of the world, God chose certain individuals to be saved — not because of anything good or meritorious in them, but solely according to His own sovereign will and good pleasure. This is one of the most awe-inspiring and humbling doctrines in all of Scripture, and it stands as a mighty fortress against all human pride and self-righteousness.

The apostle Paul sets forth this truth with magnificent clarity in Ephesians 1:4-6. He writes, "According as he hath chosen us in him before the foundation of the world, that we should be holy and without blame before him in love: Having predestinated us unto the adoption of children by Jesus Christ to himself, according to the good pleasure of his will, To the praise of the glory of his grace, wherein he hath made us accepted in the beloved." Several features of this text demand careful attention.

First, the timing of election: "before the foundation of the world." God's choice was not a reaction to human history but a decree made in eternity past, before any creature existed, before any human being had done anything good or evil. This rules out the idea that God elected people based on foreseen faith or foreseen good works. There was nothing to foresee because nothing yet existed. Election precedes creation itself.

Second, the basis of election: "according to the good pleasure of his will." The ground of God's choice is not in us but in Him. He did not choose us because we were lovable, or because He foresaw that we would believe, or because we had some quality that made us preferable to others. He chose us because it pleased Him to do so. This is what theologians mean by "unconditional" election — there are no conditions in us that moved God to elect us. The cause of election is entirely in the will of God.

Third, the purpose of election: "that we should be holy and without blame before him." Election is not merely to salvation from punishment but to holiness of life. God chose us not just to rescue us from hell but to make us like His Son. Holiness is the goal of election, not the cause of it. We are not chosen because we are holy; we are chosen in order to become holy.

Fourth, the result of election: "to the praise of the glory of his grace." The ultimate purpose of election is doxological — it is designed to display and magnify the glory of God's grace for all eternity. When the redeemed stand before the throne, they will not boast of their own faith or their own decision. They will praise the grace that chose them before they existed and the love that saved them when they were dead in sin.

In Romans 9:11-16, Paul addresses the doctrine of election with even more direct and confrontational language. He takes the case of Jacob and Esau, twin brothers in the same womb, and declares that God's choice between them was made "the children being not yet born, neither having done any good or evil, that the purpose of God according to election might stand, not of works, but of him that calleth." The deliberate point is that election has nothing to do with human merit. The twins had done nothing — no good, no evil. Yet God chose Jacob and rejected Esau. Why? "That the purpose of God according to election might stand."

Paul anticipates the objection that this seems unfair: "Is there unrighteousness with God? God forbid." He does not attempt to soften the doctrine or make it more palatable to human sensibilities. Instead, he appeals to God's own words to Moses: "I will have mercy on whom I will have mercy, and I will have compassion on whom I will have compassion." Mercy, by its very nature, cannot be demanded or earned. If it could, it would cease to be mercy and would become a debt. God is free to bestow mercy on whomever He pleases, and no creature has the right to question His decision.

The conclusion Paul draws is absolute: "So then it is not of him that willeth, nor of him that runneth, but of God that sheweth mercy." Salvation does not depend on human willing or human effort but on divine mercy. This does not mean that willing and running are unimportant — believers do will and run in the Christian life. But the ultimate cause of their salvation is not their willing or running but God's sovereign mercy that gave them the desire to will and the power to run.

Paul's letter to Timothy confirms this from a deeply personal angle. In 2 Timothy 1:9 he writes that God "hath saved us, and called us with an holy calling, not according to our works, but according to his own purpose and grace, which was given us in Christ Jesus before the world began." Here again we see that grace was given to the elect "before the world began" — a grace that existed in the eternal counsel of God before it was manifested in time through the calling of the gospel.

The Old Testament is rich with examples of unconditional election. God chose Abraham from among the idolaters of Ur (Joshua 24:2-3). He chose Israel from among all the nations, not because they were the largest or the most righteous, but simply because He loved them (Deuteronomy 7:7-8). He chose David, the youngest son of Jesse, passing over all his elder brothers (1 Samuel 16:7-12). In every case, the pattern is the same: God's choice is free, sovereign, and based solely on His own purposes.

Jesus Himself taught this doctrine plainly. In John 15:16 He told His disciples, "Ye have not chosen me, but I have chosen you." In John 6:44 He declared, "No man can come to me, except the Father which hath sent me draw him." And in John 17:9 He prayed not for the world at large but for those whom the Father had given Him: "I pray for them: I pray not for the world, but for them which thou hast given me; for they are thine." The elect are a gift from the Father to the Son, chosen in love before the ages began.

The practical comfort of this doctrine is immeasurable. If our salvation depended on our own choice, our own faith, our own perseverance — how terrified we would be, knowing the weakness and fickleness of our own hearts. But because our salvation rests on the eternal, unchangeable, unconditional choice of an omnipotent God, we can have unshakeable assurance. He who began the good work will complete it. He who chose us will keep us. He who called us will glorify us.

This biblical foundation is essential. Without it, everything else we study today would be like a house built on sand. But with it, we stand on solid rock.

SEGMENT 3: MAIN LESSON PART 2 -- PURITAN TEACHING
----------------------------------------

Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. John Owen wrote extensively on this subject in "The Doctrine of the Saints' Perseverance Explained and Confirmed." The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life.

John Owen (1616-1683), often called the prince of Puritan theologians, devoted considerable attention to the doctrine of election throughout his writings. Owen understood that election is the fountainhead from which all other saving blessings flow — calling, justification, sanctification, and glorification are all the outworking in time of what God decreed in eternity. Without a proper understanding of election, Owen argued, the entire scheme of redemption becomes confused and God's glory is obscured.

Owen carefully distinguished between the Arminian and Reformed understandings of election. The Arminian position, which Owen vigorously opposed, held that God's election is conditional — that God looked down the corridor of time, foresaw who would believe, and elected those persons on the basis of their foreseen faith. Owen demolished this view on multiple grounds. First, he argued that it makes faith the cause of election rather than the fruit of it, directly contradicting Ephesians 1:4 which states that we were chosen "before the foundation of the world" — before there was any faith to foresee. Second, he showed that this view ultimately makes the decisive factor in salvation the human will rather than the divine will, which contradicts Romans 9:16: "It is not of him that willeth, nor of him that runneth, but of God that sheweth mercy." Third, Owen observed that if election is based on foreseen faith, then faith itself must have some origin other than the electing grace of God — which leads to the untenable position that fallen man, dead in sins, can produce saving faith from his own corrupt nature.

Owen's positive teaching on election is rooted firmly in the Westminster Confession of Faith, Chapter 3, which declares: "By the decree of God, for the manifestation of His glory, some men and angels are predestinated unto everlasting life; and others foreordained to everlasting death. These angels and men, thus predestinated, and foreordained, are particularly and unchangeably designed, and their number so certain and definite, that it cannot be either increased or diminished." Owen expounded this confession with characteristic rigor, showing from Scripture that election is particular (it pertains to specific individuals, not to a vague class of believers), eternal (it was decreed before creation), unconditional (it rests on no foreseen merit in the creature), immutable (it cannot be changed or reversed), and effectual (all the elect will certainly be brought to salvation).

Owen was particularly insightful on the relationship between election and the covenant of redemption — the eternal pact between the Father and the Son in which the Father gave a people to the Son, and the Son agreed to redeem them by His death. Owen wrote extensively on John 17, where Christ prays for "them which thou hast given me" and declares "thine they were, and thou gavest them me." For Owen, the elect were the Father's gift to the Son, and the Son's death was the price paid to secure their redemption. This gives election a deeply Trinitarian and covenantal character — it is not a bare decree but an expression of eternal love within the Godhead.

Owen also addressed the pastoral implications of election with great sensitivity. He knew that many Christians were troubled by this doctrine, fearing that they might not be among the elect. Owen's counsel was characteristically biblical: he pointed such anxious souls not to the secret decree of God (which we cannot see) but to the revealed promises of God (which we can). Christ said, "Him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out" (John 6:37). Owen argued that if you desire to come to Christ, that very desire is itself evidence that the Father is drawing you. The elect are not identified by some mystical mark visible only to God — they are identified by their coming to Christ in faith and repentance. If you are coming, you are called. If you are called, you are chosen.

Owen further taught that the doctrine of election, far from producing spiritual laziness or presumption, is actually the greatest motivation for holiness. Ephesians 1:4 states that we were chosen "that we should be holy and without blame before him." Election is unto holiness. Those who claim to be elect while living in unrepentant sin have no biblical ground for their claim. The true elect are known by their fruits — by their love for God, their obedience to His commandments, their hatred of sin, and their growth in grace.

The Westminster Confession also addresses the delicate question of reprobation — the doctrine that God has not elected all persons to salvation. The Confession states this with great care, affirming that God, "for the manifestation of His glory, and for the good of His elect," has passed by others and "ordained them to dishonour and wrath for their sin." Owen was careful to maintain the biblical balance on this point. He insisted that while election is unconditional (God does not choose us because of any good in us), reprobation is judicial (God condemns sinners because of their sin). The symmetry is not exact: God is the active cause of salvation but sin is the active cause of damnation. No one will be in hell who does not deserve to be there because of their own sin. But no one will be in heaven who deserves to be there — all are there purely by grace.

Owen drew deeply from the well of God's sovereignty to provide comfort to suffering saints. In times of persecution, illness, bereavement, and spiritual darkness, the doctrine of election assures the believer that God's purposes cannot be thwarted. If God chose you before the foundation of the world, then no affliction in time can separate you from His love. If God predestined you to be conformed to the image of His Son, then every trial is a means to that glorious end. If God gave you to Christ as a gift, then Christ will never lose you. Owen pointed his readers to Romans 8:28-30, the golden chain of salvation: "For whom he did foreknow, he also did predestinate to be conformed to the image of his Son... Moreover whom he did predestinate, them he also called: and whom he called, them he also justified: and whom he justified, them he also glorified." Not a single link in that chain can be broken. Every person foreknown will be glorified. Not one will be lost.

Owen's treatment of election stands as one of the most thorough and pastorally sensitive in the history of Reformed theology. He maintained the full sovereignty of God without diminishing the responsibility of man. He upheld the mystery of the divine decree without retreating into speculation. And he applied the doctrine to the hearts of God's people with the warmth and tenderness of a true pastor, always pointing them from the deep things of God to the comforting arms of Christ.

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.

SEGMENT 4: DISCUSSION AND APPLICATION
----------------------------------------

Let us take time to discuss and apply what we have studied.

Discussion Question 1: Ephesians 1:4 says God chose us 'before the foundation of the world.' What does this timing tell us about the basis of God's choice?

Discussion Question 2: In Romans 9, Paul uses Jacob and Esau as an example of election. Why is it significant that God's choice was made before the twins were born or had done anything good or evil?

Discussion Question 3: John Owen distinguished between the Arminian view (election based on foreseen faith) and the Reformed view (unconditional election). What are the key differences, and why does it matter?

Discussion Question 4: How does the doctrine of election bring comfort to believers, especially in times of suffering and doubt? What specific promises support this comfort?

Discussion Question 5: If election is unconditional, why is it important that Ephesians 1:4 says we were chosen 'that we should be holy'? What does this tell us about the purpose of election?

Take time with each question. The Puritans valued what they called "holy conference," the practice of discussing spiritual truths together. Do not settle for quick, surface answers. Push deeper. Ask follow-up questions. Listen carefully to one another.

Application is where truth becomes life. It is not enough to know what Ephesians 1:4-6; Romans 9:11-16; 2 Timothy 1:9 says. We must ask: how does this change the way I live today? How does this shape my thoughts, my words, my actions? The Puritans insisted that a truth that does not transform is a truth that has not been truly understood.

SEGMENT 5: AFTERNOON ACTIVITY
----------------------------------------

Learning by doing was central to Puritan education. Here is today's hands-on activity.

Students will conduct a 'Scripture chain' study of election by creating a visual chain of linked paper strips, each containing a key verse about God's sovereign choice. Starting with Ephesians 1:4, students will trace the doctrine of election through at least 12 passages across both Testaments (including Deuteronomy 7:7-8, Psalm 65:4, Isaiah 43:1, John 6:37, 6:44, 10:16, 15:16, 17:9, Acts 13:48, Romans 8:29-30, 9:11-16, 2 Thessalonians 2:13). Each link should contain the verse reference on one side and a brief summary of what it teaches about election on the other. When completed, students will display their chain and explain how the passages build upon one another to present a unified biblical doctrine.

Materials needed: Strips of colored construction paper, markers, glue or stapler for linking strips, Bibles for looking up passages, printed verse list for reference, poster board for displaying the completed chain

As you complete this activity, think about how it connects to what you studied in today's lesson. The goal is not just to do something, but to reinforce the truths you have been learning. The Puritans believed that education should engage the whole person: mind, heart, and hands.

Take your time with this activity. Quality matters more than speed. When you are finished, reflect on what you learned through the process of doing. Often we understand something far better after we have tried to put it into practice.

SEGMENT 6: CROSS-SUBJECT CONNECTIONS
----------------------------------------

One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

The doctrine of election has been debated throughout church history. The fifth-century controversy between Augustine and Pelagius, the sixteenth-century Reformation debates, and the seventeenth-century Synod of Dort (1618-1619) all centered on questions of divine sovereignty and human will. The Synod of Dort, convened in the Netherlands, produced the five canons that became known as the 'Five Points of Calvinism,' of which unconditional election is the second. Study how political and cultural forces shaped these theological debates.

Literature Connection:

John Owen's writing style is famously dense and complex, often requiring multiple readings to fully comprehend. Analyze a paragraph of Owen's prose, identifying his use of subordinate clauses, biblical allusion, logical argumentation, and rhetorical questions. Compare his style with simpler Puritan writers like John Bunyan. Discuss how audience and purpose shape writing style: Owen wrote for trained theologians while Bunyan wrote for common people, yet both communicated the same essential truths.

Science Connection:

The concept of selection appears throughout the natural world. In selective breeding, farmers choose particular animals or plants with desired traits to reproduce, determining the characteristics of future generations. Compare and contrast natural selection (undirected, based on environmental fitness) with artificial selection (directed, based on a breeder's purpose) as analogies — however imperfect — for understanding the difference between a purposeless universe and a universe governed by a sovereign God who chooses according to His own wise counsel.

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.
