PURITAN GOLD — Episode 21
"Walking with God: The Puritan Daily Life"
Week 3, Day 7 (Sunday) — Devotional Episode — Proverbs 3:5-6

---

It's Sunday. The Lord's Day. And I thought that today, instead of working through another chapter of Proverbs verse by verse, we might do something a little different. I want to take you back in time. Back to England in the 1600s. Back to the homes and hearths and daily rhythms of the Puritans — those remarkable men and women who took their faith so seriously that it shaped not just their Sundays... but every single hour of every single day.

Because here's what strikes me about the Puritans. They didn't compartmentalize their faith. They didn't have a "spiritual life" and a "regular life" running on parallel tracks. For them, all of life was spiritual. Waking up was spiritual. Eating breakfast was spiritual. Going to work, tending the garden, educating children, greeting a neighbor, lying down to sleep at night — all of it was done in the conscious presence of God.

And I wonder... what would it look like if we lived that way?

Proverbs 3:5-6 gives us the foundation: "Trust in the LORD with all thine heart; and lean not unto thine own understanding. In all thy ways acknowledge him, and he shall direct thy paths."

In all thy ways. Not just your prayer times. Not just your church services. Not just the "religious" parts of your week. In all your ways — acknowledge Him. Recognize Him. Include Him. Consult Him. Worship Him. In everything.

That's what the Puritans tried to do. And while they were imperfect — as all people are — their attempt to structure daily life around devotion to God has left us a legacy of extraordinary wisdom. A legacy we desperately need to recover.

So let me walk you through what a day might have looked like for a devout Puritan household. And as I do, I want you to listen not just with historical curiosity, but with devotional attention. Ask yourself: what can I learn from this? What can I apply? What have I been missing?

The day began early. Very early. Before the household stirred, before the sun had fully risen, the head of the household — and often the wife as well — would be on their knees in what the Puritans called "secret devotion" or "closet prayer." This was private, personal communion with God. Just you and the Almighty, behind a closed door, with an open Bible and an open heart.

Richard Baxter, perhaps the most practical of all the Puritan pastors, gave detailed guidance about this morning devotion. He counseled believers to begin the day with three things: thanksgiving, self-examination, and prayer.

Thanksgiving first — because you've been kept through the night. You've been given another day. Before you think about your problems, your schedule, your anxieties... stop and thank God that you are alive. That His mercies are new this morning. That His faithfulness has not failed.

Then self-examination — a brief but honest assessment of the state of your soul. How did yesterday go? Where did you sin? Where did you grow? What temptations are likely to face you today? Baxter said this self-examination should be searching but not morbid — the goal is not to wallow in guilt but to bring your real condition before God so that He can address it.

And then prayer — deliberate, focused, unhurried prayer. Not the hurried, half-distracted prayers we so often offer, mumbled between checking our phones and pouring our coffee. Real prayer. The kind where you actually speak to God and then wait — genuinely wait — for the sense of His presence, His guidance, His peace.

Baxter said something that has haunted me ever since I first read it. He said: "Spend your time in nothing which you know must be repented of; in nothing on which you might not pray for the blessing of God; in nothing which you could not review with a quiet conscience on your dying bed."

Imagine starting every day with that filter. Before you plan your hours, before you open your calendar, before you dive into the rush — asking, "Can I ask God's blessing on everything I'm about to do today? Could I review this day with a quiet conscience on my deathbed?"

After private devotion came family worship. And this, I think, is one of the most beautiful and most neglected practices of the Puritan era.

The family would gather — parents, children, and often servants who lived in the household — for a time of worship together. This typically included the reading of Scripture, a brief exposition or discussion of the passage, singing of psalms, and prayer.

Now, this wasn't a formal church service. It wasn't stiff or ceremonial. It was a family gathered around the Word of God, learning together, praying together, beginning their day in shared communion with their Creator.

John Bunyan, despite his humble circumstances — he was a tinker by trade, a mender of pots and pans — was deeply committed to family worship. His home was a place of prayer and Scripture. And he wrote that one of the greatest gifts a parent can give a child is the daily habit of meeting God together as a family. Not just sending children to church, but leading them in worship at home. Letting them hear their father pray. Letting them see their mother weep over Scripture. Letting them know, from their earliest memories, that God is at the center of this household.

How many of us have let this practice slip? How many of us, in the busyness of modern life, have outsourced our children's spiritual formation entirely to the church — an hour or two on Sunday — and neglected the daily, consistent, cumulative power of family worship?

I don't say this to heap guilt on anyone. I say it because there is a treasure here that most of us have never picked up. And it's not too late to start.

After family worship, the Puritans went about their daily work. But even here, the consciousness of God's presence didn't disappear. The Puritans practiced what they called "ejaculatory prayers" — short, spontaneous prayers shot up to heaven throughout the day like arrows. Not long, formal prayers, but quick breathings of the soul: "Lord, help me in this task." "Father, give me patience with this person." "Jesus, keep me from this temptation." "Spirit, guide my words in this conversation."

This practice was rooted in the command of 1 Thessalonians 5:17 — "Pray without ceasing." The Puritans took that seriously. They understood that prayer is not just something you do at set times — it's a constant posture of dependence on God. It's the running conversation between a child and their Father that never fully stops, even in the midst of the busiest day.

And this is where Proverbs 3:5-6 comes alive. "In all thy ways acknowledge him." All. Not just the sacred moments. The mundane ones too. Acknowledge Him when you're washing dishes. Acknowledge Him when you're stuck in a difficult meeting. Acknowledge Him when you're driving your children to school. Acknowledge Him when you're lying awake at three in the morning with worry pressing down on your chest.

He is present in all of it. And He promises that when you acknowledge Him in all your ways... He will direct your paths. He will guide you. Not always with dramatic signs or audible voices. But with a quiet, steady, providential hand that steers your life toward His good purposes.

The Puritans also practiced a midday pause. In the middle of the day — often around the noon meal — many Puritan households would briefly stop and give thanks. Not just a quick grace before eating, but a moment of genuine reflection. How has the morning gone? Have I walked with God? Have I been faithful? Is there a sin I need to confess before the day goes further?

This practice of punctuating the day with moments of spiritual awareness is something we've largely lost. Our days run in a single unbroken stream of activity — from alarm clock to bedtime, one thing after another, with barely a moment to breathe, let alone pray. The Puritans would say that's exactly the problem. A day without spiritual pauses is a day that runs on auto-pilot. And auto-pilot, in a fallen world, will always drift toward the flesh.

Then came evening. And the evening, for the Puritans, was perhaps the most spiritually rich part of the day.

Family worship would often be repeated in the evening — another gathering around Scripture, psalms, and prayer. Children would be catechized — asked questions from a catechism to help them understand the foundations of the faith. Parents would discuss the events of the day in light of Scripture. The family would pray together for specific needs, for the church, for the nation, for the world.

And then, before sleep, came what the Puritans called "the examination of conscience." This was a private exercise — a time of looking back over the day with unflinching honesty, asking:

Where did I sin today? In thought, word, or deed?
Where did I see God's grace today? In provision, in protection, in guidance?
Where was I tempted, and how did I respond?
Is there anyone I need to forgive? Anyone I need to seek forgiveness from?
What am I grateful for today?

This evening examination was not a morbid exercise in self-loathing. It was, as Baxter described it, "a settling of accounts with God" — an honest reckoning of the day that allows you to go to sleep with a clear conscience and a heart at peace. Because you've confessed what needs confessing. You've thanked God for what needs thanking. And you've released the day — with all its failures and all its graces — into His sovereign hands.

Baxter's own daily routine was legendary. Despite chronic illness that plagued him for most of his adult life, he maintained a rigorous schedule of devotion. He rose early for prayer and study. He preached, counseled, and visited his congregation throughout the day. He wrote prodigiously — his collected works fill over twenty volumes. And yet he always made time for the inward work of the soul — the examination, the confession, the communion with God that sustained everything else.

When asked how he accomplished so much while being so often ill, Baxter reportedly said something to the effect that he simply tried to live every day as if it might be his last. Not with panic, but with purpose. Not with anxiety, but with attention. Every day a gift. Every hour an offering. Every task done as unto the Lord.

Now, I know what some of you might be thinking. "That sounds beautiful, but it's not realistic for my life. I have a demanding job. I have young children. I barely have time to eat breakfast, let alone conduct a morning worship service."

I understand. Truly, I do. And I'm not suggesting you replicate a seventeenth-century schedule in a twenty-first-century life. The forms may need to change. The principles don't.

The principle is this: structure your life around God, rather than trying to fit God into the margins of your life.

Even five minutes of genuine prayer in the morning is better than no prayer at all. Even one Bible verse read aloud with your family at dinner is better than no Scripture in the home. Even a thirty-second pause at midday to whisper, "Lord, I need You" is better than barreling through your hours without a thought of God.

Start small. Be consistent. And watch what God does.

Because here's what the Puritans discovered, and what countless believers throughout history have confirmed: when you orient your day around God — when you begin with Him, walk with Him, pause for Him, and end with Him — everything else falls into place. Not perfectly. Not without struggle. But with a sense of direction, of purpose, of peace that the world cannot give and cannot take away.

"Trust in the LORD with all thine heart; and lean not unto thine own understanding. In all thy ways acknowledge him, and he shall direct thy paths."

That's the promise. That's the invitation. That's the Puritan vision of daily life — not a grim, joyless regiment, but a life saturated with the presence and the love of God. From the moment your eyes open in the morning to the moment they close at night.

This has been our third week together in Puritan Gold. We've explored the fear of the Lord, the mortification of sin, the contrasts between the righteous and the wicked, the art of contentment, the value of integrity, the way of the righteous, and now... the daily rhythm of walking with God.

I pray that these truths have taken root in your heart. I pray that they're not just words you've heard, but seeds that are beginning to sprout.

And as we close this week, let me leave you with a Puritan benediction — a blessing that was often spoken at the close of a Lord's Day gathering.

May the Lord bless thee and keep thee. May the fear of the Lord be upon thee in all thy ways. May His Word be a lamp unto thy feet and a light unto thy path. May thy days begin with praise and end with gratitude. May thy work be faithful, thy words be truthful, and thy heart be full of Christ. And may the God of all grace, who has called thee unto His eternal glory, establish, strengthen, and settle thee — now and forevermore.

In the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit. Amen.
