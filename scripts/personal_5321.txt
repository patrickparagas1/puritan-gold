PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Guided Puritan Reading, Day 21
"The Valley of Vision"
Puritan Prayers and Devotions — Learning to Pray from the Saints

---

Good evening, friend. Tonight we open one of the most beloved devotional collections in the Reformed tradition: "The Valley of Vision," a treasury of Puritan prayers and devotions compiled by Arthur Bennett. These prayers, drawn from the private writings of men like Isaac Watts, David Brainerd, Charles Spurgeon, and many anonymous Puritan saints, give us a window into the inner spiritual life of those who walked most closely with God. Let us learn to pray as they prayed.

--- Scripture ---

Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Psalm 42:1-2:

"As the hart panteth after the water brooks, so panteth my soul after thee, O God. My soul thirsteth for God, for the living God: when shall I come and appear before God?"

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.

--- Guided Reading ---

The title prayer of this collection, "The Valley of Vision," contains one of the most striking paradoxes in all of Christian devotion. It begins with the words: "Lord, high and holy, meek and lowly." Already we see the Puritan instinct at work — holding together truths that seem to be in tension. God is both exalted beyond comprehension and gentle beyond expectation. The prayer goes on to express a theme that runs through the entire collection: that the deepest spiritual sight comes not on the mountaintop of human achievement but in the valley of humiliation and dependence.

What makes this collection so powerful is its unflinching honesty. These prayers do not pretend that the Christian life is easy. They confess sin with a specificity that makes modern ears uncomfortable. They acknowledge weakness, doubt, wandering affections, and spiritual coldness. And yet they do so in the context of absolute confidence in the mercy of God through Christ. This is the Puritan genius: the ability to hold together the deepest conviction of sin and the highest assurance of grace without letting go of either.

Consider how these prayers handle the theme of repentance. Where modern prayers might offer a vague confession — "forgive us our sins" — the Puritan prayers name the sin and trace it to its root. They confess not only the outward act but the inward disposition. They confess not only the sins of commission but the sins of omission. They confess not only the obvious transgressions but the subtle corruptions of the heart that only the Spirit can reveal. Thomas Watson taught that repentance must be particular: "He who mourns in general terms for sin does not truly mourn at all." The Valley of Vision prayers embody this counsel.

Yet for all their severity in self-examination, these prayers are saturated with joy. They overflow with praise for the attributes of God, the work of Christ, and the ministry of the Spirit. They celebrate the covenant of grace, the promises of Scripture, and the hope of glory. Richard Sibbes wrote that the bruised reed shall not be broken — and these prayers are written by bruised reeds who have found in Christ a tenderness that exceeds all their brokenness.

The prayers also teach us something about the structure of devotion. They typically begin with adoration, move to confession, proceed to thanksgiving, and conclude with supplication. This pattern — drawn from the Psalms and practiced by the Puritans with great discipline — shapes the heart even as the heart shapes the words. When we begin with who God is rather than with what we need, our entire orientation shifts. Our problems appear smaller. Our God appears greater. Our faith grows stronger.

John Owen once wrote that communion with God is the highest privilege of the Christian life. The prayers in "The Valley of Vision" are the record of men and women who pursued that communion with everything they had. They did not rush through their devotions. They lingered. They wrestled. They waited upon God until He met them. And when He did, they wrote down what happened — not for publication but for their own souls. That we now have access to these private conversations with God is a gift of extraordinary value.

--- Application ---

First, choose one prayer from "The Valley of Vision" and pray it slowly each morning this week. Do not rush through it. Let each phrase settle into your heart before moving to the next. Allow the words of these saints to teach your own lips how to pray.

Second, practice the Puritan pattern of specific confession. Rather than offering God a general apology for sin, name one particular sin each evening and bring it honestly before Him. Confess not only the act but the heart behind it, and then rest in the promise of 1 John 1:9.

Third, begin your prayers with adoration before supplication. Spend the first minutes of your prayer time simply telling God who He is — His holiness, His faithfulness, His mercy — before you bring Him your requests.

--- Closing Prayer ---

O Lord, Thou hast given us the prayers of the saints as a guide for our own halting words. Teach us to pray with honesty and with hope, with deep conviction of sin and deeper confidence in Thy grace. May our prayer closets become valleys of vision, where in the low place of humility we behold the high glory of Thy love. Through Christ our Mediator, Amen.

Rest well tonight, friend. The God who hears prayer watches over you as you sleep.
