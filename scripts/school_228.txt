SEGMENT 2: MAIN LESSON PART 1 -- BIBLICAL FOUNDATION
----------------------------------------

Now let us turn to our main lesson for today.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes in Acts 5:29; Proverbs 28:1; Daniel 3:16-18.

The Scriptures consistently teach that the fear of God is the cure for the fear of man, and that faithfulness to God must take precedence over obedience to any human authority when the two come into conflict. The Bible is filled with examples of men and women who stood boldly before kings and rulers, not because they were naturally fearless, but because they feared God more than they feared any earthly power. This pattern of holy boldness is one of the most striking and inspiring themes in all of Scripture.

In Acts 5:29, Peter and the apostles give what may be the most succinct statement of this principle in the entire Bible: "We ought to obey God rather than men." The context is critical. The apostles had been arrested, imprisoned, and brought before the Sanhedrin — the highest religious court in Israel — and commanded to stop preaching in the name of Jesus. The Sanhedrin had the power to imprison, to flog, and to put to death. Yet Peter, who had once denied Christ out of fear of a servant girl, now stood before the most powerful men in the nation and declared that no human authority can override the command of God. When God says "Go" and man says "Stop," the servant of God must go.

This does not mean that Christians are rebels against all authority. The New Testament consistently teaches submission to legitimate authority — to governments (Romans 13:1-7), to masters (Ephesians 6:5-8), to church leaders (Hebrews 13:17). But this submission has a limit: when human authority commands what God forbids or forbids what God commands, the Christian's allegiance to God must prevail. This is not anarchy — it is the recognition that all human authority is delegated authority, derived from and accountable to the sovereign God.

Proverbs 28:1 draws a sharp contrast between the wicked and the righteous: "The wicked flee when no man pursueth: but the righteous are bold as a lion." Guilt produces cowardice. A man who knows he is living in rebellion against God will always be looking over his shoulder, afraid of consequences, afraid of exposure, afraid of judgment. But a man whose conscience is clean before God — who knows that he is doing what is right — has a boldness that nothing can shake. This is not the bravado of self-confidence but the quiet, unshakeable courage that comes from knowing that God is on your side.

The book of Daniel provides some of the most dramatic examples of holy boldness in the entire Bible. In Daniel 3:16-18, Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego face a test that would terrify the bravest: Nebuchadnezzar, the most powerful monarch on earth, has commanded them to worship his golden image or be thrown into a furnace of fire. Their response is one of the most courageous statements in Scripture: "O Nebuchadnezzar, we are not careful to answer thee in this matter." The phrase "not careful" means they did not need to deliberate or hesitate. Their answer was settled before the question was asked because their allegiance to God was absolute.

Their next words reveal the theological foundation of their courage: "If it be so, our God whom we serve is able to deliver us from the burning fiery furnace, and he will deliver us out of thine hand, O king. But if not, be it known unto thee, O king, that we will not serve thy gods." They did not presume upon a miracle. They acknowledged that God is able to deliver, but they did not demand that He must deliver. Their obedience was unconditional — not contingent upon a favorable outcome. Even if God did not save them from the flames, they would not bow. This is the supreme expression of faith: trusting God regardless of consequences, obeying God without requiring escape from suffering.

Daniel himself demonstrated the same boldness when King Darius signed a decree forbidding prayer to any god except the king for thirty days. Daniel "went into his house; and his windows being open in his chamber toward Jerusalem, he kneeled upon his knees three times a day, and prayed, and gave thanks before his God, as he did aforetime" (Daniel 6:10). He did not hide his devotion. He did not close his windows or pray silently. He continued his practice of prayer openly, knowing full well that it meant the lions' den. Daniel's boldness was not recklessness — it was the settled conviction that obedience to God is more important than preservation of life.

Moses stood before Pharaoh and demanded, "Let my people go" — repeatedly, in the face of the most powerful ruler of the ancient world (Exodus 5:1). Elijah confronted King Ahab with the words, "Thou art he that troubleth Israel" — accusing the king to his face (1 Kings 18:17-18). Nathan the prophet pointed his finger at King David and declared, "Thou art the man" — confronting the king with his adultery and murder (2 Samuel 12:7). Jeremiah preached against the sins of Judah's kings even when they imprisoned him and threw him into a cistern (Jeremiah 38:6). John the Baptist rebuked Herod for his unlawful marriage, knowing it would cost him his head (Matthew 14:3-4).

In every case, the pattern is the same: a servant of God, compelled by loyalty to the truth, stands before human power and speaks the word of God without compromise. This boldness is not a personality trait — it is a spiritual gift produced by the Holy Spirit. Peter was naturally impulsive and cowardly. Moses initially protested his inability to speak. Jeremiah said he was too young. But when God filled them with His Spirit and His Word, they became lions before whom kings trembled.

The relevance of this biblical theme extends far beyond ancient history. In every generation, the people of God face pressure to compromise — to silence their convictions, to conform to cultural expectations, to bow before the idols of the age. The question that confronted the three Hebrews in Babylon confronts every believer: will you bow, or will you burn? The answer, for the faithful, is always the same: "We will not serve thy gods." The Lord of hosts is with us; the God of Jacob is our refuge. What can man do unto us?

This biblical foundation is essential. Without it, everything else we study today would be like a house built on sand. But with it, we stand on solid rock.

SEGMENT 3: MAIN LESSON PART 2 -- PURITAN TEACHING
----------------------------------------

Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. John Knox wrote extensively on this subject in "The History of the Reformation in Scotland." The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life.

John Knox (c. 1514-1572) was the thunderbolt of the Scottish Reformation — a man whose fearless preaching and uncompromising stand against both the Catholic Church and the Scottish crown transformed Scotland into a Protestant nation. His life embodied the biblical principle that the fear of God produces boldness before men, and his famous declaration that he feared God so much that he never feared the face of any man became the defining statement of his ministry.

Knox's early career brought him into the orbit of George Wishart, a Scottish reformer who was martyred by being burned at the stake in 1546. Wishart's death left a deep impression on Knox and ignited in him a fire for the Reformed faith that would never be extinguished. Shortly after Wishart's execution, Knox joined a group of Protestant sympathizers who had seized St. Andrews Castle. When the castle fell to French forces, Knox was captured and spent nineteen months as a galley slave on a French ship — chained to an oar, beaten, starved, and exposed to the elements. This brutal experience did not break Knox; it refined him. He emerged from captivity with an unbreakable resolve to preach the gospel regardless of the cost.

After his release, Knox spent time in England during the reign of Edward VI and then in exile on the continent when the Catholic Mary Tudor came to the English throne. During his time in Geneva, Knox came under the direct influence of John Calvin. He called Geneva "the most perfect school of Christ since the days of the apostles." Calvin's systematic theology and his vision of a city governed by the Word of God deeply shaped Knox's own vision for Scotland. When Knox returned to Scotland in 1559, he carried with him Calvin's theology and Calvin's passion for reform.

Knox's confrontations with Mary, Queen of Scots, are legendary. Mary was a Catholic queen ruling over an increasingly Protestant nation, and Knox was the most prominent voice of the Reformation. Their interviews were storms of conviction and defiance. Knox did not defer to the queen's rank or soften his message out of courtly politeness. He preached against the Mass as idolatry to the queen's face. When Mary wept during one of their meetings, Knox famously replied that he could better endure her tears than betray God's truth by his silence. When accused of treason for encouraging resistance to the queen's religious policies, Knox responded that to obey God is never treason.

Knox's boldness was rooted in a profound theology of the sovereignty of God and the authority of Scripture. He believed that God's Word, not the queen's word, was the supreme law of Scotland. He taught that civil magistrates are accountable to God and that when they command what God forbids, the people are not only permitted but obligated to resist. This was a radical doctrine in the sixteenth century, when the divine right of kings was widely accepted. Knox's "First Blast of the Trumpet Against the Monstrous Regiment of Women" (written against Mary Tudor, not Mary Queen of Scots) argued that female rule was contrary to Scripture — a position that was controversial even among his Protestant allies. But whatever one thinks of Knox's views on female monarchy, his underlying principle — that all human authority is subject to the authority of God — became foundational for Reformed political theology.

Knox's preaching was described by contemporaries as electric. One observer said that Knox's preaching "put more fire and life into people than five hundred trumpets blowing in their ears." Thomas Randolph, the English ambassador to Scotland, wrote that Knox was able "to put more life in us in one hour than five hundred trumpets continually blustering in our ears." Knox preached with the conviction that God's Word was a living sword that would accomplish its purpose regardless of human opposition. His sermons at St. Giles' Cathedral in Edinburgh drew enormous crowds and became the engine of Scotland's spiritual transformation.

The fruits of Knox's ministry were extraordinary. The Scottish Parliament adopted the Reformed faith in 1560, less than a year after Knox's return. The First Book of Discipline, which Knox helped compose, laid the foundation for a national system of education — from parish schools to universities — that was revolutionary in its scope. Knox envisioned a nation in which every person, rich or poor, could read the Bible for themselves. This vision was realized over the following generations, making Scotland one of the most literate nations in the world and producing a culture of theological depth and intellectual vigor that profoundly influenced the development of Western civilization.

Knox's legacy is inseparable from the Scottish Covenanting tradition that followed him. The Scottish Covenanters of the seventeenth century — men and women who pledged their lives to maintain the crown rights of King Jesus in His church — were Knox's spiritual descendants. When they stood before kings and judges and chose death rather than deny the lordship of Christ, they were walking the path Knox had blazed. The National Covenant of 1638 and the Solemn League and Covenant of 1643 were direct outgrowths of Knox's theology of the sovereignty of God over all human institutions.

Knox's influence on the Puritan movement in England was also significant. Many English Puritans had spent time in Geneva and Scotland during the Marian exile and had been shaped by Knox's theology and example. The Puritan insistence on the authority of Scripture over human tradition, the primacy of preaching, the need for church reform, and the duty of resistance to ungodly rulers all bear the stamp of Knox's teaching.

Knox died on November 24, 1572. At his funeral, the Regent of Scotland, the Earl of Morton, reportedly said: "Here lies one who never feared the face of man." It was the most fitting epitaph imaginable for a man whose entire life had been a demonstration of the truth that when the fear of God fills the heart, the fear of man loses its power. Knox was not fearless by nature — he was fearless by grace. And his example continues to inspire believers in every generation to choose faithfulness over safety, truth over popularity, and the approval of God over the applause of men.

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.

SEGMENT 4: DISCUSSION AND APPLICATION
----------------------------------------

Now let us pause and consider what we have learned. Discussion is where understanding deepens.

Discussion Question 1: Acts 5:29 says 'We ought to obey God rather than men.' In what specific situations might Christians today face a conflict between divine commands and human authority?

Discussion Question 2: The three Hebrews said 'But if not' — even if God does not deliver, we will not bow. How does this 'but if not' faith differ from a faith that only trusts God when things go well?

Discussion Question 3: John Knox said he feared God so much that he never feared the face of any man. What is the relationship between fearing God and being bold before people?

Discussion Question 4: Knox believed that civil rulers are accountable to God and can be resisted when they command what God forbids. What are the limits and safeguards of this principle? How do we prevent it from becoming mere rebellion?

Discussion Question 5: How did Knox's vision for education transform Scotland? What can we learn from his belief that every person should be able to read the Bible for themselves?

Take time with each question. The Puritans valued what they called "holy conference," the practice of discussing spiritual truths together. Do not settle for quick, surface answers. Push deeper. Ask follow-up questions. Listen carefully to one another.

Application is where truth becomes life. It is not enough to know what Acts 5:29; Proverbs 28:1; Daniel 3:16-18 says. We must ask: how does this change the way I live today? How does this shape my thoughts, my words, my actions? The Puritans insisted that a truth that does not transform is a truth that has not been truly understood.

SEGMENT 5: AFTERNOON ACTIVITY
----------------------------------------

Now we move to our afternoon activity, where we put truth into practice.

Students will research and present a 'Profiles in Courage' report on one of several biblical or historical figures who demonstrated boldness before rulers. Options include: Daniel before Darius, Shadrach/Meshach/Abednego before Nebuchadnezzar, Moses before Pharaoh, John Knox before Mary Queen of Scots, William Tyndale before Henry VIII's authorities, Dietrich Bonhoeffer before the Nazi regime, or Richard Wurmbrand before the Communist authorities. Each presentation should include: (1) the historical context, (2) the specific command or pressure to compromise, (3) the person's response and the theology behind it, (4) the consequences they faced, and (5) the lasting impact of their stand. Presentations should be 5-7 minutes with at least one visual aid.

Materials needed: Research materials on assigned historical figures, presentation template, poster board or slides for visual aids, timer for presentations, evaluation rubric, printed biographical sketches for students without internet access

As you complete this activity, think about how it connects to what you studied in today's lesson. The goal is not just to do something, but to reinforce the truths you have been learning. The Puritans believed that education should engage the whole person: mind, heart, and hands.

Take your time with this activity. Quality matters more than speed. When you are finished, reflect on what you learned through the process of doing. Often we understand something far better after we have tried to put it into practice.

SEGMENT 6: CROSS-SUBJECT CONNECTIONS
----------------------------------------

One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

The Scottish Reformation under Knox transformed Scotland's political, educational, and social structures. Knox's vision resulted in the First Book of Discipline (1560), which proposed a comprehensive national education system from parish schools to universities. Study how Scotland's commitment to universal education produced a disproportionate number of Enlightenment thinkers, scientists, and philosophers. Compare the Scottish Reformation with the English Reformation under Henry VIII — how did their different motivations (theological vs. political) produce different results?

Literature Connection:

Knox's writing style is characterized by passionate directness and prophetic urgency. Analyze excerpts from 'The History of the Reformation in Scotland,' noting his use of first-person narrative, dramatic dialogue, vivid description, and appeals to Scripture. Compare Knox's rhetorical style with that of the Old Testament prophets, particularly Elijah and Jeremiah. How does Knox consciously model his language and persona after the biblical prophets? Discuss the literary genre of historical-prophetic writing and its power to inspire courage.

Science Connection:

The Scottish Reformation's emphasis on universal education created a culture of intellectual inquiry that contributed significantly to the Scottish Enlightenment and the advancement of science. James Hutton (father of modern geology), Joseph Black (discoverer of latent heat), James Watt (improver of the steam engine), and Alexander Fleming (discoverer of penicillin) all emerged from the Scottish educational tradition that Knox helped establish. Discuss how a culture of literacy, disciplined thinking, and reverence for truth — originally motivated by the desire to read Scripture — can produce excellence in scientific inquiry.

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.
