Good morning, students. Welcome to the Puritan Academy. Let us begin this day as the Puritans did, by acknowledging God as the source of all knowledge and wisdom. Before we open our textbooks, let us open our hearts.

Almighty God, the fountain of all wisdom, we ask You to bless our studies today. Open our minds to understand, our hearts to receive, and our wills to obey the truth. Grant us diligence, humility, and a spirit of inquiry that honors You. Through Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.

Let us meditate on a portion of Scripture before we begin our main lesson.

Our Scripture for today is Acts 5:29; Proverbs 28:1; Daniel 3:16-18:

"Then Peter and the other apostles answered and said, We ought to obey God rather than men. The wicked flee when no man pursueth: but the righteous are bold as a lion. Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego, answered and said to the king, O Nebuchadnezzar, we are not careful to answer thee in this matter. If it be so, our God whom we serve is able to deliver us from the burning fiery furnace, and he will deliver us out of thine hand, O king. But if not, be it known unto thee, O king, that we will not serve thy gods, nor worship the golden image which thou hast set up."

Ask yourself honestly today: whose approval do you seek? Whose disapproval do you fear? The Scriptures tell us that the righteous are bold as a lion — not because of their own strength, but because of the God they serve. The same God who shut the mouths of lions for Daniel, who cooled the flames for the three Hebrews, and who empowered Knox to stand before queens — this God is your God. You do not need to fear any human being. You need only fear the Lord, and in that fear, you will find a courage that no threat can shake. Let the fear of the Lord so fill your heart today that every lesser fear is driven out.

As we prepare for today's lesson, remember that everything we study flows from this one fountain: the Word of God. Our lesson today is part of Heroes of the Reformation, and it is titled: John Knox — Boldness Before Kings. Let us proceed with minds engaged and hearts open.


Now let us turn to our main lesson for today.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes in Acts 5:29; Proverbs 28:1; Daniel 3:16-18.

The Scriptures consistently teach that the fear of God is the cure for the fear of man, and that faithfulness to God must take precedence over obedience to any human authority when the two come into conflict. The Bible is filled with examples of men and women who stood boldly before kings and rulers, not because they were naturally fearless, but because they feared God more than they feared any earthly power. This pattern of holy boldness is one of the most striking and inspiring themes in all of Scripture.

In Acts 5:29, Peter and the apostles give what may be the most succinct statement of this principle in the entire Bible: "We ought to obey God rather than men." The context is critical. The apostles had been arrested, imprisoned, and brought before the Sanhedrin — the highest religious court in Israel — and commanded to stop preaching in the name of Jesus. The Sanhedrin had the power to imprison, to flog, and to put to death. Yet Peter, who had once denied Christ out of fear of a servant girl, now stood before the most powerful men in the nation and declared that no human authority can override the command of God. When God says "Go" and man says "Stop," the servant of God must go.

This does not mean that Christians are rebels against all authority. The New Testament consistently teaches submission to legitimate authority — to governments (Romans 13:1-7), to masters (Ephesians 6:5-8), to church leaders (Hebrews 13:17). But this submission has a limit: when human authority commands what God forbids or forbids what God commands, the Christian's allegiance to God must prevail. This is not anarchy — it is the recognition that all human authority is delegated authority, derived from and accountable to the sovereign God.

Proverbs 28:1 draws a sharp contrast between the wicked and the righteous: "The wicked flee when no man pursueth: but the righteous are bold as a lion." Guilt produces cowardice. A man who knows he is living in rebellion against God will always be looking over his shoulder, afraid of consequences, afraid of exposure, afraid of judgment. But a man whose conscience is clean before God — who knows that he is doing what is right — has a boldness that nothing can shake. This is not the bravado of self-confidence but the quiet, unshakeable courage that comes from knowing that God is on your side.

The book of Daniel provides some of the most dramatic examples of holy boldness in the entire Bible. In Daniel 3:16-18, Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego face a test that would terrify the bravest: Nebuchadnezzar, the most powerful monarch on earth, has commanded them to worship his golden image or be thrown into a furnace of fire. Their response is one of the most courageous statements in Scripture: "O Nebuchadnezzar, we are not careful to answer thee in this matter." The phrase "not careful" means they did not need to deliberate or hesitate. Their answer was settled before the question was asked because their allegiance to God was absolute.

Their next words reveal the theological foundation of their courage: "If it be so, our God whom we serve is able to deliver us from the burning fiery furnace, and he will deliver us out of thine hand, O king. But if not, be it known unto thee, O king, that we will not serve thy gods." They did not presume upon a miracle. They acknowledged that God is able to deliver, but they did not demand that He must deliver. Their obedience was unconditional — not contingent upon a favorable outcome. Even if God did not save them from the flames, they would not bow. This is the supreme expression of faith: trusting God regardless of consequences, obeying God without requiring escape from suffering.

Daniel himself demonstrated the same boldness when King Darius signed a decree forbidding prayer to any god except the king for thirty days. Daniel "went into his house; and his windows being open in his chamber toward Jerusalem, he kneeled upon his knees three times a day, and prayed, and gave thanks before his God, as he did aforetime" (Daniel 6:10). He did not hide his devotion. He did not close his windows or pray silently. He continued his practice of prayer openly, knowing full well that it meant the lions' den. Daniel's boldness was not recklessness — it was the settled conviction that obedience to God is more important than preservation of life.

Moses stood before Pharaoh and demanded, "Let my people go" — repeatedly, in the face of the most powerful ruler of the ancient world (Exodus 5:1). Elijah confronted King Ahab with the words, "Thou art he that troubleth Israel" — accusing the king to his face (1 Kings 18:17-18). Nathan the prophet pointed his finger at King David and declared, "Thou art the man" — confronting the king with his adultery and murder (2 Samuel 12:7). Jeremiah preached against the sins of Judah's kings even when they imprisoned him and threw him into a cistern (Jeremiah 38:6). John the Baptist rebuked Herod for his unlawful marriage, knowing it would cost him his head (Matthew 14:3-4).

In every case, the pattern is the same: a servant of God, compelled by loyalty to the truth, stands before human power and speaks the word of God without compromise. This boldness is not a personality trait — it is a spiritual gift produced by the Holy Spirit. Peter was naturally impulsive and cowardly. Moses initially protested his inability to speak. Jeremiah said he was too young. But when God filled them with His Spirit and His Word, they became lions before whom kings trembled.

The relevance of this biblical theme extends far beyond ancient history. In every generation, the people of God face pressure to compromise — to silence their convictions, to conform to cultural expectations, to bow before the idols of the age. The question that confronted the three Hebrews in Babylon confronts every believer: will you bow, or will you burn? The answer, for the faithful, is always the same: "We will not serve thy gods." The Lord of hosts is with us; the God of Jacob is our refuge. What can man do unto us?

This biblical foundation is essential. Without it, everything else we study today would be like a house built on sand. But with it, we stand on solid rock.


Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. John Knox wrote extensively on this subject in "The History of the Reformation in Scotland." The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life.

John Knox (c. 1514-1572) was the thunderbolt of the Scottish Reformation — a man whose fearless preaching and uncompromising stand against both the Catholic Church and the Scottish crown transformed Scotland into a Protestant nation. His life embodied the biblical principle that the fear of God produces boldness before men, and his famous declaration that he feared God so much that he never feared the face of any man became the defining statement of his ministry.

Knox's early career brought him into the orbit of George Wishart, a Scottish reformer who was martyred by being burned at the stake in 1546. Wishart's death left a deep impression on Knox and ignited in him a fire for the Reformed faith that would never be extinguished. Shortly after Wishart's execution, Knox joined a group of Protestant sympathizers who had seized St. Andrews Castle. When the castle fell to French forces, Knox was captured and spent nineteen months as a galley slave on a French ship — chained to an oar, beaten, starved, and exposed to the elements. This brutal experience did not break Knox; it refined him. He emerged from captivity with an unbreakable resolve to preach the gospel regardless of the cost.

After his release, Knox spent time in England during the reign of Edward VI and then in exile on the continent when the Catholic Mary Tudor came to the English throne. During his time in Geneva, Knox came under the direct influence of John Calvin. He called Geneva "the most perfect school of Christ since the days of the apostles." Calvin's systematic theology and his vision of a city governed by the Word of God deeply shaped Knox's own vision for Scotland. When Knox returned to Scotland in 1559, he carried with him Calvin's theology and Calvin's passion for reform.

Knox's confrontations with Mary, Queen of Scots, are legendary. Mary was a Catholic queen ruling over an increasingly Protestant nation, and Knox was the most prominent voice of the Reformation. Their interviews were storms of conviction and defiance. Knox did not defer to the queen's rank or soften his message out of courtly politeness. He preached against the Mass as idolatry to the queen's face. When Mary wept during one of their meetings, Knox famously replied that he could better endure her tears than betray God's truth by his silence. When accused of treason for encouraging resistance to the queen's religious policies, Knox responded that to obey God is never treason.

Knox's boldness was rooted in a profound theology of the sovereignty of God and the authority of Scripture. He believed that God's Word, not the queen's word, was the supreme law of Scotland. He taught that civil magistrates are accountable to God and that when they command what God forbids, the people are not only permitted but obligated to resist. This was a radical doctrine in the sixteenth century, when the divine right of kings was widely accepted. Knox's "First Blast of the Trumpet Against the Monstrous Regiment of Women" (written against Mary Tudor, not Mary Queen of Scots) argued that female rule was contrary to Scripture — a position that was controversial even among his Protestant allies. But whatever one thinks of Knox's views on female monarchy, his underlying principle — that all human authority is subject to the authority of God — became foundational for Reformed political theology.

Knox's preaching was described by contemporaries as electric. One observer said that Knox's preaching "put more fire and life into people than five hundred trumpets blowing in their ears." Thomas Randolph, the English ambassador to Scotland, wrote that Knox was able "to put more life in us in one hour than five hundred trumpets continually blustering in our ears." Knox preached with the conviction that God's Word was a living sword that would accomplish its purpose regardless of human opposition. His sermons at St. Giles' Cathedral in Edinburgh drew enormous crowds and became the engine of Scotland's spiritual transformation.

The fruits of Knox's ministry were extraordinary. The Scottish Parliament adopted the Reformed faith in 1560, less than a year after Knox's return. The First Book of Discipline, which Knox helped compose, laid the foundation for a national system of education — from parish schools to universities — that was revolutionary in its scope. Knox envisioned a nation in which every person, rich or poor, could read the Bible for themselves. This vision was realized over the following generations, making Scotland one of the most literate nations in the world and producing a culture of theological depth and intellectual vigor that profoundly influenced the development of Western civilization.

Knox's legacy is inseparable from the Scottish Covenanting tradition that followed him. The Scottish Covenanters of the seventeenth century — men and women who pledged their lives to maintain the crown rights of King Jesus in His church — were Knox's spiritual descendants. When they stood before kings and judges and chose death rather than deny the lordship of Christ, they were walking the path Knox had blazed. The National Covenant of 1638 and the Solemn League and Covenant of 1643 were direct outgrowths of Knox's theology of the sovereignty of God over all human institutions.

Knox's influence on the Puritan movement in England was also significant. Many English Puritans had spent time in Geneva and Scotland during the Marian exile and had been shaped by Knox's theology and example. The Puritan insistence on the authority of Scripture over human tradition, the primacy of preaching, the need for church reform, and the duty of resistance to ungodly rulers all bear the stamp of Knox's teaching.

Knox died on November 24, 1572. At his funeral, the Regent of Scotland, the Earl of Morton, reportedly said: "Here lies one who never feared the face of man." It was the most fitting epitaph imaginable for a man whose entire life had been a demonstration of the truth that when the fear of God fills the heart, the fear of man loses its power. Knox was not fearless by nature — he was fearless by grace. And his example continues to inspire believers in every generation to choose faithfulness over safety, truth over popularity, and the approval of God over the applause of men.

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.


Let us now press deeper into the theological foundations of holy boldness by examining additional Scripture passages and drawing from the broader Puritan tradition.

The prophet Isaiah provides a foundational text for understanding the relationship between the fear of God and courage before men. In Isaiah 8:12-13, the Lord speaks to the prophet: "Say ye not, A confederacy, to all them to whom this people shall say, A confederacy; neither fear ye their fear, nor be afraid. Sanctify the LORD of hosts himself; and let him be your fear, and let him be your dread." The command is clear: do not fear what the world fears. Do not share in the anxieties, the panic, the dread that grip those who do not know God. Instead, sanctify the Lord — set Him apart in your heart as the one supreme object of reverence and awe. When the Lord of hosts is your fear and your dread, every other fear is displaced. You cannot fear God supremely and fear man simultaneously. The greater fear swallows the lesser, just as the ocean swallows a raindrop.

This principle is reinforced by our Lord Jesus Christ in one of His most pointed teachings. In Matthew 10:28, He instructs His disciples: "And fear not them which kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul: but rather fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body in hell." The worst that any human power can do is kill the body — and even this is temporary, for God will raise the body in glory on the last day. But God has power over both soul and body, both time and eternity. When we measure the power of human threats against the power of God, the comparison is so absurd as to make human fear irrational. The tyrant can take your life, but he cannot take your soul. He can burn your body, but he cannot touch your inheritance. He can silence your voice for a moment, but he cannot prevent the Word of God from running free.

The Psalmist expresses this confidence magnificently in Psalm 27:1: "The LORD is my light and my salvation; whom shall I fear? the LORD is the strength of my life; of whom shall I be afraid?" And again in Psalm 118:6: "The LORD is on my side; I will not fear: what can man do unto me?" These are not expressions of naive optimism or reckless bravery. They are sober, rational conclusions drawn from the character of God. If God is your light, darkness cannot overcome you. If God is your salvation, no enemy can destroy you. If God is your strength, no opposition can defeat you. The logic is irresistible: if God be for us, who can be against us?

The Puritan divine John Owen, in his treatise on the glory of Christ, connects holy boldness to the believer's union with the risen and ascended Lord. Owen argues that the Christian who is united to Christ by faith shares in Christ's victory over every power and principality. Christ has conquered sin, death, Satan, and every earthly ruler who sets himself against the purposes of God. The believer who is "in Christ" is therefore already on the winning side of every conflict. Owen writes that this is the foundation of all true Christian courage: not our own strength, not our own wisdom, not our own eloquence, but our union with the One who has already triumphed and who now reigns at the right hand of the Father.

The Puritan pastor Richard Sibbes, in his beloved work "The Bruised Reed," provides a complementary perspective on boldness. Sibbes observes that genuine boldness before men is always accompanied by genuine humility before God. The truly bold servant of God is not proud, self-confident, or arrogant — he is deeply aware of his own weakness and utterly dependent on divine strength. It is precisely because he knows his own insufficiency that he relies on God's sufficiency, and it is precisely because he relies on God's sufficiency that he is bold. Sibbes writes that the boldest saints in Scripture were also the humblest — Moses, who was the meekest man on earth, was also the man who confronted Pharaoh. Daniel, who prayed three times daily on his knees, was also the man who stood unflinching before Nebuchadnezzar. Paul, who called himself the chief of sinners, was also the man who preached Christ before kings and governors without fear.

Thomas Brooks, in "Precious Remedies Against Satan's Devices," warns that one of the devil's most effective strategies is to use the fear of man to silence the testimony of God's people. Brooks writes that Satan knows he cannot defeat the truth, so he attempts to prevent the truth from being spoken. He does this by magnifying the power and hostility of human opponents, by exaggerating the consequences of faithfulness, and by whispering that discretion is the better part of valor — that it is wise to remain silent, that the cause will survive without your particular witness, that someone else will speak up if you do not. Brooks counsels believers to resist these lies by reminding themselves that God has commanded them to speak, that God is sovereign over all consequences, and that faithfulness to God's calling is always the path of true wisdom, whatever the immediate cost may be.

The apostle Paul's example in the book of Acts powerfully illustrates the theology of holy boldness. When Paul stood before the Roman governor Felix, he "reasoned of righteousness, temperance, and judgment to come" until Felix trembled (Acts 24:25). When he stood before King Agrippa, he declared the gospel with such power that Agrippa said, "Almost thou persuadest me to be a Christian" (Acts 26:28). When he stood before Caesar's household in Rome, he preached Christ so effectively that there were "saints in Caesar's household" (Philippians 4:22). In every case, Paul did not trim his message to suit his audience, did not water down the gospel to avoid offense, and did not calculate the political consequences of his preaching. He spoke the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth — and he left the results to God.

The Puritan pastor and theologian Richard Baxter, in his monumental work "The Reformed Pastor," urges ministers to cultivate holy boldness in their preaching and pastoral work. Baxter writes that a pastor who fears the displeasure of his congregation more than the displeasure of God will inevitably compromise his message, soften his reproofs, and fail to declare the whole counsel of God. Baxter insists that faithful pastoral ministry requires a willingness to speak hard truths to powerful people, to confront sin even when it is costly, and to preach repentance even when the congregation would prefer comfort. He argues that this boldness is not rudeness or insensitivity but the highest form of love — for the pastor who withholds the truth from his people out of fear of their disapproval is not loving them but abandoning them to their sin.

Consider also the stirring words of Hebrews 11:32-38, which catalogs the extraordinary courage of the saints who have gone before us: "And what shall I more say? for the time would fail me to tell of Gedeon, and of Barak, and of Samson, and of Jephthae; of David also, and Samuel, and of the prophets: Who through faith subdued kingdoms, wrought righteousness, obtained promises, stopped the mouths of lions, Quenched the violence of fire, escaped the edge of the sword, out of weakness were made strong, waxed valiant in fight, turned to flight the armies of the aliens." Notice the phrase "out of weakness were made strong." These heroes of faith were not naturally courageous — they were naturally weak. But God made them strong. Their boldness was not a human achievement but a divine gift, produced by faith in a sovereign, faithful, almighty God.

The same chapter goes on to describe those who suffered for their faithfulness: "And others had trial of cruel mockings and scourgings, yea, moreover of bonds and imprisonment: They were stoned, they were sawn asunder, were tempted, were slain with the sword: they wandered about in sheepskins and goatskins; being destitute, afflicted, tormented; (Of whom the world was not worthy:) they wandered in deserts, and in mountains, and in dens and caves of the earth" (Hebrews 11:36-38). The parenthetical phrase is devastating: "of whom the world was not worthy." The world judged them as outcasts, fugitives, and criminals. But heaven judged them as heroes, and their names are written in the Lamb's book of life. Knox stood in this lineage. His boldness was not the bravado of a man who did not understand the danger but the settled courage of a man who understood that the approval of God outweighs the disapproval of every earthly power combined.

Jonathan Edwards, in his "Religious Affections," draws a distinction between two kinds of boldness. The first is natural boldness — a temperamental fearlessness that some people possess by nature. This kind of boldness is morally neutral and may be found in the wicked as well as the righteous. The second is spiritual boldness — a courage that is produced by the Holy Spirit in those who have a clear sense of divine truth and a deep conviction of God's sovereign power. Edwards argues that spiritual boldness is always accompanied by certain marks: a clear conscience, a willingness to suffer for the truth, a calm demeanor even in the face of danger, and an absence of personal malice toward those who oppose. Knox's boldness bore all of these marks. He was not angry at Mary — he was zealous for God's honor. He was not seeking political power — he was seeking the advancement of the gospel. He was not motivated by personal ambition — he was compelled by divine calling.


Before we move to our discussion questions, let us apply these truths to our daily lives.

First, the fear of God must be cultivated through daily communion with Him. You will not develop boldness in the moment of crisis if you have not been developing intimacy with God in the moments of quiet. Knox's boldness before Mary was the fruit of years of prayer, years of Bible study, years of preaching, and years of suffering that had deepened his knowledge of God and strengthened his confidence in God's sovereignty. The Puritan Thomas Watson writes, "The fear of God is the death of all other fears; like a fire that draws up all vapors, the fear of God draws up all other fears into itself." Cultivate the fear of God daily, and you will find that lesser fears lose their grip on your heart.

Second, meditate regularly on the character and promises of God. The reason we fear men is that we have a small view of God. When God fills the horizon of our vision, human threats shrink to their proper insignificance. Meditate on God's omnipotence — He holds all power in heaven and earth. Meditate on His omniscience — He knows every circumstance and every outcome. Meditate on His sovereignty — He governs every event according to His own wise and loving purposes. Meditate on His faithfulness — He has never broken a promise and never will. The more you know of God, the less you will fear of men.

Third, resolve in advance what you will do when the test comes. The three Hebrews did not wait until they stood before the furnace to decide whether they would bow. Their decision was made before the crisis arrived. Owen counsels believers to settle their convictions in times of peace so that they are prepared to act on them in times of conflict. Decide now what truths you will not compromise. Decide now what commands you will obey regardless of the cost. Decide now that when human authority conflicts with divine authority, you will obey God rather than men. If you wait until the pressure is on to make these decisions, you are far more likely to compromise.

Fourth, seek the company of faithful believers who will encourage your boldness and hold you accountable to your convictions. Knox did not stand alone — he was part of a community of Reformed believers who strengthened one another's faith and shared one another's burdens. The writer of Hebrews commands, "And let us consider one another to provoke unto love and to good works: Not forsaking the assembling of ourselves together, as the manner of some is; but exhorting one another: and so much the more, as ye see the day approaching" (Hebrews 10:24-25). Holy boldness is cultivated in community, not in isolation. Find brothers and sisters in Christ who share your commitment to the truth, and draw strength from their example and encouragement.

Fifth, remember that the fear of man is itself a form of idolatry. When we fear human disapproval more than divine displeasure, we are placing human opinion on the throne that belongs to God alone. Proverbs 29:25 warns, "The fear of man bringeth a snare: but whoso putteth his trust in the LORD shall be safe." The fear of man is a trap — it promises safety through compromise but delivers bondage through cowardice. Only the fear of the Lord brings true safety, because only the Lord has the power to keep every promise and fulfill every threat. Brooks writes that "the fear of man will make a man fall into that which he most fears; but the fear of God will keep a man from falling into that which he most fears." Let the fear of God set you free from the fear of man.

Discussion Question 1: Acts 5:29 says 'We ought to obey God rather than men.' In what specific situations might Christians today face a conflict between divine commands and human authority?

Discussion Question 2: The three Hebrews said 'But if not' — even if God does not deliver, we will not bow. How does this 'but if not' faith differ from a faith that only trusts God when things go well?

Discussion Question 3: John Knox said he feared God so much that he never feared the face of any man. What is the relationship between fearing God and being bold before people?

Discussion Question 4: Knox believed that civil rulers are accountable to God and can be resisted when they command what God forbids. What are the limits and safeguards of this principle? How do we prevent it from becoming mere rebellion?

Discussion Question 5: How did Knox's vision for education transform Scotland? What can we learn from his belief that every person should be able to read the Bible for themselves?

Take time with each question. The Puritans valued what they called "holy conference," the practice of discussing spiritual truths together. Do not settle for quick, surface answers. Push deeper. Ask follow-up questions. Listen carefully to one another.

Application is where truth becomes life. It is not enough to know what Acts 5:29; Proverbs 28:1; Daniel 3:16-18 says. We must ask: how does this change the way I live today? How does this shape my thoughts, my words, my actions? The Puritans insisted that a truth that does not transform is a truth that has not been truly understood.

One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

The Scottish Reformation under Knox transformed Scotland's political, educational, and social structures. Knox's vision resulted in the First Book of Discipline (1560), which proposed a comprehensive national education system from parish schools to universities. Study how Scotland's commitment to universal education produced a disproportionate number of Enlightenment thinkers, scientists, and philosophers. Compare the Scottish Reformation with the English Reformation under Henry VIII — how did their different motivations (theological vs. political) produce different results?

Literature Connection:

Knox's writing style is characterized by passionate directness and prophetic urgency. Analyze excerpts from 'The History of the Reformation in Scotland,' noting his use of first-person narrative, dramatic dialogue, vivid description, and appeals to Scripture. Compare Knox's rhetorical style with that of the Old Testament prophets, particularly Elijah and Jeremiah. How does Knox consciously model his language and persona after the biblical prophets? Discuss the literary genre of historical-prophetic writing and its power to inspire courage.

Science Connection:

The Scottish Reformation's emphasis on universal education created a culture of intellectual inquiry that contributed significantly to the Scottish Enlightenment and the advancement of science. James Hutton (father of modern geology), Joseph Black (discoverer of latent heat), James Watt (improver of the steam engine), and Alexander Fleming (discoverer of penicillin) all emerged from the Scottish educational tradition that Knox helped establish. Discuss how a culture of literacy, disciplined thinking, and reverence for truth — originally motivated by the desire to read Scripture — can produce excellence in scientific inquiry.

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.

Memory Verse:

"We ought to obey God rather than men." -- Acts 5:29

Read this verse aloud five times. Then close your eyes and try to recite it from memory. Write it out by hand on a card or in your journal. The Puritans memorized vast portions of Scripture, and it was one of the great sources of their spiritual strength. Even one verse, truly hidden in the heart, can sustain you in times of trial.

Additional Memory Verse:

"The wicked flee when no man pursueth: but the righteous are bold as a lion." -- Proverbs 28:1

This proverb captures in a single sentence the entire theology of holy boldness. The wicked are cowards even when no one threatens them — their guilty conscience makes them flee from shadows. But the righteous are bold as lions even when real danger threatens — their clean conscience and their trust in God give them an unshakeable courage. Notice that the comparison is not to a lamb or a dove but to a lion — the king of beasts, the symbol of strength, majesty, and fearlessness. When you walk in righteousness, when your conscience is clear before God, when you know that you are standing on the truth of His Word, you have a boldness that no threat can diminish. You are bold as a lion — not because of your own strength, but because the Lion of the tribe of Judah walks beside you.

Supplemental Reading Assignment:

The Puritan pastor William Gurnall (1616-1679), born in King's Lynn, Norfolk, England, wrote one of the longest and most comprehensive works on spiritual warfare in the entire Puritan tradition: "The Christian in Complete Armour." Gurnall's massive treatise, running to over twelve hundred pages, expounds Ephesians 6:10-20 with exhaustive thoroughness, examining every piece of the believer's spiritual armor and demonstrating how each equips the Christian to stand boldly in the day of battle. Gurnall writes that the Christian soldier must put on the whole armor of God daily, for the enemy never relents and the battle is never finished until we reach glory. His work is a treasury of practical wisdom for believers who face opposition, persecution, or the pressure to compromise their convictions.

For students who wish to go deeper, read the account of Peter's transformation from cowardice to courage in the Gospels and Acts. In the Gospels, Peter denied Christ three times out of fear of a servant girl (Luke 22:54-62). In Acts, Peter stood before the Sanhedrin — the same body that had condemned Jesus to death — and proclaimed Christ with fearless boldness (Acts 4:8-13). What made the difference? Acts 4:13 gives the answer: "Now when they saw the boldness of Peter and John, and perceived that they were unlearned and ignorant men, they marvelled; and they took knowledge of them, that they had been with Jesus." Their boldness was the fruit of having been with Jesus — of the resurrection, of Pentecost, of the fullness of the Holy Spirit. This is the source of all holy boldness: not education, not personality, not willpower, but the transforming power of the risen Christ working through the Holy Spirit in those who have been with Jesus. As you read this account, consider how your own times of communion with Christ in prayer and Scripture equip you for moments of bold witness.
