PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Doctrine Deep-Dive, Day 20
"The Doctrine of the Church"
Ecclesiology Basics


Good evening, friend. Tonight we turn our minds to a doctrine that is often neglected in our individualistic age: the doctrine of the church. The Puritans had a high view of the church because they had a high view of Christ, who loved the church and gave Himself for it. In a culture that treats church attendance as optional and church membership as outdated, the Puritans call us back to something deeper and more glorious.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Ephesians 5:25-27:

"Husbands, love your wives, even as Christ also loved the church, and gave himself for it; That he might sanctify and cleanse it with the washing of water by the word, That he might present it to himself a glorious church, not having spot, or wrinkle, or any such thing; but that it should be holy and without blemish."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


The Puritans drew a careful distinction between the church invisible and the church visible. The church invisible is the total number of God's elect, known only to Him, spanning every nation and every century, from Abel to the last person who will believe before Christ returns. This church cannot be fully identified by human eyes because only God knows with certainty who truly belongs to Him. But the church visible is the community of professing believers, organized into local congregations, gathered for worship, governed by elders, and marked by the preaching of the Word, the administration of the sacraments, and the exercise of discipline.

The Westminster Confession of Faith defines the visible church as consisting "of all those throughout the world that profess the true religion, together with their children." This visible church, though it inevitably contains hypocrites alongside genuine believers, is nevertheless the ordinary instrument through which God accomplishes His purposes in the world. The Puritans did not despise the visible church because it was imperfect. They loved it because Christ loved it.

What are the marks of a true church? The Puritans identified three. The first mark is the faithful preaching of the Word of God. William Perkins, the great Elizabethan Puritan, argued that the church lives or dies by its pulpit. Where the Word of God is preached faithfully, the church flourishes. Where it is neglected, corrupted, or replaced by human entertainment, the church withers. The Puritans devoted extraordinary energy to preaching because they believed it was God's primary instrument for the salvation of sinners and the sanctification of saints.

The second mark is the proper administration of the sacraments: baptism and the Lord's Supper. The Puritans regarded the sacraments as visible words — outward signs of inward grace. Baptism signifies the believer's union with Christ and his incorporation into the covenant community. The Lord's Supper signifies the believer's ongoing communion with Christ through His body and blood. Both sacraments are means of grace. Through them, the Spirit strengthens faith and deepens fellowship with God.

The third mark is church discipline. This is perhaps the most neglected mark in modern evangelicalism, but the Puritans considered it essential. Discipline means holding one another accountable to the standards of Scripture. It means confronting sin in love, calling the wayward to repentance, and in extreme cases, excluding the unrepentant from the fellowship. The Puritans followed the pattern Christ set forth in Matthew 18:15-17: private admonition, then witnesses, then the whole church, and finally excommunication if there is no repentance. The purpose of discipline is never punishment but always restoration. "Brethren, if a man be overtaken in a fault, ye which are spiritual, restore such an one in the spirit of meekness" (Galatians 6:1).

The Puritans also stressed the importance of formal church membership. They did not recognize the category of the "churchless Christian." John Owen, who pastored a gathered congregation in London, wrote extensively about the mutual obligations of church members. Members are obligated to attend the public worship of God, to submit to the teaching and oversight of the elders, to pray for one another, to bear one another's burdens, and to contribute to the material needs of the body. Owen regarded these obligations not as optional suggestions but as solemn covenant commitments flowing from our union with Christ and with one another.

Richard Baxter paints a vivid picture of the church as a family. The pastor is a shepherd, responsible for knowing, feeding, and protecting every sheep in the flock. The members are brothers and sisters, bound to love one another, encourage one another, and hold one another accountable. In this family, no one is a stranger, no one is forgotten, and no one walks alone through the valley.

Why does this doctrine matter for your personal growth? Because God has ordained that spiritual growth happens in community. You cannot grow as a Christian in isolation any more than a coal can stay hot separated from the fire. The local church is the context in which you hear the Word preached, receive the sacraments, exercise your gifts, bear one another's burdens, and are held accountable for your walk with God. To neglect the church is to neglect a primary means of grace.


First, if you are not committed to a local congregation, make it your urgent priority to find one and join it. Look for the three marks: faithful preaching, proper sacraments, and loving discipline.

Second, if you are a member of a church, ask yourself whether you are fulfilling your obligations to the body. Are you attending faithfully? Serving actively? Giving generously? Praying for your brothers and sisters? Submitting to the leadership God has placed over you?

Third, resist the modern temptation to treat church as a consumer product to be evaluated by what it offers you. The church is a family to be loved, a body to be served, and a bride to be cherished — because Christ Himself cherishes her.


Lord Jesus, Thou didst love the church and give Thyself for it. Teach us to love what Thou lovest. Make us faithful members of Thy body. Help us to gather for worship, to submit to Thy Word, to serve one another in love, and to hold one another accountable in truth. Build Thy church, O Lord, and let the gates of hell not prevail against it. Amen.

Go in peace tonight, friend. You are a member of the body of Christ. Live like it.
