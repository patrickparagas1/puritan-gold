PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Doctrine Deep-Dive, Day 20
"The Doctrine of the Church"
Ecclesiology — The Gathered People of God

---

Good evening, friend. Tonight we turn our minds to a doctrine that is often neglected in our individualistic age: the doctrine of the church. The Puritans had a high view of the church because they had a high view of Christ, who loved the church and gave Himself for it. Let us recover their vision of what the church is and why it matters.

--- Scripture ---

Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Ephesians 5:25-27:

"Husbands, love your wives, even as Christ also loved the church, and gave himself for it; That he might sanctify and cleanse it with the washing of water by the word, That he might present it to himself a glorious church, not having spot, or wrinkle, or any such thing; but that it should be holy and without blemish."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.

--- Doctrine Study ---

The Puritans drew a distinction between the church invisible and the church visible. The church invisible is the total number of God's elect, known only to Him, spanning every nation and every age, from Abel to the last person who will believe before Christ returns. This church can never be fully identified by human eyes because only God knows who truly belongs to Him. But the church visible is the community of professing believers, organized into local congregations, gathered for worship, governed by elders, and marked by the preaching of the Word, the administration of the sacraments, and the exercise of discipline.

The Westminster Confession of Faith defines the visible church as consisting "of all those throughout the world that profess the true religion, together with their children." This visible church, though it contains hypocrites alongside true believers, is nevertheless the ordinary means through which God works in the world. The Puritans did not dismiss the visible church because it was imperfect. They loved it because Christ loved it.

The first mark of a true church, according to Puritan ecclesiology, is the faithful preaching of the Word of God. William Perkins, the great Elizabethan Puritan, argued that the church lives or dies by its pulpit. Where the Word of God is preached faithfully, the church flourishes. Where it is neglected, corrupted, or replaced by human traditions, the church withers. The Puritans devoted enormous energy to preaching because they believed that preaching was God's primary instrument for the salvation of sinners and the sanctification of saints.

The second mark is the proper administration of the sacraments: baptism and the Lord's Supper. The Puritans regarded the sacraments as visible words — outward signs of inward grace. Baptism signifies the believer's union with Christ in His death and resurrection and his incorporation into the covenant community. The Lord's Supper signifies the believer's ongoing communion with Christ through His body and blood. Both sacraments are means of grace, not merely memorials. Through them, the Spirit strengthens faith and deepens the believer's fellowship with God.

The third mark is church discipline. This is perhaps the most neglected mark of the church in modern evangelicalism, but the Puritans considered it essential. Discipline means holding one another accountable to the standards of Scripture. It means confronting sin in love, calling the wayward to repentance, and, in extreme cases, excluding the unrepentant from the fellowship of the church. The Puritans followed the pattern set forth by Christ in Matthew 18:15-17: private admonition, then witnesses, then the whole church, and finally excommunication if there is no repentance. The purpose of discipline is not punishment but restoration. "Brethren, if a man be overtaken in a fault, ye which are spiritual, restore such an one in the spirit of meekness" (Galatians 6:1).

The Puritans also stressed the importance of church membership. They did not recognize the category of the "churchless Christian." John Owen, who served as pastor of a gathered congregation in London, wrote extensively about the mutual obligations of church members. Members are obligated to attend the worship of God, to submit to the teaching and discipline of the elders, to pray for one another, to bear one another's burdens, and to contribute to the material needs of the body. Owen regarded these obligations as solemn covenant commitments, not optional extras.

Richard Baxter, in "The Reformed Pastor," paints a picture of the church as a family. The pastor is a shepherd, responsible for knowing, feeding, and protecting every sheep in the flock. The members are brothers and sisters, responsible for loving one another, encouraging one another, and holding one another accountable. In this family, no one is a stranger, no one is forgotten, and no one walks alone.

The Puritans also had a profound theology of worship. They believed that corporate worship is the highest activity of the church and that it must be regulated by the Word of God. The "regulative principle of worship" teaches that the church should worship God only in the ways He has prescribed in Scripture: the reading and preaching of the Word, the singing of psalms and hymns, prayer, the administration of the sacraments, and the taking of collections for the poor. The Puritans rejected the idea that human creativity or cultural relevance should determine the form of worship. Worship belongs to God, and He has the right to determine how He is worshiped.

Why does the doctrine of the church matter for your personal growth? Because God has ordained that growth happens in community. You cannot grow as a Christian in isolation any more than a coal can remain hot separated from the fire. The local church is the context in which you hear the Word preached, receive the sacraments, exercise your gifts, bear one another's burdens, and are held accountable for your walk with God. To neglect the church is to neglect a primary means of grace.

Let the Puritans' love for the church challenge you tonight. If you are not committed to a local congregation, find one. If you are committed but passive, become active. Christ loved the church and gave Himself for it. Let us love it too.

--- Reflection ---

Here are some questions to carry with you into the coming days.

Question 1: Am I faithfully committed to a local church — attending, serving, giving, and submitting to its leadership?

Question 2: Do I view the church as a consumer views a product, or as a member views a family?

Question 3: How can I better fulfill my obligations to the body of Christ in the coming week?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.

--- Closing Prayer ---

Lord Jesus, Thou didst love the church and give Thyself for it. Teach us to love what Thou lovest. Make us faithful members of Thy body. Help us to gather for worship, to submit to Thy Word, to serve one another in love, and to hold one another accountable in truth. Build Thy church, O Lord, and let the gates of hell not prevail against it. Amen.

Go in peace tonight. You are a member of the body of Christ. Act like it.
