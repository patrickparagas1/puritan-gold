PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 20
"How Fair and How Pleasant"
Song of Solomon 7:1-9 — The Fullness of Marital Delight

---

Welcome, dear couple. Tonight the bridegroom speaks again, and his praise reaches its zenith. This is the most detailed, most intimate, most lavish celebration of the bride's beauty in the entire Song. It is a portrait of marital delight in full bloom.

Song of Solomon 7:1-9:

"How beautiful are thy feet with shoes, O prince's daughter! the joints of thy thighs are like jewels, the work of the hands of a cunning workman. Thy navel is like a round goblet, which wanteth not liquor: thy belly is like an heap of wheat set about with lilies. Thy two breasts are like two young roes that are twins. Thy neck is as a tower of ivory; thine eyes like the fishpools in Heshbon, by the gate of Bathrabbim: thy nose is as the tower of Lebanon which looketh toward Damascus. Thine head upon thee is like Carmel, and the hair of thine head like purple; the king is held in the galleries. How fair and how pleasant art thou, O love, for delights! This thy stature is like to a palm tree, and thy breasts to clusters of grapes. I said, I will go up to the palm tree, I will take hold of the boughs thereof: now also thy breasts shall be as clusters of the vine, and the smell of thy nose like apples; And the roof of thy mouth like the best wine for my beloved, that goeth down sweetly, causing the lips of those that are asleep to speak."

--- Teaching ---

In chapter four, the bridegroom praised his bride from head to toe. Now in chapter seven, he begins from the feet and moves upward — as if he cannot choose a starting point because every part of her is worthy of celebration. This is complete, full-body admiration. Nothing is overlooked. Nothing is passed over. Nothing is left uncelebrated.

The Puritans did not shy from the frank physicality of this passage. William Gouge wrote that "the body is God's creation, and the mutual delight of husband and wife in each other's bodies is a holy gift to be received with thanksgiving." Thomas Gataker added that any shame about the physical dimension of marriage has no warrant in Scripture. The Song celebrates the body of the beloved in exquisite detail because the body is good, and physical delight within marriage is good.

"How beautiful are thy feet with shoes, O prince's daughter!" He begins with her feet — the lowest, most humble part of the body. The Puritans saw in this a lesson: admiration in marriage should not be selective, praising only what is conventionally beautiful while ignoring the rest. True love celebrates the whole person — the parts the world notices and the parts the world overlooks.

"The joints of thy thighs are like jewels, the work of the hands of a cunning workman." The bride's body is compared to the work of a master craftsman. This is not accidental. Her body is God's handiwork — skillfully made, beautifully designed, worthy of the same admiration we give to a masterpiece of art. Richard Sibbes wrote that to despise the body God has given your spouse is to insult the Creator. To celebrate it is an act of worship.

"The king is held in the galleries." The bridegroom is captivated. The word "held" means captured, bound, imprisoned. He cannot look away. He is a willing prisoner of his wife's beauty. The Puritans saw this as the proper state of a married man — captivated by his own wife, not by the beauty of others. In a world that constantly offers alternatives, the bridegroom's eyes are fixed on one woman. She alone holds him.

"How fair and how pleasant art thou, O love, for delights!" Two words stand out: fair and pleasant. Fair speaks of beauty — what the eyes see. Pleasant speaks of delight — what the heart feels. True marital love engages both. The bride is not only beautiful to look at; she is pleasant to be with. She is not only attractive on the surface; she is delightful in her depths.

"I said, I will go up to the palm tree, I will take hold of the boughs thereof." The bridegroom declares his intention. He is not passive. He does not wait for the perfect moment or the perfect mood. He decides: I will pursue. I will reach for her. I will take hold. Richard Baxter wrote that passivity is the death of passion. Love requires intentional pursuit — not once, at the beginning, but continually, throughout the whole of life.

"And the roof of thy mouth like the best wine for my beloved, that goeth down sweetly." The passage closes with an image of intoxication — the kisses of the bride are like the finest wine. They go down sweetly, and even in sleep, they cause the lips to speak. Love that saturates is love that overflows — it fills the waking hours and permeates even the sleeping ones.

Tonight, let this passage teach you the art of full-hearted, unreserved admiration. Do not hold back. Do not ration your praise. Do not be embarrassed by the depth of your delight in each other. The Song gives you permission — indeed, it gives you a model — for lavish, detailed, joyful celebration of the one God has given you.

--- Reflection Prompts ---

Turn to each other and consider these questions.

Question 1: Is your admiration of your spouse selective, or does it celebrate the whole person — body, mind, and soul? Are there aspects of each other you have stopped noticing or praising?

Question 2: The bridegroom is "held in the galleries" — captivated by his bride. What most captivates you about your spouse? Tell them tonight.

Question 3: The bridegroom says "I will go up to the palm tree." Love requires initiative. In what area of your marriage do you need to take more initiative — physical, emotional, spiritual?

--- Prayer Focus ---

Tonight's prayer focus: Thank God for the gift of your spouse — body, mind, and soul. Pray for the freedom to delight in each other without reservation, and for the faithfulness to keep your eyes and your heart fixed on the one God has given you.

--- Closing ---

How fair and how pleasant art thou, O love, for delights. May this be the song your heart sings over your spouse — tonight and every night. Goodnight, beloved couple.
