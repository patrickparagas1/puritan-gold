Are they enemies, locked in an irreconcilable war? Are they strangers, occupying entirely separate territories with nothing to say to each other? Or are they allies, two different ways of exploring the same magnificent reality created by the same magnificent God? The answer to this question matters enormously — not only for your intellectual life but for your daily walk as a disciple and for your witness to a world that often assumes that choosing science means rejecting God. This is a lesson you will carry with you for the rest of your lives. Let us pray and begin.

PART ONE: THE MYTH OF WARFARE

Students, one of the most persistent and damaging myths of the modern age is the belief that science and Christianity are fundamentally at war — that the advance of scientific knowledge has progressively undermined religious belief, and that an honest, educated person must eventually choose between the laboratory and the sanctuary. This narrative — sometimes called the "conflict thesis" or the "warfare model" — has been repeated so often and so confidently that many people accept it as an obvious fact of history. But it is not a fact. It is a myth. And like all myths, it crumbles under careful examination.

The conflict thesis was popularized in the late nineteenth century by two books: John William Draper's "History of the Conflict between Religion and Science" (1874) and Andrew Dickson White's "A History of the Warfare of Science with Theology in Christendom" (1896). Both books painted a picture of heroic scientists struggling against the ignorance and oppression of the Christian church. But modern historians of science have thoroughly debunked this narrative. Historian Ronald Numbers, himself not a Christian, has called the conflict thesis "the greatest myth in the history of science and religion." Historian John Hedley Brooke, in his definitive study "Science and Religion: Some Historical Perspectives," has shown that the actual historical relationship between science and faith is far more complex, far more positive, and far more interesting than the simplistic warfare narrative suggests. Historian David Lindberg has demonstrated that the medieval church was, far from being an enemy of learning, the primary institution responsible for preserving and advancing knowledge in Western civilization.

The truth is that modern science did not develop in spite of Christianity. It developed because of Christianity. The scientific revolution of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries was overwhelmingly the work of devout Christians who believed that the natural world was worth studying precisely because it was the creation of a rational, orderly, faithful God. The very foundations of the scientific method — the conviction that the universe operates according to regular, discoverable laws; the belief that human reason is capable of comprehending those laws; and the confidence that the natural world is real and worthy of investigation — are all rooted in the Christian worldview.

Consider the Scriptural foundation for this conviction. The Psalmist declared: "The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork. Day unto day uttereth speech, and night unto night sheweth knowledge" (Psalm 19:1-2, KJV). The creation speaks. It communicates. It reveals knowledge about its Creator. The study of the natural world — which is precisely what science is — is, from a Christian perspective, the study of God's handiwork. It is a form of worship. It is reading the book that God has written in the stars, the mountains, the oceans, and the intricacies of every living cell.

The Apostle Paul made the same point: "For the invisible things of him from the creation of the world are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made, even his eternal power and Godhead; so that they are without excuse" (Romans 1:20, KJV). The created world reveals God's invisible attributes — His power, His wisdom, His creativity, His goodness. Science, properly understood, is the systematic investigation of this revelation. It is not an alternative to faith. It is a complement to faith — another way of knowing the God who has revealed Himself in both nature and Scripture.

PART TWO: WHY CHRISTIANITY MADE SCIENCE POSSIBLE

Before we survey the great Christian scientists of history, we must understand why the Christian worldview — and the Christian worldview alone — provided the intellectual soil in which modern science could take root and flourish. This is a point of enormous importance that is almost never taught in secular education. Modern science did not arise in China, India, Greece, or the Islamic world — civilizations that were in many ways more advanced than medieval Europe. It arose in Christian Europe. Why?

The answer lies in several core Christian doctrines that provide the necessary preconditions for scientific inquiry.

First, the doctrine of creation. Christianity teaches that the physical world is real, not illusory. It is not maya (illusion), as some Eastern philosophies teach. It is not a prison from which the soul must escape, as the Gnostics taught. It is the handiwork of God, created with purpose and declared "very good" (Genesis 1:31, KJV). This means that the physical world is worthy of careful investigation. A culture that regards matter as evil or illusory will never develop experimental science, because it will see no point in studying something that is ultimately unreal or corrupt.

Second, the doctrine of divine rationality. Christianity teaches that God is rational and consistent — that He is a God of order, not of chaos. "For God is not the author of confusion, but of peace" (1 Corinthians 14:33, KJV). Because God is rational, the universe He created operates according to rational, discoverable laws. It is not governed by the whims of capricious deities or the arbitrary forces of fate. It follows predictable patterns because it reflects the consistent character of its Maker. This conviction is the indispensable foundation of the scientific method. Without it, there would be no reason to expect that experiments performed today will yield the same results tomorrow.

Third, the doctrine of the image of God. Christianity teaches that human beings are created in the image of God — that our rational minds are designed to comprehend the rational order of creation. "In the image of God made he man" (Genesis 9:6, KJV). Because our minds are made in the image of the Mind that designed the universe, there is a fundamental correspondence between human thought and physical reality. We can understand the universe because the same God who made the universe also made our minds. The atheistic worldview, by contrast, has no explanation for why the human mind — a product, supposedly, of blind evolutionary processes optimized for survival, not for truth — should be capable of comprehending the deep mathematical structure of the cosmos.

Fourth, the doctrine of stewardship. Christianity teaches that God has given humanity dominion over the created world — a responsibility to study, understand, and wisely manage the resources of the earth. "And God said, Let us make man in our image, after our likeness: and let them have dominion over the fish of the sea, and over the fowl of the air, and over the cattle, and over all the earth" (Genesis 1:26, KJV). This mandate to exercise dominion provides a moral motivation for scientific inquiry — not merely to satisfy curiosity but to fulfill a divine calling.

These four doctrines — the reality and goodness of the physical world, the rationality of the Creator, the correspondence between the human mind and the natural order, and the mandate of stewardship — are the theological foundations upon which modern science was built. Without them, the scientific revolution would not have happened.

The Puritan pastor Cotton Mather (1663-1728) expressed this conviction with characteristic vigor. Mather was not only a theologian and pastor but also a serious student of natural philosophy who was elected to the Royal Society in London. He wrote: "The works of the glorious God in the creation of the world are what I now invite you to survey. Do it with the eye of a philosopher, but also with the eye of a Christian. And you will find that the one eye assists the other — that the more you know of God's creation, the more you will adore the Creator."

PART THREE: THE GREAT CHRISTIAN SCIENTISTS

The historical record is overwhelming: the founders of modern science were, with remarkably few exceptions, deeply committed Christians whose scientific work was motivated and shaped by their faith. Let us survey some of the most significant figures.

Nicolaus Copernicus (1473-1543), the Polish astronomer who proposed the heliocentric model of the solar system, was a canon of the Catholic Church. His revolutionary work "On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres" was dedicated to the Pope and was motivated by a desire to discover the mathematical harmony that God had embedded in the cosmos.

Galileo Galilei (1564-1642), the Italian physicist and astronomer who is often held up as the supreme example of the conflict between science and faith, was in fact a devout Catholic who never renounced his faith. His conflict with the church authorities was not a conflict between science and Christianity but a conflict between a new scientific interpretation and a particular ecclesiastical interpretation of certain Scripture passages. Galileo himself wrote: "The Holy Scripture and nature are both emanations from the divine word — the former dictated by the Holy Spirit, the latter the faithful executrix of God's commands."

Johannes Kepler (1571-1630), the German astronomer who discovered the laws of planetary motion, described his scientific work as "thinking God's thoughts after Him." Kepler believed that the mathematical order of the universe was a direct reflection of the mind of God, and that studying astronomy was a form of worship. He wrote: "The chief aim of all investigations of the external world should be to discover the rational order which has been imposed on it by God, and which He revealed to us in the language of mathematics."

Sir Isaac Newton (1643-1727), widely regarded as the greatest scientist in history, devoted more of his writing to theology than to physics. Newton believed that the laws of nature he discovered — the laws of motion, the law of universal gravitation — were expressions of God's sovereign governance of the physical world. In the "General Scholium" appended to his masterwork "Principia Mathematica," Newton wrote: "This most beautiful system of the sun, planets, and comets could only proceed from the counsel and dominion of an intelligent and powerful Being."

Robert Boyle (1627-1691), the father of modern chemistry and a contemporary of the Puritans, was a devout Christian who funded Bible translations, endowed lectures in defense of Christianity, and saw his scientific work as a religious vocation. Boyle called scientists "priests of nature" and wrote: "The study of the Book of Nature is a religious duty, for it is the study of the works of the Creator."

The Puritan movement, in particular, had an extraordinarily positive influence on the development of science. The historian Robert K. Merton, in his landmark study "Science, Technology, and Society in Seventeenth-Century England," demonstrated that Puritans were dramatically overrepresented among the founding members of the Royal Society — the premier scientific institution in England, established in 1660. Merton argued that the Puritan emphasis on glorifying God through the study of His works, combined with the Puritan ethic of diligence, rationality, and practical usefulness, created an intellectual environment uniquely conducive to scientific inquiry.

The Puritan theologian Richard Baxter (1615-1691) wrote: "God hath given us two books — the book of Scripture and the book of nature. He that would be truly wise must study both. The one teaches us what God has said; the other teaches us what God has done. And both are needful for the full knowledge of the Creator."

Beyond these towering figures, we should also note the contributions of Blaise Pascal (1623-1662), the French mathematician, physicist, and philosopher whose work on fluid dynamics, probability theory, and calculating machines was inseparable from his profound Christian devotion. Pascal's "Pensees" — his collected thoughts on religion and philosophy — remain one of the most brilliant defenses of the Christian faith ever written. And in England, the Puritan-influenced natural philosopher John Ray (1627-1705) produced "The Wisdom of God Manifested in the Works of the Creation," a comprehensive survey of the natural world that was, in essence, a scientific argument for the existence and attributes of God. Ray was one of the first scientists to develop a systematic method of classifying plants and animals, and his work laid the groundwork for modern taxonomy. His motivation was explicitly theological: "The number, variety, and beauty of living creatures is a perpetual feast to the mind of a contemplative man, and leads him directly to the wisdom, power, and goodness of their Creator."

Michael Faraday (1791-1867), the self-educated son of a blacksmith who became one of the greatest experimental scientists in history, was a devout member of the Sandemanian church — a small Christian denomination that emphasized simplicity and biblical fidelity. Faraday's discoveries in electromagnetism and electrochemistry laid the foundations for much of modern electrical technology. He saw no conflict between his faith and his science, writing: "The book of nature, which we have to read, is written by the finger of God."

James Clerk Maxwell (1831-1879), the Scottish physicist whose equations unified electricity, magnetism, and light into a single theoretical framework — arguably the most important achievement in physics between Newton and Einstein — was a committed Presbyterian who prayed regularly and took his faith with the utmost seriousness. Maxwell wrote: "I think that men of science as well as other men need to learn from Christ, and I think that Christians whose minds are scientific are bound to study science that their view of the glory of God may be as extensive as their being is capable of."

The point of this historical survey is not to claim that every scientist must be a Christian. It is to demonstrate that the popular narrative of inevitable conflict between science and faith is historically false. The greatest scientific minds in history were also minds devoted to the God of Scripture. Their faith did not hinder their science. It inspired it.

PART FOUR: THE TWO BOOKS — NATURE AND SCRIPTURE

The metaphor of "two books" — the book of nature and the book of Scripture — has a long and venerable history in Christian theology. It was used by the early church fathers, by the medieval theologians, by the Reformers, and by the Puritans. The idea is simple but profound: God has revealed Himself in two complementary ways — through the natural world (which theologians call "general revelation") and through the written Word of God (which theologians call "special revelation"). Both books have the same Author, and therefore they cannot ultimately contradict each other.

The Westminster Confession of Faith, in its opening chapter, acknowledges the reality of general revelation: "The light of nature, and the works of creation and providence, do so far manifest the goodness, wisdom, and power of God, as to leave men inexcusable" (Chapter 1, Section 1). The Confession goes on to affirm the necessity and supremacy of special revelation — the Scriptures — for the knowledge of salvation. But it does not deny the reality or value of general revelation. The works of creation reveal the goodness, wisdom, and power of God. Science, as the systematic study of the works of creation, is therefore a legitimate and valuable source of knowledge about God and His world.

The Belgic Confession (1561), written by Guido de Bres, a Reformed pastor who was martyred for his faith, states this principle beautifully: "We know God by two means: first, by the creation, preservation, and government of the universe, since that universe is before our eyes like a beautiful book in which all creatures, great and small, are as letters to make us ponder the invisible things of God... Second, God makes himself known to us more clearly by his holy and divine Word, as much as we need in this life, for God's glory and for our salvation."

This "two books" doctrine has an important practical implication that every young Christian must grasp firmly and hold with confidence: since both books have the same divine Author — the God who cannot lie (Titus 1:2) and who is the fountain of all truth — any apparent conflict between them must be the result of a misinterpretation of one book or the other — or both. When science and Scripture seem to disagree, the solution is not to dismiss science in the name of faith or to dismiss Scripture in the name of science. The solution is to study both more carefully, with humility and patience, trusting that the truth of nature and the truth of Scripture will ultimately prove to be harmonious.

The Puritan pastor and scientist John Wilkins (1614-1672), who was one of the founders of the Royal Society and also the Bishop of Chester, wrote: "When there seems to be a contradiction between the book of nature and the book of Scripture, we may be sure that we have misread one or the other. For both books have the same Author, and God cannot contradict Himself. Our duty is not to choose between them but to study both with greater diligence and humility until the harmony becomes apparent."

PART FIVE: WHAT SCIENCE CAN AND CANNOT DO

A proper understanding of the relationship between science and faith requires a clear grasp of what science is — and what it is not. Science is a method of investigating the natural world through observation, hypothesis, experimentation, and analysis. It is extraordinarily powerful within its proper domain. It can tell us how the physical world operates, what natural processes produce certain effects, and how to harness natural forces for practical purposes. The achievements of modern science — in medicine, agriculture, technology, engineering, and countless other fields — are genuine and praiseworthy.

But science has inherent limitations. There are questions that the scientific method, by its very nature, cannot answer. These include questions of meaning and purpose: Why does the universe exist? What is the purpose of human life? Questions of morality and ethics: What is right and wrong? What is justice? What ought we to do? Questions of beauty and value: What makes something beautiful? Why does beauty matter? Questions of origin and destiny: Where did everything come from ultimately? Where is everything going? And the deepest metaphysical questions of all: Why is there something rather than nothing? What is the nature of consciousness? What happens after death?

These are not minor or peripheral questions. They are the most important questions a human being can ask. And they are questions that science, operating within its proper domain of empirical investigation, is not equipped to answer. They belong to the domain of philosophy, theology, and revelation. The person who insists that science is the only legitimate source of knowledge — a position sometimes called "scientism" — is making a claim that is not itself scientific. It is a philosophical claim, and it is a self-defeating one, because it cannot be established by the scientific method it claims to champion.

The Apostle Paul warned against the kind of intellectual pride that elevates human wisdom above divine revelation: "For the wisdom of this world is foolishness with God. For it is written, He taketh the wise in their own craftiness. And again, The Lord knoweth the thoughts of the wise, that they are vain" (1 Corinthians 3:19-20, KJV). This is not an anti-intellectual statement. Paul is not condemning the pursuit of knowledge. He is condemning the arrogance of a wisdom that refuses to acknowledge its own limitations and sets itself up as the final arbiter of all truth.

The Puritan theologian John Owen (1616-1683) wrote with characteristic precision about the limits of human reason: "Reason is an excellent gift of God, and within its proper sphere it is a faithful servant. But when reason exalts itself above revelation — when it presumes to sit in judgment upon the Word of God — it becomes a tyrant and an idol. The wise man uses reason to understand God's Word, not to overrule it."

This point deserves special emphasis because the philosophical position called "scientism" — the belief that the scientific method is the only reliable path to knowledge — is extraordinarily influential in our culture today. You will encounter it in textbooks, in popular media, in university classrooms, and in casual conversation. People will tell you that science has "disproved" God, or that religious beliefs are "unscientific" and therefore invalid. But notice the self-defeating nature of this claim. The statement "Only scientific knowledge is real knowledge" is not itself a scientific statement. It cannot be verified by experiment, tested in a laboratory, or measured with an instrument. It is a philosophical claim — and a highly questionable one at that.

Moreover, some of the most important things we know — and know with absolute certainty — are not scientific at all. We know that torturing innocent people for amusement is morally wrong. We know that mathematical truths like "two plus two equals four" are necessarily and eternally true. We know that we exist as conscious beings with subjective experiences. We know that the law of non-contradiction (a thing cannot be both true and false at the same time and in the same sense) is universally valid. None of these truths can be established by the scientific method, yet no sane person would deny them. Science is a powerful tool, but it is not the only tool in the human intellectual toolkit — and to treat it as such is to impoverish our understanding of reality.

The Puritan pastor Thomas Watson (c. 1620-1686) expressed the proper relationship between different kinds of knowledge with his customary clarity: "God has given us the light of nature, the light of Scripture, and the light of His Spirit. Each light has its proper sphere. The light of nature shows us the works of God. The light of Scripture shows us the will of God. The light of His Spirit shows us the grace of God. He that would walk safely must walk in all three lights together."

PART SIX: SPECIFIC AREAS OF DIALOGUE

Let us briefly consider several specific areas where science and faith interact.

First, the origin of the universe. Modern cosmology has established that the universe had a beginning — a conclusion summarized in the Big Bang theory. This is a remarkable confirmation of the opening words of the Bible: "In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth" (Genesis 1:1, KJV). For most of human history, many philosophers — including Aristotle — believed that the universe was eternal. The biblical claim that the universe had a beginning was considered naive and unscientific. Yet modern physics has vindicated the biblical position. The universe is not eternal. It began to exist. And anything that begins to exist requires a cause. The greatest question in cosmology — Why is there something rather than nothing? — points beyond science to the reality of a transcendent Creator.

The Puritan theologian Stephen Charnock (1628-1680) argued this point with force long before modern cosmology confirmed it: "The world could not make itself. Nothing cannot produce something. The very existence of the world cries aloud for a Maker, as a building cries aloud for a builder, and a painting cries aloud for a painter. He that looks upon a stately palace and concludes that it built itself is not a philosopher — he is a fool."

Second, the fine-tuning of the universe. Physicists have discovered that the fundamental constants of the universe — the strength of gravity, the mass of the electron, the charge of the proton, the rate of cosmic expansion, and dozens of other values — are calibrated with astonishing precision. If any of these values were altered by even a minuscule amount, the universe would be incapable of supporting life. This is known as the fine-tuning argument, and it is one of the most powerful lines of evidence for the existence of a cosmic Designer. The probability of these constants aligning by chance is so vanishingly small that many physicists — including some who are not Christians — have acknowledged that the universe appears to have been designed for life.

The Psalmist anticipated this discovery: "When I consider thy heavens, the work of thy fingers, the moon and the stars, which thou hast ordained; What is man, that thou art mindful of him? and the son of man, that thou visitest him?" (Psalm 8:3-4, KJV). The more we learn about the universe through science, the more evidence we find for the wisdom and intentionality of its Creator.

Third, the origin and complexity of life. The living cell is a world of staggering complexity. A single human cell contains approximately six feet of DNA — a molecular code consisting of three billion base pairs that encodes the instructions for building and maintaining an entire human body. The information content of the human genome is equivalent to approximately eight hundred thick volumes of closely printed text. The origin of this information — how it first arose, how it became organized into the precise sequences necessary for life — remains one of the deepest unsolved problems in biology.

The Christian position is that information requires an intelligent source. Code requires a coder. Instructions require an instructor. The biological information encoded in DNA points to an intelligent Author — just as the information encoded in a book points to an intelligent author. This is not an argument from ignorance. It is an argument from what we know about the nature of information: that in every other context, complex, specified information is always the product of an intelligent mind.

Fourth, the nature of human consciousness. Despite extraordinary advances in neuroscience, the nature of conscious experience remains deeply mysterious. Why do physical processes in the brain give rise to subjective experience — to the sensation of seeing red, or hearing music, or tasting sweetness? This is what philosophers call the "hard problem of consciousness," and no purely materialistic theory has come close to solving it. The Christian worldview provides a natural explanation: human beings are not merely physical organisms. They are image-bearers of God, created with rational souls that transcend the material order. "And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul" (Genesis 2:7, KJV). The mystery of consciousness points to a dimension of human existence that science alone cannot explain — the spiritual dimension that the Bible affirms from its opening pages.

Fifth, the reliability of the laws of nature. Science depends upon the assumption that the laws of nature are uniform — that the same causes will produce the same effects tomorrow as they did today, and that the laws of physics operating here also operate in distant galaxies. But what justifies this assumption? The Scottish philosopher David Hume famously argued that the uniformity of nature cannot be established by science itself, because every scientific argument for the uniformity of nature already presupposes the very uniformity it is trying to prove. This is a genuine philosophical problem for the atheistic worldview. But it is no problem at all for the Christian worldview. The laws of nature are uniform because they are the expression of the consistent, faithful character of God. "While the earth remaineth, seedtime and harvest, and cold and heat, and summer and winter, and day and night shall not cease" (Genesis 8:22, KJV). God has promised to maintain the regular order of nature, and His faithfulness is the ultimate ground of scientific confidence. Every time a scientist relies on the uniformity of nature to conduct an experiment, he is — whether he knows it or not — relying on the faithfulness of God.

The Puritan theologian Thomas Goodwin (1600-1680) made this connection explicit: "The constancy of the natural world is a mirror of the constancy of its Creator. The sun rises daily because God is faithful. The seasons follow their appointed course because God keeps His covenant with the day and with the night (Jeremiah 33:20). The laws of nature are nothing other than the habits of God — His regular, orderly, and dependable way of governing His creation."

Sixth, the mathematical structure of nature. One of the most astonishing features of the physical world is its mathematical beauty. The laws of physics can be expressed in elegant mathematical equations. The spiral of a galaxy follows the same mathematical pattern as the spiral of a seashell. The mathematical constant pi appears in countless natural phenomena that seem to have no connection with each other. Albert Einstein was famously puzzled by what he called "the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics" — the fact that abstract mathematical concepts, invented by human minds, turn out to describe physical reality with extraordinary precision. Why should this be so? The Christian answer is that mathematics is a reflection of the mind of God — that the same rational Mind that created both the human intellect and the physical world designed them to correspond. As Kepler said, the scientist is "thinking God's thoughts after Him."

PART SEVEN: HUMILITY IN BOTH SCIENCE AND THEOLOGY

A proper relationship between science and faith requires humility on both sides. Scientists must be humble enough to acknowledge the limitations of their discipline — that the scientific method, powerful as it is, cannot answer every question, and that the natural world points beyond itself to a transcendent reality. Theologians must be humble enough to acknowledge that not every interpretation of Scripture is infallible, and that sometimes our understanding of the biblical text needs to be refined in light of what we learn from careful study of God's creation.

The history of the church provides sobering examples of what happens when theological interpreters are too hasty in their pronouncements about the natural world. The geocentric controversy of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries is perhaps the most famous example. Many church leaders insisted that the Bible taught a geocentric cosmology — that the earth was stationary at the center of the universe — and condemned the heliocentric model as heretical. But the church leaders were wrong — not because the Bible was wrong but because they had confused their interpretation of the Bible with the Bible itself. The Bible does not teach geocentrism. It uses phenomenological language — describing the world as it appears to the human observer — without making scientific pronouncements about the mechanics of the solar system.

This historical lesson teaches us to hold our interpretations of Scripture with appropriate humility, especially on questions where the Bible is speaking in poetic, phenomenological, or non-technical language. It does not teach us to doubt the authority of Scripture. The Bible is infallible. Our interpretations of the Bible are not.

The Westminster Confession itself models this humility. While it affirms the fundamental truths of creation — that God made the world, that He made it from nothing, that He made it good — it does not pronounce upon every question of natural philosophy. The Confession provides a theological framework within which scientific investigation can proceed — the framework of a sovereign, wise, and good Creator who has made a rational, orderly world that is worthy of study.

The Puritan scientist and theologian Jonathan Edwards (1703-1758) embodied this integration of scientific curiosity and theological depth. Edwards kept detailed notebooks of his observations of natural phenomena — spiders, rainbows, weather patterns, the behavior of light — and saw in every discovery a new revelation of the glory and wisdom of God. He wrote: "The beauties of nature are really emanations or shadows of the excellency of the Son of God. So that when we are delighted with flowery meadows and gentle breezes, we may consider that we see only the emanations of the sweet benevolence of Jesus Christ."

PART EIGHT: PRACTICAL APPLICATION

How should these truths shape your life as a young Christian?

First, refuse to accept the false choice between science and faith. When someone tells you that you must choose between being a thinking person and being a believing person, recognize this as the myth it is. The greatest scientists in history were believers. The Christian worldview is what made modern science possible in the first place. You do not honor God by being anti-intellectual, and you do not honor science by being anti-God.

Second, study the natural world with wonder and worship. When you study biology, chemistry, physics, astronomy, or any other science, remember that you are studying the works of God. Every law of nature is an expression of His faithful governance. Every living creature is a display of His creative genius. Every mathematical pattern in nature is a reflection of His rational mind. "The works of the LORD are great, sought out of all them that have pleasure therein" (Psalm 111:2, KJV). Let your science lead you to praise.

Third, develop a well-informed understanding of the key areas where science and faith interact. Read thoughtful Christian authors who have engaged seriously with the sciences. Be familiar with the fine-tuning argument, the cosmological argument, the argument from information in DNA, and the argument from consciousness. These are powerful lines of evidence for the Christian worldview, and they equip you to engage confidently with skeptics who claim that science has disproved God.

Fourth, practice intellectual humility. Recognize that both science and theology are human endeavors — they are both subject to error, revision, and growth. The Word of God is infallible, but our interpretations of it are not. The laws of nature are constant, but our scientific theories about them are provisional and subject to correction. Hold your convictions firmly but your interpretations humbly, and be willing to learn from both books that God has given you.

Fifth, be a bridge-builder, not a culture warrior. The world does not need more Christians who are afraid of science, and it does not need more scientists who are contemptuous of faith. It needs thoughtful, humble, well-informed Christians who can demonstrate that the pursuit of truth in every domain — scientific, philosophical, theological — leads ultimately to the God who is the source of all truth. "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge" (Proverbs 1:7, KJV). Let this be the foundation of everything you learn, in every subject, for the rest of your life.

Sixth, consider whether God might be calling you to a career in the sciences. The world desperately needs Christians in the sciences — not Christians who abandon their faith at the laboratory door but Christians who bring their faith with them into every experiment, every equation, every discovery. A Christian physicist, a Christian biologist, a Christian chemist, a Christian physician — each one has the opportunity to demonstrate the compatibility of faith and science, to bear witness to the Creator in the very act of studying His creation, and to serve the common good through the application of scientific knowledge. The Puritan doctrine of vocation — the teaching that every lawful calling is a service to God — applies to the sciences as much as to the ministry. If God has given you a mind for science, use it for His glory.

Seventh, learn to distinguish between scientific data and philosophical interpretations of that data. Much of the apparent conflict between science and faith arises not from the data itself but from the materialistic philosophical framework within which the data is interpreted. The data tell us what happens in the natural world. The philosophical interpretation tells us what the data mean. A fossil is a fact. The theory that the fossil proves there is no God is not a fact — it is a philosophical conclusion that goes far beyond the data. Learn to spot the difference between empirical evidence and metaphysical interpretation, and you will find that many of the supposed conflicts between science and faith simply dissolve.

The Puritan minister Matthew Poole (1624-1679), in his Synopsis Criticorum, demonstrated the importance of careful interpretation: "Let us not confound the word of God with our interpretation of the word of God, nor the works of God with our interpretation of the works of God. God's word and God's works are both infallible. Our interpretations of them may err. Humility in interpretation is the mark of true learning."

--- Closing ---

Science and faith are not enemies. They are fellow travelers on the road to truth — two streams flowing from the same divine fountain. The God who inspired the Scriptures is the same God who created the natural world. The God who speaks through prophets and apostles is the same God who speaks through the stars and the cells and the laws of physics. To study His Word is worship. To study His world is worship. And both lead the humble heart to the same destination: the throne of the Almighty, where all knowledge finds its source and all understanding finds its rest. As the Puritan Robert Boyle wrote: "The study of the Book of Nature does not draw us away from the Book of Scripture, but leads us more deeply into it. For both books tell the same story — the story of a God whose wisdom is unsearchable, whose power is limitless, and whose goodness endures forever." Let that truth inspire you, students, as you pursue knowledge in every field, for the glory of God and the good of His world. Until next time.
