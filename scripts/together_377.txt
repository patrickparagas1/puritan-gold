PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 16
"What Is Thy Beloved More Than Another?"
Song of Solomon 5:9-16 — Describing the One You Love

---

Welcome, dear couple. After the painful separation of last night's passage, the bride is asked a question by the daughters of Jerusalem — and her answer reveals the depth of her love even in the midst of distance. Tonight is about knowing your spouse so well that you can describe them to the world.

Song of Solomon 5:9-16:

"What is thy beloved more than another beloved, O thou fairest among women? what is thy beloved more than another beloved, that thou dost so charge us? My beloved is white and ruddy, the chiefest among ten thousand. His head is as the most fine gold, his locks are bushy, and black as a raven. His eyes are as the eyes of doves by the rivers of waters, washed with milk, and fitly set. His cheeks are as a bed of spices, as sweet flowers: his lips like lilies, dropping sweet smelling myrrh. His hands are as gold rings set with the beryl: his belly is as bright ivory overlaid with sapphires. His legs are as pillars of marble, set upon sockets of fine gold: his countenance is as Lebanon, excellent as the cedars. His mouth is most sweet: yea, he is altogether lovely. This is my beloved, and this is my friend, O daughters of Jerusalem."

--- Teaching ---

The daughters of Jerusalem ask a pointed question: "What is thy beloved more than another beloved?" In other words: What makes him special? Why are you so desperate to find him? What sets him apart from every other man? This is a question that every married person should be able to answer. If someone asked you, "What makes your spouse different from everyone else?" — could you answer at length, with specificity, with genuine admiration?

The bride's response is extraordinary. She launches into a head-to-toe catalogue of her beloved's beauty — his head, his locks, his eyes, his cheeks, his lips, his hands, his belly, his legs, his countenance, his mouth. She has studied him. She knows him. She can describe him in vivid, personal detail. This is not a generic appreciation; this is intimate knowledge.

William Gouge wrote that one of the distinguishing marks of a healthy marriage is that each spouse is a student of the other. You should know your spouse better than anyone else knows them — their quirks, their fears, their deepest desires, the way their face changes when they are thinking, the tone of their voice when they are holding something back. This knowledge is not acquired in a day. It is the fruit of years of attentive, loving observation.

"My beloved is white and ruddy, the chiefest among ten thousand." He stands out. He is not merely one among many. He is the chiefest — the preeminent one. The Puritans applied this practically: when you speak about your spouse to others, do you elevate them or diminish them? It is a dangerous habit to complain about your spouse to friends, to make jokes at their expense, to compare them unfavorably to others. The bride sets a different standard. When asked about her beloved, she speaks only in superlatives.

Thomas Gataker urged married couples to develop what he called "the habit of commendation" — the regular practice of speaking well of each other, both in private and in public. He wrote that a wife who praises her husband in the hearing of others builds a shield around their marriage. A husband who speaks highly of his wife in public creates a fortress of honor around her. Conversely, a spouse who criticizes their partner publicly tears down the very walls that protect their home.

Notice the details: "His hands are as gold rings set with the beryl." She admires his hands — the instruments of his work, his provision, his tenderness. "His legs are as pillars of marble, set upon sockets of fine gold." She admires his strength, his stability, his reliability. These are not merely physical descriptions; they are character descriptions clothed in imagery. His hands are precious because of what they do. His legs are pillars because of where they stand.

"His mouth is most sweet." She saves the best for last. His words, his kisses, his conversation — they are sweet. Richard Sibbes wrote that in marriage, the mouth serves a double purpose: it speaks words that build up the soul, and it offers kisses that warm the heart. Both are essential. A marriage without kind words is as loveless as a marriage without physical affection.

And then the climactic declaration: "Yea, he is altogether lovely. This is my beloved, and this is my friend." Two words: beloved and friend. These two dimensions of marriage — romantic love and genuine friendship — must be held together. The passion of lovers without the companionship of friends burns out. The companionship of friends without the passion of lovers grows cold. The Song holds them together: this is my beloved, and this is my friend.

Tonight, answer the question: What is your beloved more than another? Take time to describe your spouse — in detail, with admiration, with the specificity of someone who has studied their text well.

--- Reflection Prompts ---

Turn to each other and consider these questions.

Question 1: Take turns answering the daughters' question: What makes your spouse different from anyone else? Be specific. Name at least five things.

Question 2: When you speak about your spouse to friends, family, or colleagues, what do you typically say? Is it characterized by commendation or complaint?

Question 3: "This is my beloved, and this is my friend." On a scale of one to ten, how strong is the friendship dimension of your marriage? What would strengthen it?

--- Prayer Focus ---

Tonight's prayer focus: Thank God for the specific qualities you see in your spouse. Pray over each other by name, giving thanks for the particular beauties — physical, emotional, spiritual — that make your beloved unlike any other person in the world.

--- Closing ---

He is altogether lovely. She is altogether lovely. This is your beloved, and this is your friend. May those words ring true in your hearts tonight and every night. Goodnight, dear couple.
