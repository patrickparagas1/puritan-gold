PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Reading, Day 15
"Keeping the Heart"
John Flavel's Classic on Guarding the Heart

---

Good evening, friend. Tonight we turn to one of the most searching and practical works in the Puritan library: John Flavel's "Keeping the Heart." This small but mighty book takes a single verse of Scripture and unfolds it into a comprehensive guide for the inner life. If you have ever struggled with wandering thoughts, divided affections, or a heart that feels cold toward God, Flavel writes for you.

--- Scripture ---

Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Proverbs 4:23:

"Keep thy heart with all diligence; for out of it are the issues of life."

Hold this word in your heart as we continue. It is the single verse around which Flavel builds his entire treatise, and it is the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.

--- Teaching ---

John Flavel was a Puritan pastor in Dartmouth, England, during the latter half of the seventeenth century. He was ejected from his pulpit by the Act of Uniformity in 1662 and spent years preaching in secret — in homes, in the woods, even on an island in the harbor. His writings bear the marks of a man who knew what it meant to keep the faith under pressure. "Keeping the Heart" is perhaps his most enduring work, and its central argument is simple but profound: the greatest and most important business of the Christian life is the diligent keeping of the heart.

Flavel defines heart-keeping as the diligent, constant care and oversight of the inner life. It is the work of maintaining the soul in a fit condition for communion with God and for faithful service. The heart is the seat of all spiritual life. As the fountain is to the streams, so the heart is to the thoughts, words, and actions. If the fountain is polluted, every stream that flows from it will carry the pollution. If the heart is neglected, every department of life will suffer the consequences. This is why Solomon says to keep it "with all diligence" — not with casual attention, not with occasional effort, but with the vigilance of a soldier guarding a city under siege.

Flavel identifies several seasons in which the heart is particularly difficult to keep. The first is prosperity. When life goes well, the heart has a natural tendency to become careless, proud, and worldly. Flavel observes that more Christians are ruined by the sunshine of prosperity than by the storms of adversity. The remedy is to remember that every good thing is a gift from God, not a reward for your goodness, and that the same hand that gave can also take away. Thomas Brooks similarly warned that a prosperous condition is a slippery condition, and the soul must hold fast to God with both hands when the world offers its best.

The second season is adversity. When suffering comes — illness, loss, disappointment, persecution — the heart tends toward despondency, bitterness, and unbelief. Flavel counsels the suffering believer to fix his eyes not on the present trial but on the sovereign God who ordains it. Every affliction is measured, timed, and purposeful. Not a single tear falls to the ground unnoticed. "Weeping may endure for a night, but joy cometh in the morning" (Psalm 30:5).

The third season is spiritual desertion — those dark nights when God seems to have withdrawn His presence and the soul feels utterly alone. Flavel writes with great tenderness here, for he knew this darkness from personal experience. He counsels the deserted soul not to judge God's love by present feelings but by His unchangeable promises. The absence of felt comfort is not the absence of real grace. Richard Sibbes taught the same truth: God sometimes hides His face not because He is angry but because He wants us to seek Him more earnestly and to value His presence more deeply when it returns.

Flavel's method for keeping the heart is not complicated, but it requires daily effort. He prescribes self-examination, prayer, meditation upon Scripture, and the faithful use of the means of grace. The gardener does not tend his garden once a year and expect a harvest. He works at it daily — pulling weeds, watering roots, trimming branches. So must the Christian tend the garden of his heart.

--- Application ---

First, begin the practice of daily self-examination. Set aside a few minutes each evening to ask: Where was my heart today? Was it drawn toward God or away from Him? Was it ruled by faith or by fear? This is not morbid introspection; it is the honest maintenance of the inner life.

Second, identify which season you are in right now — prosperity, adversity, or spiritual dryness — and apply Flavel's specific counsel for that season. The heart needs different care in different conditions, just as the body needs different treatment in different climates.

Third, guard the inputs to your heart. Flavel understood that the heart does not corrupt itself in a vacuum. What you read, what you dwell upon, what you give your attention to in idle moments — these are the streams that feed the fountain. Guard the streams, and you guard the fountain.

--- Closing ---

O Lord, we confess that we have too often neglected the keeping of our hearts. We have guarded our reputations and our possessions while leaving our inner lives untended. Teach us the diligence that Flavel described. Set a watch over our hearts, and let the issues that flow from them bring glory to Thy name. Through Christ our Lord, Amen.

Goodnight, friend. Keep thy heart. Out of it are the issues of life.
