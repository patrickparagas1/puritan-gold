PURITAN GOLD -- Study Topic: The Book of Melchizedek, Part 1 of 8
"The Mysterious King of Salem"
The Book of Melchizedek — Genesis 14:17-20 — Melchizedek's first appearance

---

Beloved friends, welcome to a new study series on Puritan Gold. Over the next eight episodes, we shall examine one of the most mysterious and glorious figures in all of Scripture — Melchizedek, the King of Salem, the priest of the most high God. His appearance in the Bible is brief, his origins are hidden, and his significance is immense. The writer of Hebrews devoted an entire chapter to him, and the Puritan divines — men like John Owen, Thomas Goodwin, and Stephen Charnock — returned to him again and again as one of the most profound types of Christ in all the Old Testament.

Let us read from Genesis 14:17-20:

"And the king of Sodom went out to meet him after his return from the slaughter of Chedorlaomer, and of the kings that were with him, at the valley of Shaveh, which is the king's dale. And Melchizedek king of Salem brought forth bread and wine: and he was the priest of the most high God. And he blessed him, and said, Blessed be Abram of the most high God, possessor of heaven and earth: And blessed be the most high God, which hath delivered thine enemies into thy hand. And he gave him tithes of all."

--- Teaching ---

The scene is set in the aftermath of battle. Abraham — still called Abram at this point — has just conducted a daring military rescue. His nephew Lot had been captured when the kings of the east, led by Chedorlaomer king of Elam, defeated the coalition of Sodom, Gomorrah, and their allies. Upon hearing of Lot's captivity, Abraham armed his 318 trained servants, pursued the invaders, defeated them in a night raid, and recovered Lot along with all the people and goods that had been taken.

It is upon Abraham's victorious return that two kings come out to meet him: the king of Sodom and Melchizedek, king of Salem. The contrast between these two figures is deliberate and instructive. The king of Sodom represents the world — its corruption, its values, its temptations. Melchizedek represents something altogether different — a heavenly priesthood, a divine blessing, a ministry that points forward to Christ Himself.

Notice the details of Melchizedek's appearance. He comes bearing bread and wine. He is identified as both a king and a priest — "king of Salem" and "priest of the most high God." He blesses Abraham in the name of God Most High, possessor of heaven and earth. And Abraham, the father of the faithful, the friend of God, gives him tithes of all the spoils of battle.

Each of these details is laden with theological significance. The bread and wine are, to the Christian reader, inescapably suggestive of the Lord's Supper. The Puritan commentator Matthew Henry observed that Melchizedek's offering of bread and wine was "a type and figure of the eucharist, in which Christ confirms His covenant with believers." While we must be careful not to over-allegorize, the typological connection is ancient — the early church fathers saw it, the medieval theologians saw it, and the Reformers saw it.

The dual office of king and priest is remarkable because in the Levitical system that would later be established under Moses, these two offices were strictly separated. The kings of Israel came from the tribe of Judah; the priests came from the tribe of Levi. When King Uzziah attempted to burn incense in the temple — a priestly function — he was struck with leprosy (2 Chronicles 26:16-21). The separation was absolute and inviolable. Yet here, centuries before Moses, centuries before the Levitical priesthood was even conceived, stands a figure who holds both offices simultaneously. He is a king who is also a priest, a ruler who is also a mediator between God and man.

The name "Melchizedek" is itself a compound of two Hebrew words: melek, meaning "king," and tsedeq, meaning "righteousness." He is the "King of Righteousness." And he is king of "Salem" — a word related to shalom, meaning "peace." He is thus the King of Righteousness and the King of Peace. As the writer of Hebrews would later expound, these names are not coincidental but providential — they point to the character of the One whom Melchizedek foreshadows.

Who was this man? The text of Genesis tells us nothing of his genealogy, his birth, or his death. He appears without introduction and disappears without farewell. In any other narrative, this silence would be unremarkable — many minor figures in Genesis receive no genealogical notice. But the writer of Hebrews seizes upon this silence and draws from it a profound theological conclusion: Melchizedek is "without father, without mother, without descent, having neither beginning of days, nor end of life; but made like unto the Son of God; abideth a priest continually" (Hebrews 7:3).

The silence of Scripture about Melchizedek's origins is not an accident of incomplete records but a deliberate feature of the divine narrative — a gap left by the Holy Spirit for the purpose of typology. The absence of genealogical information makes Melchizedek a fitting type of Christ, whose priesthood is not derived from human ancestry but from divine appointment.

John Owen, in his monumental commentary on the Epistle to the Hebrews, devoted extensive attention to this point. Owen argued that the Holy Spirit deliberately withheld information about Melchizedek's parentage, birth, and death so that he might serve as a more perfect type of Christ. The silence was not an oversight but a design. God was constructing a figure in the historical narrative that would foreshadow the eternal priesthood of His Son — a priesthood not based on genealogy, not limited by mortality, not confined to a single tribe or nation.

The fact that Abraham gave tithes to Melchizedek is also profoundly significant. Abraham was the greatest patriarch of Israel — the father of the nation, the recipient of God's covenant promises. Yet he acknowledged the superiority of Melchizedek by giving him a tenth of all the spoils. The greater blesses the lesser, and the lesser pays tithes to the greater. If Melchizedek was greater than Abraham, then his priesthood was greater than any priesthood that would descend from Abraham — including the Levitical priesthood of Aaron and his sons.

This is the argument that the writer of Hebrews will develop at length in chapter 7, and it is the argument that we shall trace through the remaining episodes of this series. But for now, let us simply marvel at the appearance of this extraordinary figure in the pages of Genesis — a king-priest who blesses the father of the faithful, who receives tithes from the friend of God, and who foreshadows the One who is both our King and our great High Priest, the Lord Jesus Christ.

--- Application ---

1. See in Melchizedek the beauty of Christ's dual office. Jesus is not only your King who rules over you but your Priest who intercedes for you. He governs your life with authority and mediates your salvation with mercy.

2. Recognize that God was preparing the way for Christ from the earliest chapters of Scripture. Melchizedek appears in Genesis 14 — long before the law, long before the Levitical priesthood — to show that God's plan of redemption through a king-priest was ancient and eternal.

3. Learn from Abraham's humility. The greatest man in the land gave tithes to Melchizedek without hesitation. True greatness recognizes and submits to the authority of God's appointed ministers.

4. Receive the bread and wine of communion with fresh eyes. When you come to the Lord's Table, remember that this meal has typological roots stretching back to the valley of Shaveh, where Melchizedek brought forth bread and wine to bless the father of the faithful.

--- Closing ---

O God Most High, possessor of heaven and earth, who didst appoint Melchizedek as both king and priest to bless Thy servant Abraham — we praise Thee for the mystery and beauty of this ancient type. Open our eyes to see in Melchizedek the shadow of our Lord Jesus Christ, who is our King of Righteousness and our King of Peace, our eternal Priest who ever liveth to make intercession for us. In His most holy name we pray. Amen.

Grace and peace be unto you, beloved. May the King of Salem bless you with His peace until we meet again.
