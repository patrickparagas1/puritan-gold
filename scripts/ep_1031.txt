PURITAN GOLD -- Melchizedek: A Complete Biblical Study, Part 1 of 8
Genesis 14:1-24 — The Historical Encounter

---

Beloved friends, welcome to the beginning of a new study series on Puritan Gold — a complete biblical study of Melchizedek. Over the next eight episodes, we shall read every relevant passage of Scripture concerning this mysterious and glorious figure, and draw out the deep theological treasures that the Puritan divines found in these texts. This is not a brief survey. This is a full audiobook study — the kind of thorough, reverent, passage-by-passage exposition that our Puritan forefathers believed every doctrine of Scripture deserved.

We begin where the Bible begins with Melchizedek — in Genesis chapter 14. But we shall not merely read the four verses where Melchizedek appears. We shall read the entire chapter, for the context matters deeply. The Puritan commentators — men like Matthew Henry and John Gill — always insisted that we understand the circumstances surrounding a text. So let us hear the full account.

Genesis 14, beginning at verse 1:

"And it came to pass in the days of Amraphel king of Shinar, Arioch king of Ellasar, Chedorlaomer king of Elam, and Tidal king of nations; that these made war with Bera king of Sodom, and with Birsha king of Gomorrah, Shinab king of Admah, and Shemeber king of Zeboiim, and the king of Bela, which is Zoar. All these were joined together in the vale of Siddim, which is the salt sea. Twelve years they served Chedorlaomer, and in the thirteenth year they rebelled. And in the fourteenth year came Chedorlaomer, and the kings that were with him, and smote the Rephaims in Ashteroth Karnaim, and the Zuzims in Ham, and the Emims in Shaveh Kiriathaim, and the Horites in their mount Seir, unto Elparan, which is by the wilderness. And they returned, and came to Enmishpat, which is Kadesh, and smote all the country of the Amalekites, and also the Amorites, that dwelt in Hazezontamar."

"And there went out the king of Sodom, and the king of Gomorrah, and the king of Admah, and the king of Zeboiim, and the king of Bela (the same is Zoar;) and they joined battle with them in the vale of Siddim; with Chedorlaomer the king of Elam, and with Tidal king of nations, and Amraphel king of Shinar, and Arioch king of Ellasar; four kings with five. And the vale of Siddim was full of slimepits; and the kings of Sodom and Gomorrah fled, and fell there; and they that remained fled to the mountain. And they took all the goods of Sodom and Gomorrah, and all their victuals, and went their way. And they took Lot, Abram's brother's son, who dwelt in Sodom, and his goods, and departed."

"And there came one that had escaped, and told Abram the Hebrew; for he dwelt in the plain of Mamre the Amorite, brother of Eshcol, and brother of Aner: and these were confederate with Abram. And when Abram heard that his brother was taken captive, he armed his trained servants, born in his own house, three hundred and eighteen, and pursued them unto Dan. And he divided himself against them, he and his servants, by night, and smote them, and pursued them unto Hobah, which is on the left hand of Damascus. And he brought back all the goods, and also brought again his brother Lot, and his goods, and the women also, and the people."

Now we come to the critical passage — verses 17 through 24:

"And the king of Sodom went out to meet him after his return from the slaughter of Chedorlaomer, and of the kings that were with him, at the valley of Shaveh, which is the king's dale. And Melchizedek king of Salem brought forth bread and wine: and he was the priest of the most high God. And he blessed him, and said, Blessed be Abram of the most high God, possessor of heaven and earth: and blessed be the most high God, which hath delivered thine enemies into thy hand. And he gave him tithes of all."

"And the king of Sodom said unto Abram, Give me the persons, and take the goods to thyself. And Abram said to the king of Sodom, I have lift up mine hand unto the LORD, the most high God, the possessor of heaven and earth, that I will not take from a thread even to a shoelatchet, and that I will not take any thing that is thine, lest thou shouldest say, I have made Abram rich: save only that which the young men have eaten, and the portion of the men which went with me, Aner, Eshcol, and Mamre; let them take their portion."

--- Teaching ---

Now, beloved, let us draw out the riches of this passage as the Puritan fathers taught us to do.

First, observe the setting. This is the first war recorded in Scripture. The invasion of the four eastern kings under Chedorlaomer represents the violence and turmoil of a fallen world. Into this scene of conquest and chaos, two figures emerge to meet Abraham after the battle — Melchizedek king of Salem, and the king of Sodom. Matthew Henry writes that these two figures represent the two great choices that face every believer after a spiritual victory: the way of blessing from God, or the way of worldly enrichment from ungodly alliances. Abraham chose rightly. He received Melchizedek's blessing and refused the king of Sodom's offer.

Consider the name Melchizedek itself. It is composed of two Hebrew words — melek, meaning king, and tsedek, meaning righteousness. He is the King of Righteousness. And he is king of Salem — the word shalom — meaning peace. The writer of Hebrews will later draw this out explicitly. He is first King of Righteousness, and after that King of Peace. The Puritans noted the order with great care. John Owen observes that righteousness must precede peace. There can be no true peace with God apart from the establishment of righteousness. Christ first satisfies the demands of divine justice, and then — and only then — does He become our peace.

Notice the actions Melchizedek performs. He brought forth bread and wine. He blessed Abraham. And Abraham gave him tithes of all. Each of these actions carries immense typological significance.

The bread and wine have been understood by the church throughout history as a foreshadowing of the Lord's Supper. The Puritan Thomas Goodwin sees in this a picture of Christ refreshing His people after their spiritual battles. Just as Melchizedek met Abraham with sustenance after the weariness of combat, so Christ meets His church at the table of communion with spiritual nourishment — His own body and blood, given for us. This is not a mere coincidence of elements. The Spirit of God, who inspired the Scriptures, chose to record these particular details because they point forward to the priestly ministry of Christ.

The blessing Melchizedek pronounced is twofold. He blessed Abraham in the name of the most high God, possessor of heaven and earth. And he blessed God for delivering Abraham's enemies into his hand. Observe that Melchizedek blesses downward — from a position of superiority. The writer of Hebrews will argue from this that Melchizedek was greater than Abraham, for the lesser is blessed of the greater. If Melchizedek was greater than Abraham, then the priesthood after the order of Melchizedek is greater than the priesthood of Levi, for Levi was in Abraham's loins when Abraham received this blessing.

The title El Elyon — the most high God, possessor of heaven and earth — is used four times in this passage. It is the first occurrence of this divine title in Scripture. Matthew Henry notes that this title declares God's absolute sovereignty over all creation. He possesses — He owns, He rules, He governs — all things in heaven and earth. This is the God whom Melchizedek serves as priest.

And then we read that Abraham gave him tithes of all. This is a voluntary act of worship and recognition. No law commanded it. The Mosaic code had not yet been given. Abraham tithed freely, acknowledging Melchizedek's priestly authority. John Owen draws from this a powerful argument: the principle of tithing did not originate with Moses but with the patriarchs. It is rooted not in Levitical law but in the recognition of God's sovereign ownership and His appointed priesthood. Abraham tithed to Melchizedek because he recognized in this priest-king a divinely appointed representative of the most high God.

Finally, observe the stunning contrast between Melchizedek and the king of Sodom. One offers bread and wine — spiritual sustenance and blessing. The other offers worldly goods — take the spoils for yourself. Abraham receives from the priest and refuses from the king of wickedness. He will not have it said that the king of Sodom made him rich. His wealth comes from God alone. Stephen Charnock remarks that this is the pattern for every believer. We must receive our blessings from Christ our High Priest and refuse the entanglements of the world, lest we become indebted to those who would enslave our souls.

This is the first appearance of Melchizedek — sudden, majestic, and deeply significant. He appears without introduction, without genealogy, without explanation. He simply is there — priest of the most high God, king of righteousness, king of peace. And then he vanishes from the narrative as suddenly as he appeared. For a thousand years, Scripture will be silent about him. But when the silence breaks, it will break with thunderous force — in the prophetic declaration of Psalm 110.

That shall be our study in the next episode. Until then, may the blessing of the most high God, possessor of heaven and earth, rest upon you. Amen.
