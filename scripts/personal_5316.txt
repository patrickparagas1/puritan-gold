PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Pastoral Counsel, Day 16
"Fasting and Self-Denial"
The Forgotten Discipline


Good evening, friend. Tonight we take up a spiritual discipline that has largely fallen out of practice in the modern church: the discipline of fasting. The Puritans regarded fasting not as a legalistic burden but as a powerful aid to prayer, repentance, and the deepening of the soul's hunger for God. In an age of comfort and instant gratification, their wisdom on this subject is more needed than ever.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Matthew 6:17-18:

"But thou, when thou fastest, anoint thine head, and wash thy face; That thou appear not unto men to fast, but unto thy Father which is in secret: and thy Father, which seeth in secret, shall reward thee openly."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


Notice that Jesus does not say "if you fast" but "when you fast." He assumes that His followers will practice this discipline. It is not a question of whether but of when and how. And yet, in many churches today, fasting is treated as an oddity, an extreme measure reserved for extraordinary circumstances. The Puritans would find this neglect baffling. For them, fasting was a regular and ordinary part of the Christian life.

What is fasting? In its simplest form, fasting is the voluntary abstinence from food for a spiritual purpose. It is not a diet. It is not a health regimen. It is not an act of self-punishment. It is a deliberate setting aside of physical appetite in order to focus more intently upon God. The Puritans understood that the body and the soul are intimately connected, and that the discipline of the body can serve the devotion of the soul.

Richard Baxter identifies several purposes for fasting in his pastoral writings. The first is to express and deepen repentance. When the soul is burdened with the consciousness of sin, fasting gives physical expression to spiritual grief. The body joins the soul in humbling itself before God. This is the pattern we see throughout Scripture: Daniel fasted when he confessed the sins of his people. The people of Nineveh fasted when they heard the prophet's warning. David fasted when he pleaded for mercy. In each case, fasting was not a means of earning God's favor but an expression of earnest dependence upon it.

The second purpose is to sharpen prayer. The Puritans observed that fasting clears the mind and intensifies the soul's focus. When the body is denied its ordinary comforts, the spirit is freed to seek God with undivided attention. Matthew Henry writes that fasting is the wing of prayer — it helps the soul ascend higher than it otherwise could. Many believers throughout the centuries have found that their most powerful experiences of prayer came during seasons of fasting.

The third purpose is to cultivate self-denial. The Puritans believed that the Christian life requires a constant discipline of saying no to the flesh and yes to the Spirit. Fasting is one of the most basic exercises in this discipline. If you cannot deny your stomach for a single day, how will you deny more powerful temptations when they come? Fasting trains the will. It teaches the body that it is not master. It reminds the believer that "man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of God" (Matthew 4:4).

Thomas Watson adds a fourth purpose: fasting expresses our longing for God Himself. There is a kind of fasting that goes beyond repentance and petition — a fasting that simply says, "Lord, I want more of Thee. I want Thee more than food, more than comfort, more than the ordinary pleasures of life. I am willing to go without these lesser things in order to seek the greater thing: Thy presence." This is the hunger of the Psalmist who cried, "As the hart panteth after the water brooks, so panteth my soul after thee, O God" (Psalm 42:1).

How should we begin? The Puritans offer practical counsel. First, start modestly. If you have never fasted, do not attempt three days. Begin with a single meal. Skip breakfast and spend the time you would have spent eating in prayer and Scripture reading. As your body and spirit grow accustomed to the discipline, you may extend the fast gradually. Second, fast with a specific purpose. Do not fast merely to fast. Bring a specific burden, a specific question, a specific sin before the Lord. Third, fast in secret. Jesus warns against fasting for the admiration of others. The reward of fasting is not human applause but divine communion. Fourth, always combine fasting with prayer and the Word. Fasting without prayer is merely going hungry. The time freed by abstaining from meals should be devoted to seeking God.


First, consider setting aside one day this month for a simple fast. Choose a day, plan your spiritual focus, and give the hours you would normally spend eating to prayer and Scripture.

Second, examine your relationship to comfort. Are you a servant of your appetites, or do your appetites serve you? Fasting reveals the answer with uncomfortable clarity.

Third, remember that self-denial is not self-punishment. It is the joyful discipline of one who has found something better than what he is giving up. You fast not because food is bad but because God is better.


O Lord, teach us the forgotten discipline of fasting. Free us from our slavery to comfort and appetite. Give us a hunger for Thee that surpasses our hunger for bread. When we deny ourselves, fill us with Thy presence. Through Christ, who fasted forty days and conquered every temptation, Amen.

Goodnight, friend. May your hunger for God grow deeper than your hunger for anything this world can offer.
