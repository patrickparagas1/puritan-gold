PURITAN GOLD — Episode 8
"Flee from Temptation"
Week 2, Day 1 (Monday) — Proverbs 5

---

"For the ways of man are before the eyes of the Lord, and He pondereth all his goings." Proverbs 5:21.

Let that sink in for a moment... Every step you take. Every thought that crosses your mind. Every glance, every inclination of the heart — God sees it all. He doesn't merely observe from a distance, the way we might glance at a stranger across the street. No. He ponders it. He weighs it. He considers every path you walk down.

This is Puritan Gold, and today we begin our second week together. We're walking through Proverbs chapters five through eight this week, and we're pairing that journey with the rich theology of A.W. Pink. Our theme this week is "Walking in Righteousness," and I can think of no better place to begin than right here — with the warning that opens Proverbs chapter five.

Now, if you've read Proverbs 5 before, you know that on the surface, this chapter is a father's warning to his son about the danger of adultery. And we should take that at face value — it is exactly that. But as we dig deeper, I want you to see something broader here. Solomon isn't just warning against one particular sin. He's giving us a picture — a vivid, unforgettable picture — of how all temptation works. How it lures. How it flatters. How it destroys.

Let's begin at the start of the chapter. Proverbs 5, verses 3 and 4:

"For the lips of a strange woman drop as an honeycomb, and her mouth is smoother than oil: But her end is bitter as wormwood, sharp as a two-edged sword."

Do you see the contrast? Honey... and wormwood. Smoothness... and a sword. That's what temptation does, friends. It presents itself draped in sweetness. It speaks with a voice that's smoother than oil. It promises pleasure, comfort, satisfaction. But its end — and notice Solomon doesn't say "its possible end" or "its occasional end" — its end... is bitterness. Its end is a blade that cuts to the bone.

A.W. Pink, in his Studies in the Scriptures, made an observation that I think is deeply relevant here. He noted that Satan is never more dangerous than when he appears as an angel of light. The temptations that destroy us are rarely the ones that announce themselves with trumpets and red flags. No... the most dangerous temptations are the ones that whisper. The ones that seem reasonable. The ones that dress themselves up in almost-truth.

Think about it in your own life. When have you been most vulnerable to sin? I'd wager it wasn't in the moments when the choice was obvious — when good and evil were clearly marked. It was in the gray areas. It was when the temptation came wearing a disguise. "It's not that bad." "Everyone does this." "God will understand." "Just this once."

Honeycomb. Smooth oil. That's how it starts.

Solomon continues in verses 7 through 9:

"Hear me now therefore, O ye children, and depart not from the words of my mouth. Remove thy way far from her, and come not nigh the door of her house: Lest thou give thine honour unto others, and thy years unto the cruel."

Now I want you to notice something critically important here. Solomon does not say, "Walk up to the door and resist." He does not say, "Stand at the threshold and prove your strength." He says remove thy way far from her. Come not nigh the door. In other words — flee. Don't test yourself. Don't see how close you can get to the fire without being burned. Get away from it entirely.

This is wisdom that our modern age desperately needs to hear. We live in a culture that celebrates proximity to temptation. We're told that real strength is being able to stand in the middle of the fire and not get burned. But that's not what Scripture teaches. Scripture teaches that wisdom... is not walking into the fire in the first place.

The Apostle Paul echoed this same principle centuries later when he wrote to young Timothy: "Flee also youthful lusts." Not "resist." Not "manage." Flee. Run. Get out. There is no shame in running from what would destroy you. In fact, there is great wisdom in it.

Pink wrote powerfully about this in his treatment of the believer's daily walk. He argued that the Christian life is not merely about resisting sin — it's about cultivating such a love for holiness, such a delight in God, that sin loses its attractiveness. And that begins with honesty. Honesty about our own weakness. Honesty about the places where we're vulnerable. Honesty about the doors we keep walking past, telling ourselves we'd never go in... even as our steps slow down every time.

Let me ask you something, and I want you to sit with this honestly... What is your honeycomb? What is that thing in your life that speaks with a smooth voice? That promises satisfaction? That seems harmless on the surface but, if you're honest, you know is leading you away from the Lord?

For some of you, it may be literal — a relationship that's pulling you somewhere you shouldn't go. For others, it may be more subtle. An addiction you keep minimizing. A habit of gossip you've dressed up as "concern." A love of money that you've renamed "being responsible." A screen that's become a window into things your eyes were never meant to see.

The specifics differ, but the pattern is always the same. Always. Honeycomb first. Wormwood later.

Now let's turn to the latter part of the chapter, because Solomon doesn't just warn — he provides a beautiful alternative. Look at verses 15 through 18:

"Drink waters out of thine own cistern, and running waters out of thine own well. Let thy fountains be dispersed abroad, and rivers of waters in the streets. Let them be only thine own, and not strangers' with thee. Let thy fountain be blessed: and rejoice with the wife of thy youth."

Here is the positive vision. Solomon is saying: the antidote to forbidden pleasure is not grim, joyless abstinence. The antidote is right pleasure. Godly pleasure. The deep, satisfying, life-giving water of the good gifts God has already given you.

This is so important, friends. Too many Christians think of obedience as deprivation. As if God is a cosmic killjoy who dangles pleasures in front of us and then snatches them away. But that's not the God of Scripture. The God of Scripture is the One who gives us "richly all things to enjoy," as Paul wrote to Timothy. He's the God who invented joy. Who created pleasure. Who designed the human heart to delight.

The problem with temptation is never that it offers pleasure — it's that it offers a counterfeit pleasure. A poisoned cup dressed up in fine crystal. And God says: Why drink from a poisoned well when I've given you a living fountain?

Pink, in The Attributes of God, wrote about God's goodness in a way that bears directly on this. He argued that everything God commands flows from His goodness. His prohibitions are not arbitrary restrictions — they are the guardrails of love. When God says "flee from this," He is saying "I have something better for you." When He says "do not go near that door," He is saying "there is a door I want to open for you that leads to life."

Do you believe that? Do you really believe that God's commands are good? That His restrictions are kindness? Because if you do — truly, deeply, in the marrow of your bones — then obedience stops being a burden and starts becoming a delight.

Now let's come to those closing verses that we began with. Proverbs 5:21 through 23:

"For the ways of man are before the eyes of the Lord, and He pondereth all his goings. His own iniquities shall take the wicked himself, and he shall be holden with the cords of his own sins. He shall die without instruction; and in the greatness of his folly he shall go astray."

Three things strike me here.

First — God sees. "The ways of man are before the eyes of the Lord." There is no hidden sin. There is no secret indulgence. There is no private corner of your life where God's gaze does not reach. And this truth should function in two ways in your heart. For the unrepentant, it should be terrifying. You are not getting away with anything. But for the believer — for the one who has been washed in the blood of Christ — this truth is actually comforting. Because the God who sees your sin is the same God who has already forgiven it. He sees... and He loves you still. Not because your sin doesn't matter, but because Christ has already borne its penalty.

Second — sin is its own prison. "He shall be holden with the cords of his own sins." What a picture. The sinner is not bound by external chains. He's bound by his own choices. His own habits. His own patterns. Every time you give in to temptation, you're not just committing an act — you're weaving a cord. And cord by cord, thread by thread, those choices become chains. What began as something you chose becomes something that holds you.

And third — the one who refuses wisdom will "die without instruction." He had every opportunity to learn. Every warning was available. Every signpost was visible. But he chose folly. And in the greatness — notice that word, the greatness — of his folly, he goes astray. Sin doesn't diminish over time if left unchecked. It grows. It expands. Its folly becomes great.

So what do we do with all of this? Let me give you three practical applications as we close.

First, know your weakness. Stop pretending you're stronger than you are. Stop walking past the door and telling yourself you'd never go in. Identify the areas where you're vulnerable, and put distance between yourself and temptation. Change your route. Set up boundaries. Ask someone to hold you accountable. There's no shame in admitting weakness — there's only danger in denying it.

Second, cultivate right pleasures. Don't just run from sin — run toward God. Fill your life with the good gifts He's given you. Invest in your marriage. Nurture your friendships. Delight in your work. Enjoy His creation. Read His Word not as a duty but as a feast. The heart that is full of God has no room for the counterfeits.

Third, live in the light. Remember that God sees all your ways. Let that truth drive you not to despair but to transparency. Confess your sins — to God and to trusted brothers and sisters. Walk in the light, as John says, and the blood of Jesus Christ cleanses us from all sin. The secret life is a dangerous life. The transparent life is a free life.

Friends, temptation will come. It came to Adam in the garden. It came to David on the rooftop. It came even to our Lord in the wilderness. You will not escape it in this life. But you do not have to be mastered by it. The same God who warns you is the God who empowers you. The same Spirit who convicts is the Spirit who strengthens. And the same Christ who was tempted in every way as you are — yet without sin — stands ready to help you in your hour of need.

Let's pray.

Father, we come before You humbled by the honesty of Your Word. You do not flatter us. You do not tell us we are stronger than we are. You warn us, because You love us. Lord, show us our honeycomb — the temptations we've been minimizing, the doors we've been lingering near, the cords we've been weaving around our own hearts. Give us the courage to flee. Give us the wisdom to run. And fill us so full of Your goodness, so satisfied with Your living water, that the poisoned wells of this world lose their appeal entirely. We ask this in the name of Jesus Christ, who conquered every temptation on our behalf. Amen.
