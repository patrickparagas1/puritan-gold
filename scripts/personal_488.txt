PURITAN GOLD -- My Growth, Day 27
"The Doctrine of the Church"
Theological Day — The Nature and Mission of the Body of Christ

---

Welcome. Today is a Theological day. We examine the Puritan understanding of the church — not as a building or an institution, but as the living body of Christ, called out by God for His glory and the advancement of His kingdom.

Let us read Ephesians 1:22-23 and 1 Peter 2:9 (KJV):

"And hath put all things under his feet, and gave him to be the head over all things to the church, Which is his body, the fulness of him that filleth all in all." "But ye are a chosen generation, a royal priesthood, an holy nation, a peculiar people; that ye should shew forth the praises of him who hath called you out of darkness into his marvellous light."

--- Teaching ---

The Puritans had a high doctrine of the church. They did not view church attendance as optional or fellowship as a luxury. They considered the church to be a divine institution — established by Christ, empowered by the Spirit, and essential to the Christian life.

The Puritans distinguished between the church invisible and the church visible. The invisible church consists of all the elect in every age — every person whom God has chosen and redeemed, from Adam to the last believer who will ever live. This church is known perfectly only by God. The visible church consists of all professing believers and their children — the observable community of faith gathered in local congregations around the Word, sacraments, and discipline. The visible church contains both genuine believers and hypocrites, wheat and tares growing together until the final harvest.

The marks of a true church, according to the Puritans, are three. First, the faithful preaching of the Word of God. Where the Scriptures are proclaimed truly and fully, there is a church. Where the Word is silenced, corrupted, or replaced by human invention, the church ceases to function as a church. The Puritans staked their careers and their freedom on this principle, refusing to compromise the preaching of the Gospel even when it cost them dearly.

Second, the proper administration of the sacraments. Baptism and the Lord's Supper, administered according to Christ's institution, are visible marks of a true church. The Puritans debated the details — mode of baptism, frequency of communion, the nature of Christ's presence — but they agreed that these ordinances are essential to the church's identity and life.

Third, the exercise of church discipline. This is the mark most neglected in modern Christianity and most valued by the Puritans. Church discipline means the loving, careful correction of members who fall into persistent sin. It is not punitive but restorative — aimed at bringing the wayward back to repentance and protecting the purity of the congregation. Richard Baxter practiced discipline with such pastoral tenderness that his entire town was transformed. He visited every family, confronted sin with tears rather than thunder, and saw hundreds brought to genuine faith.

The purposes of the church, as the Puritans understood them, are worship, edification, and mission. Worship is the primary purpose — gathering to praise God, hear His Word, celebrate the sacraments, and offer prayer. Edification is the mutual building up of the body — through preaching, teaching, fellowship, and the exercise of spiritual gifts. Mission is the outward thrust — carrying the Gospel to the world, making disciples, serving the poor, and extending the kingdom of God.

The Puritans stressed the necessity of church membership. They did not accept the idea of a free-floating Christian who attends services occasionally but belongs to no congregation and submits to no pastoral oversight. Hebrews 13:17 commands: "Obey them that have the rule over you, and submit yourselves: for they watch for your souls." This presupposes membership — a settled, accountable relationship with a specific congregation and its leaders.

John Owen wrote that the local church is the primary context for spiritual growth. It is in the church that you hear the Word preached, receive the sacraments, exercise your gifts, learn to love difficult people, practice forgiveness, experience discipline, and grow in Christlikeness. The Christian who isolates from the church cuts himself off from the very means God has ordained for his growth.

The Puritans also emphasized the unity of the church. While they held strong convictions about doctrine and practice, they recognized that all true believers are members of one body. Denominational divisions grieved them. Richard Baxter spent much of his later years working for the reunion of Protestant churches, insisting that agreement on essential truths should be enough to maintain fellowship.

--- Closing ---

The church is not optional. It is essential. Find your place. Commit. Serve. Grow. Lord, I thank You for the church — imperfect, messy, sometimes disappointing, but Yours. Help me to love her as Christ loved her. Give me a deeper commitment to my local congregation and a broader love for Your universal body. Amen.
