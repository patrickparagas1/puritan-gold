PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Pastoral Encouragement, Day 2
"Shepherding Your Wife's Soul"
Thomas Watson — Pastoral Counsel for Husbands


Welcome, friend. There are nights when what we need most is not more information but more encouragement. Tonight is one of those nights. Let the Lord speak directly to your heart.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Ephesians 5:25-28:

"Husbands, love your wives, even as Christ also loved the church, and gave himself for it; That he might sanctify and cleanse it with the washing of water by the word, That he might present it to himself a glorious church, not having spot, or wrinkle, or any such thing; but that it should be holy and without blemish. So ought men to love their wives as their own bodies. He that loveth his wife loveth himself."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


The apostle Paul gives husbands the most staggering command in all of Scripture: love your wife as Christ loved the church. This is not a call to mere affection, romantic feeling, or domestic courtesy. It is a call to self-sacrificing, sanctifying, purposeful love that has as its goal the spiritual flourishing of the beloved. Christ's love for the church was not passive; it was active. It was not occasional; it was constant. It was not self-serving; it was self-giving, even unto death.

Thomas Watson, one of the most practical and warm-hearted of the Puritan divines, addresses the duties of the Christian husband with both tenderness and directness. Watson writes in 'The Godly Man's Picture' that the godly man is known not merely by his public piety but by his private character, and nowhere is private character more clearly revealed than in the home. The man who is a saint at church and a tyrant at home is no saint at all.

Watson identifies several marks of a husband who truly loves his wife as Christ loves the church. First, he leads her spiritually. This does not mean lecturing her, correcting her theology at every opportunity, or positioning himself as her spiritual superior. It means taking responsibility for the spiritual atmosphere of the home. It means initiating prayer together, reading Scripture together, discussing the things of God together. Watson writes that the husband who neglects his wife's soul while caring for her body is like a man who polishes the casket while ignoring the corpse within.

Second, Watson counsels that a godly husband bears with his wife's weaknesses patiently. Watson is realistic about marriage: two sinners living in close quarters will inevitably irritate, disappoint, and wound each other. The question is not whether conflict will arise but how it will be handled. Watson writes that the husband who demands perfection from his wife has forgotten how much imperfection God bears in him. The man who is quick to anger and slow to forgive in his marriage has not understood the gospel he professes.

Third, Watson emphasizes that a husband must be tender in his words. The tongue, Watson notes elsewhere, is a small member but capable of enormous destruction. A husband who speaks harshly, sarcastically, or dismissively to his wife is wounding the one he has vowed to cherish. Watson counsels that the Christian husband should speak to his wife as Christ speaks to the church: with words of encouragement, comfort, instruction, and love. Even necessary correction should be delivered with gentleness, not with the harshness that comes from irritation or superiority.

Fourth, Watson addresses the husband's duty to provide not merely material provision but emotional and spiritual presence. The husband who works long hours to provide financial security but is emotionally absent from his wife and children has exchanged the greater duty for the lesser. Watson writes that a wife needs her husband's heart more than his wallet, his attention more than his income, his companionship more than his accomplishments.

Patrick, consider how these counsels apply to your own marriage. Are you shepherding Janelle's soul? Not merely providing for her material needs, not merely sharing a household, but actively pursuing her spiritual good? Do you know the state of her heart — her fears, her hopes, her struggles, her joys? Do you pray for her by name, with specificity, bringing her needs before the throne of grace? Do you create space in your home for spiritual conversation, or has the noise of daily life drowned out the things that matter most?

Watson reminds us that the husband who loves his wife well is imitating Christ. And the husband who fails in this duty is failing in the most basic application of the gospel to daily life. Let today be a day of honest self-examination and renewed commitment to the sacred calling of Christian marriage.


Before we close, take a few moments with these reflection questions.

Question 1: Am I actively shepherding Janelle's soul, or have I allowed our spiritual life together to become passive and routine?

Question 2: When was the last time I asked my wife about the state of her heart and truly listened to her answer?

Question 3: In what specific way can I more faithfully love my wife as Christ loved the church this week?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Father, forgive me for the ways I have fallen short as a husband. Teach me to love Janelle as Christ loves the church: sacrificially, tenderly, and with her spiritual flourishing as my goal. Give me ears to hear, patience to bear, and words that build up rather than tear down. Make me the husband You have called me to be. Amen.

Until next time. May the Lord deepen your faith with every passing day.
