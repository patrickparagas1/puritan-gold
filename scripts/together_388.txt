PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 27
"The Song's Wisdom on Conflict"
Song of Solomon 5:2-6:3 Revisited — Puritan Counsel on Marital Strife

---

Welcome, dear couple. Tonight we revisit the dark center of the Song — the sequence of missed connection, separation, and reconciliation in chapters five and six — and we draw from it the concentrated wisdom of the Puritans on the subject of conflict in marriage.

Let us re-read Song of Solomon 5:6 and 6:3:

"I opened to my beloved; but my beloved had withdrawn himself, and was gone: my soul failed when he spake: I sought him, but I could not find him; I called him, but he gave me no answer... I am my beloved's, and my beloved is mine: he feedeth among the lilies."

--- Teaching ---

The Song of Solomon does not present a fairy tale. It presents a real love story — and real love stories include conflict, distance, and pain. The Puritans were not idealists about marriage. They knew that even the best marriages would face storms. What distinguished their approach was not the avoidance of conflict but the wisdom with which they counseled couples to navigate it.

Richard Baxter, whose Christian Directory contains some of the most extensive pastoral counsel on marriage ever written, identified several principles for handling conflict that remain profoundly relevant today.

First, Baxter taught the principle of charitable interpretation. When your spouse says something that could be taken two ways — one innocent, one hurtful — choose the innocent interpretation. Assume the best. The bride in the Song hesitated not from malice but from comfort. She was not rejecting her beloved; she was merely settled in for the night. But the result was the same as rejection. In marriage, the impact of our actions often exceeds our intent. Charitable interpretation gives your spouse the benefit of the doubt and prevents unnecessary escalation.

Second, Baxter taught the principle of quick reconciliation. "Let not the sun go down upon your wrath" (Ephesians 4:26). The bride's delay turned a small hesitation into a painful ordeal. Had she opened the door immediately, the night would have been one of joy rather than anguish. Baxter wrote that the passage of time does not heal conflict in marriage; it hardens it. What could be resolved with a soft word tonight becomes an entrenched position by tomorrow. Resolve quickly. Open the door while the beloved is still knocking.

Third, William Gouge taught the principle of mutual humility. He wrote that in most marital conflicts, both parties bear some responsibility. The beloved withdrew when the bride hesitated. The bride hesitated when the beloved knocked. Neither was entirely innocent; neither was entirely guilty. Gouge urged couples to resist the temptation of self-righteousness — the posture that says, "I did nothing wrong; this is entirely your fault." Instead, approach every conflict with the question: "What is my part in this?"

Fourth, Thomas Gataker taught the principle of persistent pursuit. The bride did not give up when her beloved could not be found. She kept searching. She asked for help. She endured the blows of the watchmen. And eventually, she found him. Gataker wrote that the spouse who gives up the search too quickly — who says, "I tried, and they did not respond, so I am done" — has not yet loved enough. Persistence is the hallmark of covenant love.

Fifth, Jeremiah Burroughs taught the principle of restored delight. When the bride finally found her beloved, she did not dwell on the pain of the separation. She immediately returned to the language of admiration: "He is altogether lovely." Burroughs wrote that after reconciliation, a couple must deliberately choose delight over residual resentment. Do not carry the memory of the conflict into the next season. Let it go. Return to the language of love. Choose to celebrate the reunion rather than rehearse the injury.

The sequence in the Song is instructive: distance, pursuit, description of the beloved's beauty, reunion, and then even more lavish praise than before. Conflict does not have to damage a marriage. When navigated with humility, persistence, and grace, it can actually deepen love. The bridegroom's praise in chapter six is more glorious than in chapter four — because it comes after the night of separation. Love tested and proven is love deepened.

Tonight, if there is any unresolved conflict in your marriage — any lingering hurt, any door left unopened, any searching left unfinished — bring it to the light. Apply these Puritan principles: charitable interpretation, quick reconciliation, mutual humility, persistent pursuit, and restored delight.

--- Reflection Prompts ---

Turn to each other and consider these questions.

Question 1: Which of these five principles do you most need in your marriage right now — charitable interpretation, quick reconciliation, mutual humility, persistent pursuit, or restored delight? Discuss honestly.

Question 2: Is there any unresolved conflict between you — even a small one — that needs to be addressed tonight? Can you practice the Song's pattern: seek, find, hold on, and praise?

Question 3: Think of a past conflict that was resolved well. What did you do right? How can you replicate that approach in the future?

--- Prayer Focus ---

Tonight's prayer focus: Pray for the wisdom and humility to handle conflict well. Ask God to make you quick to listen, slow to speak, and slow to wrath. Pray that every conflict in your marriage would lead not to deeper division but to deeper love.

--- Closing ---

The Song does not end in the night. It moves through the darkness into a dawn of renewed love. Whatever night you may be in, dear couple, the dawn is coming. Hold on. Keep seeking. The reunion will be sweeter than the separation was painful. Goodnight.
