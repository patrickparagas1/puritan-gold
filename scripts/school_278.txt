PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 18
"Common Objections to Christianity"
Apologetics for Young Christians — Lesson 18


Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we address some of the most common and most powerful objections raised against the Christian faith — objections that you will certainly encounter in conversations with unbelievers, in the books you read, in the media you consume, and even, at times, in the quiet doubts of your own heart. We will examine the charge that the Bible endorses violence, that Christians are hypocrites, that religion has done more harm than good, that Christianity is anti-intellectual, and that a loving God would never send anyone to hell. We will answer each objection honestly, thoughtfully, and with the confidence that the truth of God can withstand any question humanity can raise. Let us pray and begin.

Lord God of truth, Thou who art not afraid of honest questions, give us wisdom and courage as we face the objections raised against Thy Word and Thy people. Help us to acknowledge genuine failures while pointing always to the perfection of Christ. Let our answers be rooted not in cleverness but in Thy truth, and let our manner be marked not by defensiveness but by the meekness and gentleness of Thy Son. In His holy name we pray, Amen.


Students, it is a mark of intellectual maturity and spiritual strength to face objections to your faith honestly rather than to avoid them. The Apostle Peter commanded Christians to "be ready always to give an answer to every man that asketh you a reason of the hope that is in you with meekness and fear" (1 Peter 3:15). Notice that Peter does not say "avoid every question" or "dismiss every objector." He says "give an answer." The Christian faith is not a delicate flower that wilts under scrutiny. It is a fortress built upon the rock of God's truth, and it has withstood every assault leveled against it for two thousand years. The objections we will study today are serious, and they deserve serious responses. But as we shall see, not one of them succeeds in undermining the truth of the Christian faith.

Some of the most common objections to Christianity are not intellectual but moral. People do not merely doubt whether Christianity is true — they question whether it is good. These moral objections carry enormous emotional weight, and we must address them with both honesty and compassion. Let us take them one at a time.

Objection One: "The Bible endorses violence and is morally outdated."

This objection is one of the most frequently raised in our time, and it usually points to Old Testament passages involving divine judgment — the destruction of the Canaanites, the flood of Noah, the plagues of Egypt, or the imprecatory psalms that call down curses upon the wicked. The skeptic Richard Dawkins, in his book published in 2006, famously described the God of the Old Testament as "a vindictive, bloodthirsty ethnic cleanser." This characterization has been repeated endlessly in popular atheist literature and on social media. How should Christians respond?

First, we must understand these passages in their proper theological and historical context. The judgments described in the Old Testament are not random acts of divine rage. They are acts of divine justice — the righteous response of a perfectly holy God to extreme and persistent wickedness. Consider the case of the Canaanites, which is the example most often cited by critics. The Canaanite nations — including the Amorites, the Hittites, the Perizzites, the Hivites, and the Jebusites — had practiced the most appalling forms of wickedness for centuries before God's judgment fell upon them. Archaeological discoveries in the twentieth century have confirmed what the Bible indicates: these cultures practiced child sacrifice, ritualized sexual immorality of the vilest kind, bestiality, and other forms of moral corruption that are almost beyond modern comprehension. The discovery of child burial sites at Carthage, a Phoenician colony that inherited Canaanite religious practices, revealed the remains of thousands of infants who had been sacrificed to the god Molech — burned alive in the flames while drums were beaten to drown out their screams.

God did not act hastily against these nations. He told Abraham, centuries before the conquest, that the judgment would not come yet "for the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet full" (Genesis 15:16). God waited four hundred years — four centuries of patience — before executing judgment. During that time, the Canaanites had every opportunity to repent, and some did. Rahab the harlot of Jericho believed in the God of Israel and was spared, along with her entire family (Joshua 2:1-21; 6:22-25). She is even included in the genealogy of Christ (Matthew 1:5). The Gibeonites made peace with Israel and were spared (Joshua 9). God's judgment on Canaan was not arbitrary genocide. It was the measured, patient, and just response of a holy God to centuries of unrepentant wickedness — and even in the midst of judgment, mercy was available to all who would seek it.

Second, we should note that God's judgment on the Canaanites served a specific and limited purpose within redemptive history. Israel was being established as the covenant nation through whom the Messiah would come — the Saviour of the entire world. The moral corruption of the Canaanite nations posed a direct threat to the spiritual purity of Israel. As the subsequent history of Israel demonstrates, when the Israelites failed to fully separate themselves from Canaanite influence, they fell into the very same sins of idolatry and child sacrifice (2 Kings 16:3; 21:6; Jeremiah 32:35). God's judgment was not merely punitive — it was protective, preserving the line of promise through which the world's Redeemer would come.

Third, we must recognize that the moral objection often rests on a profound inconsistency. The skeptic who objects to God's judgment is, in that very act of objection, appealing to a moral standard — a standard of justice, fairness, and human value. But where does that standard come from? If there is no God and no objective moral law, on what basis can anyone call anything "immoral"? The materialistic worldview, which denies the existence of God and reduces human beings to the accidental products of blind evolutionary processes, has no foundation for objective morality. If we are merely sophisticated animals, the products of random mutation and natural selection, then there is no objective standard by which anything can be called truly right or truly wrong. Moral judgments become nothing more than personal preferences or cultural conventions — and personal preferences cannot ground objective moral outrage.

The Puritan theologian Stephen Charnock (1628-1680) addressed this point with characteristic depth in his great work "The Existence and Attributes of God." Charnock argued that the very concept of justice presupposes a just God: "Take away God, and you take away the foundation of all morality. If there is no supreme Lawgiver, there is no supreme law; and if there is no supreme law, there is no real right and wrong, only the shifting opinions of men." The critic who charges God with immorality is borrowing from the Christian worldview to make his argument. He is using a standard that only makes sense if God exists — to argue that God does not exist or is not good. This is a profoundly self-defeating strategy.

Fourth, we must set the Old Testament in the context of the full sweep of biblical revelation. The same Bible that records God's judgments also contains some of the most beautiful and humane moral teaching in the ancient world — teaching that predated the moral progress of other civilizations by centuries and even millennia. The command to love your neighbor as yourself appears in Leviticus 19:18, written approximately 1400 years before Christ. The requirement to care for the poor, the widow, the orphan, and the stranger is woven throughout the Mosaic law (Deuteronomy 10:18-19; 24:17-22). The institution of the Sabbath provided rest for all people, including servants and animals (Exodus 20:10). The prophets — Isaiah, Amos, Micah, Hosea — thundered against injustice and championed the cause of the oppressed. "He hath shewed thee, O man, what is good; and what doth the Lord require of thee, but to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God?" (Micah 6:8). The Bible, far from being a morally outdated book, is the foundation of Western civilization's highest moral ideals.

The Puritan commentator Matthew Henry (1662-1714), in his comprehensive commentary on the whole Bible, addressed the question of divine judgment with pastoral wisdom. Commenting on Joshua's conquest of Canaan, Henry wrote: "We must not think that God was unjust in giving the land of Canaan to Israel and commanding the destruction of the Canaanites. The earth is the Lord's, and He may give it to whom He pleases. The Canaanites had forfeited their right to the land by their abominable wickedness, and God, in His righteous judgment, transferred it to a people who would, at least for a time, maintain the worship of the true God." Henry understood that the sovereignty of God over all the earth — including the right to execute judgment and to distribute the land — is a fundamental biblical truth that, while difficult for our sensibilities, is perfectly consistent with divine justice.

We should also address the specific claim that the Bible is "morally outdated" by considering the extraordinary moral advances that the Bible introduced to the ancient world. When the Bible was written, slavery was universal, women were treated as property in most cultures, infanticide was practiced openly in Greece and Rome, and the weak and vulnerable had virtually no legal protections. Into this moral darkness, the Bible introduced revolutionary concepts. The creation account in Genesis established the equal dignity of all human beings — male and female — as bearers of the image of God (Genesis 1:27). The Mosaic law introduced protections for slaves that were unheard of in the ancient Near East, including the requirement to free Hebrew slaves after six years of service (Exodus 21:2) and the provision that a slave who was injured by his master must be set free (Exodus 21:26-27). The law required just treatment of foreigners: "Thou shalt neither vex a stranger, nor oppress him: for ye were strangers in the land of Egypt" (Exodus 22:21). The prophets thundered against the exploitation of the poor and the perversion of justice: "Woe unto them that decree unrighteous decrees, and that write grievousness which they have prescribed; To turn aside the needy from judgment, and to take away the right from the poor of my people, that widows may be their prey, and that they may rob the fatherless!" (Isaiah 10:1-2). These moral standards were not merely progressive for their time — they laid the foundation for the moral consciousness of Western civilization.

Objection Two: "Christians are hypocrites."

This is perhaps the most emotionally powerful objection to Christianity, because it often comes from personal experience. Someone has been hurt by a professing Christian — perhaps a church leader who was exposed in scandal, a friend who was judgmental and unkind, or a family member whose life did not match their profession. The pain behind this objection is real, and it must be acknowledged with humility and compassion.

The honest response begins with a simple admission: yes, many professing Christians have been hypocrites, and this hypocrisy is a genuine sin that has caused real harm. Christians have sometimes been guilty of terrible failures — failures of integrity, failures of love, failures of justice. The history of the church includes dark chapters: the medieval Inquisition, the complicity of some Christians in slavery, the abuse scandals that have rocked various denominations in recent decades, and the countless individual acts of unkindness, judgmentalism, and pride that have driven people away from the church. These failures are real, and they are grievous.

However, several critical qualifications must be made.

First, hypocrisy does not disprove the truth of Christianity. A mathematics teacher who cannot balance his own checkbook does not thereby prove that mathematics is false. A doctor who smokes does not thereby prove that medicine is a fraud. The truth of a belief system is independent of the behavior of its adherents. Christianity should be judged on the basis of its claims, its evidence, and its internal consistency — not on the behavior of every person who claims the label "Christian." The Puritan preacher Thomas Watson (c. 1620-1686) made this point with characteristic clarity: "Christianity is to be judged not by its caricatures but by its Christ. Look not to the failures of the servants but to the perfection of the Master."

Second, Christianity itself condemns hypocrisy in the strongest possible terms. Jesus reserved His most severe criticism not for the openly sinful — the tax collectors, the prostitutes, the thieves — but for the religious hypocrites. In Matthew chapter 23, Jesus pronounced a series of devastating "woes" upon the scribes and Pharisees, calling them "whited sepulchres, which indeed appear beautiful outward, but are within full of dead men's bones, and of all uncleanness" (Matthew 23:27). The fact that Christians sometimes fail to live up to their own standards is not evidence that the standards are wrong — it is evidence that the standards are extraordinarily high and that we need grace to meet them.

Third, the very concept of hypocrisy presupposes an objective standard from which the hypocrite falls short. If there is no objective moral standard, then there is no such thing as hypocrisy — only different choices made by different people. The fact that we are outraged by hypocrisy confirms that we believe in a real moral standard, and that belief, as we have argued throughout this course, points to the existence of a moral Lawgiver.

Fourth, it is worth observing that Jesus Himself predicted that the church would include false professors and hypocrites. In the parable of the wheat and the tares (Matthew 13:24-30), Jesus taught that genuine believers and false professors would grow together in the church until the day of judgment, when God would separate them. In the parable of the ten virgins (Matthew 25:1-13), Jesus warned that not all who profess faith are genuinely converted. He declared bluntly: "Not every one that saith unto me, Lord, Lord, shall enter into the kingdom of heaven; but he that doeth the will of my Father which is in heaven. Many will say to me in that day, Lord, Lord, have we not prophesied in thy name? and in thy name have cast out devils? and in thy name done many wonderful works? And then will I profess unto them, I never knew you: depart from me, ye that work iniquity" (Matthew 7:21-23). The presence of hypocrites in the church is not an argument against Christianity. It is a confirmation of what Jesus Himself predicted. The existence of counterfeit currency does not prove that genuine currency does not exist — it proves precisely the opposite. Counterfeits exist only because the genuine article is valuable enough to imitate.

Fifth, while some Christians have been hypocrites, many others have been extraordinary examples of virtue, courage, and sacrificial love. Christians have been at the forefront of some of the greatest moral movements in human history. William Wilberforce (1759-1833), a devout evangelical Christian, spent nearly fifty years campaigning for the abolition of the slave trade in the British Empire. He was motivated entirely by his Christian convictions. In his diary, Wilberforce wrote: "God Almighty has set before me two great objects: the suppression of the slave trade and the reformation of manners." His faith did not make him a hypocrite — it made him a hero.

Consider also the work of William Carey (1761-1834), the "father of modern missions," who spent forty years in India, translating the Bible into more than thirty languages and dialects, establishing schools and hospitals, and campaigning against the practice of sati — the burning of widows on their husbands' funeral pyres. Consider Elizabeth Fry (1780-1845), a Quaker Christian who reformed the horrific conditions in British prisons. Consider George Mueller (1805-1898), who cared for over ten thousand orphans in Bristol, England, relying entirely on prayer and never once asking for money. Consider Amy Carmichael (1867-1951), who spent fifty-five years in India rescuing children from temple slavery and prostitution. Consider Corrie ten Boom (1892-1983), whose family hid Jews during the Holocaust and who later traveled the world preaching forgiveness. Consider Dietrich Bonhoeffer (1906-1945), the German pastor and theologian who resisted the Nazi regime and was executed for his participation in the plot to assassinate Hitler.

The Puritan tradition itself is filled with men and women of extraordinary integrity. Richard Baxter (1615-1691) spent his ministry in Kidderminster, England, where his tireless preaching and pastoral care transformed an entire town. When Baxter arrived, the town was notorious for its ignorance and immorality. When he left, it was a model of Christian piety and community life. Baxter's pastoral method was simple but demanding: he visited every family in his congregation, catechized their children, counseled the troubled, rebuked the wayward, and encouraged the faithful. He wrote: "I preached as never sure to preach again, and as a dying man to dying men." This was not hypocrisy. This was the genuine article — a life transformed by the gospel and poured out in service to others.

John Bunyan (1628-1688), the author of "The Pilgrim's Progress," spent twelve years in Bedford prison for the crime of preaching without a license. During his imprisonment, he was separated from his beloved wife and his blind daughter, whom he missed with agonizing intensity. He was offered his freedom if he would promise to stop preaching, but he refused. Bunyan wrote from prison: "I have determined, the Almighty God being my help and shield, yet to suffer, if frail life might continue so long, even till the moss shall grow on mine eyebrows, rather than thus to violate my faith and principles." This is not hypocrisy. This is the courage of a man who valued the truth of God more than his own comfort.

The fruits of genuine Christian faith are undeniable. The Christian church has built more hospitals, established more schools and universities, fed more hungry people, clothed more naked, housed more homeless, and cared for more orphans and widows than any institution in the history of the world. These fruits do not prove that every individual Christian is sincere, but they do prove that the Christian faith, when genuinely believed and faithfully practiced, produces extraordinary goodness.

Objection Three: "Religion has done more harm than good."

This claim is often made with reference to the Crusades (1096-1291), the Inquisition (which in its various forms spanned from the twelfth to the nineteenth centuries), the European Wars of Religion (particularly the Thirty Years' War of 1618-1648), and various other historical episodes of religiously motivated violence. These events are real, and Christians should not minimize or excuse them. Terrible things have been done in the name of Christ, and honest Christianity must acknowledge this without flinching.

However, a fuller picture reveals a very different story than the one commonly told.

First, the claim that religion causes most of the world's violence is simply not supported by the historical evidence. The Encyclopedia of Wars, compiled by Charles Phillips and Alan Axelrod, catalogued 1,763 wars throughout human history and found that only 123 — less than seven percent — had any religious component, and of those, more than half were related to Islam, not Christianity. The greatest atrocities of the twentieth century — the century with, by far, the highest death tolls in human history — were perpetrated by explicitly atheistic and anti-religious regimes. Joseph Stalin's Soviet Union killed an estimated twenty million people. Mao Zedong's People's Republic of China killed an estimated forty-five to seventy-five million people during the Great Leap Forward and the Cultural Revolution. Pol Pot's Khmer Rouge killed approximately two million people in Cambodia — roughly a quarter of the entire population. Adolf Hitler's National Socialism, while not strictly atheist, was profoundly anti-Christian and drew upon evolutionary and racial ideology rather than Christian theology. These secular ideologies produced more bloodshed in a single century than all the religious conflicts of the previous two thousand years combined.

Second, we must distinguish between things done in the name of Christ that were consistent with His teaching and things done in His name that were in direct violation of it. The Crusades, the Inquisition, and the witch trials were not expressions of authentic Christianity. They were perversions of it. Jesus taught His followers to love their enemies (Matthew 5:44), to turn the other cheek (Matthew 5:39), to put away the sword (Matthew 26:52), and to overcome evil with good (Romans 12:21). Those who committed atrocities in His name were not following His teaching — they were betraying it. To blame Christianity for the Crusades is like blaming medicine for poisoning. The abuse of a good thing does not invalidate the thing itself.

Third, as we noted in our discussion of hypocrisy, the positive contributions of Christianity to human civilization are immense and well-documented. The historian and sociologist Rodney Stark, in his book published in 2005, documented the ways in which Christianity transformed Western civilization. Christians pioneered universal education — the earliest schools and universities in Europe were established by the church. Christians built the first hospitals — the word "hospital" itself derives from the Latin "hospitale," referring to places of Christian hospitality for the sick. Christians laid the foundations of modern science, as we explored in an earlier lesson. Christians developed the concept of universal human rights — the idea that every human being possesses inherent dignity and worth, regardless of race, class, sex, or social status — which is rooted directly in the biblical teaching that all people are made in the image of God (Genesis 1:27). Christians have been at the forefront of virtually every great humanitarian movement in Western history: the abolition of slavery, prison reform, the care of orphans and widows, the establishment of charities and relief organizations, and the defense of the vulnerable and oppressed.

The Puritan contribution to these developments is particularly notable. The Puritans championed universal literacy because they believed every person should be able to read the Bible for themselves. They established Harvard College in 1636 — just sixteen years after the Pilgrims landed at Plymouth — because they believed in the importance of an educated ministry and an educated populace. They developed the concept of covenant community, which laid the groundwork for democratic self-governance. They championed the rule of law, the rights of conscience, and the importance of public education. Far from doing more harm than good, Christianity — and the Puritan tradition in particular — has been one of the greatest forces for human flourishing in the history of the world.

The Puritan divine Thomas Manton (1620-1677), in his commentary on the Epistle of James, reflected on the relationship between faith and works in a way that speaks directly to the charge that religion does more harm than good. Manton wrote: "True religion doth not consist in bare opinions and speculations, but in a holy, active life. The tree is known by its fruits, and the faith is known by its works. Where true faith is, it will produce true goodness — not perfectly, for we are sinners still, but genuinely, for we are new creatures in Christ." Manton recognized what the historical record abundantly confirms: when Christianity is genuinely believed and faithfully practiced, it produces extraordinary goodness. The failures of professing Christians are real, but they are failures despite Christianity, not because of it.

We should also note that the positive influence of Christianity extends far beyond institutional achievements. The Christian faith has shaped the deepest moral intuitions of Western civilization — intuitions that even secular people share without realizing their origin. The belief that every human being has inherent dignity and worth. The conviction that the strong have a duty to protect the weak. The ideal of sacrificial love — giving oneself for the good of others. The commitment to truth, justice, mercy, and compassion. These moral convictions are so deeply embedded in Western culture that they seem self-evident, but they are not self-evident at all. They are the fruit of centuries of Christian moral influence. As the historian Tom Holland argued in his work published in 2019, even the most strident critics of Christianity are, in their moral commitments, far more Christian than they realize. The very values they use to critique the church — equality, compassion, justice for the oppressed — are values that Christianity brought into the world.

Objection Four: "Christianity is anti-intellectual and demands blind faith."

This objection reflects a widespread cultural assumption — but it is historically and theologically inaccurate. Christianity does not demand blind faith. It demands informed, reasonable, evidence-based faith. The Bible itself commends the Berean Jews who "searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so" (Acts 17:11). They did not accept Paul's teaching blindly — they investigated it, tested it against the Scriptures, and then believed. Jesus Himself said: "Ye shall know the truth, and the truth shall make you free" (John 8:32). He did not say, "You shall ignore the truth" or "You shall fear the truth." He said, "You shall know the truth." Christianity is a faith that seeks understanding, not a faith that suppresses inquiry.

The history of Christian intellectual achievement is staggering. As we studied in our lesson on science and faith, many of the greatest scientists in history were devout Christians who saw their scientific work as an exploration of God's creation. But beyond science, Christians have made foundational contributions to philosophy, literature, music, art, law, and political theory. Augustine of Hippo was one of the greatest philosophers who ever lived. Thomas Aquinas synthesized Christian theology with Aristotelian philosophy in a monumental intellectual achievement. Blaise Pascal was a mathematical genius and a profound Christian thinker. Isaac Newton wrote more about theology than about physics. Johann Sebastian Bach composed his masterworks "to the glory of God alone" — Soli Deo Gloria. The Puritans themselves were among the most intellectually rigorous Christians in history. Jonathan Edwards (1703-1758), widely considered the greatest American philosopher-theologian, was a Puritan in spirit and practice. His works — "Freedom of the Will," "Religious Affections," "The End for Which God Created the World" — remain landmarks of intellectual depth and spiritual insight.

The great scientist Blaise Pascal (1623-1662), who was both a mathematical genius and a devout Christian, addressed the relationship between faith and reason in his celebrated "Pensees." Pascal wrote: "Faith certainly tells us what the senses do not, but not the contrary of what they see. It is above them, not against them." Pascal understood that Christian faith does not require the abandonment of reason. Rather, faith completes what reason begins. Reason can take us far — it can demonstrate the existence of God, the reliability of historical testimony, and the coherence of the Christian worldview — but ultimately, the embrace of God in personal relationship requires a step of faith. This step, however, is not a leap into the dark. It is a step into the light, grounded in evidence, confirmed by experience, and sustained by the Holy Spirit.

We should also note that many of the great universities of the Western world — Oxford, Cambridge, Harvard, Yale, Princeton — were founded by Christians for explicitly Christian purposes. Harvard's original motto was "Veritas Christo et Ecclesiae" — "Truth for Christ and the Church." Yale was founded by Puritan ministers who wanted to train godly leaders for the colony of Connecticut. Princeton was established during the Great Awakening to prepare ministers for the gospel. The modern university, with all its intellectual achievements, is a product of Christian civilization. The notion that Christianity is anti-intellectual is not merely wrong — it is precisely backwards. Christianity is the mother of Western intellectualism.

The Puritan tradition valued the life of the mind as an essential component of the life of faith. John Owen (1616-1683), the vice-chancellor of Oxford University, was one of the most learned men of his age. His theological writings fill twenty-four volumes and cover every major topic of Christian doctrine with a depth and precision that have never been surpassed. William Perkins (1558-1602), one of the earliest and most influential Puritans, was a fellow of Christ's College, Cambridge, and a brilliant theologian who laid the foundations for Puritan practical divinity. Richard Sibbes (1577-1635), known as "the sweet dropper" for the tenderness of his preaching, was a master of both theological precision and pastoral warmth. These men were anything but anti-intellectual. They were among the finest minds of their generation, and they brought all their intellectual powers to bear upon the study and proclamation of divine truth. The Puritan approach to education was holistic: they believed that the cultivation of the mind was inseparable from the cultivation of the soul. Cotton Mather (1663-1728), the prolific Puritan minister and scholar of Boston, wrote over four hundred works on topics ranging from theology and history to natural science and medicine. He was one of the first advocates of inoculation against smallpox in America — a stance that was based on his scientific curiosity and his Christian conviction that God had provided means for the preservation of human life. Mather exemplified the Puritan belief that rigorous intellectual inquiry and deep Christian devotion are not enemies but allies. The Christian mind is not a closed mind — it is a mind that has been opened by the light of divine truth and then directed toward the exploration of all of God's creation.

The charge of anti-intellectualism, then, says more about the ignorance of the accuser than about the nature of Christianity. It is an objection that collapses under the weight of the historical evidence. Christianity has produced more schools, more universities, more scientists, more philosophers, more artists, more writers, and more original thinkers than any other worldview in history. The God of the Bible is the God who said, "Come now, and let us reason together" (Isaiah 1:18). He invites inquiry. He welcomes questions. And He rewards honest seekers with the treasure of His truth.

Objection Five: "A loving God would never send anyone to hell."

This objection strikes at the heart of Christian theology, and it is one that many Christians themselves struggle with. The doctrine of hell is difficult, sobering, and deeply uncomfortable. It is also, unfortunately, one of the clearest and most emphatic teachings of the New Testament — and it comes primarily from the lips of Jesus Himself.

Jesus spoke of hell more than anyone else in the Bible. He described it as "outer darkness" where there is "weeping and gnashing of teeth" (Matthew 8:12). He called it "everlasting fire, prepared for the devil and his angels" (Matthew 25:41). He warned: "Fear not them which kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul: but rather fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body in hell" (Matthew 10:28). He told the parable of the rich man and Lazarus, in which the rich man, after death, found himself in torment and begged for a drop of water to cool his tongue (Luke 16:19-31). These are not the words of a mild moral teacher who wants everyone to feel comfortable. These are the words of the Son of God who loves humanity too much to lie about the consequences of sin.

The doctrine of hell is not in conflict with God's love — it is a necessary corollary of God's justice, holiness, and respect for human freedom. God is not only loving — He is also holy, righteous, and just. He cannot simply overlook sin without ceasing to be just. A judge who refuses to punish the guilty is not a good judge — he is a corrupt one. God's justice demands that sin be punished. The miracle of the gospel is not that God eliminates the punishment for sin — it is that God Himself bears the punishment in the person of His Son, so that guilty sinners can go free. "For he hath made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin; that we might be made the righteousness of God in him" (2 Corinthians 5:21). Hell is the consequence of rejecting this offer — of refusing the free gift of grace and insisting on facing the judgment of God in one's own merit.

Furthermore, hell respects human freedom. C.S. Lewis (1898-1963) argued that the doors of hell are locked from the inside. People are not dragged into hell against their will. They choose it — by rejecting God, by refusing His grace, by insisting on their own autonomy. As Lewis wrote in "The Great Divorce": "There are only two kinds of people in the end: those who say to God, 'Thy will be done,' and those to whom God says, in the end, 'Thy will be done.'" Hell is, in a terrifying sense, God giving people what they have chosen — an eternity without Him.

The Puritan pastor Thomas Brooks (1608-1680) wrote with solemn urgency about the reality of hell: "Sinner, remember that God's patience will not last forever. The door of mercy stands open now, but it will not stand open always. Today, if ye will hear his voice, harden not your hearts. There is no repentance in the grave. There is no gospel in hell. Now is the accepted time; behold, now is the day of salvation." Brooks's words echo the urgent tone of Scripture itself: "How shall we escape, if we neglect so great salvation?" (Hebrews 2:3).

The Puritan theologian Jonathan Edwards (1703-1758) preached what is perhaps the most famous sermon in American history — "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God" — delivered in Enfield, Connecticut, on July 8, 1741. Edwards did not preach this sermon to terrorize his hearers but to awaken them to the reality of their danger and to drive them to the mercy of Christ. Edwards wrote: "The God that holds you over the pit of hell, much as one holds a spider or some loathsome insect over the fire, abhors you, and is dreadfully provoked. His wrath towards you burns like fire; He looks upon you as worthy of nothing else but to be cast into the fire." These words sound harsh to modern ears, but Edwards's intention was profoundly compassionate. He wanted his hearers to understand the gravity of their situation so that they would flee to the only place of safety — the outstretched arms of Christ. And many did. The sermon produced a powerful response, with people crying out for mercy and turning to Christ in repentance and faith.

Edwards understood, as all the Puritans did, that the doctrine of hell is inseparable from the doctrine of the cross. If there is no hell, then the cross is unnecessary. If sin carries no eternal consequences, then Christ died for nothing. The doctrine of hell, rightly understood, does not diminish the gospel — it magnifies it beyond measure. The greater the danger, the greater the rescue. The deeper the pit, the more glorious the salvation.

The proper response to the doctrine of hell is not to deny it but to flee to the only refuge from it — the cross of Jesus Christ. The reality of hell makes the gospel infinitely more precious. If there is no danger, there is no need for a rescue. But the danger is real, and the rescue is glorious. God "is not willing that any should perish, but that all should come to repentance" (2 Peter 3:9). The same God who warns of hell pleads with sinners to repent and be saved. The same Christ who spoke of everlasting fire wept over Jerusalem because its people would not come to Him (Luke 19:41-42). The doctrine of hell does not contradict God's love — it reveals the depth of it. God loved us enough to warn us, enough to provide a way of escape, and enough to suffer in His own body the punishment we deserved.

Before we close, let us step back and observe something remarkable about all five of these objections. Each one, upon careful examination, actually points back toward the truth of Christianity rather than away from it. The objection about violence presupposes a moral standard that only the Christian worldview can ground. The objection about hypocrisy presupposes moral ideals that Christianity itself established. The objection about religion doing harm ignores the overwhelming evidence of Christian contributions to human flourishing. The objection about anti-intellectualism contradicts the historical record of Christian intellectual achievement. And the objection about hell, far from undermining the gospel, reveals the infinite gravity of sin and the infinite value of Christ's atoning sacrifice. In every case, the objection, honestly examined, leads us closer to the truth it was meant to undermine.

The Puritan pastor William Bridge (1600-1671), in his excellent work "A Lifting Up for the Downcast," offered this encouragement to believers who face difficult objections to their faith: "Be not discouraged by the strength of the enemy's arguments. Truth is mighty and will prevail. The objections of the ungodly are like waves beating against a rock — they make much noise and spray, but the rock stands unmoved. So stands the truth of God, firm and unshakable, against every assault."

Students, as we conclude this lesson, remember that the proper response to objections against Christianity is not defensiveness but honesty — honesty about the genuine failures of Christians, honesty about the difficult doctrines of Scripture, honesty about what we know and what we do not know. We answer objections not to win arguments but to remove obstacles that stand between people and the saving knowledge of Jesus Christ. Every objection, honestly faced and faithfully answered, becomes an opportunity to point someone to the truth of the gospel.

The Puritan minister John Flavel (1627-1691) captured this spirit perfectly: "The Christian's duty is not to demolish every opponent but to commend every truth. Let your answers be seasoned with grace, your manner be marked by love, and your aim be always the glory of God and the good of souls." May this be our aim in every conversation we have about the faith.

Moral and intellectual objections to Christianity deserve honest, thoughtful, and humble responses. We must acknowledge the genuine failures of Christians throughout history while pointing always to the perfection of Christ. We must face the hard doctrines of Scripture with courage and trust, knowing that the God who reveals these truths is infinitely wise, infinitely just, and infinitely good.
