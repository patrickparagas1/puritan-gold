PURITAN GOLD -- Study Topic: Female Piety, Part 9 of 10
"The Devotional Life of a Godly Woman — Prayer, Scripture Reading, and Meditation"
John Angell James — Female Piety

---

Welcome back, dear listener, to Puritan Gold. We are nearing the end of our study of Female Piety by John Angell James, and today we come to the very heart of the matter: the devotional life. If the character of the pious woman is the fruit, then her devotional life is the root. James was profoundly convinced that the health of every other dimension of a woman's life — her marriage, her motherhood, her influence, her endurance in trial — depends upon the vitality of her communion with God. Let us attend carefully.

Let us read from Psalm 63:1-3, in the King James Version:

"O God, thou art my God; early will I seek thee: my soul thirsteth for thee, my flesh longeth for thee in a dry and thirsty land, where no water is; To see thy power and thy glory, so as I have seen thee in the sanctuary. Because thy lovingkindness is better than life, my lips shall praise thee."

And from Psalm 1:1-3:

"Blessed is the man that walketh not in the counsel of the ungodly, nor standeth in the way of sinners, nor sitteth in the seat of the scornful. But his delight is in the law of the LORD; and in his law doth he meditate day and night. And he shall be like a tree planted by the rivers of water, that bringeth forth his fruit in his season; his leaf also shall not wither; and whatsoever he doeth shall prosper."

--- Teaching ---

John Angell James opens his discussion of the devotional life with a simple but profound observation: the godly woman is, first and foremost, a woman who walks with God. Her piety is not an outward performance but an inward reality. It is sustained not by the opinions of others or the habits of religious culture but by a living, personal, daily communion with the God who made her, redeemed her, and dwells within her by His Spirit. Without this communion, every other aspect of her life will eventually wither. With it, she can endure anything and flourish anywhere.

James identifies three pillars of the devotional life: prayer, the reading of Scripture, and meditation. Let us consider each.

On the subject of prayer, James writes with the authority of a man who had spent a lifetime on his knees. He urges the godly woman to make prayer the first business of her day and the last exercise of her evening. Morning prayer, he says, sets the tone for the entire day. It is the act by which the soul turns its face toward God before it turns toward the world. It consecrates the hours that follow, inviting God's presence and guidance into every task, every conversation, and every decision. Evening prayer, conversely, is the act by which the soul reviews the day in God's presence, confessing its sins, giving thanks for its mercies, and committing itself anew to divine keeping.

But James does not confine prayer to these set times. He encourages what the Puritans called ejaculatory prayer — brief, spontaneous communications with God throughout the day. When the woman faces a sudden temptation, she lifts her heart to God for strength. When she receives an unexpected blessing, she whispers a word of thanks. When she is burdened with anxiety, she casts her care upon the Lord. This habit of continual prayer transforms the whole of life into an act of worship. It fulfills the apostolic injunction to "pray without ceasing" (1 Thessalonians 5:17).

The Puritans were masters of prayer. John Bunyan, in his treatise Prayer, described true prayer as a sincere, sensible, affectionate pouring out of the soul to God, through Christ, in the strength and assistance of the Holy Spirit, for such things as God hath promised. James commends this definition warmly. He wants the godly woman to understand that prayer is not a ritual but a relationship, not a duty merely but a privilege — the privilege of speaking directly to the King of kings, who has invited her to come boldly to the throne of grace and to find mercy and help in her time of need.

On the reading of Scripture, James is equally earnest. He observes that many women read the Bible haphazardly, opening it at random and reading a few verses without any systematic plan or spiritual engagement. James urges a different approach. He recommends that the godly woman read the Bible regularly, systematically, and devotionally. Regularly, meaning every day without exception — not because God requires a legalistic quota, but because the soul needs daily nourishment just as the body does. Systematically, meaning with some plan or order, so that over time the whole counsel of God is read and understood. Devotionally, meaning with a heart that is open to the Holy Spirit's teaching, that listens for God's voice, and that responds with faith and obedience.

Thomas Watson, in his Body of Divinity, wrote that the Word of God is the glass in which we see the face of God, the food by which our souls are nourished, the sword by which our enemies are defeated, and the lamp by which our feet are guided. James applies these metaphors to the devotional life of the godly woman. When she reads the Scriptures, she is not merely gathering information. She is encountering the living God. She is being transformed by the renewing of her mind. She is being equipped for every good work. And she is being drawn into a deeper love for the Savior who is revealed on every page.

James also recommends the practice of Scripture memorization. He notes that the godly women of earlier generations stored great portions of the Bible in their hearts, so that in times of trial, temptation, or opportunity, the appropriate word of God rose readily to their lips. This practice, he says, is of incalculable value. The Word hidden in the heart is a weapon against sin (Psalm 119:11), a comfort in sorrow, a guide in perplexity, and a witness to the lost.

On the subject of meditation, James writes with particular warmth. Meditation, he explains, is the practice of taking a truth of Scripture and turning it over in the mind, considering it from every angle, applying it to one's own condition, and drawing from it the nourishment it was meant to give. The Puritan Thomas Manton compared meditation to the chewing and digesting of food: reading is the taking of the food into the mouth, but meditation is the process by which it is broken down and absorbed into the system, becoming part of the very life of the soul.

James urges the godly woman to set aside time each day — even a few minutes — for deliberate meditation upon some portion of Scripture, some attribute of God, some doctrine of grace, or some promise of the gospel. This practice, he says, deepens the roots of faith, enriches the life of prayer, strengthens the soul against temptation, and fills the heart with a joy that the world can neither give nor take away.

James also addresses the enemies of the devotional life. Chief among them are busyness, distraction, spiritual laziness, and the neglect of the means of grace. He observes that many women intend to pray and read the Scriptures but are constantly swept along by the demands of daily life until the day is gone and the opportunity has passed. To these women, James counsels vigilance and discipline. The devotional life does not sustain itself. It must be guarded, prioritized, and fought for, just as any precious thing must be protected from the forces that would destroy it.

Richard Baxter, in The Saints' Everlasting Rest, urged Christians to keep the heart in a state of heavenly-mindedness by the regular practice of meditation. He warned that the neglect of meditation would leave the soul dry, weak, and vulnerable to every assault of the enemy. James echoes this warning with pastoral urgency. The godly woman who neglects her devotional life is like a soldier who goes into battle without armor, a traveler who sets out on a journey without food, or a tree that has been cut off from its source of water. She may survive for a time, but she will not thrive, and eventually she will fall.

--- Application ---

1. Evaluate the current state of your devotional life. Are you praying daily — in the morning, in the evening, and throughout the day? Are you reading the Scriptures regularly and systematically? Are you taking time for meditation? Be honest with yourself, and ask the Lord for the grace to grow.

2. Establish a simple devotional rhythm if you do not already have one. Set a time each morning for prayer and Scripture reading. Begin with fifteen minutes and build from there. The key is consistency, not duration.

3. Choose one verse of Scripture this week to memorize and meditate upon. Carry it with you throughout the day. Let it shape your thoughts, your prayers, and your responses to the situations you face.

4. Identify the greatest enemy of your devotional life — whether it is busyness, distraction, laziness, or discouragement — and take one practical step to overcome it. Ask a trusted friend to hold you accountable.

--- Closing ---

Let us pray.

O Lord, who art the fountain of living waters, we confess that we have too often forsaken Thee for broken cisterns that can hold no water. Forgive our neglect of prayer, our carelessness in the reading of Thy Word, and our failure to meditate upon Thy truth. Rekindle in our hearts a hunger for Thy presence and a thirst for Thy Word. Teach us to pray with sincerity and fervency, to read with understanding and delight, and to meditate with depth and devotion. May our devotional lives be as trees planted by rivers of water, bearing fruit in every season to the glory of Thy name. Through Jesus Christ our Lord, who is Himself the living Word. Amen.
