PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"Why Do the Nations Rage?"
May 2, 2026

---

"Why do the heathen rage, and the people imagine a vain thing? The kings of the earth set themselves, and the rulers take counsel together, against the Lord, and against his anointed, saying, Let us break their bands asunder, and cast away their cords from us. He that sitteth in the heavens shall laugh: the Lord shall have them in derision." -- Psalm 2:1-4

There is a scene here presented to us that ought to both humble and comfort every child of God. On the one hand, we behold the nations of the earth in furious conspiracy against the Almighty. Kings and rulers, princes and peoples, gather themselves in solemn counsel, not to seek the face of God, but to cast off His authority. They conspire against the Lord and against His Anointed, His Messiah, His Christ. On the other hand, we behold the Lord Himself, seated in the heavens, utterly untroubled by all their plots and machinations. He laughs. He holds them in derision. And then, in a single act of sovereign authority, He declares His decree: "Thou art my Son; this day have I begotten thee."

This psalm, beloved, is one of the great Messianic psalms, and it carries us from the raging of earthly kingdoms to the eternal throne of Jesus Christ. The apostles themselves recognized this when, in the fourth chapter of the Acts, they quoted these very words and applied them to the conspiracy of Herod and Pontius Pilate against the Lord Jesus. What happened at Calvary was not merely a miscarriage of Roman justice. It was the fulfilment of this ancient prophecy. The kings of the earth did indeed set themselves, and the rulers did indeed take counsel together, against the Lord's Christ. And yet their rage accomplished nothing but the eternal purpose of God.

Let us first consider the nature of this rebellion. The psalmist asks, "Why do the heathen rage?" It is a question that carries its own answer, for the rage of the ungodly against God is the most irrational thing in all the universe. What can the creature accomplish against the Creator? What can the clay say to the Potter? The nations rage because sin has made them mad. They imagine a vain thing, a thing of absolute emptiness and futility. They say among themselves, "Let us break their bands asunder, and cast away their cords from us." They regard the commandments of God and the claims of Christ as bands and cords, as intolerable restraints upon their liberty, as chains from which they must free themselves at any cost.

And is this not the very spirit of our own age? Do we not hear on every side the cry of men and women who would cast off the authority of God's Word, who regard the moral law as an ancient relic unfit for modern minds, who treat the claims of Christ upon their lives as an insufferable imposition? The language of this psalm is as fresh as this morning's news. The nations still rage. The rulers still take counsel. The cry is still the same: "We will not have this man to reign over us."

But now lift your eyes from earth to heaven, and behold the response of the Almighty. "He that sitteth in the heavens shall laugh." This is not the laughter of amusement, as though God found the rebellion of His creatures entertaining. It is the laughter of absolute sovereignty, the laughter of One who knows that all the combined power of all the kingdoms of the earth is less than nothing before Him. Stephen Charnock, that profound theologian of the Puritan era, taught that the sovereignty of God is the foundation upon which every other divine attribute rests. If God were not absolutely sovereign, He could not be absolutely faithful, or absolutely just, or absolutely merciful. But because He sits enthroned above all the tumults of this fallen world, every promise He has made shall stand, and every purpose He has formed shall come to pass.

Consider how small the mightiest empires appear when viewed from the throne of God. Nebuchadnezzar thought himself the lord of the world, yet he was driven out to eat grass like an ox until he learned that the Most High ruleth in the kingdom of men. Pharaoh hardened his heart against the Lord and said, "Who is the Lord, that I should obey his voice?" And the Red Sea became his answer. Herod arrayed himself in royal apparel and received the worship of the people, and an angel of the Lord smote him because he gave not God the glory. The annals of history are strewn with the wreckage of kingdoms that set themselves against the Lord, and not one of them has prevailed.

Yet the psalm does not end with judgment alone. In the midst of this thundering declaration of divine sovereignty, there is a gospel invitation of surpassing tenderness. "Kiss the Son, lest he be angry, and ye perish from the way, when his wrath is kindled but a little. Blessed are all they that put their trust in him." Here is the way of escape from the wrath to come. Here is the door that stands open even for the most rebellious sinner. Kiss the Son. Submit to Christ. Bow before His authority, not as a slave bows before a tyrant, but as a child bows before a loving father. For this Son, this Anointed One against whom the nations rage, is the very same Jesus who wept over Jerusalem, who opened His arms to little children, who said to every weary and heavy-laden soul, "Come unto me, and I will give you rest."

Richard Sibbes, that sweet and heavenly Puritan, often spoke of Christ as the meeting place of justice and mercy. In Him, the wrath of God against sin is fully satisfied, and the love of God toward sinners is fully displayed. The same Son whom the nations reject is the Son in whom all the fullness of the Godhead dwells bodily. To kiss the Son is to embrace Him by faith, to receive Him as Lord and Saviour, to find in Him all that the restless human heart has ever sought.

And so we stand today between these two great realities. On the one side is the raging of a world in rebellion against its Maker, a world that will not bow, that will not submit, that imagines it can break free from the cords of divine authority and fashion its own destiny. On the other side is the serene and unshakeable sovereignty of God, who has set His King upon the holy hill of Zion and who shall give Him the nations for His inheritance and the uttermost parts of the earth for His possession.

Where do you stand, dear listener? Are you among those who rage against the Lord, who chafe under His authority, who regard His commandments as burdensome chains? Or are you among those who have kissed the Son, who have found in Christ a refuge from the storm, a shelter from the wrath to come? The psalm begins with a question and ends with a benediction: "Blessed are all they that put their trust in him." May it please the Lord to number us among that blessed company, now and forevermore. Amen.
