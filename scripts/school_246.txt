"John Knox and Scotland"
The Reformation -- Lesson 6

---

Welcome back, students. Today we travel north, across the border from England into the rugged and fiercely independent nation of Scotland, to meet one of the most remarkable and formidable figures of the entire Reformation era. His name is John Knox, and his story is one of exile and return, of thundering preaching and quiet scholarship, of confrontation with queens and communion with God. Knox did not merely bring the Reformation to Scotland -- he forged it into the very soul of the Scottish nation, shaping its church, its education, its civil government, and its culture in ways that endure to this day. By the end of today's lesson, you will understand why John Knox has been called the founder of the Scottish Reformation and why his legacy extends far beyond the borders of his beloved homeland.

--- Lesson ---

To understand the Scottish Reformation, we must first understand what Scotland looked like before John Knox appeared on the scene. In the early sixteenth century, Scotland was a small, poor, and politically unstable kingdom on the northern edge of Europe. Its population was perhaps seven hundred thousand souls, scattered across a landscape of mountains, lochs, and windswept lowlands. The Scottish crown was weak, constantly threatened by powerful noble families -- the Hamiltons, the Douglases, the Gordons, the Campbells -- who controlled vast territories and commanded private armies. Relations with England to the south were defined by centuries of warfare, border raids, and mutual suspicion. The devastating defeat at the Battle of Flodden in 1513, where King James IV and much of the Scottish nobility were slain by the English forces of the Earl of Surrey, had left the nation traumatized and leaderless, with an infant king -- James V -- on the throne and rival factions competing for control of the regency.

The old alliance with France -- the Auld Alliance, as the Scots called it, dating back to the Treaty of Paris in 1295 -- shaped Scottish foreign policy and tied the nation's fortunes to the great Catholic power across the Channel. Scottish students studied at French universities. Scottish merchants traded with French ports. Scottish soldiers served in the French army. And the French crown used its influence in Scotland to maintain a Catholic, anti-English bulwark on England's northern border. This French connection would become one of the central obstacles to the Reformation in Scotland, and overcoming it was one of Knox's greatest achievements.

The Catholic Church in Scotland, as in much of late medieval Europe, was in a state of deep corruption. The higher offices of the church -- bishoprics, abbacies, and priories -- were treated as political prizes, distributed by the crown to royal favorites and illegitimate sons of the nobility. James V, who ruled from 1513 to 1542, placed at least five of his illegitimate sons in senior church positions. Cardinal David Beaton, the Archbishop of St. Andrews and the most powerful churchman in Scotland in the 1540s, was notorious for his wealth, his political scheming, his open immorality -- he fathered at least ten children -- and his fierce persecution of Protestants. Many parish priests were poorly educated, some unable to read Latin, and negligent in their duties. The monasteries, once centers of learning and devotion, had in many cases become little more than comfortable residences for the well-connected. The common people of Scotland, devout in their own rough way, received little genuine spiritual nourishment from the institutional church. They paid their tithes, they attended Mass, they venerated their local saints, but they were hungry for the bread of life -- the Word of God in their own tongue.

Yet there were stirrings of reform even before Knox. The teachings of Luther and the Continental reformers had begun to filter into Scotland through trade routes, university contacts, and smuggled books. As early as 1525, the Scottish Parliament passed an act forbidding the importation of Lutheran books -- a sure sign that such books were already circulating. Patrick Hamilton, a young nobleman and scholar of royal blood -- he was the great-grandson of King James II of Scotland -- had studied at the University of Paris and at Marburg, where he came under the influence of Lutheran teaching. He studied directly with the reformer Francis Lambert and absorbed the Continental Reformation's central doctrines: justification by faith alone, the authority of Scripture over tradition, and the sufficiency of Christ's sacrifice for the forgiveness of sins.

Hamilton returned to Scotland in 1527 and began preaching boldly. He composed a set of theological propositions known as "Patrick's Places," which set forth the distinction between the law and the gospel with remarkable clarity. He taught that "the law showeth us our sin; the gospel showeth us the remedy for it. The law showeth us our condemnation; the gospel showeth us our redemption." Hamilton's preaching attracted attention and followers, but it also attracted the hostility of Archbishop James Beaton (the uncle of the later Cardinal David Beaton). On February 29, 1528, Hamilton was arrested, tried for heresy in a hasty proceeding that violated even the canons of the medieval church, and burned at the stake before the gates of St. Salvator's College in St. Andrews. He was only twenty-four years old. His death was agonizingly slow -- the fire was poorly laid, and it took six hours for him to die. Throughout his ordeal, Hamilton continued to pray and to confess his faith. His last words were reported as: "How long, O Lord, shall darkness overwhelm this realm? How long wilt Thou suffer this tyranny of men? Lord Jesus, receive my spirit."

Hamilton's martyrdom, far from silencing the reform movement, gave it its first martyr and its first rallying cry. As one observer reportedly said, "The reek of Patrick Hamilton infected as many as it blew upon." The smoke of his burning carried the seeds of reformation across Scotland. In the years that followed, others took up the cause. Henry Forrest, a young Benedictine friar who possessed a copy of the New Testament in English, was burned at St. Andrews in 1533. Thomas Forret, the vicar of Dollar, who preached the gospel to his people and distributed catechisms, was burned in Edinburgh in 1539. George Wishart, a gifted preacher and scholar who had traveled in England and Switzerland, returned to Scotland in the early 1540s and began an itinerant preaching ministry that attracted large crowds and the hostile attention of the authorities.

Wishart was a man of remarkable character -- learned, eloquent, gentle in spirit, and absolutely fearless in his proclamation of the truth. He preached from the books of Romans and the epistle to the Hebrews, proclaiming the sufficiency of Christ's sacrifice and the free offer of the gospel. During an outbreak of plague in Dundee in 1544, Wishart remained in the city to minister to the sick and dying, preaching from the city gate while the healthy stood at a distance. He was eventually arrested by Cardinal Beaton's agents in January 1546 and burned at the stake in St. Andrews on March 1, 1546, while Beaton watched from the castle walls. Wishart's final prayer was notable for its charity: "I beseech Thee, Father of heaven, forgive them that have, from ignorance or an evil mind, forged lies of me: I forgive them with all my heart. I beseech Christ to forgive them that have condemned me to death this day ignorantly." Among Wishart's followers and bodyguards during his preaching tours was a young priest named John Knox. The martyrdom of his beloved teacher would set Knox's life on a new and irrevocable course.

Now we come to the man himself. John Knox was born around the year 1514 in or near the town of Haddington in East Lothian, Scotland -- a prosperous market town in the fertile lowlands southeast of Edinburgh. The exact date of his birth is uncertain, and some older accounts place it as early as 1505, but the weight of modern scholarship favors the later date. His family was of modest but respectable means -- his father, William Knox, may have been a farmer or tradesman. Little is known of his mother. Knox was educated locally and then at the University of St. Andrews, where he studied under the great scholar John Major (or Mair), one of the last and most distinguished representatives of the medieval scholastic tradition. Major was a man of wide learning -- a philosopher, historian, and theologian who had taught at the University of Paris before returning to Scotland. Interestingly, Major held relatively progressive views on the authority of the people in relation to their rulers, ideas that may have planted early seeds in his young student's mind.

Knox was ordained as a Catholic priest, probably around 1536, and worked for a time as a notary -- a legal professional who drafted documents and witnessed transactions. He also served as a tutor to the sons of several Protestant-leaning lairds (landowners), including the families of Hugh Douglas of Longniddry and John Cockburn of Ormiston. In these households, Knox encountered the Protestant faith in its domestic setting -- in family Bible reading, in table conversation, in the quiet conviction of ordinary men and women who had come to believe that the gospel of grace was the true treasure of the church.

Knox's conversion to Protestant convictions came gradually, shaped by his study of the Church Fathers -- particularly Jerome and Augustine -- and, most profoundly, by the ministry of George Wishart. When Wishart was arrested and martyred, Knox was shattered. He had offered to accompany Wishart to his confrontation with the cardinal, but Wishart had turned him away, saying: "Return to your bairns, and God bless you. One is sufficient for a sacrifice." Knox obeyed, but Wishart's death transformed him. The comfortable obscurity of his previous life was over.

The murder of Cardinal David Beaton by a group of Protestant conspirators on May 29, 1546 -- just three months after Wishart's execution -- led to the occupation of St. Andrews Castle by a band of Protestant sympathizers known as the Castilians. The conspirators hung Beaton's body from the castle wall and fortified themselves against retaliation. Knox joined the Castilians in April 1547, bringing his pupils with him, and it was there, in the castle chapel, that he received a dramatic and unexpected call to the ministry.

The story of Knox's call is one of the most striking episodes in Reformation history. The Protestant garrison had invited the preacher John Rough to serve as their minister, and Rough, recognizing Knox's gifts, publicly called upon him to take up the preaching office during a sermon. Rough addressed the congregation and then turned directly to Knox, charging him in the name of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ not to refuse the call. Knox was overwhelmed. According to his own account in "The History of the Reformation in Scotland," he burst into tears and withdrew to his room, refusing to accept the call for several days. He felt utterly unworthy and unprepared. The weight of the task, the danger of the times, and the inadequacy of his own gifts pressed upon him with crushing force. But the congregation persisted, and Knox finally yielded to what he came to regard as the irresistible call of God. As he would later reflect, the compulsion of the Lord was upon him, and he could not resist it, for the words of Jeremiah burned in his heart: "Then I said, I will not make mention of him, nor speak any more in his name. But his word was in mine heart as a burning fire shut up in my bones, and I was weary with forbearing, and I could not stay" (Jeremiah 20:9).

His first sermon in the parish church of Holy Trinity in St. Andrews was a thunderbolt. He attacked the papacy as the Antichrist and the Mass as idolatry, with a boldness, a clarity, and a scriptural rigor that stunned his hearers. One of the Catholic dignitaries present reported that whereas other preachers "lopped the branches of the papistry," Knox "struck at the root, to destroy the whole." From that moment, Knox was recognized as the most powerful voice of the Protestant cause in Scotland.

But his ministry in St. Andrews was short-lived. In July 1547, a French fleet arrived -- twenty-one galleys carrying over a thousand soldiers -- and bombarded the castle into submission. Knox and the other Castilians were captured. Some were imprisoned in French dungeons; Knox and others were sentenced to serve as galley slaves on French warships. For nineteen months -- from July 1547 to February 1549 -- Knox endured the brutal conditions of the French galleys. He was chained to an oar bench, exposed to blazing sun and freezing rain, fed on meager rations, beaten by overseers, and subjected to constant pressure to recant his Protestant faith by kissing Catholic images and attending Mass. He refused every inducement and every threat.

According to tradition, when a wooden image of the Virgin Mary was brought for the galley slaves to kiss, Knox seized it and threw it into the sea, declaring: "Let our Lady now save herself: she is light enough; let her learn to swim." His spirit was unbroken, though his body was weakened for the rest of his life -- he suffered from chronic kidney problems and digestive ailments that plagued him until his death. During his captivity, when the galleys passed near St. Andrews, a fellow prisoner asked Knox if he recognized the place. Knox replied: "Yes, I know it well, for I see the steeple of that place where God first in public opened my mouth to his glory; and I am fully persuaded, how weak that ever I now appear, that I shall not depart this life till that my tongue shall glorify his godly name in the same place." It was a prophecy that would be fulfilled.

Upon his release in early 1549 -- secured through the diplomatic efforts of the English government -- Knox made his way to England, where the young Protestant king Edward VI had come to the throne. Knox was appointed as a licensed preacher by the Privy Council and served congregations first in Berwick-upon-Tweed, on the Scottish border, and then in Newcastle. His preaching was powerful, direct, and uncompromising, and it attracted large congregations. In Berwick, he met and married Marjory Bowes, the daughter of a prominent local family, despite the initial opposition of her father (though her mother, Elizabeth Bowes, became one of Knox's most devoted spiritual correspondents).

Knox was called to London, where he preached before the young king Edward VI and his council. He was offered the bishopric of Rochester but declined it, partly because he sensed that the English Reformation under Edward was fragile -- the king was sickly, and there was no guarantee that a Protestant successor would follow -- and partly because he had growing reservations about the structure of episcopal church government. Knox was already moving toward the Presbyterian convictions that would define his later career. He believed that the New Testament pattern of church government was government by elders, not by bishops, and he was unwilling to accept a position that he believed lacked scriptural warrant. He also spoke out against the practice of kneeling at communion, which he regarded as a remnant of the Catholic doctrine of the real presence, and his objections were influential enough to prompt the addition of the so-called "Black Rubric" to the 1552 Book of Common Prayer, explaining that kneeling did not imply adoration of the elements.

When the Catholic Mary Tudor came to the English throne in July 1553, following the death of Edward VI, Knox fled to the Continent. He spent the next five years in exile -- years of wandering, writing, and preparation that would prove decisive for his future work. He traveled first to Dieppe in France, then to Geneva, then to Frankfurt am Main, where he served as pastor to a congregation of English-speaking Protestant exiles. In Frankfurt, Knox became embroiled in a bitter controversy over the form of worship to be used by the exile congregation. Knox and his allies favored a simpler, more thoroughly Reformed order of worship; others, led by Richard Cox, insisted on retaining the Book of Common Prayer. The dispute -- known as the "Troubles at Frankfurt" -- ended with Knox's departure from the city, but it clarified his convictions about the regulative principle of worship and deepened his commitment to a thoroughgoing reformation of every aspect of church life.

Knox then went to Geneva, where he encountered the most fully reformed church and city he had ever seen -- John Calvin's Geneva. Knox was deeply impressed. He famously described Geneva as "the most perfect school of Christ that ever was in the earth since the days of the apostles." In Geneva, Knox served as pastor to a congregation of English-speaking exiles and immersed himself in Calvin's theology and ecclesiology. He studied Calvin's "Institutes of the Christian Religion" in depth, observed the Genevan system of church government with its consistory of elders and its discipline of morals, and saw firsthand how a city could be organized according to biblical principles. He met Calvin personally and the two men developed a warm and respectful relationship, though they were temperamentally very different -- Calvin, the careful, systematic scholar; Knox, the passionate, combative preacher. The experience in Geneva was decisive for Knox. It gave him the model for what he would later build in Scotland: a church governed by elders, a worship purified of all human inventions, a system of education rooted in biblical learning, and a society ordered by the law of God.

It was during his years of exile that Knox produced some of his most controversial and influential writings. In 1558, he published "The First Blast of the Trumpet Against the Monstrous Regiment of Women," a fierce polemic arguing that the rule of women over men was contrary to Scripture, natural law, and the practice of the ancient world. The word "regiment" in Knox's title means "rule" or "government" -- he was arguing against the government of women, not against women themselves. The tract was aimed primarily at Mary Tudor of England, who was burning Protestants at the stake, and Mary of Guise, the French-born regent of Scotland, who was using French troops to suppress the Protestant movement. Knox drew his arguments from the Old Testament, from the writings of the apostle Paul, and from the Church Fathers. He cited 1 Timothy 2:12: "But I suffer not a woman to teach, nor to usurp authority over the man, but to be in silence." He also invoked the words of Isaiah 3:12: "As for my people, children are their oppressors, and women rule over them. O my people, they which lead thee cause thee to err, and destroy the way of thy paths."

The timing of Knox's pamphlet could not have been worse for his own interests. Mary Tudor died in November 1558, and the Protestant Elizabeth I came to the English throne. Elizabeth, understandably, was not pleased with Knox's argument that female rule was a violation of divine law, and she never forgave him for it. The tract closed the door to England for Knox and earned him the lasting enmity of the English queen -- a significant political liability, since Scotland's Protestants would later need English military support. Calvin himself gently rebuked Knox for the timing and tone of the work, though he shared some of Knox's theological reservations about female magistrates. Yet Knox never apologized for his position. He believed he had spoken the truth of Scripture, and he was willing to bear the consequences. As he would say on many occasions, his conscience was bound to the Word of God, and no earthly considerations could move him from it.

Knox also published "A Letter to the Commonalty of Scotland" (1558), in which he appealed directly to the common people -- not just the nobility -- to take up the cause of reformation. This was a radical move. In an age when political and religious action was considered the prerogative of princes and nobles, Knox addressed the ordinary farmers, tradesmen, and laborers of Scotland as responsible moral agents with a duty before God to reform the church, even if their rulers refused to act. He wrote: "It appertaineth to you to provide true teachers for yourselves... and unto you doth it pertain to defend them from danger of persecution." This democratic impulse in Knox's thought -- the conviction that all the people, not just the elite, bear responsibility for the state of the church and the nation -- was one of his most revolutionary contributions.

Knox returned to Scotland on May 2, 1559, arriving at the port of Leith near Edinburgh. He came home to a nation on the brink of revolution. The Protestant movement had grown significantly during his absence, nurtured by preachers like John Willock, Paul Methven, and John Douglas, and supported by a powerful coalition of Protestant nobles known as the Lords of the Congregation -- including Lord James Stewart (the future Earl of Moray and half-brother of Mary, Queen of Scots), the Earl of Argyll, the Earl of Glencairn, and Lord Ruthven. Mary of Guise, the queen regent, was attempting to suppress the movement by force, backed by French troops stationed in Scotland. The country was poised between the old faith and the new, between the French alliance and a new alignment with Protestant England.

Knox's return was the spark that ignited the conflagration. On May 11, 1559, he preached a sermon in the church of St. John the Baptist in Perth that was so powerful, so devastating in its denunciation of the Mass as idolatry, that it provoked a popular uprising. The congregation, inflamed by Knox's words and by years of accumulated resentment against the corruption of the church, poured out of the building and began stripping the religious houses of Perth of their images, altars, vestments, and ornaments. The friaries of the Dominicans, the Franciscans, and the Carmelites were ransacked. The "rascal multitude," as Knox somewhat dismissively called them, went further than he intended -- but the die was cast. Perth had declared for the Reformation, and other towns soon followed: St. Andrews, Dundee, Stirling, Edinburgh itself.

The Scottish Reformation was accomplished not only by preaching but by war. The Lords of the Congregation raised an army and confronted the forces of the queen regent. The military struggle was bitter, uncertain, and marked by reverses on both sides. The Protestants briefly captured Edinburgh but were forced to withdraw. Knox preached, prayed, encouraged, rebuked, and kept the spirit of the movement alive during its darkest hours. He wrote to his allies: "God shall send you comfort, despite the raging of your enemies. Their prosperity shall be short, and their end shall be destruction." The struggle was finally resolved when Elizabeth I of England -- despite her personal dislike of Knox -- was persuaded by her secretary William Cecil to send an English fleet and an English army to Scotland to support the Protestant cause against the French. The combined Scottish-English siege of the French garrison at Leith proved decisive. The Treaty of Edinburgh, signed on July 6, 1560, removed French troops from Scotland and opened the way for a Protestant settlement. Mary of Guise had died on June 11, 1560, worn out by illness and the stress of a failing cause, and with her death the last major obstacle to the Reformation was removed.

In August 1560, the Scottish Parliament -- known as the Reformation Parliament -- met in Edinburgh and enacted the legislation that formally established the Reformation in Scotland. In a series of dramatic acts passed on August 17 and 24, 1560, the Parliament abolished the authority of the pope in Scotland, prohibited the celebration of the Mass under penalty of escalating punishments (confiscation of goods for the first offense, exile for the second, death for the third), and adopted a Protestant confession of faith -- the Scots Confession -- as the doctrinal standard of the Scottish church. The Scots Confession, drafted in just four days primarily by Knox and five other ministers whose names all happened to be John (John Winram, John Spottiswoode, John Willock, John Douglas, and John Row -- "the Six Johns"), was a vigorous, forthright, and occasionally pugnacious statement of Reformed theology. It affirmed the authority of Scripture as "the Word of God, sufficient to instruct and make the man of God perfect." It taught the doctrine of election, justification by faith alone, and the true preaching of the Word and right administration of the sacraments as the marks of the true church. It repudiated the Catholic doctrines of transubstantiation, purgatory, the merit of works, and the intercession of saints.

Let us pause here, students, to consider the magnitude of what had been accomplished. In the space of a single year -- from Knox's return in May 1559 to the parliamentary settlement of August 1560 -- Scotland had been transformed from a Catholic nation allied with France into a Protestant nation increasingly aligned with England. It was one of the most rapid and thoroughgoing national reformations in all of European history. And at the center of it all stood John Knox, the galley slave turned thundering preacher, the exile turned national reformer.

But Knox's work was far from finished. The Reformation had been enacted by Parliament, but it needed to be built in the parishes, the schools, and the homes of Scotland. Knox and his colleagues drew up a remarkable document known as the First Book of Discipline (completed in January 1561), which laid out a comprehensive plan for the organization of the reformed church, the establishment of a national system of education, and the care of the poor. The First Book of Discipline is one of the most impressive documents of the entire Reformation era -- a detailed blueprint for a godly commonwealth.

The educational provisions were visionary. Knox proposed a school in every parish to teach reading, grammar, and the catechism; a grammar school in every notable town to teach Latin and the foundations of classical learning; and a college or university in every major city to provide advanced education in the arts, theology, law, and medicine. He believed, as the Reformers universally did, that every Christian must be able to read the Bible for themselves, and that the education of the young was one of the church's most sacred responsibilities. The First Book of Discipline declared: "Seeing that God hath determined that his Church here in earth shall be taught not by angels but by men... of necessity it is that your Honours be most careful for the virtuous education and godly upbringing of the youth of this Realm." Remarkably, Knox also proposed that education should be made available to children of all social classes, and that talented students from poor families should be supported through their education by the church. This was a revolutionary idea in a rigidly hierarchical society.

The system of church government established by Knox and the Scottish reformers was Presbyterian in character -- that is, it was governed not by bishops appointed by the crown but by elders (presbyters) chosen by the congregation. The minister preached the Word and administered the sacraments; the elders, together with the minister, governed the local congregation through the kirk session; congregations were grouped into presbyteries, presbyteries into synods, and synods into a national General Assembly. This structure was designed to prevent the concentration of ecclesiastical power in the hands of any one man or office, and to ensure that the church was governed according to Scripture rather than according to human tradition or royal prerogative. The Presbyterian system would become one of the most influential models of church government in the Protestant world, exported from Scotland to Ireland, to the American colonies, to Australia, and wherever Scottish and Scots-Irish immigrants carried their faith.

Knox's relationship with Mary, Queen of Scots, is one of the most dramatic chapters in his biography and in the history of the Reformation. Mary Stuart, born on December 8, 1542, had been sent to France as a child and raised at the glittering French court. She married the French dauphin Francis in 1558, and when he became King Francis II in 1559, Mary became Queen of France. But Francis died on December 5, 1560, and the young widow -- barely eighteen years old, beautiful, cultured, accomplished, and devoutly Catholic -- returned to Scotland in August 1561 to rule a nation that had, in her absence, embraced the Protestant Reformation.

The confrontations between Knox and Mary became legendary. Knox was summoned to the queen's presence at Holyrood Palace on multiple occasions, and their encounters were marked by fierce argument, royal tears, and Knox's unyielding insistence on the supremacy of God's Word over the authority of monarchs. In their first interview, in September 1561, Knox explained his views on the authority of Scripture and the duty of subjects to obey God rather than princes. Mary challenged him: "Ye think, then, that I have no just authority?" Knox replied that he was content to live under her government as the apostle Paul had lived under the government of Nero, so long as she did not exceed the bounds of her office by commanding him to violate his conscience.

In a later exchange, Mary accused Knox of stirring up her subjects to rebellion. Knox replied that he had taught them the truth of God's Word, and that if princes exceed their bounds, the people are not bound to obey them. Mary asked whether subjects could resist their princes. Knox answered plainly: "If their princes exceed their bounds, Madam, no doubt they may be resisted, even by power." This was radical political theology -- the assertion that the authority of rulers is not absolute but is limited by the law of God, and that the people have the right, indeed the duty, to resist tyranny. Knox grounded this conviction in Scripture. He pointed to the example of the Hebrew midwives in Exodus 1, who disobeyed Pharaoh's command to kill the Hebrew infant boys: "And the midwives feared God, and did not as the king of Egypt commanded them, but saved the men children alive" (Exodus 1:17). He cited the example of Daniel, who continued to pray despite the royal decree forbidding it. He invoked the words of the apostles in Acts 5:29: "We ought to obey God rather than men."

Mary reportedly wept during several of these encounters, and Knox was unmoved. He later wrote: "I have looked in the faces of many angry men, and yet have not been affrighted above measure." Some historians have criticized Knox for his harshness toward the young queen, but Knox believed that the eternal souls of the Scottish people and the future of the Reformed church were at stake. He would not soften his message to spare the feelings of any monarch. As he declared from the pulpit of St. Giles, the great church of Edinburgh where he served as minister from 1560 until his death: "I am in the place where I am demanded of conscience to speak the truth, and therefore the truth I speak, impugn it whoso list."

Knox's preaching was the engine of the Scottish Reformation. He was not an elegant speaker in the manner of some Continental reformers -- his style was direct, urgent, passionate, and often thunderous. Thomas Randolph, the English ambassador to Scotland, reported that Knox was able "in one hour to put more life in his hearers than five hundred trumpets continually blustering in their ears." James Melville, the nephew of the reformer Andrew Melville, recalled hearing Knox preach toward the end of his life, when he was so frail that he had to be lifted into the pulpit: "He was so active and vigorous that he was like to ding the pulpit in blads and fly out of it" -- that is, he preached with such energy that he seemed about to break the pulpit to pieces and fly out of it. Knox drew his preaching power from deep wells of Scripture. He was a man saturated in the Word of God, and his sermons were dense with biblical exposition, application, and exhortation. He preached through the books of the Old Testament prophets with particular relish, finding in the history of Israel a pattern for the experience of Scotland -- a covenant nation called to faithfulness, warned against idolatry, and chastened by God's providential judgments.

The words of Deuteronomy 28:1-2 resonated throughout his preaching: "And it shall come to pass, if thou shalt hearken diligently unto the voice of the LORD thy God, to observe and to do all his commandments which I command thee this day, that the LORD thy God will set thee on high above all nations of the earth: And all these blessings shall come on thee, and overtake thee, if thou shalt hearken unto the voice of the LORD thy God." And the warning of Deuteronomy 28:15 was equally prominent: "But it shall come to pass, if thou wilt not hearken unto the voice of the LORD thy God, to observe to do all his commandments and his statutes which I command thee this day; that all these curses shall come upon thee, and overtake thee."

Knox's theology was thoroughly Calvinistic. He affirmed the sovereignty of God in all things, the total depravity of fallen human nature, the unconditional election of the saints, the irresistible grace of the Holy Spirit in regeneration, and the perseverance of the saints to final glory. He held to the Reformed understanding of the sacraments -- that baptism and the Lord's Supper are genuine means of grace, but that they depend for their efficacy not on any priestly act but on the work of the Holy Spirit in the hearts of believers. He rejected all forms of worship not warranted by Scripture, applying what later theologians would call the regulative principle of worship -- the conviction that the church should worship God only in the ways He has commanded in His Word, and that all human additions and inventions in worship are a form of idolatry.

This principle had profound practical consequences. Under Knox's leadership, the Scottish church stripped its worship of images, altars, vestments, organs, and all the ceremonial apparatus of medieval Catholicism. The worship of the Reformed Kirk of Scotland was stark and simple -- the reading and preaching of the Word, the singing of the Psalms without instrumental accompaniment, prayer, and the administration of the sacraments. This austere beauty of Reformed worship became one of the defining characteristics of Scottish Presbyterianism, and it endured for centuries.

Knox was also a man of extraordinary prayer, and his prayers were as legendary as his preaching. Mary, Queen of Scots, reportedly said that she feared the prayers of John Knox more than all the assembled armies of Europe. This was no mere rhetoric -- it reflected a genuine recognition that Knox was a man who had access to the throne of grace and who wielded spiritual weapons of extraordinary power. Knox's prayers were not polished, formal petitions -- they were urgent, passionate, agonized cries to God for the salvation of Scotland and the triumph of the gospel. He would pray with his hands raised toward heaven, his voice broken with weeping, pleading: "Give me Scotland, or I die!" The Scriptures teach us in James 5:16: "The effectual fervent prayer of a righteous man availeth much." And the prophet Isaiah records the Lord's promise in Isaiah 62:6-7: "I have set watchmen upon thy walls, O Jerusalem, which shall never hold their peace day nor night: ye that make mention of the LORD, keep not silence, and give him no rest, till he establish, and till he make Jerusalem a praise in the earth." Knox was such a watchman upon the walls of Scotland.

Let us consider now the broader significance of Knox's work for the history of the church and the world. The Scottish Reformation was not merely a religious event -- it was a social, educational, and political revolution of the first order. The church that Knox built was deeply woven into the fabric of national life. The parish kirk was the center of community -- it was where people worshiped, where the poor were cared for, where education was provided, and where moral discipline was maintained. The kirk session supervised the spiritual and moral life of the parish with a thoroughness that could be both admirable and, on occasion, oppressive.

The educational legacy of the Scottish Reformation was particularly remarkable. Knox's vision of a school in every parish was gradually implemented over the following century and a half, and Scotland became one of the most literate nations in Europe. By the late seventeenth century, Scotland had achieved near-universal male literacy -- an astonishing accomplishment for a poor, sparsely populated country. The Scottish universities -- St. Andrews, Glasgow, Aberdeen, and Edinburgh (founded in 1583) -- became centers of Reformed learning that attracted students from across the Protestant world. The Scottish tradition of universal education, rooted in Knox's conviction that every believer must be able to read the Word of God, produced a disproportionate number of scholars, scientists, philosophers, engineers, and inventors in the centuries that followed.

Knox's political theology was equally consequential. His insistence that rulers are bound by the law of God and that the people have the right and duty to resist tyranny was developed further by his successors. Andrew Melville, the great Presbyterian leader of the generation after Knox, confronted King James VI in 1596 and told him bluntly: "There are two kings and two kingdoms in Scotland: there is Christ Jesus the King, and His kingdom the Kirk, whose subject James the Sixth is, and of whose kingdom not a king, nor a lord, nor a head, but a member." Samuel Rutherford, the great Covenanting theologian, wrote "Lex, Rex" in 1644, arguing systematically that the law is king, not the king law, and that rulers who violate the law of God forfeit their authority. The Scottish Covenanters of the 1660s, 1670s, and 1680s endured decades of savage persecution -- field preachings, imprisonments, executions, and the "Killing Time" under the Stuart monarchs -- rather than surrender their Presbyterian convictions. These ideas flowed from Scotland into the English Puritan movement, across the Atlantic to the American colonies, and eventually into the founding documents of the United States.

Knox spent his final years in Edinburgh, his health broken by decades of labor, the lingering effects of his time in the French galleys, and a stroke that partially incapacitated him in 1570. He continued to preach at St. Giles, though with declining strength. In 1572, he was strong enough to participate in the installation of his successor, James Lawson, and to preach a final series of sermons. He preached his last sermon at St. Giles on November 9, 1572. He was so weak that he had to be assisted to the church. The political situation in Scotland remained turbulent -- Mary, Queen of Scots, had been deposed in 1567 and imprisoned in England, and her infant son James VI had been placed on the throne under Protestant regents.

On November 24, 1572, John Knox died in his home in Edinburgh. His wife Margaret (his second wife, whom he had married in 1564) and several friends were at his bedside. His last recorded words were: "Now it is come." At his burial in the churchyard of St. Giles on November 26, 1572, the Regent Morton -- James Douglas, Earl of Morton -- spoke the famous epitaph: "Here lies one who neither flattered nor feared any flesh." It was a fitting tribute to a man who had spent his life speaking the truth of God to the powerful and the powerless alike, without regard for the consequences.

Knox's legacy was immense and enduring. The Kirk of Scotland became one of the most thoroughly Reformed churches in the world, and its Presbyterian form of government became the model for Reformed churches across the globe. The Scots Confession was eventually replaced by the Westminster Confession of Faith in 1647, but the spirit of Knox's theology -- its emphasis on the sovereignty of God, the authority of Scripture, the simplicity of worship, and the centrality of preaching -- remained the animating force of Scottish Christianity for centuries. The Puritan writer Thomas M'Crie observed in his biography of Knox, published in 1811, that Knox "accomplished a greater work than any reformer of his age." While that assessment may be debated, it contains a profound truth. Knox did not merely reform the doctrine and worship of the Scottish church -- he reformed the entire nation.

His influence on the Puritan tradition -- the tradition we study in Puritan Gold -- was deep and pervasive. The English Puritans shared Knox's commitment to the authority of Scripture, his zeal for the purity of worship, his conviction that the church must be governed by elders rather than bishops, and his insistence that every believer must be educated in the truths of the faith. The Puritans who emigrated to New England carried with them a vision of church and society that owed much to the Scottish model that Knox had built. The words of the apostle Paul in 1 Corinthians 15:58 were Knox's life verse and may serve as the motto of his entire career: "Therefore, my beloved brethren, be ye stedfast, unmoveable, always abounding in the work of the Lord, forasmuch as ye know that your labour is not in vain in the Lord."

--- Closing ---

John Knox once declared that he feared the face of no living man, because he had first feared the face of God. That holy courage -- born of deep conviction, nurtured by Scripture, and tested by suffering in the galleys, in exile, and in the courts of queens -- is the mark of every true reformer. Knox's Scotland was built on the Word of God, and its legacy endures wherever the Reformed faith is cherished and proclaimed. As Knox himself prayed: "Lord, give me Scotland, or I die." God answered that prayer, and Scotland became a light to the nations. Until next time, students.
