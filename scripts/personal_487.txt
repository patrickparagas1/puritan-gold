PURITAN GOLD -- My Growth, Day 26
"The Tongue — Governing the Most Dangerous Member"
Pastoral Day — Practical Counsel on Speech

---

Welcome. Today is a Pastoral day. Our subject is the tongue — the small member that the book of James calls a fire, a world of iniquity, and an unruly evil. The Puritans had much to say about governing this most dangerous instrument.

Let us read James 3:5-8 (KJV):

"Even so the tongue is a little member, and boasteth great things. Behold, how great a matter a little fire kindleth! And the tongue is a fire, a world of iniquity: so is the tongue among our members, that it defileth the whole body, and setteth on fire the course of nature; and it is set on fire of hell. For every kind of beasts, and of birds, and of serpents, and of things in the sea, is tamed, and hath been tamed of mankind: But the tongue can no man tame; it is an unruly evil, full of deadly poison."

--- Teaching ---

James's assessment is sobering. The tongue is a fire. It defiles the whole body. It is set on fire of hell. It is an unruly evil full of deadly poison. And no human being can tame it. If this is true — and it is — then the governance of the tongue is one of the most urgent and difficult disciplines of the Christian life.

The Puritans identified several sins of the tongue that require constant vigilance.

Gossip. The Puritan Thomas Watson defined gossip as speaking about a person's faults behind their back in a way that damages their reputation. He distinguished between legitimate concern — sharing a need with someone who can help — and gossip, which takes pleasure in another's failings. The test is simple: Would you say this in front of the person you are talking about? If not, you are gossiping.

Flattery. Richard Baxter warned that flattery is the opposite of gossip but equally dangerous. Gossip tears down behind a person's back. Flattery builds up falsely to a person's face. Both are forms of deception. The Puritans counseled honest, kind speech — not flattery that inflates, but encouragement that edifies.

Complaining. Jeremiah Burroughs, in his work on contentment, identified murmuring as one of the most common and most destructive sins of the tongue. Complaining about your circumstances is, at root, an accusation against God's wisdom and goodness. Every complaint says, in effect, "God has not ordered my life correctly." The remedy is thanksgiving — deliberately choosing to speak gratitude rather than grievance.

Harsh words. The Puritans distinguished between speaking the truth and speaking the truth harshly. Ephesians 4:15 commands us to speak the truth in love. Truth without love is cruelty. Love without truth is sentimentality. The Christian must hold both together — saying what needs to be said but saying it with gentleness, respect, and genuine concern for the other person's good.

Idle words. Jesus said that for every idle word that men shall speak, they shall give account in the day of judgment (Matthew 12:36). The Puritans took this seriously. They were not opposed to conversation, humor, or ordinary talk. But they warned against the habit of filling the air with words that have no purpose — chatter that neither edifies nor delights but simply kills time and clutters the atmosphere.

The Puritans offered several practical counsels for governing the tongue. First, think before you speak. Proverbs 29:20 says, "Seest thou a man that is hasty in his words? there is more hope of a fool than of him." The discipline of pausing before speaking — asking yourself, "Is this true? Is this kind? Is this necessary?" — prevents more damage than any other single practice.

Second, speak less. Proverbs 10:19: "In the multitude of words there wanteth not sin: but he that refraineth his lips is wise." The person who speaks little speaks well. The person who speaks much inevitably sins.

Third, season your words with grace. Colossians 4:6: "Let your speech be alway with grace, seasoned with salt." Salt preserves and flavors. Gracious speech preserves relationships and flavors conversation with kindness.

Fourth, pray over your tongue daily. Psalm 141:3: "Set a watch, O Lord, before my mouth; keep the door of my lips." Since no human being can tame the tongue, we must ask God to do what we cannot. This is a prayer that should be prayed every morning.

--- Closing ---

The tongue is small but mighty. It can build or destroy, heal or wound, bless or curse. Guard it diligently. Lord, set a watch before my mouth. Keep the door of my lips. Let my words build up, not tear down. Let my speech be seasoned with grace, guided by truth, and governed by love. Tame what I cannot tame. Amen.
