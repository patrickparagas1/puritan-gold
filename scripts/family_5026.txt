PURITAN GOLD -- Family Devotional, Day 26
"Contentment in Seasons of Want"
Jeremiah Burroughs — The Rare Jewel of Christian Contentment for Families


Good evening, dear family. Welcome back to Puritan Gold. Tonight Burroughs speaks to the seasons that test our contentment most — the seasons of want, loss, and difficulty — and shows us how faith transforms scarcity into sufficiency. Let us begin with prayer.

Let us pray together. Lord, when we walk through seasons of lack and loss, help us to cling to You. You are our provider and our comfort. Teach us tonight that contentment is possible even when our circumstances are hard, because You are with us. In Christ's name, Amen.

Our Scripture reading this evening comes from Habakkuk 3:17-18. Let us hear the Word of the Lord:

"Although the fig tree shall not blossom, neither shall fruit be in the vines; the labour of the olive shall fail, and the fields shall yield no meat; the flock shall be cut off from the fold, and there shall be no herd in the stalls: Yet I will rejoice in the LORD, I will joy in the God of my salvation."

That is Habakkuk 3:17-18. Let those words settle in your heart for a moment before we continue.


The prophet Habakkuk paints a picture of total loss. The fig tree has failed. The vines are barren. The olive crop has been destroyed. The fields produce nothing. The livestock are gone. In the agricultural economy of ancient Israel, this was complete financial ruin. There was nothing left. And yet, in the face of absolute destitution, Habakkuk declares: "Yet I will rejoice in the LORD."

This is the summit of Christian contentment, and Jeremiah Burroughs held it up as the aspiration of every believing family. Contentment in plenty is admirable. Contentment in moderate circumstances is commendable. But contentment in severe want — rejoicing in God when everything else has been stripped away — this is the rare jewel at its most brilliant.

Burroughs acknowledged that contentment in want does not mean the absence of pain. It does not mean pretending that loss does not hurt. It does not mean plastering on a smile while your heart is breaking. The content Christian in a season of want feels the loss keenly. But beneath the pain, there is a bedrock of trust that refuses to give way. The content Christian says, "This hurts. This is hard. But God is still good. God is still sovereign. God is still my Father. And He has not abandoned me."

Burroughs taught that the key to contentment in want is understanding the difference between our needs and our wants. In our affluent culture, we often confuse the two. We think we need things that are actually luxuries. We think we cannot survive without comforts that millions of people around the world have never possessed. Burroughs would gently challenge families to examine their expectations. Are you discontent because you lack necessities, or because you lack luxuries? Are you in genuine need, or are you simply unable to have everything you desire?

Even in genuine need, Burroughs taught that God has promised to supply. "My God shall supply all your need according to his riches in glory by Christ Jesus" (Philippians 4:19). This promise does not guarantee wealth or comfort. It guarantees sufficiency. God promises that His children will have what they truly need — food, shelter, clothing, and above all, His presence. And His presence, Burroughs insisted, is worth more than all the material wealth in the world.

Burroughs told families in seasons of want to practice what he called "spiritual arithmetic." Instead of counting what they have lost, they should count what they still have. You have lost your income — but you still have your family. You have lost your health — but you still have your salvation. You have lost your comfortable home — but you still have the presence of God. When you begin to count the blessings that remain rather than the blessings that have been taken, you discover that even in your worst season, you are immeasurably rich.

Burroughs also pointed families in want toward the cross of Christ. If God did not spare His own Son but delivered Him up for us all, how shall He not with Him also freely give us all things? (Romans 8:32). The cross is the ultimate proof that God is for us, that He loves us, and that He will provide for us. A God who gave His Son will not withhold the lesser things we need.

Tonight, if your family is in a season of want — whether financial, relational, emotional, or spiritual — take courage. The God who sustained Habakkuk in total loss, who sustained Burroughs in persecution, and who sustained Paul in chains is the same God who sustains you tonight. He is enough. He has always been enough. And He always will be enough.


Let us pause and talk as a family. Here are tonight's discussion questions. Everyone gets a turn to share.

Question 1: Habakkuk rejoiced in God even when everything was gone. What made this possible? How can we develop this kind of faith?

Question 2: Burroughs said we should practice "spiritual arithmetic" — counting what we still have rather than what we have lost. What blessings remain in our family that we sometimes overlook?

Question 3: How does the cross of Christ give us confidence that God will provide for us in every season?

Take a few minutes to talk about these questions together. Let everyone share, and listen to one another with patience and love.


Now, here is our memory verse for today. Let us say it together:

"Yet I will rejoice in the LORD, I will joy in the God of my salvation." -- Habakkuk 3:18

Try to say it out loud three times right now. Then, before bed tonight, see if you can say it from memory.


Let us pray together. Lord, even if everything else were taken from us, You would be enough. Help us to trust You in every season — in abundance and in want. Give us the faith of Habakkuk, who rejoiced in You when everything else was gone. You are our salvation, our provision, and our joy. In Jesus' name, Amen.

Sleep well tonight, resting in a God who is always enough.
