From "Exposition of 1 John" by Arthur Pink:

Pink treats the anointing of the Holy Spirit as the believer's safeguard against deception. Those who have received this anointing possess an instinctive discernment of truth and error. They do not need any man to teach them, not in the sense that they are independent of the ministry of the Word, but in the sense that the Spirit within them confirms the truth and exposes the lie. THE ABIDING LIFE AND DIVINE SONSHIP The final chapters address abiding in Christ, the nature of true righteousness, and the amazing grace of divine sonship. Pink shows that abiding in Christ is not a mystical experience reserved for an elite few but the normal posture of every genuine believer. It involves continued dependence upon Christ, continued obedience to His Word, and continued communion with His Person. The exposition reaches its climax with the apostle's exclamation in chapter three, verse one: Behold, what manner of love the Father hath bestowed upon us, that we should be called the sons of God. Pink treats this as the summit of the apostle's teaching, showing that the new birth, with all its evidences in righteousness and love, flows from the sovereign and electing love of the Father. The world does not recognize the children of God, just as it did not recognize Christ Himself. But this misunderstanding by the world is no cause for dismay; it is rather a mark of conformity to the Master. SELF-EXAMINATION AND THE MARKS OF THE REGENERATE One of the most distinctive features of Pink's exposition is his relentless emphasis on self-examination. Following the apostle's own method, Pink sets forth a series of tests by which the professing Christian may determine whether his faith is genuine or counterfeit. These tests are not designed to torment sensitive consciences but to expose empty profession and to confirm the true believer in his assurance. The marks of the regenerate, as Pink draws them from the epistle, include walking in the light, keeping the commandments of God, loving the brethren, overcoming the world, and confessing the truth concerning the Person of Christ. Where these marks are present, even in imperfect measure, they constitute evidence of the new birth. Where they are altogether absent, no amount of doctrinal knowledge or religious activity can compensate for their lack. Pink is unsparing in his application of these tests, yet he is always careful to distinguish between the habitual direction of a life and isolated failures or momentary lapses. THE NATURE OF ASSURANCE Closely connected with his treatment of self-examination is Pink's handling of Christian assurance. He shows that the epistle was written so that believers might know that they have eternal life. This assurance is not presumption; it is the settled confidence that arises from the witness of the Spirit and the evidence of a transformed life.
