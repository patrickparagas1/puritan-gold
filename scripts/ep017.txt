PURITAN GOLD — Episode 17
"The Righteous and the Wicked"
Week 3, Day 3 (Wednesday) — Proverbs 10

---

Have you ever noticed how the book of Proverbs loves contrasts?

Black and white. Light and dark. Wisdom and folly. Life and death. And nowhere is this more vivid than in Proverbs chapter 10 — where nearly every single verse sets two kinds of people side by side. The righteous... and the wicked. The wise... and the foolish. The diligent... and the sluggard.

There's no gray area in Proverbs 10. No comfortable middle ground. And I think that's intentional. Because God wants us to see clearly. He wants us to look at the path of righteousness and the path of wickedness and understand — really understand — where each one leads.

This is Puritan Gold. And today, we're going to walk through Proverbs 10 together and let these sharp, vivid contrasts do their work on our hearts. We'll focus on three themes that run through this chapter: integrity, the tongue, and diligence. And we'll see how the Puritans understood these contrasts — not as mere moral lessons, but as descriptions of two fundamentally different ways of being in the world.

Let's begin with integrity.

Proverbs 10:9 says: "He that walketh uprightly walketh surely: but he that perverteth his ways shall be known."

There's a beautiful simplicity in that verse. The person who walks uprightly — honestly, transparently, with nothing to hide — that person walks surely. Securely. With confidence. Not because they're perfect, but because they have nothing to fear from exposure. Their private life matches their public life. Their words match their deeds. They are the same person in the dark that they are in the light.

But the one who perverts his ways — who twists, who deceives, who lives a double life — shall be known. Eventually, the mask comes off. Eventually, the truth comes out. You can maintain a facade for a while... sometimes a long while... but not forever.

Thomas Watson spoke often about the danger of hypocrisy — the sin of appearing righteous while harboring wickedness within. He called hypocrisy "the worst kind of deceit, for it uses the things of God as a mask for the things of the devil." That's a piercing thought. Using godliness as a disguise for ungodliness. Playing the part of the righteous while walking the path of the wicked.

And Watson would say that the reason hypocrisy is so dangerous is not just that it deceives others — it deceives the hypocrite himself. You can play a role for so long that you begin to believe you are the character you're playing. You go through the motions of faith — prayer, church attendance, religious language — and you mistake the motions for the reality.

But Proverbs 10:9 says: the one who perverts his ways shall be known. God sees. God knows. And in His time, the truth always surfaces.

So the question for us is not, "Can I maintain a good reputation?" The question is, "Am I walking uprightly? Is my inner life consistent with my outer life? Am I the same person when no one is watching?"

The Puritans called this "walking before the face of God" — coram Deo. Living every moment with the awareness that God is present. That He sees not just your actions but your motives. Not just your words but your thoughts. When you live coram Deo, integrity isn't a burden — it's a relief. Because you stop trying to manage multiple versions of yourself. You become... one person. Whole. Integrated. That's what the word "integrity" actually means — wholeness. Being undivided.

Now, let's talk about the tongue. Because Proverbs 10 has much to say about our words. And I dare say this is where most of us feel the sharpest conviction.

Proverbs 10:12 says: "Hatred stirreth up strifes: but love covereth all sins."

What a contrast. Hatred stirs up strife. It takes a small offense and fans it into a flame. It takes a misunderstanding and turns it into a war. It takes a wound and picks at it until it festers.

But love... love covers. Not in the sense of hiding or excusing — we talked about that when we discussed Proverbs 28:13. This is a different kind of covering. This is the covering of grace. Of mercy. Of choosing not to broadcast someone else's failure. Of choosing not to gossip. Of choosing not to hold a grudge.

Think about the people in your life who have hurt you. And think about how easy it would be — how satisfying it might feel in the moment — to stir up strife. To tell someone else about what they did. To build your case. To rehearse the offense again and again in your mind until the bitterness is calcified into something hard and unyielding.

Now think about what love would do instead.

Love would cover it. Love would absorb the blow. Love would go to that person privately, gently, seeking reconciliation rather than revenge. Love would choose silence over slander, mercy over malice, peace over the petty pleasure of being right.

The Apostle Peter, quoting this very principle, wrote: "Above all things have fervent love among yourselves: for love shall cover the multitude of sins." Above all things. That's not a suggestion. That's a priority. And it starts with the tongue.

Elsewhere in Proverbs 10, we read verse 19: "In the multitude of words there wanteth not sin: but he that refraineth his lips is wise." The more you talk, the more likely you are to sin. That's just... honest reality. We've all experienced it. The conversation goes on too long, and suddenly you've said something you shouldn't have. You've shared something that wasn't yours to share. You've exaggerated. You've complained. You've torn someone down.

The wise person restrains their lips. Not because they have nothing to say, but because they've learned that silence is often more godly than speech.

John Bunyan, in his allegory The Pilgrim's Progress, created a character named Talkative — a man who could speak eloquently about spiritual things but whose life didn't match his words. Christian said of him, "He talketh of prayer, of repentance, of faith, and of the new birth; but he knows but only to talk of them." Talkative had a gifted tongue but an unchanged heart. And Bunyan uses him as a warning — the tongue can be the most deceptive organ in the body. It can create an entire illusion of godliness without the substance.

James agrees: "If any man among you seem to be religious, and bridleth not his tongue, but deceiveth his own heart, this man's religion is vain."

So guard your tongue, friend. It's not just about avoiding profanity or obvious lies. It's about the subtle sins of speech — the cutting remark disguised as a joke, the "prayer request" that's really gossip, the complaint dressed up as a concern, the flattery that manipulates, the silence when you should speak truth, and the speech when you should be silent.

Now, let's turn to our third theme: diligence.

Proverbs 10:4 says: "He becometh poor that dealeth with a slack hand: but the hand of the diligent maketh rich."

And verse 5: "He that gathereth in summer is a wise son: but he that sleepeth in harvest is a son that causeth shame."

The Puritans had a very high view of work. Not because they worshipped productivity — but because they understood that all of life is lived unto the Lord. Every task, no matter how humble, is an act of worship when it's done for God's glory. The cobbler making shoes, the farmer plowing fields, the mother tending children, the merchant keeping honest books — all of it, in the Puritan vision, was sacred labor.

Richard Baxter, in his monumental work "A Christian Directory," wrote at length about the duty of diligence. He said that laziness is not just a social problem — it's a spiritual one. The sluggard is not merely unproductive; he is unfaithful. He has been given time, talent, and opportunity by God — and he buries them. He sleeps through the harvest. He wastes the daylight. And the shame of it, Baxter would say, is not just that he fails to provide for himself... but that he fails to glorify the God who made him for good works.

But notice — Proverbs is not glorifying workaholism. The diligence it commends is purposeful, steady, faithful effort applied at the right time. "He that gathereth in summer" — that's timing. That's wisdom. It's knowing when to work and working when it's time to work. Not frantic, anxious busyness driven by fear. But calm, faithful, steady labor driven by trust in the One who gives the harvest.

And here's where all three themes — integrity, the tongue, and diligence — come together. Look at Proverbs 10:27: "The fear of the LORD prolongeth days: but the years of the wicked shall be shortened."

There it is again. The fear of the Lord. The thread that runs through everything we've been studying this week. When you fear the Lord, you walk with integrity — because you know He sees all things. When you fear the Lord, you guard your tongue — because you know He hears every word. When you fear the Lord, you work with diligence — because you know every day is a gift and every task is an offering.

The fear of the Lord is not just an emotion or a doctrine. It's a way of life. It shapes the righteous person from the inside out — forming their character, directing their speech, energizing their labor.

And the contrast with the wicked? The wicked have no such anchor. Their integrity is situational — they're honest when it benefits them and dishonest when it doesn't. Their speech is self-serving — they flatter when they want something and slander when they're angry. Their work ethic is driven by greed or fear — not by love for God.

And the end of these two paths could not be more different. The righteous, says Proverbs 10:28, have an expectation of gladness. But the expectation of the wicked shall perish.

Let me bring this home. The contrasts in Proverbs 10 aren't meant to make you say, "Well, I'm glad I'm one of the righteous ones." They're meant to make you examine yourself. Honestly. Searchingly. In the light of God's Word.

Where is your integrity compromised? Where have you allowed a gap between who you appear to be and who you really are?

Where has your tongue been a source of strife rather than peace? Where have you stirred up conflict instead of covering offenses with love?

Where has laziness crept in — not just in your work, but in your spiritual life? Have you been sleeping through the harvest? Have you neglected prayer, neglected Scripture, neglected the means of grace that God has given you?

John Owen reminded us that the Christian life requires constant vigilance. Not anxious, fearful vigilance — but the watchful, steady attention of a soldier who knows the enemy is real and the battle is ongoing.

Walk uprightly. Guard your tongue. Work diligently. Fear the Lord.

These are not burdens. They are the marks of a life that is truly alive — a life rooted in the wisdom of God, shaped by the Spirit of God, and aimed at the glory of God.

Let me pray for us.

Father, we see ourselves in these contrasts — and we confess that we have not always walked the path of the righteous. Our integrity has wavered. Our tongues have sinned. Our hands have been slack. Forgive us, Lord. And by Your Spirit, conform us to the image of Your Son — who walked in perfect integrity, who spoke only truth and grace, and who labored faithfully unto death for our salvation. Make us more like Him today. For His glory and our good. Amen.
