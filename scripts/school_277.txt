PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 17
"Other Religions and Christianity"
Apologetics for Young Christians — Lesson 17

---

Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we address one of the most culturally sensitive and profoundly important questions in all of apologetics: How does Christianity relate to the other major religions of the world? Is Jesus really the only way to God, or are all religions equally valid paths to the same destination? We will examine this question with both intellectual rigor and genuine compassion, seeking to understand what other religions teach, where they differ from Christianity, and why the exclusive claims of Christ are not arrogance but grace. Let us pray and begin.

Lord Jesus Christ, Thou who didst declare Thyself to be the Way, the Truth, and the Life, grant us wisdom and discernment as we study the religions of the world. Help us to treat those who hold different beliefs with genuine love and respect, while never compromising the truth of Thy gospel. Give us hearts that ache for those who do not yet know Thee, and tongues that speak Thy truth with both boldness and tenderness. In Thy holy name we pray, Amen.

--- Lesson ---

Students, we live in a world of extraordinary religious diversity. There are approximately 4,300 religions in the world today. The major world religions — Christianity, Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism, Judaism, and Sikhism — together encompass the vast majority of the world's population. In an age of globalization, the internet, and international travel, you will inevitably encounter people of different faiths — in your neighborhoods, your workplaces, your schools, and even within your extended families. You must be prepared not only to understand what these religions teach but also to articulate, with clarity and charity, why the Christian faith stands apart as the one true revelation of the one true God.

In a culture that celebrates diversity and tolerance above almost all other values, the Christian claim that Jesus is the only way to God sounds, to many ears, arrogant, narrow-minded, and even dangerous. "How can you say that your religion is the only right one?" people will ask. "Aren't all religions basically the same? Don't they all teach the Golden Rule? Aren't they all different paths up the same mountain?" These are sincere questions that deserve thoughtful and respectful answers. Let us begin by examining what the major world religions actually teach and then consider whether the claim that they are all "basically the same" withstands scrutiny.

Let us begin with Hinduism, the world's oldest major religion, with roots stretching back more than three thousand years to the ancient Vedic civilization of the Indian subcontinent. Hinduism is extraordinarily diverse — it encompasses a vast range of beliefs, practices, and traditions. However, several core tenets characterize most expressions of Hinduism. Hinduism generally teaches that ultimate reality, called Brahman, is impersonal — an infinite, formless, all-pervading spiritual essence that underlies all of existence. The individual soul, or Atman, is believed to be identical with Brahman. The goal of human life, in most Hindu traditions, is to achieve moksha — liberation from the cycle of birth, death, and rebirth (samsara) — by realizing one's essential unity with Brahman. This liberation is typically sought through one or more paths: the path of knowledge (jnana yoga), the path of devotion (bhakti yoga), the path of disciplined action (karma yoga), or the path of meditation (raja yoga). Hinduism embraces a vast pantheon of gods and goddesses — Brahma, Vishnu, Shiva, Lakshmi, Saraswati, Ganesh, and thousands more — which are generally understood as manifestations or aspects of the one ultimate reality, Brahman.

Hinduism also teaches the doctrine of karma — the moral law of cause and effect. Every action produces consequences, either in this life or in future lives. Good actions produce positive karma; bad actions produce negative karma. One's present circumstances — including one's birth into a particular caste, family, or condition — are determined by the accumulated karma of past lives. This doctrine has profound implications for Hindu ethics and social structure, including the traditional caste system, which organized Indian society into rigid hereditary social classes.

Now consider how this compares with Christianity. The differences are not superficial — they are fundamental and irreconcilable. Christianity teaches that God is personal, not impersonal. The God of the Bible is not an abstract spiritual essence but a living, conscious, intelligent, and loving Person — indeed, three Persons in one God: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. The Westminster Shorter Catechism, beloved by the Puritans, begins with this foundational truth: "What is the chief end of man? Man's chief end is to glorify God, and to enjoy him for ever." We can glorify and enjoy an impersonal force no more than we can have a conversation with a mathematical equation. The personal nature of God is essential to the Christian faith.

Furthermore, Christianity teaches that the human soul is not identical with God. We are creatures, not sparks of the divine. We were made by God, for God, but we are distinct from God. The distance between Creator and creature is infinite, and it is bridged not by mystical realization but by divine grace. The Christian doctrine of creation stands in stark contrast to the Hindu doctrine of the essential identity of Atman and Brahman.

Christianity also rejects the doctrine of karma and reincarnation. The Bible teaches that "it is appointed unto men once to die, but after this the judgment" (Hebrews 9:27). We do not live multiple lives. We live one life, we die one death, and we face one judgment. Our standing before God is determined not by the accumulated karma of countless past lives but by our response to the gospel of Jesus Christ in this one life we are given.

Let us turn next to Buddhism, which emerged in the sixth century before Christ in the Indian subcontinent. Siddhartha Gautama (c. 563-483 BC), the founder of Buddhism, was born a prince in what is now Nepal. According to Buddhist tradition, Siddhartha lived a sheltered life of luxury in his father's palace until, as a young man, he encountered the realities of old age, sickness, and death for the first time. These encounters shattered his illusions about worldly happiness and set him on a spiritual quest that culminated in his experience of "enlightenment" (bodhi) while meditating under a fig tree in Bodh Gaya, India. From that point forward, he was known as the Buddha — "the Awakened One."

The core teachings of Buddhism are summarized in the Four Noble Truths: (1) Life is characterized by suffering (dukkha). (2) Suffering is caused by desire or craving (tanha). (3) Suffering can be ended by eliminating desire. (4) The way to eliminate desire is to follow the Noble Eightfold Path — right understanding, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration. The ultimate goal of Buddhism is nirvana — the cessation of suffering, the extinguishing of desire, and liberation from the cycle of rebirth. In many Buddhist traditions, the concept of a personal creator God is either denied or treated as irrelevant. Buddhism focuses not on God but on the individual's quest for liberation from suffering through self-effort and self-discipline.

The contrasts with Christianity are again profound. Christianity does not teach that the fundamental problem of human existence is suffering caused by desire. The fundamental problem is sin — rebellion against a holy God. The solution is not the elimination of desire but the transformation of desire — learning to desire God above all things. As Augustine of Hippo (AD 354-430) wrote in the opening words of his "Confessions": "Thou hast made us for Thyself, O Lord, and our hearts are restless until they rest in Thee." The Christian solution to the human predicament is not self-effort but divine grace — not the extinguishing of the self but the redemption and restoration of the self through the saving work of Jesus Christ. As the Apostle Paul wrote: "For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: Not of works, lest any man should boast" (Ephesians 2:8-9).

Furthermore, Christianity teaches that suffering, while real and painful, is not meaningless. It is not simply a problem to be escaped but a context in which God works to refine our character, deepen our dependence upon Him, and prepare us for eternal glory. "We glory in tribulations also: knowing that tribulation worketh patience; And patience, experience; and experience, hope" (Romans 5:3-4). The Puritan minister Thomas Watson (c. 1620-1686) wrote in his work "All Things for Good": "Affliction is God's flail to thresh off our husks. It is not our comfort but our character that God is most concerned with." This understanding of suffering stands in dramatic contrast to the Buddhist goal of escaping suffering altogether.

It is also worth noting that Buddhism, in its original Theravada form, offers no concept of a personal God who loves, cares for, and redeems His creatures. The universe, in classical Buddhist thought, is self-existing and self-governing, operating according to impersonal laws of karma. While later forms of Buddhism — particularly Mahayana Buddhism and Pure Land Buddhism — introduced quasi-divine figures called bodhisattvas, beings who postpone their own nirvana to help others achieve enlightenment, these figures bear no resemblance to the God of the Bible. They do not create the world, they do not judge sin, they do not atone for guilt, and they do not offer personal, covenantal relationship with their followers. The bodhisattva offers guidance; Christ offers Himself. The bodhisattva points the way; Christ is the Way. As He said to His disciples on the night before His crucifixion: "I will not leave you comfortless: I will come to you" (John 14:18). The personal, intimate, covenantal love of the God of the Bible has no parallel in any form of Buddhism.

The historical contrast between the founders of Buddhism and Christianity is also instructive. Siddhartha Gautama was a seeker — a man searching for answers to the problem of suffering. He tried various paths, experimented with asceticism and meditation, and eventually arrived at his own system of thought. Jesus Christ was not a seeker. He was the Answer. He did not come searching for truth; He declared Himself to be the Truth. He did not come to escape suffering; He came to enter into suffering on behalf of those He loved, to bear their sins in His own body on the cross, and to conquer death by rising from the grave. Siddhartha Gautama died at the age of approximately eighty and was cremated. His remains were distributed as relics. Jesus Christ died at the age of approximately thirty-three, was buried in a tomb, and rose bodily from the dead on the third day. The empty tomb of Christ stands as the decisive difference between Christianity and every other religion on earth.

Now let us consider Islam, the world's second-largest religion, with approximately 1.8 billion adherents worldwide. Islam was founded by Muhammad ibn Abdullah (c. AD 570-632) in the Arabian Peninsula in the seventh century after Christ. Muslims believe that Muhammad was the final prophet in a line of prophets that includes Abraham, Moses, and Jesus. The central confession of Islam is the Shahada: "There is no god but Allah, and Muhammad is the messenger of Allah." The holy book of Islam is the Quran, which Muslims believe was dictated by the angel Gabriel to Muhammad over a period of approximately twenty-three years.

Islam teaches that God (Allah) is absolutely one — a strict, uncompromising monotheism that explicitly rejects the Christian doctrine of the Trinity. The Quran states: "They do blaspheme who say: Allah is one of three in a Trinity, for there is no god except One Allah" (Surah 5:73). Islam teaches that Jesus (known as Isa in Arabic) was a great prophet but not the Son of God, not divine, and — crucially — that He was not crucified. The Quran states: "They killed him not, nor crucified him, but so it was made to appear to them" (Surah 4:157). According to Islamic teaching, Jesus was taken up to heaven without dying and will return at the end of time to bear witness against those who falsely deified Him.

Islam teaches that salvation comes through submission to Allah and through good works — the Five Pillars of Islam: the confession of faith (Shahada), prayer five times daily (Salat), charitable giving (Zakat), fasting during the month of Ramadan (Sawm), and pilgrimage to Mecca (Hajj). A Muslim's eternal destiny is determined by the weighing of good deeds against bad deeds on the Day of Judgment.

The differences between Islam and Christianity are, once again, fundamental and irreconcilable. Christianity affirms the Trinity — one God eternally existing in three Persons. Islam denies the Trinity as blasphemy. Christianity affirms the deity of Christ — that Jesus is the eternal Son of God, the second Person of the Trinity, God incarnate. Islam denies the deity of Christ. Christianity affirms the crucifixion of Christ as the central event of redemptive history — the atoning sacrifice for the sins of the world. Islam denies that the crucifixion even occurred. Christianity teaches that salvation is by grace alone, through faith alone, in Christ alone. Islam teaches that salvation is earned through submission and the accumulation of good deeds. These are not minor theological nuances. They are contradictions at the most fundamental level.

Consider the implications of these differences for the individual believer. In Islam, a person can never have full assurance of salvation. Even Muhammad himself, according to the Hadith, expressed uncertainty about his own eternal fate. The Quran teaches that Allah forgives whom He wills and punishes whom He wills (Surah 2:284), and the faithful Muslim must simply hope that his good deeds will outweigh his bad deeds on the Day of Judgment. By contrast, Christianity offers the believer absolute assurance of salvation — not based on personal merit but based on the finished work of Christ. The Apostle John wrote: "These things have I written unto you that believe on the name of the Son of God; that ye may know that ye have eternal life" (1 John 5:13). Not hope, not wish, not guess — but know. The Puritan pastor Thomas Brooks (1608-1680) wrote: "Assurance is the beauty and the top of a Christian's glory in this life. It is usually attended with the strongest joy, with the sweetest comfort, and with the greatest peace." This assurance — this settled, unshakable confidence in one's standing before God — is unique to Christianity and flows directly from the finished atoning work of Christ on the cross.

Furthermore, the Islamic and Christian conceptions of God's nature differ profoundly. In Islam, Allah is transcendent, sovereign, and merciful, but he is not described in terms of self-sacrificing love. The concept of God humbling Himself, taking on human nature, and suffering and dying for the sins of His creatures is, in Islamic theology, not merely unnecessary but inconceivable and offensive. Yet this is precisely the heart of the Christian gospel. "But God commendeth his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8). "Herein is love, not that we loved God, but that he loved us, and sent his Son to be the propitiation for our sins" (1 John 4:10). The cross reveals a God whose love is not abstract and distant but concrete and costly — a God who enters into the suffering of His creatures and bears their punishment in His own body. This concept of divine love is absolutely unique to Christianity. No other religion in the history of the world has dared to make such a claim about the nature of God.

The great Puritan preacher Samuel Rutherford (1600-1661), writing from his place of exile in Aberdeen, expressed this truth with characteristic warmth: "Oh, what a fair one, what an only one, what an excellent, lovely, ravishing one is Christ! Put the beauty of ten thousand thousand worlds of paradises, like the garden of Eden, together, and ten thousand thousand Christs would far surmount them all. Oh, what pity that so few will go to the door of His chamber and knock to get in!" Rutherford understood that the uniqueness of Christianity is not an abstract theological proposition. It is a Person — the incomparably beautiful Person of Jesus Christ, who offers Himself freely to all who come to Him.

The Puritan theologian John Owen (1616-1683), often called the "Prince of the Puritans" and the greatest theological mind of his generation, wrote extensively on the uniqueness of Christ. In his masterwork "The Death of Death in the Death of Christ," Owen argued with devastating logical precision that the atoning work of Christ is sufficient for all and efficient for the elect — a work that no merely human prophet, however great, could ever accomplish. Owen wrote: "The blood of Christ is the foundation of all our hopes. It is the way into the holiest. It is what procures every mercy for us, whether temporal or eternal. Without the shedding of blood, there is no remission." For Owen, the uniqueness of Christianity rests not on subjective preference but on the objective reality of what Christ accomplished on the cross — something no other religion even claims, much less provides.

Let us also briefly consider Judaism, from which Christianity historically emerged. Judaism and Christianity share the Old Testament Scriptures — what Jews call the Tanakh. They share belief in one personal, holy, righteous, and sovereign God. They share the history of God's covenant with Abraham, the giving of the Law at Sinai, the Davidic kingdom, and the prophetic tradition. And yet, at the most critical point, Judaism and Christianity diverge irreconcilably. Christianity teaches that Jesus of Nazareth is the promised Messiah, the fulfillment of all the Law and the Prophets. Traditional Judaism rejects this claim. The question of whether Jesus is the Messiah is not a peripheral theological debate — it is the hinge upon which all of redemptive history turns. If Jesus is the Messiah, then the Old Testament finds its fulfillment in Him, and the New Covenant He established supersedes the Old. If He is not, then the Christian faith is built upon a colossal error.

The question of Jesus's Messiahship has been debated between Jews and Christians for two thousand years, and the biblical evidence overwhelmingly supports the Christian position. The Old Testament contains over three hundred prophecies concerning the Messiah, and Jesus fulfilled them all — His birth in Bethlehem (Micah 5:2), His birth of a virgin (Isaiah 7:14), His descent from the line of David (2 Samuel 7:12-16), His ministry in Galilee (Isaiah 9:1-2), His triumphal entry into Jerusalem on a donkey (Zechariah 9:9), His betrayal for thirty pieces of silver (Zechariah 11:12-13), His suffering and death as described in Isaiah 53 and Psalm 22, and His resurrection from the dead (Psalm 16:10). The mathematical probability of any single individual fulfilling even a fraction of these prophecies by chance is astronomically small. The fulfillment of these prophecies in the person of Jesus of Nazareth is among the most powerful evidences that He is who He claimed to be — the promised Messiah, the Saviour of the world.

The early Christians, who were themselves Jews, understood this with absolute clarity. The Apostle Peter, standing before the Jewish Sanhedrin, declared: "Neither is there salvation in any other: for there is none other name under heaven given among men, whereby we must be saved" (Acts 4:12). Paul, trained as a Pharisee under the great Rabbi Gamaliel, wrote to Timothy: "For there is one God, and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus; Who gave himself a ransom for all, to be testified in due time" (1 Timothy 2:5-6). And Jesus Himself made the claim that settles the matter beyond dispute: "I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man cometh unto the Father, but by me" (John 14:6). Notice the definite articles: not "a way" but "the way," not "a truth" but "the truth," not "a life" but "the life." Jesus claims not merely to know the way but to be the Way. He claims not merely to teach truth but to be the Truth. He claims not merely to offer life but to be the Life.

Now, let us address the popular claim that all religions are "basically the same." This claim sounds generous and open-minded, but it is, upon examination, demonstrably false and, in fact, deeply disrespectful to all religions. Hinduism teaches that ultimate reality is impersonal. Christianity, Islam, and Judaism teach that ultimate reality is personal. These cannot both be true. Buddhism teaches that the self is an illusion to be extinguished. Christianity teaches that the self is a creation of God with eternal significance. These cannot both be true. Islam teaches that Jesus was not crucified. Christianity teaches that the crucifixion is the central event of human history. These cannot both be true. Christianity teaches salvation by grace through faith. Islam and many other religions teach salvation through human effort and religious observance. These cannot both be true.

The philosopher and apologist G.K. Chesterton (1874-1936) once quipped that the person who claims all religions are the same has not studied any of them. Chesterton was right. The claim that all religions are basically the same can only be maintained by someone who has not taken the time to understand what any of them actually teaches. Far from showing respect for other religions, this claim trivializes them by reducing their deep and carefully articulated theological systems to a vague, feel-good sentiment.

The Puritan scholar Richard Baxter (1615-1691) addressed the question of religious pluralism in his great work "The Reasons of the Christian Religion." Baxter argued that the uniqueness of Christianity rests not on the subjective preferences of its adherents but on the objective truth of its central claims — the existence of the one true God, the sinfulness of man, the incarnation of the Son of God, His atoning death and resurrection, and the offer of free grace to all who believe. Baxter wrote: "Christianity doth not bid us shut our eyes and believe, but open our eyes and see; and then believe what we see. It is built upon the most solid evidence — the testimony of God Himself, confirmed by miracles, fulfilled prophecies, and the transformation of human hearts."

The great Puritan preacher Thomas Manton (1620-1677) similarly argued in his commentary on the epistle of Jude that the Christian faith is not one option among many but the faith that was "once delivered unto the saints" (Jude 1:3) — a singular, definitive, and unrepeatable revelation from God that admits of no rivals and no supplements. Manton wrote: "The faith was delivered once, not to be supplemented by new revelations or adjusted to the fashions of each succeeding age. What God hath spoken in His Son is final, complete, and sufficient for all people in all times."

But why would God provide only one way of salvation? The answer lies in the nature of the problem that salvation addresses. As we have studied in previous lessons, the human predicament is not primarily ignorance (which could be solved by better teaching), nor is it primarily suffering (which could be addressed by better circumstances), nor is it primarily moral imperfection (which could be improved by greater effort). The human predicament is guilt — real, objective, legally binding guilt before the bar of a perfectly holy and just God. "For all have sinned, and come short of the glory of God" (Romans 3:23). "The wages of sin is death" (Romans 6:23). "Cursed is every one that continueth not in all things which are written in the book of the law to do them" (Galatians 3:10).

No amount of religious observance, moral effort, mystical experience, or philosophical insight can remove this guilt. Only the shed blood of a perfect substitute can satisfy the demands of divine justice. And there is only one such substitute in the history of the world — Jesus Christ, the sinless Son of God, who "his own self bare our sins in his own body on the tree, that we, being dead to sins, should live unto righteousness: by whose stripes ye were healed" (1 Peter 2:24). The exclusivity of Christ is not arbitrary. It flows necessarily from the nature of the human predicament and the nature of the divine remedy. There is only one disease, and there is only one cure.

The Puritan pastor John Flavel (1627-1691), in his beloved work "The Fountain of Life," wrote: "The person of Christ is the treasury in which all the riches of divine grace are stored. There is no mercy to be had from God but through Christ. No pardon but through His blood. No acceptance but through His righteousness. No access to God but by His mediation. Christ is all, and in all." Flavel's words echo the testimony of the entire Bible: Christ is not one way among many. He is the way — the only way, the sufficient way, the glorious way.

We should also consider the popular metaphor of the mountain — the idea that all religions are different paths up the same mountain, all leading to the same summit. This analogy sounds appealing, but it contains a fatal flaw. It assumes, without evidence, that all the paths lead to the same destination. But as we have seen, the religions do not agree about what is at the top of the mountain. Hinduism says the summit is absorption into impersonal Brahman. Buddhism says the summit is nirvana — the extinction of individual existence. Islam says the summit is paradise, earned through submission and works. Christianity says the summit is personal communion with the Triune God, received by grace through faith in Christ. These are not the same destination. They are radically different destinations, and the paths leading to them are radically different paths. The mountain metaphor fails precisely because it ignores the actual content of what the religions teach.

Another common analogy is that of the blind men and the elephant — the ancient Indian parable in which several blind men each touch a different part of an elephant and each describes the elephant differently. One feels the trunk and says the elephant is like a snake. One feels the leg and says it is like a tree. One feels the side and says it is like a wall. The moral of the story is supposed to be that each religion grasps a part of the truth but none sees the whole picture. The problem with this analogy, as the philosopher Alvin Plantinga and others have pointed out, is that it assumes a vantage point above all religions — a God's-eye view that sees the whole elephant. But if no one can see the whole truth, then the person telling the parable cannot see the whole truth either, and has no basis for claiming that all religions are partially right. The parable is self-defeating. It claims to know that no one can know the full truth, which is itself a claim to know the full truth. Christianity, by contrast, does not claim that human beings have figured out the truth by their own unaided reason. It claims that God has revealed the truth — decisively, finally, and completely — in the person and work of Jesus Christ. "God, who at sundry times and in divers manners spake in time past unto the fathers by the prophets, Hath in these last days spoken unto us by his Son" (Hebrews 1:1-2).

The Puritan divine William Gurnall (1617-1679), in his massive devotional work "The Christian in Complete Armour," addressed the temptation to water down the exclusive claims of the gospel in order to gain acceptance from the world. Gurnall warned: "The Christian who trims his sails to please the world will soon find himself shipwrecked upon the rocks. Hold fast to the whole truth of God, for a half-truth is more dangerous than a lie. The world will respect a consistent Christian long before it respects an accommodating one." Gurnall understood that the pressure to relativize the claims of Christ is not new — it existed in his own day as it exists in ours — and the answer is not compromise but courageous fidelity to the truth.

Let us also consider the testimony of those who have come to Christ from other religious backgrounds. Throughout history, countless Hindus, Buddhists, Muslims, Jews, and adherents of other religions have encountered the risen Christ and been transformed by His grace. The testimony of these converts is a powerful confirmation of the uniqueness and sufficiency of the Christian gospel. Consider the remarkable story of Sundar Singh (1889-1929), a Sikh from Punjab, India, who hated Christianity with such passion that, as a teenager, he publicly burned a Bible page by page. Yet just three days later, in a moment of deep spiritual crisis, he had a vision of the risen Christ and was converted. He spent the rest of his life as an itinerant Christian preacher, traveling barefoot across India and into Tibet, enduring extreme hardship and persecution. Singh once said: "I have found in Christianity the fulfillment of everything I was searching for in Hinduism and Sikhism — the personal, loving God who did not merely point the way but became the way." The testimony of converts like Sundar Singh demonstrates that the exclusive claims of Christ are not a barrier to faith but the very thing that draws seeking hearts to their true and final rest.

Now, students, how should we communicate this truth to those of other faiths? This is a question of enormous practical importance, and the answer is rooted in Peter's command: "Be ready always to give an answer to every man that asketh you a reason of the hope that is in you with meekness and fear" (1 Peter 3:15). Notice that Peter says "with meekness and fear" — that is, with gentleness, humility, and reverence. The exclusivity of Christ is not a club to beat people with. It is a gift to offer them. We must approach people of other faiths with genuine respect, sincere curiosity about their beliefs, and a deep recognition that apart from the grace of God, we ourselves would be in the same spiritual condition.

We should learn about other religions — not superficially, but seriously. When we understand what a Hindu or a Muslim or a Buddhist actually believes, we can engage in meaningful conversation rather than attacking a caricature. We should listen to their spiritual longings — their desire for peace, for meaning, for deliverance from suffering, for connection with the divine — and show them how those longings find their true and final fulfillment in Christ. We should be honest about the failures of professing Christians throughout history, while pointing always to the perfection of Christ Himself. And we should pray — fervently, persistently, expectantly — for the Holy Spirit to open blind eyes and soften hard hearts, knowing that it is God alone who can bring a person from darkness into His marvelous light.

The Puritan missionary John Eliot (1604-1690), who spent decades bringing the gospel to the Algonquin people of New England and translated the entire Bible into the Algonquin language, modeled this approach beautifully. Eliot did not approach the Native Americans with contempt for their existing beliefs. He learned their language, lived among them, understood their culture, and then presented Christ to them with patience, love, and persistence. His work resulted in the establishment of fourteen "praying towns" — communities of converted Native Americans who embraced the Christian faith. Eliot's example reminds us that the exclusivity of Christ does not produce cultural arrogance. It produces missionary compassion. Because Jesus is the only way, and because the need is so great, and because the stakes are so high, we must go to all people everywhere with the message of salvation.

Let us also remember that the exclusive claims of Christ are actually extraordinarily inclusive in one crucial respect: the invitation is extended to all. "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life" (John 3:16). The word "whosoever" shatters every barrier of race, class, culture, and nationality. The gospel is not the private possession of any one people or culture. It is the universal offer of a universal God to a universal human race with a universal problem. The door is narrow — there is only one way — but it is wide open to everyone who will enter. "Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest" (Matthew 11:28).

It is also vital to understand that the Christian claim of exclusivity is not a claim of the superiority of Christians over other people. It is a claim about the uniqueness of Christ. Christians are not saved because they are smarter, more moral, or more spiritual than Hindus, Muslims, or Buddhists. Christians are saved because they have received, by grace and through faith, a gift they did nothing to earn and everything to need. The Apostle Paul makes this point emphatically: "For who maketh thee to differ from another? and what hast thou that thou didst not receive? now if thou didst receive it, why dost thou glory, as if thou hadst not received it?" (1 Corinthians 4:7). Every Christian was once lost, blind, dead in trespasses and sins, and utterly unable to save himself. The only difference between a Christian and a non-Christian is the grace of God — and grace, by definition, is undeserved. Therefore, the exclusivity of Christ humbles Christians rather than exalting them. It drives us to our knees in gratitude rather than lifting us up in pride.

The Puritan theologian Jeremiah Burroughs (1600-1646), in his beloved work "The Rare Jewel of Christian Contentment," captured this spirit beautifully. Burroughs wrote: "A Christian is the greatest debtor in the world. He owes everything to grace — his conversion, his perseverance, his comfort, his hope, and his glory. He has nothing to boast of and everything to be thankful for." When we share the exclusive claims of Christ with people of other faiths, we do so not as proud possessors of a private treasure but as unworthy recipients of an unmerited gift, eager for others to share in the same blessing.

Consider also the words of the missionary Hudson Taylor (1832-1905), who spent fifty-one years in China bringing the gospel to people who had never heard the name of Jesus: "Christ is either Lord of all, or He is not Lord at all." Taylor understood that the exclusive claims of Christianity are not optional addenda to the faith — they are its very essence. If Jesus is merely one teacher among many, one prophet among many, one path among many, then He is not the Jesus of the New Testament. The Jesus of the New Testament claims absolute authority over every human life, every human religion, and every human destiny. "All power is given unto me in heaven and in earth. Go ye therefore, and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost: Teaching them to observe all things whatsoever I have commanded you: and, lo, I am with you alway, even unto the end of the world" (Matthew 28:18-20). This Great Commission presupposes the exclusivity of Christ. If all religions led to God, there would be no need for missions. The very existence of the missionary enterprise — from the apostles to the present day — testifies to the church's conviction that Jesus alone saves.

As young apologists, you will face pressure from the culture to abandon the exclusive claims of Christ in the name of tolerance and inclusivity. Resist that pressure, but resist it with grace. Remember that true tolerance does not require agreement. It requires treating people who disagree with you with dignity and respect. You can hold firmly to the truth that Jesus is the only way to God while treating Muslims, Hindus, Buddhists, Jews, and atheists with genuine kindness and love. In fact, your firm conviction about the truth should make you more compassionate, not less — because you know the value of what you have received, and you long for others to receive it too.

The Puritan theologian Stephen Charnock (1628-1680), in his great work "The Existence and Attributes of God," wrote: "The exclusiveness of God's provision for salvation through Christ is not a restriction upon divine love. It is the very expression of divine love. For God, in His infinite wisdom, has provided the one remedy that perfectly addresses the one disease — and He has made that remedy freely available to all who will receive it. What greater love could there be than this?"

--- Activity ---

Create a detailed comparison chart of five major world religions: Christianity, Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism, and Judaism. For each religion, research and summarize its teaching on five key topics: (1) the nature of God or ultimate reality, (2) the nature of humanity, (3) the fundamental human problem, (4) the solution or path to salvation, and (5) the afterlife or ultimate destiny. Identify specific points where these teachings directly contradict one another, and highlight them on your chart. Then write a two-paragraph reflection explaining, first, why the claim that "all religions are basically the same" is inaccurate, and second, how the Christian message uniquely addresses the deepest needs of the human heart. Use at least two Scripture references and one Puritan quotation in your reflection. Present your findings to your family or study group and discuss the differences.

As a bonus challenge, research the testimony of one person who converted from another religion to Christianity (such as Sundar Singh, Nabeel Qureshi, or Rosaria Butterfield). Write a short biographical paragraph about their journey, including what they believed before, what convinced them of the truth of Christianity, and how their life was transformed by Christ.

Materials needed: Bible (KJV), access to a library or reputable online sources for information on world religions, poster board or large paper, colored pencils or markers, and writing materials.

--- Closing ---

The claim that Jesus is the only way to God is not arrogance — it is the gospel itself, the very heart of the good news. And the gospel is the best news the world has ever heard, for it tells a lost and dying world that God Himself has provided the one perfect remedy for the one universal disease. In a world of confused and competing religious claims — where Hinduism says God is impersonal, Buddhism says there is no God, Islam says God does not become incarnate, and secularism says there is no God at all — Christianity announces the staggering truth that the eternal God of heaven and earth has drawn near to His creatures in love. He has taken on human flesh, lived a perfect life, died a substitutionary death, and risen in triumph over the grave. He now sits at the right hand of the Father, making intercession for all who come to God through Him (Hebrews 7:25). And He extends His invitation to every tribe, tongue, nation, and people under heaven: "Him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out" (John 6:37). Share this message with confidence and with love, knowing that the invitation extends to all people everywhere. Christ is the Way, the Truth, and the Life. There is no other. Until next time, students.
