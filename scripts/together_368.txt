PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 7
"The Little Foxes"
Song of Solomon 2:14-17 — Guarding the Vineyard of Love

---

Welcome, dear couple. We close our first week in the Song of Solomon with one of the most practical and memorable images in the entire book — the little foxes that spoil the vines. This is a passage about protecting your marriage from the small things that do the most damage.

Song of Solomon 2:14-17:

"O my dove, that art in the clefts of the rock, in the secret places of the stairs, let me see thy countenance, let me hear thy voice; for sweet is thy voice, and thy countenance is comely. Take us the foxes, the little foxes, that spoil the vines: for our vines have tender grapes. My beloved is mine, and I am his: he feedeth among the lilies. Until the day break, and the shadows flee away, turn, my beloved, and be thou like a roe or a young hart upon the mountains of Bether."

--- Teaching ---

"O my dove, that art in the clefts of the rock." The bridegroom addresses his bride as a dove — gentle, pure, faithful. But she is hiding in the clefts of the rock, in the secret places. Something has caused her to withdraw. Perhaps it is the insecurity we saw earlier. Perhaps it is fear, or hurt, or simply the instinct to protect herself by hiding. His response is not to force her out but to gently invite: "Let me see thy countenance, let me hear thy voice; for sweet is thy voice, and thy countenance is comely."

Richard Sibbes wrote beautifully about this dynamic in marriage. He said that when one spouse withdraws, the other must pursue with gentleness, not force. "The soul is a shy thing," Sibbes wrote, "and must be wooed, not commanded." The same is true in marriage. When your spouse pulls away — emotionally, physically, spiritually — the instinct to demand their return will only drive them further into the cleft. Instead, approach with the tenderness of the bridegroom: "Let me see your face. Let me hear your voice. I find you beautiful."

Then comes the famous warning: "Take us the foxes, the little foxes, that spoil the vines: for our vines have tender grapes." The vineyard is their love. The tender grapes are the new growth in their relationship — the fragile, emerging fruit of intimacy. And the foxes? They are the small, seemingly insignificant things that destroy marriages from within.

The Puritans were remarkably perceptive about the little foxes. William Gouge identified several in his Domestical Duties: the fox of sarcasm, which slowly erodes respect; the fox of neglect, which starves a marriage of attention; the fox of busyness, which crowds out quality time; the fox of bitterness, which holds onto offenses until they become walls; the fox of comparison, which measures your spouse against others and always finds them wanting.

Notice that these are little foxes, not lions or bears. They are small enough to seem harmless. A cutting remark here. A forgotten kindness there. An eye roll during an argument. A failure to say "I love you" before sleep. A habit of checking your phone while your spouse is talking. Individually, each one seems trivial. But collectively, over weeks and months and years, they devastate the vineyard.

Thomas Gataker urged married couples to be vigilant vine-keepers. He wrote that the couple who allows small offenses to accumulate without addressing them will wake one day to find their vineyard stripped bare. The remedy is not occasional grand gestures but daily, diligent care — catching the foxes while they are still little, before they grow into habits that become character, and character that becomes destiny.

How do you catch the little foxes? The Puritans offered three counsels. First, cultivate awareness. Pay attention to the small dynamics in your marriage. Notice when a pattern is forming — when sarcasm is becoming a default, when physical distance is becoming normal, when conversations are becoming transactions. Second, speak early and gently. Do not wait until a small irritation becomes a deep wound. Bring it to the light while it is still small. "I noticed that we have not really talked this week. Can we change that tonight?" Third, forgive quickly. The little foxes thrive in the soil of unforgiveness. When you hold onto minor offenses, you create a breeding ground for resentment. Let them go. As Baxter wrote, "A happy marriage is the union of two good forgivers."

"My beloved is mine, and I am his." After the warning about foxes comes this declaration of mutual belonging. This is the antidote to every threat. Whatever comes — little foxes or roaring lions — the foundation remains: we belong to each other. This is covenant language. It is not "my beloved is mine as long as he pleases me" or "I am his until someone better comes along." It is absolute, unconditional, permanent. My beloved is mine, and I am his. Period.

The Puritans grounded this belonging in the covenant of marriage. Gataker wrote that the marriage covenant is the strongest human bond that exists — stronger than the bond between parent and child, stronger than friendship, stronger than any other earthly tie. When you said "I do," you entered into a covenant that only death can dissolve. And within that covenant, you are utterly, irrevocably each other's.

Tonight, as the first week closes, take inventory. What little foxes have been nibbling at the tender grapes of your marriage? Name them together. Not to accuse, but to protect. You are vine-keepers, and the vineyard of your love is worth guarding.

--- Reflection Prompts ---

Turn to each other and consider these questions.

Question 1: What "little foxes" can you identify in your marriage right now — small habits, patterns, or attitudes that are slowly damaging the tender grapes of your relationship? Name them together without blame.

Question 2: When one of you withdraws emotionally, how does the other typically respond? Does the bridegroom's gentle approach — "Let me see your face, let me hear your voice" — characterize your pursuit of each other?

Question 3: Say to each other tonight: "My beloved is mine, and I am his/hers." Let the weight of that covenant declaration settle over you. What does it mean to you right now, in this season?

--- Prayer Focus ---

Tonight's prayer focus: Pray together for the protection of your marriage vineyard. Ask God to reveal the little foxes — the small, seemingly harmless patterns that are stealing your fruit. Ask Him for the grace to catch them early and the wisdom to cultivate tender grapes of new intimacy.

--- Closing ---

Guard the vineyard, dear couple. The tender grapes of your love are worth protecting. And remember: My beloved is mine, and I am his. That covenant is your fortress. Rest in it tonight. Goodnight.
