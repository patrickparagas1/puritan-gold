PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Doctrine Deep-Dive, Day 7
"The Doctrine of Predestination"
God's Sovereign Choice — Elected by Grace


Good evening, friend. Tonight we enter one of the most debated and yet most comforting doctrines in all of Scripture: the doctrine of predestination. The Puritans did not shy away from this truth. They embraced it, preached it, and found in it a deep well of assurance and worship. Let us approach it tonight with open Bibles and humble hearts.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Ephesians 1:4-5:

"According as he hath chosen us in him before the foundation of the world, that we should be holy and without blame before him in love: Having predestinated us unto the adoption of children by Jesus Christ to himself, according to the good pleasure of his will."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


The doctrine of predestination teaches that before the foundation of the world, God chose a people for Himself — not on the basis of foreseen faith or merit, but according to the good pleasure of His own sovereign will. This is not a doctrine invented by John Calvin or the Puritans. It is a doctrine drawn directly from the pages of Holy Scripture, affirmed by Augustine, recovered by the Reformers, and treasured by the Puritans as one of the most precious truths of the faith.

The Westminster Confession of Faith states it with characteristic precision: "By the decree of God, for the manifestation of His glory, some men and angels are predestinated unto everlasting life, and others foreordained to everlasting death. These angels and men, thus predestinated and foreordained, are particularly and unchangeably designed; and their number is so certain and definite that it cannot be either increased or diminished."

Why did the Puritans treasure this doctrine? Because it placed their salvation entirely in the hands of God. Thomas Watson, in "A Body of Divinity," writes that election is the foundation of all the other blessings of the gospel. If God chose us, then our calling is certain. If our calling is certain, our justification is secure. If our justification is secure, our glorification is guaranteed. The golden chain of Romans 8:29-30 holds firm: "For whom he did foreknow, he also did predestinate to be conformed to the image of his Son... Moreover whom he did predestinate, them he also called: and whom he called, them he also justified: and whom he justified, them he also glorified."

Watson further argues that election is the purest expression of grace. If God chose us on the basis of foreseen faith, then our faith becomes a meritorious work, and salvation is no longer wholly of grace. But if God chose us apart from any foreseen merit — while we were yet sinners, while we were dead in trespasses and sins, while there was nothing in us to commend us to His favor — then salvation is grace from beginning to end. This is precisely what Paul teaches in Romans 9:11: "For the children being not yet born, neither having done any good or evil, that the purpose of God according to election might stand, not of works, but of him that calleth."

John Owen addresses predestination extensively in his theological works. Owen emphasizes what he calls the "discriminating grace" of God. Why did God choose Jacob rather than Esau? Not because Jacob was more righteous — he was a deceiver. Not because Jacob would believe more readily — faith itself is the gift of God (Ephesians 2:8-9). God chose Jacob because it pleased Him to do so. This is what Scripture means by "the good pleasure of his will." The ultimate ground of election is not in the creature but in the Creator.

This doctrine raises objections, and the Puritans were not afraid to address them. The first objection is that predestination makes God unjust. The Puritan answer follows Paul's own response in Romans 9:14-15: "What shall we say then? Is there unrighteousness with God? God forbid. For he saith to Moses, I will have mercy on whom I will have mercy, and I will have compassion on whom I will have compassion." Justice means giving people what they deserve. If God gave everyone what they deserved, all would be condemned. Election is not an act of injustice; it is an act of mercy. God is not obligated to save anyone. That He saves anyone at all is pure grace.

The second objection is that predestination destroys human responsibility. The Puritans responded that Scripture holds both truths simultaneously without contradiction. God is sovereign in salvation, and man is responsible to believe the gospel. The Puritans did not attempt to resolve this tension philosophically; they simply affirmed both truths because Scripture affirms both. The gospel is to be preached to all. All are commanded to repent and believe. And those who do believe discover that they were chosen before the foundation of the world.

The third objection is that this doctrine promotes either pride or despair. The Puritans argued precisely the opposite. Election destroys pride because it removes every ground for boasting. You did not choose God; He chose you. You did not earn salvation; He gave it freely. And election destroys despair because it assures the believer that his salvation rests on the immutable decree of God, not on his own fluctuating faith or faltering obedience. Watson writes that the elect man is safer than an angel who stands by his own power, for the elect man stands by the decree of God.

The practical fruit of this doctrine, as the Puritans understood it, is not speculation about who is and who is not elect. It is worship, humility, and assurance. Worship, because a God who saves by sovereign grace is worthy of the highest praise. Humility, because the saved sinner knows he has nothing that he did not receive. And assurance, because the one who chose us will never let us go. "My sheep hear my voice," Jesus said, "and I know them, and they follow me: And I give unto them eternal life; and they shall never perish, neither shall any man pluck them out of my hand" (John 10:27-28).

Let this doctrine settle your heart tonight. Your salvation did not begin with your decision. It began with God's decision, made in eternity past, sealed by the blood of Christ, and applied by the Spirit of God. Nothing can separate you from such love.


Here are some questions to carry with you into the coming days.

Question 1: Does the doctrine of predestination fill me with worship or with anxiety? Why?

Question 2: How does knowing that God chose me before the foundation of the world affect my assurance of salvation?

Question 3: Does this doctrine humble me, removing all ground for spiritual pride?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Sovereign Lord, we bow before the mystery of Thy electing grace. We did not choose Thee; Thou didst choose us. We did not find Thee; Thou didst find us. All glory, all praise, and all thanks belong to Thee alone. Keep us humble, keep us grateful, and keep us secure in the knowledge of Thy unchangeable decree. Through Christ our Lord, Amen.

Rest in His sovereign grace tonight. You are held by hands that will never let you go.
