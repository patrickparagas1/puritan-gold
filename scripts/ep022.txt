PURITAN GOLD — Episode 22
"The Fruit of Righteousness"
Week 4, Day 1 (Monday) — Proverbs 13

---

"Hope deferred makes the heart sick, but a desire fulfilled is a tree of life."

That's Proverbs 13, verse 12. And if you've ever waited for something... truly waited... with every fiber of your being aching for it to arrive — then you know exactly what Solomon means here. Hope deferred. It doesn't just disappoint you. It makes the heart sick. It wears you down from the inside.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves. Let me welcome you to this new week on Puritan Gold. We've arrived at Week Four of our journey — "Living Wisely" — and over the next seven days, we'll be walking through Proverbs chapters 13 through 16, alongside some of the richest theological reflections the Reformed tradition has ever produced. And today, we begin where all wisdom begins — with the fruit that righteousness bears in a life yielded to God.

Proverbs 13 is one of those chapters that, if you sit with it long enough, will rearrange the furniture of your soul. It's practical. It's piercing. And it's deeply pastoral. Solomon isn't writing abstract philosophy here. He's writing about your life — your words, your friendships, your discipline, your hopes.

So let's walk through it together.

---

The chapter opens with a statement that many modern ears will find uncomfortable. Proverbs 13:1 says: "A wise son hears his father's instruction, but a scoffer does not listen to rebuke."

Now... notice the contrast. This is the form Solomon uses throughout Proverbs — the antithetical parallelism, where one line presents the way of wisdom and the next presents the way of folly. A wise son hears instruction. A scoffer refuses rebuke.

And here's the question that should press upon us: Which one are we? Not which one do we think we are — but which one are we really? Because it's easy to nod along when wisdom is presented in a podcast or a sermon. It's another thing entirely to receive correction when it comes from a spouse, a friend, a pastor, or even a painful circumstance that God has ordained for your good.

Charles Spurgeon once said, "The Lord gets His best soldiers out of the highlands of affliction." And part of what he meant is this: God uses hard words, hard providences, and hard seasons to shape the character of His children. The wise son doesn't just tolerate instruction — he hears it. He leans into it. He receives it as a gift.

The scoffer, on the other hand, has made himself unteachable. And there is no more dangerous spiritual condition than that.

---

As we move through the chapter, we come to one of the most searching themes in all of Proverbs — the power of the tongue. Verse 2 tells us, "From the fruit of his mouth a man eats what is good, but the desire of the treacherous is for violence." And verse 3 adds: "Whoever guards his mouth preserves his life; he who opens wide his lips comes to ruin."

Friends, your words are not neutral. They are never neutral. Every sentence you speak is either building something or tearing something down. Every word carries weight — moral weight, relational weight, spiritual weight.

Thomas Watson, the great Puritan divine, wrote that "the tongue is a small member but it has great power. It can bless God or blast a man's reputation in a moment." And he's right. How many marriages have been wounded — not by great betrayals, but by careless words spoken in frustration? How many friendships have been lost — not because of malice, but because of a tongue that wasn't guarded?

Solomon is telling us: guard your mouth. Preserve your life. The wise person thinks before they speak, and often... they choose silence over speech.

---

Now, let's come to that verse I opened with — Proverbs 13:12. "Hope deferred makes the heart sick, but a desire fulfilled is a tree of life."

This is one of the most emotionally honest verses in all of Scripture. And I want to sit with it for a moment because I suspect some of you are living in this verse right now. You're waiting for something. Maybe it's healing. Maybe it's reconciliation. Maybe it's a prodigal child coming home. Maybe it's a door that God seems to have shut, and you don't understand why.

Hope deferred makes the heart sick. Solomon doesn't minimize this. He doesn't say, "Just pray harder and you'll feel better." He acknowledges the sickness. He names it. And there is a profound pastoral kindness in that.

But notice the second half of the verse — "a desire fulfilled is a tree of life." The fulfillment comes. Maybe not on our timeline. Maybe not in the form we expected. But for the child of God, there is always a tree of life at the end of the road. Always. Because our ultimate hope is not in circumstances — it's in Christ Himself.

A.W. Pink, in his writings on the sovereignty of God, reminds us that "God's delays are not God's denials." The Lord is working — even when we cannot see it, even when the heart is sick with waiting. He is working all things together for good, for those who love Him and are called according to His purpose. Your hope is not misplaced. It's anchored in the character of a God who cannot fail.

---

Let's move to verse 20 — and here Solomon gives us one of the most practical principles in all of Proverbs: "Whoever walks with the wise becomes wise, but the companion of fools will suffer harm."

Now... this isn't complicated, is it? And yet how often do we ignore it? The people you spend time with will shape who you become. Not might shape — will shape. You will, over time, begin to resemble the people you walk with. Their values will influence your values. Their habits will pull on your habits. Their speech will color your speech.

Spurgeon preached on this very principle and said, "Tell me who your friends are, and I will tell you what you are." And he wasn't being harsh — he was being honest. The Puritans understood that fellowship is not optional for the Christian life. It's essential. You need people around you who love God, who fear His Word, who will speak truth to you even when it's hard.

And the reverse is equally true. The companion of fools will suffer harm. Not may suffer harm — will suffer harm. This is Solomon's gentle but firm warning: choose your companions wisely. Your spiritual life depends on it more than you may realize.

---

And now we come to verse 24 — perhaps the most countercultural verse in the chapter. Proverbs 13:24: "Whoever spares the rod hates his son, but he who loves him is diligent to discipline him."

I know... this verse makes many people uncomfortable. But before we rush past it, let's understand what Solomon is actually saying. He's saying that love and discipline are not opposites — they are inseparable. The parent who never corrects, who never sets boundaries, who never says "no" — that parent, according to Solomon, is not being loving. That parent is being negligent.

The key word here is "diligent." The loving parent is diligent to discipline. This isn't about anger. This isn't about harshness. This is about a parent who cares enough to shape the character of their child — even when it's inconvenient, even when the child resists, even when it would be easier to look the other way.

And here's the beautiful theology underneath this verse: this is exactly how God treats us. Hebrews 12:6 tells us, "For the Lord disciplines the one he loves, and chastises every son whom he receives." Every act of divine discipline in your life is an act of divine love. Every hard providence, every closed door, every painful lesson — it's the hand of a Father who loves you too much to leave you as you are.

Richard Baxter, the Puritan pastor, wrote that "God's rod is as much a token of His love as His staff." The shepherd carries both — the staff to guide, and the rod to correct. And both are held in the same loving hand.

---

As we draw toward the end of Proverbs 13, I want to highlight one more verse — verse 22: "A good man leaves an inheritance to his children's children, but the sinner's wealth is laid up for the righteous."

This is a verse about legacy. And it asks us a searching question: What are you building that will outlast you? Not just financially — though stewardship matters — but spiritually. What are you passing down to the next generation? What patterns of faithfulness, what habits of prayer, what love for God's Word are you weaving into the fabric of your family's story?

The Puritans were extraordinary at thinking generationally. They didn't just think about their own souls — they thought about their children's children. They wrote catechisms for their households. They prayed together as families. They understood that the faith is not just received — it must be transmitted.

And that transmission requires intentionality. It requires discipline. It requires walking with the wise. It requires guarding your tongue. It requires holding fast to hope even when the heart is sick.

All of which is to say — Proverbs 13 isn't a collection of random sayings. It's a portrait of the righteous life. And the fruit of that life... is life itself. Life that overflows. Life that blesses others. Life that endures.

---

So let me leave you with this as we close today's episode. Proverbs 13:13 says, "Whoever despises the word brings destruction on himself, but he who reveres the commandment will be rewarded."

Revere the commandment, friends. Not as a burden — but as a gift. God's Word is not a chain that binds you. It's a lamp that guides you. It's the voice of your Father, calling you into the path of life.

And if your heart is sick today from hope deferred... if you're weary from the waiting... take this with you: the tree of life is coming. It's coming. Your God is faithful, and He has not forgotten you.

Let's pray.

Father, we thank You for the wisdom of Proverbs 13. We thank You that You are a God who instructs us, who disciplines us, who walks with us through every season of waiting. Teach us to guard our words, to choose our companions wisely, and to hold fast to the hope that is anchored in Christ. When our hearts are sick, remind us that You are still working. When we are tempted to despise correction, soften our hearts to receive it as love. Make us the kind of people who leave an inheritance of faith to the generations that follow. We ask this in the strong and saving name of Jesus Christ, our Lord. Amen.
