PURITAN GOLD -- The Heart of Christ by Thomas Goodwin: Complete Summary The Heart of Christ in Heaven Towards Sinners on Earth, by Thomas Goodwin. This beloved Puritan classic opens a window into the tender, compassionate heart of the risen and glorified Christ toward struggling believers. Goodwin's great aim is to assure every Christian that Christ's affections did not change when He ascended to glory — His heart remains as gentle, as pitiful, and as ready to receive sinners as it was during His earthly ministry. In the Introduction, Goodwin sets forth the problem: believers fear that Christ, now exalted in glory, may have become distant or cold toward them. The whole treatise exists to remove this stumbling block and encourage bold access to the throne of grace. Chapter 1 draws demonstrations from Christ's farewell discourse in John 13 through 17. Even as He faced the cross, Christ's chief concern was comforting His disciples. He promised the Holy Spirit, assured them of His continued love, prayed for them and for all future believers, and spoke of preparing a place for them — all revealing a heart overflowing with tenderness. Chapter 2 examines Christ's behavior after His resurrection. His first word to Mary was "Woman, why weepest thou?" His first message was "Go tell my brethren" — not servants, but brethren. He sought out the doubting Thomas, restored the denying Peter, and walked with the confused disciples on the road to Emmaus. Every post-resurrection encounter displays a heart of surpassing gentleness. Chapter 3 follows Christ to His ascension and session at God's right hand. Even in glory, He sent the Holy Spirit as promised, He intercedes for His people continually, and His exaltation has not diminished but rather increased His capacity for compassion. His glorified humanity retains all the tenderness of His earthly life. Chapter 4 begins Part II of the treatise, examining how Christ's heart responds to both the afflictions and the sins of His people. Goodwin carefully distinguishes between these two sources of suffering and shows from Hebrews 4:15 that Christ is "touched with the feeling of our infirmities" in both respects. His sympathy with our sorrows and His pity toward our failings are equally real and equally powerful. Chapter 5 presents a second sort of demonstration — the inward, theological reasons why Christ's heart must remain tender. His divine nature, His covenant engagement, His union with believers as Head to body, His role as Intercessor, and the very nature of glorified human sympathy all guarantee that His compassion can never diminish. Chapter 6 explores how Christ's heart is specifically touched with the feeling of our infirmities. Goodwin shows that Christ's knowledge of our condition is not merely intellectual but experiential — He was "in all points tempted like as we are, yet without sin." His own experience of weakness, sorrow, and temptation means He understands from the inside what His people endure.
