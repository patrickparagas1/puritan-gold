PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Pastoral Encouragement, Day 9
"Dealing with Discouragement"
Spiritual Depression — Finding Light in the Darkness

---

Welcome, friend. If you have come to this devotional tonight carrying a heavy heart, you are in the right place. The Puritans knew spiritual depression intimately. They wrote about it with honesty, compassion, and hard-won wisdom. Tonight, let their counsel be a balm to your soul.

--- Scripture ---

Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Psalm 42:5:

"Why art thou cast down, O my soul? and why art thou displeased within me? hope thou in God: for I shall yet praise him for the help of his countenance."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.

--- Pastoral Word ---

There is a kind of discouragement that is unique to the Christian. It is not the ordinary sadness of a difficult day or the grief of a loss, though it may be compounded by both. It is a spiritual heaviness — a sense that God is distant, that prayer is futile, that growth has stalled, and that the promises of Scripture belong to someone else. The Puritans called this condition "spiritual desertion" or "the dark night of the soul," and they took it very seriously.

William Bridge, in his wonderful work "A Lifting Up for the Downcast," addresses twelve different causes of spiritual depression and provides biblical remedies for each. Bridge himself had endured significant hardship: ejected from his ministry, exiled to Holland, separated from his congregation. He wrote not as a theorist but as a man acquainted with sorrow.

Bridge's first counsel to the discouraged believer is deceptively simple: preach to yourself. The psalmist in Psalm 42 does not listen to himself; he speaks to himself. "Why art thou cast down, O my soul?" This is a man who has taken himself in hand and begun to address his own heart with truth. Bridge argues that the discouraged Christian must stop listening to the voice of his feelings and start speaking the voice of faith. Your feelings tell you God has abandoned you. Faith tells you He has promised never to leave you nor forsake you. Your feelings tell you your sins are too great. Faith tells you that the blood of Jesus Christ cleanseth from all sin. Your feelings tell you there is no hope. Faith tells you that hope maketh not ashamed.

The second cause of discouragement that Bridge addresses is the consciousness of sin. Many believers are crushed by a sense of their own unworthiness. They look at their continued failures and conclude that they cannot truly be Christians. Bridge responds with the gospel: the ground of your acceptance before God is not your performance but Christ's performance. The most discouraged believer, if he is in Christ, is as justified as the apostle Paul. Justification does not fluctuate with your spiritual temperature. It is a settled decree of God based on the finished work of His Son.

Richard Sibbes, in "The Bruised Reed and Smoking Flax," provides another layer of comfort. Sibbes takes his title from Matthew 12:20, where Matthew applies Isaiah's prophecy to Christ: "A bruised reed shall he not break, and smoking flax shall he not quench." Sibbes argues that Christ is tender with weak and struggling believers. He does not despise small faith. He does not reject faltering love. He does not crush the bruised reed of a discouraged soul. Rather, He tends it, nurtures it, and brings it to full strength.

Sibbes writes that there is more mercy in Christ than there is sin in us. This is a truth that the discouraged believer must hear again and again until it penetrates the darkness. Your sin, however great, is finite. God's mercy is infinite. Your failures have an end. God's faithfulness does not. The devil would have you believe that your case is unique, that your sin is the one sin too great for grace. Sibbes responds that this is a lie from the pit of hell. Christ came to save sinners — not righteous people, not strong people, not people who have their lives together — sinners. And the weakest sinner who comes to Christ will not be turned away.

What practical steps can the discouraged believer take? The Puritans offer several.

First, do not abandon the means of grace. When you are discouraged, every fiber of your being will tell you to stop praying, stop reading Scripture, stop attending worship. This is precisely the wrong response. Continue in the means of grace even when they feel empty. The feelings will follow the obedience, not the other way around. Watson writes that the man who waits for the right feelings before he prays will never pray, but the man who prays despite his feelings will find, in time, that the feelings return.

Second, examine whether there is a specific cause for your depression. Sometimes discouragement has a spiritual cause: unconfessed sin, neglected duties, broken relationships. Sometimes it has a physical cause: exhaustion, illness, lack of sleep. The Puritans were surprisingly attentive to the connection between body and soul. Richard Baxter counsels the discouraged man to examine his physical health as well as his spiritual health. Sometimes what the soul needs most is what the body needs: rest.

Third, seek the counsel and prayers of godly friends. Do not suffer in isolation. The body of Christ exists for this very purpose: to bear one another's burdens. James 5:16 commands us to confess our faults to one another and pray for one another, that we may be healed. There is healing in honest fellowship that solitary devotion cannot provide.

Fourth, remember the faithfulness of God in the past. The psalmist says, "I will remember the works of the LORD: surely I will remember thy wonders of old" (Psalm 77:11). When the present is dark, look back. Has God delivered you before? Has He provided in times of need? Has He kept His promises? Then He will do so again. His character does not change with your circumstances.

Take heart tonight. The darkness you feel is not the final word. The God who began a good work in you will bring it to completion. The bruised reed will not be broken. The smoking flax will not be quenched. Hope thou in God, for thou shalt yet praise Him.

--- Reflection ---

Before we close, sit quietly with these questions.

Question 1: Am I currently experiencing spiritual discouragement? What is its primary cause — sin, circumstances, physical exhaustion, or spiritual attack?

Question 2: Am I preaching truth to myself, or am I passively listening to my feelings?

Question 3: Have I sought help from a godly friend or counselor, or am I carrying this burden alone?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.

--- Closing Prayer ---

Lord Jesus, Thou art gentle with the bruised and tender with the broken. We bring our discouraged hearts to Thee tonight. Lift up the light of Thy countenance upon us. Remind us that Thy mercies are new every morning. Restore the joy of our salvation. We trust Thee even when we cannot feel Thee. Amen.

Sleep in peace tonight. Weeping may endure for a night, but joy cometh in the morning.
