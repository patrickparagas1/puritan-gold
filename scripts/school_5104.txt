PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 4
"Of God's Eternal Decree"
The Westminster Standards — Lesson 4

---

Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we explore one of the most awe-inspiring and sometimes controversial doctrines in the Westminster Standards — God's eternal decree. This doctrine teaches that God, from all eternity, has determined everything that comes to pass — the rise and fall of nations, the salvation of sinners, the course of every human life, and the destiny of every soul. This is a doctrine that has comforted the afflicted, sustained the persecuted, and anchored the faith of believers throughout the centuries. It is also a doctrine that has been misunderstood, distorted, and attacked. Our task today is to understand what the Westminster Standards actually teach about God's decree, why this doctrine is taught in Scripture, and how it should affect the way we live. Let us pray and begin.

Sovereign Lord, Thy decrees are from everlasting, and Thy providence governs every atom and every moment. Give us wisdom and humility as we study these deep truths. Help us to trust what we cannot fully comprehend, and to worship Thee as the sovereign, wise, and holy God that Thou art. Keep us from error on every side — from denying Thy sovereignty on the one hand, or from impugning Thy goodness on the other. In Christ's name, Amen.

--- Lesson ---

Part One: What Is God's Eternal Decree?

The Westminster Confession addresses God's eternal decree in Chapter 3, which bears the title "Of God's Eternal Decree." This chapter is one of the most carefully worded passages in the entire Confession, and every word and phrase was weighed by the assembly with painstaking care — for the divines knew they were articulating truths of enormous consequence and sensitivity.

Chapter 3, Section 1 of the Confession states: "God from all eternity did by the most wise and holy counsel of his own will, freely and unchangeably ordain whatsoever comes to pass; yet so as thereby neither is God the author of sin; nor is violence offered to the will of the creatures, nor is the liberty or contingency of second causes taken away, but rather established."

This is a masterpiece of theological precision. Every clause carries weight, and the careful student will find that the divines have anticipated and addressed the major objections to the doctrine even within this single paragraph. Let us examine each clause in detail.

"God from all eternity..." The decree is eternal — it was established before the creation of the world, before time itself began. This means that God's plan is not reactive. He did not create the world and then scramble to respond to unexpected developments. His plan was complete and settled before He spoke a single word of creation. As the prophet Isaiah recorded: "Declaring the end from the beginning, and from ancient times the things that are not yet done, saying, My counsel shall stand, and I will do all my pleasure" (Isaiah 46:10, KJV).

"...did by the most wise and holy counsel of his own will..." God's decree is not arbitrary. It is not capricious. It does not proceed from indifference or randomness. It flows from the infinite wisdom and perfect holiness of God's own will. Every element of God's plan reflects His perfect wisdom — He always chooses the best end and the best means to achieve it. And every element reflects His perfect holiness — there is nothing in His plan that contradicts His righteous character. The Puritan Obadiah Sedgwick, a member of the Westminster Assembly, wrote: "The decrees of God are the most wise, the most just, and the most holy; for they flow from the most wise, just, and holy nature of God."

"...freely and unchangeably ordain whatsoever comes to pass." Two words here deserve special attention: "freely" and "unchangeably." God's decree is free — He was under no external compulsion. No one advised Him, no one pressured Him, no one contributed to His plan. As the apostle Paul exclaimed: "For who hath known the mind of the Lord? or who hath been his counsellor?" (Romans 11:34, KJV). And the decree is unchangeable — once established, it stands forever. God does not revise His plan. He does not have second thoughts. He does not encounter unforeseen circumstances that force Him to adjust. His plan is as eternal and immutable as He is.

The scope of the decree is comprehensive: "whatsoever comes to pass." Not merely some things, not merely the major events of history, but all things — "whatsoever comes to pass." The fall of a sparrow, the casting of a lot, the words we speak, the thoughts we think, the nations that rise and the empires that fall — all are embraced within the scope of God's eternal decree. As the Shorter Catechism, Question 7, puts it: "The decrees of God are his eternal purpose, according to the counsel of his will, whereby, for his own glory, he hath foreordained whatsoever comes to pass."

Part Two: The Critical Qualifications

But the Confession does not stop with the affirmation of God's comprehensive sovereignty. It immediately introduces three critical qualifications that protect the doctrine from distortion and misunderstanding.

First: "yet so as thereby neither is God the author of sin." This is an unequivocal denial that God causes or creates evil. God's decree encompasses everything that happens, including sinful actions — but God is not the efficient cause of sin. Sin arises from the free choices of moral agents — angels and humans — who act according to their own corrupt natures. God permits sin, overrules sin, and uses sin to accomplish His purposes, but He does not commit sin, approve of sin, or originate sin. "Let no man say when he is tempted, I am tempted of God: for God cannot be tempted with evil, neither tempteth he any man" (James 1:13, KJV).

How God can decree all things without being the author of sin is one of the deepest mysteries in theology. The Westminster divines did not claim to resolve this mystery fully. They affirmed both truths — God's comprehensive sovereignty and God's perfect holiness — and trusted that in the infinite mind of God, these truths are perfectly harmonious, even though our finite minds cannot fully see how. This is an act of faith, not of intellectual laziness. It is the recognition that God's thoughts are higher than our thoughts and His ways higher than our ways.

Second: "nor is violence offered to the will of the creatures." God's decree does not turn human beings into puppets or robots. People make real choices. Those choices are genuine expressions of their own wills. When a person chooses to believe the gospel, that is a real choice. When a person chooses to reject the gospel, that is also a real choice. God's sovereignty does not override human agency — it works through it and alongside it.

Third: "nor is the liberty or contingency of second causes taken away, but rather established." This is a remarkable affirmation. God's decree does not eliminate secondary causes — it establishes them. The natural world operates according to real causes and effects. Human decisions are real and have real consequences. Historical events unfold through genuine processes of action and reaction. God's decree is not an alternative to these secondary causes — it is the foundation that makes them possible and meaningful. God typically accomplishes His purposes not by overriding the natural order but by working through it.

Part Three: The Decree of Election

The most sensitive aspect of the doctrine of God's decree is the doctrine of election — God's eternal choice of certain individuals for salvation. The Confession addresses this in Section 3: "By the decree of God, for the manifestation of his glory, some men and angels are predestinated unto everlasting life, and others foreordained to everlasting death."

Section 5 elaborates on election: "Those of mankind that are predestinated unto life, God, before the foundation of the world was laid, according to his eternal and immutable purpose, and the secret counsel and good pleasure of his will, hath chosen in Christ unto everlasting glory, out of his mere free grace and love, without any foresight of faith or good works, or perseverance in either of them, or any other thing in the creature, as conditions, or causes moving him thereunto; and all to the praise of his glorious grace."

Let us note the key elements of this statement. Election is eternal — "before the foundation of the world." It is immutable — "according to his eternal and immutable purpose." It is Christological — God "hath chosen in Christ." It is gracious — "out of his mere free grace and love." And it is unconditional — "without any foresight of faith or good works, or perseverance in either of them, or any other thing in the creature."

This last point is crucial and was a major point of contention between the Reformed and Arminian traditions. The Arminian position, associated with the Dutch theologian Jacob Arminius (1560-1609) and his followers, taught that God's election was based on His foreknowledge of who would believe — that God looked down the corridors of time, saw who would choose to accept the gospel, and then elected those individuals on the basis of their foreseen faith. The Westminster divines, following Calvin, Augustine, and (they believed) the apostle Paul, rejected this view. They taught that election is unconditional — that God chose His people not because He foresaw that they would believe but because He freely and sovereignly determined that they would believe. Faith is not the cause of election; faith is the result of election. God does not choose us because we believe. We believe because God chose us.

The biblical basis for this doctrine is extensive. The apostle Paul teaches in Ephesians 1:4-5: "According as he hath chosen us in him before the foundation of the world, that we should be holy and without blame before him in love: having predestinated us unto the adoption of children by Jesus Christ to himself, according to the good pleasure of his will" (KJV). In Romans 9:11-13, Paul writes: "For the children being not yet born, neither having done any good or evil, that the purpose of God according to election might stand, not of works, but of him that calleth; it was said unto her, The elder shall serve the younger. As it is written, Jacob have I loved, but Esau have I hated" (KJV). And in Romans 8:29-30: "For whom he did foreknow, he also did predestinate to be conformed to the image of his Son, that he might be the firstborn among many brethren. Moreover whom he did predestinate, them he also called: and whom he called, them he also justified: and whom he justified, them he also glorified" (KJV).

Our Lord Himself taught the doctrine of election with unmistakable clarity: "Ye have not chosen me, but I have chosen you" (John 15:16, KJV). "All that the Father giveth me shall come to me; and him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out" (John 6:37, KJV). "No man can come to me, except the Father which hath sent me draw him" (John 6:44, KJV).

Part Four: The Doctrine of Reprobation

The Confession also addresses the doctrine of reprobation — the other side of election — though it does so with notable restraint and sobriety. Section 7 states: "The rest of mankind, God was pleased, according to the unsearchable counsel of his own will, whereby he extendeth or withholdeth mercy as he pleaseth, for the glory of his sovereign power over his creatures, to pass by, and to ordain them to dishonour and wrath for their sin, to the praise of his glorious justice."

This is the most difficult aspect of the doctrine, and the divines handled it with care. Note that reprobation is described as God's decision to "pass by" — that is, to leave sinners in their sin, to withhold the special grace that would bring them to faith. The reprobate are not damned arbitrarily or without cause. They are ordained to "dishonour and wrath for their sin." The cause of their condemnation is their own sin, not an arbitrary divine decision. God is just in His condemnation of the wicked because the wicked are genuinely guilty.

The asymmetry between election and reprobation is important. In election, God actively intervenes — He chooses, calls, regenerates, justifies, and glorifies His people. In reprobation, God withholds His special grace and allows sinners to continue in the path they have freely chosen. The elect are saved by grace. The reprobate are condemned by justice. No one receives less than they deserve. Many receive infinitely more than they deserve. And the ultimate result is the display of both God's mercy (in the salvation of the elect) and God's justice (in the condemnation of the wicked).

Part Five: The Decree and Providence

It is important to distinguish between God's eternal decree and His providence, though the two are intimately related. The decree is what God has determined from eternity. Providence is how God carries out that decree in time and history. The decree is the plan. Providence is the execution of the plan.

The Westminster Confession addresses providence in Chapter 5. Section 1 states: "God, the great Creator of all things, doth uphold, direct, dispose, and govern all creatures, actions, and things, from the greatest even to the least, by his most wise and holy providence, according to his infallible foreknowledge, and the free and immutable counsel of his own will, to the praise of the glory of his wisdom, power, justice, goodness, and mercy."

Four verbs define God's providential activity. He upholds — sustaining all things in existence moment by moment. "He is before all things, and by him all things consist" (Colossians 1:17, KJV). The universe does not run on its own power, like a clock wound up and left to tick. It is sustained every instant by the active power of God. If God withdrew His sustaining hand for a single moment, the entire universe would cease to exist.

He directs — guiding all things toward His appointed purposes. History is not a random sequence of meaningless events. It is a purposeful narrative, directed by an infinitely wise Author toward a glorious conclusion.

He disposes — arranging circumstances to accomplish His will. The casting of lots, the rise and fall of kings, the timing of births and deaths, the seemingly random events of daily life — all are disposed by God's sovereign hand. "The lot is cast into the lap; but the whole disposing thereof is of the LORD" (Proverbs 16:33, KJV).

He governs — ruling over all with absolute authority. "The LORD hath prepared his throne in the heavens; and his kingdom ruleth over all" (Psalm 103:19, KJV). There is no rogue molecule in the universe, no event that escapes His governance, no creature that operates outside His authority.

This governance extends "from the greatest even to the least." The movements of galaxies and the movements of molecules are equally subject to God's providential control. The rise and fall of empires and the fall of a sparrow are alike embraced within His sovereign governance. "Are not two sparrows sold for a farthing? and one of them shall not fall on the ground without your Father" (Matthew 10:29, KJV).

The Confession also addresses the difficult question of God's providence in relation to evil. Section 4 of Chapter 5 acknowledges that God sometimes permits sin to occur: "The almighty power, unsearchable wisdom, and infinite goodness of God, so far manifest themselves in his providence, that it extendeth itself even to the first fall, and all other sins of angels and men, and that not by a bare permission, but such as hath joined with it a most wise and powerful bounding, and otherwise ordering and governing of them, in a manifold dispensation, to his own holy ends; yet so, as the sinfulness thereof proceedeth only from the creature, and not from God; who being most holy and righteous, neither is nor can be the author or approver of sin."

This is a remarkably careful statement. God's providence extends even to sin — not by a "bare permission" (as if God merely stood aside and allowed sin to happen without any involvement), but by a deliberate, wise, and powerful ordering that sets limits on sin, directs it toward His purposes, and overrules it for good. Yet the sinfulness of sinful acts proceeds only from the creature, never from God. God is neither the author nor the approver of sin. He is the sovereign Lord who permits what He does not approve and overrules what He does not cause.

The greatest illustration of this is the crucifixion of Christ. Peter declared at Pentecost: "Him, being delivered by the determinate counsel and foreknowledge of God, ye have taken, and by wicked hands have crucified and slain" (Acts 2:23, KJV). The death of Christ was decreed by God from eternity — it was according to His "determinate counsel and foreknowledge." Yet those who crucified Him acted freely and sinfully — "by wicked hands." God ordained the event. Men committed the sin. God's purpose was accomplished. And the sinners were held responsible. This is the biblical pattern that the Confession faithfully articulates.

Part Six: The Decree and the Doctrine of Predestination in Puritan Thought

The Puritans devoted enormous theological energy to the doctrine of predestination, and their writings on this subject constitute some of the most profound theological reflection in the history of the church.

William Perkins (1558-1602), often called the father of English Puritanism, wrote one of the most influential treatises on predestination in the Reformed tradition. His "Golden Chain" — published in 1590 and named after the chain of salvation described in Romans 8:29-30 — traced the unbroken chain of God's saving purposes from eternal election through effectual calling, justification, sanctification, and glorification. Perkins's work was widely read and deeply influential, shaping the theological framework that would later be codified in the Westminster Confession.

John Owen (1616-1683), perhaps the greatest of all Puritan theologians, explored the doctrine of predestination in connection with his defense of particular redemption — the doctrine that Christ died specifically for the elect, not merely in general for all humanity. In his "Death of Death in the Death of Christ" (1647), Owen argued with devastating logical force that if Christ's death actually accomplished salvation (rather than merely making it possible), then it must have been designed for those who are actually saved — namely, the elect. This work, though published the same year the Westminster Confession was completed, reflects the same theological convictions.

Thomas Goodwin, one of the leading Independents at the Westminster Assembly, wrote extensively on the relationship between election and the believer's assurance. Goodwin taught that the knowledge of one's election — which comes through the fruits of regeneration, faith, and holiness — is one of the greatest sources of comfort and stability in the Christian life. The believer who knows that God chose them from eternity, redeemed them through Christ, and sealed them by the Spirit has a foundation for assurance that no trial or temptation can shake.

Part Seven: Historical Context — The Arminian Controversy

The doctrine of God's decree, and particularly the doctrine of election, was one of the most hotly debated theological issues of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. To understand why the Westminster Confession articulates this doctrine with such care, it is helpful to understand the historical controversy that lay behind it.

Jacob Arminius was a Dutch Reformed theologian who studied under Theodore Beza, Calvin's successor in Geneva. Despite his Reformed training, Arminius came to doubt the Calvinistic doctrine of unconditional election and proposed instead that God's election was conditioned on foreseen faith. After Arminius's death in 1609, his followers published the Remonstrance of 1610, which articulated five points of disagreement with the Reformed consensus: conditional election, universal atonement, partial depravity, resistible grace, and the possibility of falling from grace.

The Dutch Reformed Church responded by convening the Synod of Dort (or Dordrecht) in 1618-1619 — an international Reformed council that included representatives from the Netherlands, Great Britain, the Palatinate, Hesse, Switzerland, and other Reformed territories. The Synod produced the Canons of Dort, which systematically refuted the Remonstrant position and affirmed the doctrines that would later be summarized as the "Five Points of Calvinism": total depravity, unconditional election, limited (or definite) atonement, irresistible (or effectual) grace, and the perseverance of the saints.

The Westminster divines were thoroughly familiar with the Arminian controversy and the Canons of Dort. Many of them had participated in the English response to the controversy. Several members of the assembly, including Edmund Calamy and Lazarus Seaman, had been involved in the debates over Arminianism that roiled the English church in the 1620s and 1630s. The English delegates to the Synod of Dort — including Samuel Ward and John Davenant — had shaped the English Reformed response to the Remonstrants, and their influence was felt at Westminster.

It is also important to note that the controversy between Calvinism and Arminianism was not merely a theological debate conducted in ivory towers. It had profound pastoral and practical implications. The Arminian position, by making salvation dependent on human faith (foreseen by God but originating in the human will), introduced an element of uncertainty into the believer's assurance. If my salvation ultimately depends on my continued faith and perseverance, how can I be sure I will persevere? The Calvinistic doctrine of unconditional election, by contrast, grounds the believer's assurance in the eternal, unchangeable purpose of God. My salvation does not depend on the strength of my grip on God but on the strength of God's grip on me. This pastoral concern — the concern for robust, God-centered assurance — was a driving motivation behind the Westminster divines' careful articulation of the doctrine of God's decree.

Their treatment of God's decree in Chapter 3 of the Confession reflects a mature, careful, and thoroughly Reformed articulation of these truths — one that takes full account of the biblical evidence, the historical debate, and the pastoral concerns that the doctrine raises.

Part Eight: The Comfort of the Decree

The doctrine of God's decree was not, for the Puritans, a source of anxiety or despair. It was a source of profound comfort and assurance. Consider how this doctrine comforts the believer.

First, the decree assures us that God is in control. In a world of chaos, suffering, and injustice, the knowledge that all things are governed by an infinitely wise and holy God is a source of immense comfort. Nothing happens by accident. Nothing falls outside God's plan. Even the darkest moments of human history serve His sovereign purposes. As Joseph said to his brothers who had sold him into slavery: "But as for you, ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save much people alive" (Genesis 50:20, KJV).

Second, the decree assures us that our salvation is secure. If our salvation depended on our own faith, our own perseverance, our own strength — we would have every reason to fear. But if our salvation rests on the eternal, unchangeable decree of God — then it is as secure as God Himself. "For I am persuaded, that neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor principalities, nor powers, nor things present, nor things to come, nor height, nor depth, nor any other creature, shall be able to separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord" (Romans 8:38-39, KJV).

Third, the decree gives meaning to suffering. If the trials of this life were random and meaningless, they would be unbearable. But if they are part of God's wise and loving plan — ordained from eternity for our good and His glory — then they can be endured with patience and even joy. "And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose" (Romans 8:28, KJV).

The Puritan Samuel Rutherford, writing from his exile in Aberdeen, expressed this comfort with extraordinary beauty. In letter after letter, Rutherford affirmed that his sufferings were not random afflictions but the appointments of a loving and sovereign God. He wrote to one friend: "Our Lord hideth His purposes till His own time come, and then He letteth His people see that He hath ordered all things for the best." And to another: "I see that grace groweth best in winter." Rutherford's confidence was rooted in the doctrine of God's decree — the assurance that an infinitely wise and loving God was governing every detail of his life.

Fourth, the decree assures us that God's purposes will ultimately triumph. In a world where evil seems to prosper and righteousness seems to suffer, the doctrine of God's decree assures us that the final chapter has already been written — and it is a chapter of victory, justice, and glory. Evil will not have the last word. Sin will not triumph. Death will not prevail. The decree guarantees that God's kingdom will come, that His will shall be done on earth as it is in heaven, and that every enemy of righteousness will be vanquished. "The LORD of hosts hath sworn, saying, Surely as I have thought, so shall it come to pass; and as I have purposed, so shall it stand" (Isaiah 14:24, KJV).

Fifth, the decree teaches us that nothing is wasted. Every experience of your life — every joy and every sorrow, every success and every failure, every gain and every loss — has been ordained by God for a purpose. Nothing is meaningless. Nothing is accidental. Nothing is wasted. The great tapestry of God's plan weaves together every thread of every life into a pattern that will one day be revealed in its full and breathtaking beauty. As the hymn writer William Cowper, who struggled with severe depression throughout his life but found comfort in the sovereignty of God, wrote: "God moves in a mysterious way His wonders to perform; He plants His footsteps in the sea, and rides upon the storm. Deep in unfathomable mines of never-failing skill, He treasures up His bright designs, and works His sovereign will."

The Puritan Jeremiah Burroughs, in his beloved work "The Rare Jewel of Christian Contentment," drew extensively on the doctrine of God's decree to teach believers how to be content in all circumstances. Burroughs argued that the Christian who understands God's sovereignty can say with the apostle Paul: "I have learned, in whatsoever state I am, therewith to be content" (Philippians 4:11, KJV). Contentment is not the absence of suffering. It is the settled confidence that a sovereign, wise, and loving God is governing all things for our good and His glory.

Part Nine: Objections and Answers

Several common objections to the doctrine of God's decree deserve attention.

Objection 1: "If God has decreed all things, then prayer is useless." Answer: The decree includes not only the ends but also the means. God has decreed that certain blessings will come through prayer. Prayer is one of the appointed means by which God accomplishes His purposes. We pray not to change God's mind but to participate in His plan. Prayer is not made useless by the decree — it is made effective by the decree.

Objection 2: "If God has decreed all things, then evangelism is unnecessary." Answer: Again, the decree includes the means as well as the ends. God has decreed to save His elect through the preaching of the gospel. Evangelism is the appointed means by which God brings His chosen people to faith. The apostle Paul, who wrote the most extensive passages on election in the New Testament (Romans 8-9, Ephesians 1), was also the most passionate evangelist in the history of the church. The doctrine of election did not paralyze his evangelistic zeal — it fueled it. He knew that God had people in every city, and that the gospel was the power of God unto salvation.

Objection 3: "If God has decreed all things, then human choices do not matter." Answer: The Confession explicitly affirms that "the liberty or contingency of second causes" is not "taken away, but rather established" by the decree. Human choices are real. They matter. They have consequences. God works through human choices to accomplish His purposes. His sovereignty does not eliminate human responsibility — it undergirds it.

Objection 4: "The doctrine of reprobation makes God unjust." Answer: The Confession insists that the reprobate are ordained to wrath "for their sin." No one is condemned who does not deserve condemnation. Justice demands that sin be punished. The wonder is not that God condemns some — that is simply justice. The wonder is that He saves any — that is pure grace. As the apostle Paul wrote: "Nay but, O man, who art thou that repliest against God? Shall the thing formed say to him that formed it, Why hast thou made me thus?" (Romans 9:20, KJV).

Part Ten: The Larger Catechism on God's Decrees

The Westminster Larger Catechism provides additional depth on the doctrine of God's decrees that supplements and enriches what we have already studied in the Confession and the Shorter Catechism.

Question 12 of the Larger Catechism asks: "What are the decrees of God?" and answers: "God's decrees are the wise, free, and holy acts of the counsel of his will, whereby, from all eternity, he hath, for his own glory, unchangeably foreordained whatsoever comes to pass in time, especially concerning angels and men." Notice that the Larger Catechism specifies that the decrees concern "especially angels and men" — moral agents who bear particular significance in the drama of redemption.

Question 13 asks: "What hath God especially decreed concerning angels and men?" and answers: "God, by an eternal and immutable decree, out of his mere love, for the praise of his glorious grace, to be manifested in due time, hath elected some angels to glory; and in Christ hath chosen some men to eternal life, and the means thereof; and also, according to his sovereign power, and the unsearchable counsel of his own will (whereby he extendeth or withholdeth favour as he pleaseth), hath passed by, and foreordained the rest to dishonour and wrath, to be for their sin inflicted, to the praise of the glory of his justice."

This answer provides a comprehensive summary of the decree of election and reprobation, drawing together the various threads we have already examined. It emphasizes that election is rooted in God's love — "out of his mere love." It emphasizes that election is "in Christ" — for Christ is the Mediator of the covenant of grace and the means by which all the blessings of election flow to the elect. It affirms that God has chosen not only the end (eternal life) but also "the means thereof" — the preaching of the gospel, the work of the Spirit, the gift of faith and repentance. And it reaffirms that reprobation is just — the rest are foreordained to wrath "to be for their sin inflicted."

Question 14 asks: "How doth God execute his decrees?" and answers: "God executeth his decrees in the works of creation and providence." This simple answer connects the doctrine of the decree to the unfolding story of Scripture. The decrees are not abstract principles floating in eternity. They are executed — brought to reality — through the concrete events of creation and providence. God's plan takes shape in the real world, through real events, involving real people, guided by real providence toward a real consummation.

Part Eleven: The Decree and the Glory of God

The Shorter Catechism reminds us that the ultimate purpose of all God's decrees is "for his own glory." This is not divine egoism. It is the highest possible good. God's glory is the most beautiful, the most satisfying, the most worthy reality in all existence. When God acts for His own glory, He is acting for the highest good of the universe and the deepest satisfaction of His creatures.

The display of God's glory through His decrees unfolds in several dimensions. His wisdom is glorified in the intricate plan of redemption — a plan that satisfies justice while extending mercy, that honors law while forgiving lawbreakers, that defeats evil while respecting human agency. His power is glorified in the creation and governance of the universe, in the mighty acts of providence, and in the irresistible work of regeneration. His justice is glorified in the condemnation of sin, in the punishment of the wicked, and in the satisfaction made by Christ on the cross. His mercy is glorified in the salvation of sinners who deserved condemnation. His faithfulness is glorified in the fulfillment of every promise and the execution of every purpose. And His love is glorified supremely in the gift of His only Son — "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life" (John 3:16, KJV).

The Puritan Thomas Watson expressed this beautifully: "The decree of God is the cause of all things that exist. Nothing comes to pass but what God has decreed. The decrees of God are His eternal purposes, whereby He has determined what shall come to pass. God's decrees are not made upon the spur of the moment; they are from eternity. They are not rash resolves, but the counsels of infinite wisdom."

Part Twelve: Practical Guidance for the Student

As a homeschool student encountering this doctrine — perhaps for the first time — you may find yourself grappling with questions that have challenged the greatest minds in the history of the church. This is to be expected and even welcomed. The doctrine of God's decree is not a simple truth that can be mastered in a single lesson. It is a profound mystery that will engage your mind and deepen your faith throughout your entire life.

Here is some practical guidance for approaching this doctrine faithfully.

First, start with Scripture, not with logic. The doctrine of God's decree is taught in the Bible. It is not a philosophical invention imposed on the Bible from outside. Begin by reading the key passages — Romans 8:28-39, Romans 9:1-29, Ephesians 1:3-14, John 6:37-44, Acts 2:23, Acts 4:27-28, Isaiah 46:9-11 — and let the Bible's own teaching shape your understanding.

Second, hold both truths in tension. The Bible teaches both that God is sovereign and that humans are responsible. It does not resolve this tension by denying either truth. Neither should we. Hold both truths firmly, even when you cannot fully see how they fit together. The Westminster divines modeled this approach by affirming both God's comprehensive decree and the reality of human freedom in the same paragraph.

Third, let the doctrine lead to worship, not to speculation. The apostle Paul did not respond to the doctrine of election with cold philosophical analysis. He responded with doxology — "O the depth of the riches both of the wisdom and knowledge of God!" Let the greatness of God's sovereign plan drive you to your knees in worship, not merely to your desk for debate.

Fourth, be humble about what you do not understand. The Confession repeatedly uses words like "unsearchable," "secret," and "incomprehensible" when speaking of God's decrees. There are aspects of this doctrine that exceed our capacity to understand. This is not a defect in the doctrine but a reflection of the infinite greatness of the God who decrees. Be willing to say, "I do not fully understand this, but I trust the God who reveals it."

Part Thirteen: The Decree and the Christian Life

The doctrine of God's decree should produce several responses in the heart of the believer.

First, worship. A God who has planned all things from eternity, who governs all things in time, and who will bring all things to their appointed end — such a God is worthy of awe, reverence, and unceasing praise. The apostle Paul, after expounding the doctrines of election and predestination in Romans 9-11, burst into doxology: "O the depth of the riches both of the wisdom and knowledge of God! how unsearchable are his judgments, and his ways past finding out! For who hath known the mind of the Lord? or who hath been his counsellor? Or who hath first given to him, and it shall be recompensed unto him again? For of him, and through him, and to him, are all things: to whom be glory for ever. Amen" (Romans 11:33-36, KJV).

Second, humility. The doctrine of unconditional election shatters every vestige of human pride. We have nothing to boast in. We were chosen not because of any merit in ourselves but solely because of God's free grace. If we are saved, the glory belongs entirely to God. If others are lost, we have no right to look down on them, for apart from God's grace, we would be in the same condition.

Third, trust. If God has ordained all things and governs all things according to His wise and holy will, then we can trust Him — completely, unreservedly, in every circumstance of our lives. We may not understand His purposes in the moment. We may not see how present suffering will lead to future glory. But we can trust that the God who planned all things in eternity and who holds all things in His hands will bring His purposes to a glorious completion.

Fourth, diligence. The doctrine of God's decree does not encourage passivity. If God has decreed the means as well as the ends, then our diligence in study, prayer, worship, evangelism, and obedience matters deeply. We are called to "work out your own salvation with fear and trembling. For it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do of his good pleasure" (Philippians 2:12-13, KJV). God's sovereign working and our diligent effort are not in competition. They are in harmony.

--- Closing ---

The doctrine of God's eternal decree is among the deepest waters in all of theology. It reveals a God of breathtaking sovereignty, unfathomable wisdom, and unsearchable grace. Let this doctrine not merely inform your mind but comfort your heart, humble your pride, and strengthen your trust in the God who holds all things — including your life, your salvation, and your eternal destiny — in His sovereign, wise, and loving hands. As the Puritan Samuel Rutherford wrote from his place of exile: "The Lord's decrees are the best news that ever came to our door." May you find them so as well. Rest in the sovereign goodness of your God, and let His eternal decrees be the anchor of your soul in every storm of life. Until next time, students.
