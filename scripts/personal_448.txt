PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Pastoral Encouragement, Day 17
"Finding Comfort in Affliction"
Richard Sibbes — Pastoral Counsel for the Suffering

---

Good evening. If you are weary tonight, you are in good company. The saints of old knew weariness well. And yet they also knew the God who gives strength to the faint. Let us draw near to Him together.

--- Scripture ---

Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is 2 Corinthians 1:3-4:

"Blessed be God, even the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of mercies, and the God of all comfort; Who comforteth us in all our tribulation, that we may be able to comfort them which are in any trouble, by the comfort wherewith we ourselves are comforted of God."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.

--- Pastoral Word ---

Affliction is not an interruption of the Christian life; it is a defining feature of it. The apostle Paul writes that 'we must through much tribulation enter into the kingdom of God' (Acts 14:22). The question is not whether affliction will come but how we will respond when it does. Will we be crushed by it, hardened by it, embittered by it — or will we be comforted, refined, and deepened by it?

Richard Sibbes, known as 'the heavenly Doctor Sibbes' for the sweetness of his ministry, was the Puritan par excellence of comfort. His most beloved work, 'The Bruised Reed and Smoking Flax,' is a sustained meditation on Isaiah 42:3: 'A bruised reed shall he not break, and the smoking flax shall he not quench.' Sibbes takes this prophecy of Christ and unfolds it as a message of hope for every struggling, suffering, barely-holding-on believer.

Sibbes begins by identifying who the 'bruised reed' is. It is the believer who has been weakened by affliction, crushed by circumstances, beaten down by trials until he feels he has nothing left. The bruised reed is not the strong, confident Christian who strides through life with unshakable faith. It is the one who is barely standing — the one who wonders whether he has any faith at all, whether God has forgotten him, whether the promises of Scripture apply to someone as weak as he.

And Christ's promise to this bruised reed is not that He will rebuild it into a mighty oak. It is that He will not break it. He will handle it with infinite gentleness. He will not add to its burdens but will lighten them. He will not condemn its weakness but will pity it. Sibbes writes that Christ's care for the weak is one of the most beautiful aspects of His character. He does not measure the value of His people by the strength of their faith but by the genuineness of it. A spark is as truly fire as a bonfire, and the feeblest faith is as truly faith as the faith that moves mountains.

Sibbes then addresses the 'smoking flax' — the believer whose grace is so faint that it seems about to go out. The flax is not blazing; it is barely smoldering. There is more smoke than fire. But Christ will not quench it. He will fan it into flame. He will nurture the smallest evidence of grace, the faintest desire for holiness, the weakest motion of love toward Himself, and bring it to fullness.

Sibbes identifies several sources of comfort for the afflicted believer. First, affliction is a mark of sonship, not of abandonment. 'Whom the Lord loveth he chasteneth' (Hebrews 12:6). The man who never experiences affliction has reason to question whether he belongs to God at all. Second, affliction is purposeful. God uses it to purify faith, deepen dependence, expose idols, and produce the peaceable fruit of righteousness. Third, affliction is temporary. 'Our light affliction, which is but for a moment, worketh for us a far more exceeding and eternal weight of glory' (2 Corinthians 4:17). The suffering of this present time is not worthy to be compared with the glory that shall be revealed.

Sibbes also addresses the temptation to compare our suffering with that of others. The afflicted believer often looks at others who seem to prosper and wonders why he must suffer while they are spared. Sibbes counsels against this comparison. Each person's path is unique, ordered by a wise Providence who knows exactly what each soul needs. The medicine that heals one patient would harm another. Trust the Physician who has prescribed your particular trial.

Patrick, if you are in a season of affliction — whether physical pain, emotional turmoil, relational difficulty, or spiritual dryness — hear the words of Sibbes: Christ sees you. He has not forgotten you. He will not break the bruised reed of your spirit or quench the smoking flax of your faith. He is gentle with you in your weakness, and He is at work in your suffering, producing something of eternal value that you cannot yet see.

--- Reflection ---

Here are some questions to carry with you into the coming days.

Question 1: Am I currently experiencing a season of affliction, and am I allowing it to drive me toward Christ or away from Him?

Question 2: Do I believe that Christ is gentle with me in my weakness, or do I imagine Him as impatient with my struggles?

Question 3: How can I extend to others the same comfort that God has given me in my own times of suffering?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.

--- Closing Prayer ---

Father of mercies and God of all comfort, I bring my bruised and battered heart to You today. I am weary, and I feel as though my faith is more smoke than fire. Thank You for the promise that You will not break the bruised reed or quench the smoking flax. Fan the faint flame of my faith into a steady blaze. Sustain me in this trial, and let me emerge from it more refined, more dependent on You, and more useful to others. Through Christ, the gentle Savior, Amen.

Rest in His grace tonight. Tomorrow is a new day of growth.
