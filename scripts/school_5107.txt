PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 7
"Of God's Covenant with Man"
The Westminster Standards — Lesson 7

---

Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we study one of the most important and unifying doctrines in all of Reformed theology — the doctrine of God's covenant with man. The concept of covenant is the great organizing principle of Scripture. It is the thread that runs through every page of the Bible, connecting creation to consummation, Genesis to Revelation, promise to fulfillment. Without understanding the covenant, we cannot properly understand the Bible's message, the plan of salvation, or the relationship between the Old and New Testaments. The Westminster Standards devote careful and extensive attention to this doctrine, and today we will explore their teaching with the seriousness and depth that it deserves. Let us pray and begin.

Covenant God, Thou hast condescended to deal with Thy creatures by way of covenant — binding Thyself by promise to undeserving sinners, pledging Thy faithfulness to those who deserve only Thy wrath. Open our minds to understand the glory of Thy covenant dealings and our hearts to trust in the covenant promises that are sealed in the blood of Thy Son. In Christ's name, Amen.

--- Lesson ---

Part One: What Is a Covenant?

Before we examine the specific covenants that the Westminster Standards describe, we must first understand what a covenant is. In the most general sense, a covenant is a solemn agreement between two or more parties, involving mutual obligations and commitments. In human affairs, covenants can take many forms — marriage covenants, treaties between nations, legal contracts, and binding agreements of various kinds. But in the Bible, the word "covenant" takes on a far deeper and more significant meaning, because one of the parties is Almighty God.

A divine covenant is a relationship initiated by God, established on terms that God defines, and secured by promises that God guarantees. It is not a negotiation between equals — it is a gracious condescension in which the infinite, sovereign Creator stoops to enter into a binding relationship with His finite, dependent creatures. The Confession, Chapter 7, Section 1, expresses this with remarkable precision: "The distance between God and the creature is so great, that although reasonable creatures do owe obedience unto him as their Creator, yet they could never have any fruition of him, as their blessedness and reward, but by some voluntary condescension on God's part, which he hath been pleased to express by way of covenant."

This opening statement is of enormous importance. It establishes two foundational truths. First, the distance between God and the creature is so vast that no natural relationship can bridge it. God is infinite; we are finite. God is eternal; we are temporal. God is self-sufficient; we are utterly dependent. Left to ourselves, we could never attain to the enjoyment of God as our blessedness and reward. We could never earn a claim upon God's favor, never achieve a standing that would entitle us to His fellowship, never work our way into His presence by our own merit.

Second, the only way for the creature to enjoy fellowship with God is through God's "voluntary condescension" — His gracious initiative in reaching down to us by way of covenant. God was under no obligation to enter into covenant with His creatures. He did so freely, voluntarily, and graciously. The covenant is entirely a gift of divine condescension — an act of incomprehensible kindness by which the God who needs nothing chose to bind Himself in relationship to creatures who deserve nothing.

The Puritan theologian Herman Witsius, whose work "The Economy of the Covenants Between God and Man" was one of the most comprehensive treatments of covenant theology ever written, defined a divine covenant as "an agreement between God and man, about the way of obtaining consummate happiness; including a commination of eternal destruction, with which the contemner of the happiness, offered in that way, is to be punished." In simpler terms, a covenant between God and man establishes the way of salvation — the way by which sinful creatures can be reconciled to a holy God and brought to eternal blessedness.

The importance of covenant theology for the Puritans can scarcely be overstated. The covenant was, for them, the key that unlocked the whole of Scripture. It explained why God dealt with Adam as He did, why the fall had such devastating consequences, why the Old Testament ceremonies were instituted, why Christ came in the fullness of time, and why the gospel is freely offered to sinners. The Puritan William Ames, whose Marrow of Theology shaped generations of students at Harvard and in English universities, placed the covenant at the very center of his theological system. For Ames, all of God's dealings with humanity — whether in judgment or in mercy, whether under the old administration or the new — are covenant dealings. To understand the covenant is to understand God's way of salvation. To miss the covenant is to miss the forest for the trees.

The Larger Catechism, Question 20, asks: "What was the providence of God toward man in the estate in which he was created?" and answers: "The providence of God toward man in the estate in which he was created, was the placing him in the garden of Eden, appointing him to dress it, giving him liberty to eat of the fruit of the earth; putting the creatures under his dominion, and ordaining marriage for his help; affording him communion with himself; instituting the Sabbath; entering into a covenant of life with him, upon condition of personal, perfect, and perpetual obedience, of which the tree of life was a pledge; and forbidding to eat of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, upon the pain of death." This comprehensive answer situates the covenant of works within the larger context of God's providential care for Adam — showing that the covenant was not an isolated transaction but part of a rich, comprehensive relationship between Creator and creature.

Part Two: The Covenant of Works

The Westminster Standards identify two great covenants that structure the entire biblical narrative — the covenant of works and the covenant of grace. The Confession, Chapter 7, Section 2, describes the first: "The first covenant made with man was a covenant of works, wherein life was promised to Adam, and in him to his posterity, upon condition of perfect and personal obedience."

We have already encountered the covenant of works in our lesson on the fall. Let us now examine it more fully in the context of the broader covenant framework.

The covenant of works was made with Adam in the Garden of Eden. God promised Adam that if he rendered "perfect and personal obedience" — if he kept God's commandments fully and without fail — he would receive life. This was not merely the continuation of the life Adam already enjoyed. The Puritans understood it as a confirmed, glorified, eternal life — a life that would be his permanent, irrevocable possession, secured by his obedience and guaranteed by God's promise.

The specific test of Adam's obedience was the prohibition against eating from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. "But of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, thou shalt not eat of it: for in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die" (Genesis 2:17, KJV). The penalty for disobedience was death — not merely physical death but spiritual death, relational death, and ultimately eternal death.

The Shorter Catechism, Question 12, summarizes: "When God had created man, he entered into a covenant of life with him, upon condition of perfect obedience; forbidding him to eat of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, upon the pain of death."

Several features of the covenant of works deserve careful attention. First, it was a genuine covenant — a binding arrangement with clearly defined terms and consequences. God was not making a suggestion or expressing a preference. He was establishing a solemn covenant that carried the weight of His sovereign authority.

Second, Adam stood in this covenant as the federal head and representative of the entire human race. His obedience or disobedience would determine the destiny of all his descendants. This principle of representation — sometimes called federal headship — is one of the most important concepts in Reformed theology. It explains how Adam's sin could bring guilt upon all humanity, and it provides the framework for understanding how Christ's righteousness can be credited to all who believe in Him.

Third, the condition of the covenant was works — perfect, personal obedience. This is why it is called the covenant of works. Salvation under this covenant depended entirely on human performance. There was no provision for failure, no allowance for repentance, and no promise of a mediator. If Adam obeyed, he would live. If he disobeyed, he would die. The terms were absolute and unconditional.

The Puritan Anthony Burgess, who served as a member of the Westminster Assembly, preached an extensive series of sermons on the covenant theology that found expression in the Confession. Burgess emphasized that the covenant of works was not unjust or unreasonable. Adam had the full ability to obey. He was created in the image of God, with a nature perfectly suited to obedience. The command was simple and clear. The reward was glorious. The penalty was plainly stated. God gave Adam every advantage and every reason to obey. The failure was entirely Adam's — not God's.

Fourth, the covenant of works was a gracious arrangement even in its character as a works-based covenant. God was not obligated to promise Adam anything. By the mere fact of creation, Adam owed God total obedience — he was God's creature, and obedience was his duty regardless of any reward. But God condescended to promise life to Adam upon the condition of obedience — adding a reward where none was required. The Puritans called this the "condescension" or "pact" element of the covenant of works — God's gracious willingness to establish a covenant relationship with Adam and to promise him a reward that his obedience alone could never earn.

Part Three: The Covenant of Works and the Moral Law

The relationship between the covenant of works and the moral law is one of the most significant topics in Reformed theology. The moral law — summarized in the Ten Commandments — is the enduring expression of God's moral will for all humanity. The covenant of works required perfect obedience to this law as the condition of life. When Adam broke the covenant, he violated the moral law in its totality.

But the moral law did not cease to exist when the covenant of works was broken. It remains as God's permanent standard of righteousness — binding upon all people in all ages. The Confession, Chapter 19, teaches that the moral law continues to function in three capacities. First, it serves as a mirror — revealing human sinfulness and driving the sinner to Christ for mercy. "By the law is the knowledge of sin" (Romans 3:20, KJV). Second, it serves as a restraint — curbing the outward expression of sin in society through the fear of punishment. Third, it serves as a guide — directing believers in the path of grateful obedience. The moral law is no longer a covenant of works for the believer — we are not under law but under grace. But it remains the beloved rule of life by which the regenerate heart desires to walk.

The Puritan Samuel Bolton explored this topic with particular clarity in his treatise "The True Bounds of Christian Freedom." Bolton argued that while believers are freed from the law as a covenant — freed from the obligation to earn their salvation by perfect obedience — they are not freed from the law as a rule. The law that once condemned them now instructs them. The standard that once crushed them now guides them. Christ has fulfilled the covenant of works on their behalf, satisfying all its demands and bearing all its penalties. And the Holy Spirit now writes the law upon their hearts, enabling them to obey it — not perfectly, but sincerely — out of love for the God who has saved them.

This understanding of the moral law's continuing relevance is critical for the Christian life. Some have argued that because believers are under grace, the moral law has no further claim upon them — a position called antinomianism, which the Puritans vigorously rejected. The Puritans insisted that grace does not abolish the law but fulfills it. Grace does not set believers free to sin but free from sin. And the moral law remains the God-appointed standard by which believers measure their conduct, examine their hearts, and grow in holiness.

Part Four: The Failure of the Covenant of Works

As we studied in our previous lesson, Adam failed the test. He broke the covenant of works by eating the forbidden fruit, and in doing so he plunged himself and all his posterity into a state of sin and misery. The covenant of works now stands as a broken covenant — its promises forfeited, its penalties inflicted, and its terms impossible for fallen man to fulfill.

This is a truth of critical importance for understanding the gospel. Fallen man cannot be saved by the covenant of works. The covenant of works demands perfect obedience, and fallen man is incapable of perfect obedience. "Therefore by the deeds of the law there shall no flesh be justified in his sight: for by the law is the knowledge of sin" (Romans 3:20, KJV). The law — which is the enduring expression of the covenant of works' demands — can only condemn the sinner. It cannot save him. It reveals his guilt, exposes his helplessness, and drives him to seek salvation elsewhere.

The Puritan Samuel Bolton, in his treatise "The True Bounds of Christian Freedom," argued that the covenant of works, though broken and unable to save, continues to serve a vital purpose in God's economy. It reveals God's standard of righteousness. It exposes human sinfulness. It strips away all pretensions of self-righteousness. And it drives the sinner to Christ — the only One who has ever perfectly fulfilled the covenant of works on behalf of His people.

The apostle Paul explained this function of the law with great clarity: "Wherefore the law was our schoolmaster to bring us unto Christ, that we might be justified by faith" (Galatians 3:24, KJV). The law — the expression of the covenant of works — serves as a tutor, pointing us away from ourselves and toward Christ. It does not save us, but it shows us our need of a Savior.

Part Four: The Covenant of Grace

Having described the covenant of works and its failure, the Confession turns to the covenant of grace — the glorious remedy that God provided for fallen sinners. Chapter 7, Section 3, states: "Man, by his fall, having made himself incapable of life by that covenant, the Lord was pleased to make a second, commonly called the covenant of grace: wherein he freely offereth unto sinners life and salvation by Jesus Christ, requiring of them faith in him, that they may be saved, and promising to give unto all those that are ordained unto eternal life his Holy Spirit, to make them willing and able to believe."

This is one of the most important paragraphs in the entire Westminster Confession. Let us examine it carefully.

"Man, by his fall, having made himself incapable of life by that covenant..." The covenant of works is a dead end for fallen humanity. There is no way back to God through perfect obedience, for we are now incapable of rendering it. Every attempt at self-salvation — every effort to earn God's favor by our own moral achievement — is doomed to fail, because the standard is perfection and we are hopelessly corrupt.

"...the Lord was pleased to make a second, commonly called the covenant of grace..." God did not leave humanity in its ruin. He was "pleased" — it was His gracious will, His sovereign good pleasure — to establish a new covenant, on entirely different terms. This new covenant is called the covenant of grace because it is founded not on human works but on divine grace. Salvation is not earned but given. It is not the reward of human merit but the gift of divine mercy.

"...wherein he freely offereth unto sinners life and salvation by Jesus Christ..." The substance of the covenant of grace is life and salvation through Jesus Christ. Christ is the heart of the covenant, the mediator of the covenant, and the fulfillment of the covenant. Every blessing of the covenant flows through Him and to Him. Without Christ, there is no covenant of grace — for He is the One who secures all its blessings by His life, death, and resurrection.

"...requiring of them faith in him, that they may be saved..." The condition of the covenant of grace is faith — not faith as a meritorious work that earns salvation, but faith as the instrument by which we receive the salvation that Christ has earned. Faith is the empty hand that grasps the gift. Faith is the eye that looks to the Savior. Faith is the foot that steps onto the solid rock of Christ's finished work. The Puritan Thomas Watson described faith as "the hand of the soul that receives Christ and all His benefits."

"...and promising to give unto all those that are ordained unto eternal life his Holy Spirit, to make them willing and able to believe." This is the crowning glory of the covenant of grace. Not only does God offer salvation through Christ; He also provides the Holy Spirit to enable sinners to receive it. The covenant of grace does not merely present an opportunity — it creates the capacity to respond. God gives the Spirit, and the Spirit gives faith. The entire work of salvation — from election to glorification — is the work of God. "For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: Not of works, lest any man should boast" (Ephesians 2:8-9, KJV).

Part Six: The Unconditional and Conditional Aspects of the Covenant of Grace

A careful reading of the Westminster Confession's treatment of the covenant of grace reveals both unconditional and conditional elements — and it is important to understand how these relate to each other without contradiction.

On the one hand, the covenant of grace is unconditional in its source and foundation. God freely initiates the covenant. God provides the Mediator. God gives the Holy Spirit. God creates faith in the human heart. God preserves the believer to the end. From start to finish, the covenant of grace is the work of God's sovereign, unmerited favor. It is not conditioned upon any human merit, achievement, or worthiness. "Not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to his mercy he saved us" (Titus 3:5, KJV).

On the other hand, the covenant of grace has a conditional element in its application. God requires faith as the instrument by which sinners receive the blessings of the covenant. Those who believe are saved. Those who refuse to believe are not. Faith is the condition of the covenant — not as a meritorious work but as the God-given means of receiving God's gift. And even this condition is itself a gift of grace — for God promises to give His Holy Spirit to make His people "willing and able to believe."

The Puritan John Ball, in his "Treatise of the Covenant of Grace," carefully distinguished between the covenant's foundation and its administration. The foundation is entirely gracious — resting on God's sovereign election, Christ's atoning work, and the Spirit's regenerating power. The administration includes the call to repentance and faith — the responsive obedience of the human heart to the gospel invitation. But even this response is the fruit of grace, not the cause of it. As the Puritan Tobias Crisp insisted, faith does not make a person elect; rather, election produces faith. We believe because we are chosen, not the other way around.

This understanding preserves both the sovereignty of God and the responsibility of man. God is sovereign in salvation — He chooses, He calls, He saves. But man is responsible to believe — he must repent and trust in Christ. These two truths are not contradictory but complementary. They are held together in the beautiful, mysterious, and glorious framework of the covenant of grace.

Part Seven: The Covenant of Grace in the Old Testament

The Westminster Confession teaches that the covenant of grace has been operative throughout all of human history since the fall — not only in the New Testament era but also in the Old. Chapter 7, Section 5, states: "This covenant was differently administered in the time of the law, and in the time of the gospel."

Under the Old Testament administration, the covenant of grace was revealed through "promises, prophecies, sacrifices, circumcision, the paschal lamb, and other types and ordinances delivered to the people of the Jews, all fore-signifying Christ to come." The Old Testament saints were saved by the same covenant of grace that saves believers today — they looked forward to the coming Messiah, while we look back to the Messiah who has come. The substance of the covenant has always been the same — salvation by grace through faith in Christ. But the external administration has changed.

This is a vitally important principle for understanding the unity of the Bible. The Old and New Testaments are not two different religions with two different ways of salvation. They are two administrations of one covenant of grace. Abraham was saved by faith in the promised seed (Genesis 15:6). David was saved by faith in God's mercy (Psalm 32:1-2). Isaiah was saved by faith in the suffering servant (Isaiah 53). And we are saved by faith in the same Christ — the seed of Abraham, the son of David, the suffering servant of Isaiah — who has now been revealed in the fullness of time.

The Puritan John Owen devoted much of his massive commentary on the Epistle to the Hebrews to demonstrating the continuity between the Old and New Covenant administrations. Owen argued that the Old Testament ceremonies — the sacrifices, the priesthood, the tabernacle, the feasts — were not empty rituals but genuine means of grace through which Old Testament believers participated in the blessings of the covenant. They were "shadows of good things to come" (Hebrews 10:1, KJV) — pointing forward to the reality that would be revealed in Christ. When Christ came, the shadows gave way to the substance. The sacrifices ceased because the final sacrifice had been offered. The priesthood was superseded because the great High Priest had entered the heavenly sanctuary. The ceremonies were fulfilled because their purpose had been accomplished.

Part Six: The Covenant of Grace in the New Testament

Under the New Testament administration, the covenant of grace reaches its fullest and clearest expression. The Confession, Chapter 7, Section 6, states that in the New Testament, the covenant of grace is "held forth in the preaching of the Word, and the administration of the sacraments of baptism and the Lord's Supper: wherein grace and salvation are held forth in more fullness, evidence, and efficacy, to all nations."

Several advances characterize the New Testament administration. First, there is greater fullness — the truths that were partially revealed in the Old Testament are now fully disclosed. The identity of the Messiah, the nature of His atoning work, the outpouring of the Holy Spirit, the inclusion of the Gentiles — all of these truths, which were veiled in shadow and type under the Old Testament, are now openly proclaimed.

Second, there is greater evidence — the proofs of God's saving work are more abundant and clear. The resurrection of Christ, the outpouring of the Spirit at Pentecost, the testimony of the apostles, the spread of the gospel to the nations — all of these provide compelling evidence that the covenant of grace has entered its definitive phase.

Third, there is greater efficacy — the New Testament means of grace are more powerful in their operation. The preaching of the gospel, empowered by the outpoured Spirit, brings salvation to multitudes from every nation and every tongue. The sacraments of baptism and the Lord's Supper communicate the blessings of the covenant with a directness and clarity that surpasses the Old Testament types and ceremonies.

Fourth, the covenant of grace now extends "to all nations" — no longer confined to the Jewish people but embracing people from every tribe, language, and nation on earth. "Go ye therefore, and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost" (Matthew 28:19, KJV). This is the Great Commission — the marching orders of the New Covenant church, sent forth to proclaim the covenant of grace to the ends of the earth.

Part Nine: The New Covenant in Christ's Blood

The capstone of the covenant of grace is the new covenant that Christ established by His death and resurrection. At the Last Supper, our Lord took the cup and declared: "This cup is the new testament in my blood, which is shed for you" (Luke 22:20, KJV). The word "testament" here is the same Greek word translated "covenant" — diatheke. Christ's blood is the seal of the new covenant, the guarantee of all its promises, and the price of all its blessings.

The prophet Jeremiah had foretold this new covenant centuries earlier: "Behold, the days come, saith the LORD, that I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel, and with the house of Judah... I will put my law in their inward parts, and write it in their hearts; and will be their God, and they shall be my people" (Jeremiah 31:31, 33, KJV). The new covenant promises internal transformation — not merely an external law written on tablets of stone, but an internal law written on the heart by the Holy Spirit. It promises intimate knowledge of God — "they shall all know me, from the least of them unto the greatest of them" (Jeremiah 31:34, KJV). And it promises complete forgiveness — "for I will forgive their iniquity, and I will remember their sin no more" (Jeremiah 31:34, KJV).

The Epistle to the Hebrews develops this theme extensively, showing that Christ is the Mediator of a "better covenant, which was established upon better promises" (Hebrews 8:6, KJV). The new covenant is better not because it replaces the substance of the old but because it fulfills and perfects it. The shadows have given way to the reality. The types have given way to the antitype. The provisional has given way to the permanent. And the Mediator of this better covenant is not a fallible human priest but the eternal Son of God, whose sacrifice needs never be repeated and whose intercession never fails.

Part Ten: The Covenant of Redemption

Behind the covenant of works and the covenant of grace, the Puritans discerned a third covenant — the covenant of redemption, also known as the pactum salutis or the counsel of peace. While the Westminster Confession does not use the term "covenant of redemption" explicitly, the concept is present in its teaching that the Father chose a people and gave them to the Son, and that the Son undertook to accomplish their salvation by His obedience and sacrifice.

The covenant of redemption is an eternal agreement within the Trinity — between the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit — in which the Father appointed the Son to be the Mediator of the elect, the Son agreed to take on human nature and fulfill the covenant of works on their behalf, and the Spirit agreed to apply the benefits of the Son's work to the chosen people. This covenant was made in eternity, before the foundation of the world, and it is the ultimate source of the covenant of grace in history.

The Puritan David Dickson, a Scottish theologian who was deeply involved in the development of Westminster theology, described the covenant of redemption as the eternal blueprint of salvation — the plan drawn up in the counsels of the Godhead before time began, which is then executed in time through the covenant of grace. The covenant of grace is, in effect, the historical outworking of the eternal covenant of redemption.

Scripture provides glimpses of this eternal covenant. In John 17, our Lord prays to the Father about the people whom the Father has "given" Him: "I have manifested thy name unto the men which thou gavest me out of the world: thine they were, and thou gavest them me" (John 17:6, KJV). In Titus 1:2, Paul speaks of "eternal life, which God, that cannot lie, promised before the world began." And in Ephesians 1:4, Paul declares that God "hath chosen us in him before the foundation of the world." These passages reveal that the plan of salvation did not originate as a response to human sin but was determined in eternity — before sin ever entered the world — in the sovereign counsels of the triune God.

Part Eight: The Unity of the Covenant of Grace

One of the most important contributions of the Westminster Standards to covenant theology is their insistence on the unity of the covenant of grace throughout all of history. The Confession, Chapter 7, Section 6, concludes with this statement: "There are not therefore two covenants of grace, differing in substance, but one and the same, under various dispensations."

This is a claim of profound significance. Despite the many differences between the Old and New Testaments — differences in ceremonies, in clarity, in scope — the underlying covenant is one and the same. The same God who promised salvation to Abraham promises it to us. The same faith that justified David justifies us. The same Mediator who was foreshadowed in Moses is revealed in Christ. The substance of the covenant — salvation by grace through faith in the Mediator — has never changed and will never change.

The Puritan Thomas Blake, in his treatise "Vindiciae Foederis," defended the unity of the covenant of grace against various objections. Blake argued that the differences between the Old and New Testaments are differences of administration, not of substance — like the same precious wine served in different vessels. The vessel may change, but the wine remains the same. Abraham drank from an earthen cup; we drink from a golden chalice. But the wine of God's saving grace is identical in both cases.

This unity has enormous practical implications. It means that the Old Testament is not merely an outdated prelude to the real story. It is part of the same story. The God of Abraham is our God. The faith of Abraham is our faith. The promises of Abraham are our promises — fulfilled and confirmed in Christ. "And if ye be Christ's, then are ye Abraham's seed, and heirs according to the promise" (Galatians 3:29, KJV).

Part Twelve: The Seals of the Covenant

Every covenant in Scripture is accompanied by signs and seals — visible, tangible markers that confirm the covenant and signify its blessings. Under the Old Testament administration of the covenant of grace, the primary seal was circumcision, given to Abraham as "a seal of the righteousness of the faith which he had" (Romans 4:11, KJV). The Passover lamb was another covenantal sign, pointing forward to Christ, "our passover," who was "sacrificed for us" (1 Corinthians 5:7, KJV).

Under the New Testament administration, the covenant seals are baptism and the Lord's Supper. Baptism replaces circumcision as the sign of entrance into the visible covenant community. The Lord's Supper replaces the Passover as the sign of ongoing covenant fellowship and remembrance of the covenant sacrifice. The Confession teaches that these sacraments are not mere symbols or empty rituals — they are genuine means of grace through which God confirms His covenant promises and strengthens the faith of His people.

The Puritan Thomas Watson compared the sacraments to the seal upon a royal decree. The decree itself contains the promise, but the seal confirms and authenticates it. In the same way, the gospel contains the promises of the covenant, but the sacraments seal those promises upon the hearts of believers, giving them visible, tangible assurance that what God has promised He will surely perform.

Part Thirteen: Christ the Mediator of the Covenant

The covenant of grace has a Mediator — the Lord Jesus Christ. The Confession, Chapter 8, identifies Christ as the One whom God appointed "to be the Mediator between God and man." The Shorter Catechism, Question 21, teaches that "the only Redeemer of God's elect is the Lord Jesus Christ."

A mediator is one who stands between two estranged parties and brings them together. Humanity is estranged from God by sin. God is holy and cannot tolerate sin. Man is sinful and cannot approach God. The gap between them is infinite and unbridgeable — except by a Mediator who can represent both sides.

Christ is uniquely qualified to be this Mediator because He is both God and man. As God, He can represent the divine side — bringing God's grace, God's promises, and God's power to bear on the problem of human sin. As man, He can represent the human side — standing in our place, bearing our guilt, fulfilling our obligations under the covenant of works, and offering the perfect obedience that we could never offer. "For there is one God, and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus" (1 Timothy 2:5, KJV).

The Puritan Thomas Goodwin wrote with great depth about Christ's mediatorial role. Goodwin emphasized that Christ is not merely the means by which salvation is accomplished — He is the sum and substance of salvation itself. To know Christ is to know the covenant. To trust Christ is to enter the covenant. To enjoy Christ is to experience all the blessings of the covenant. Everything that the covenant of grace promises is found in Christ — forgiveness, righteousness, adoption, sanctification, perseverance, and glory. "For all the promises of God in him are yea, and in him Amen, unto the glory of God by us" (2 Corinthians 1:20, KJV).

Part Ten: Practical Applications

Several practical applications flow from the doctrine of God's covenant with man.

First, the covenant teaches us that salvation is all of grace. We cannot be saved by the covenant of works — that door is closed forever by the fall. Our only hope is the covenant of grace, in which God freely gives what we could never earn. This should produce deep humility and overwhelming gratitude.

Second, the covenant teaches us to trust God's promises. A covenant is only as reliable as the one who makes it. And the One who made the covenant of grace is the eternal, unchanging, all-powerful God who cannot lie. "God is not a man, that he should lie; neither the son of man, that he should repent: hath he said, and shall he not do it? or hath he spoken, and shall he not make it good?" (Numbers 23:19, KJV). His promises are as certain as His own nature.

Third, the covenant gives us assurance. If salvation depended on our performance, we would have no security — for our performance is always imperfect. But salvation depends on God's promise, sealed in the blood of Christ and guaranteed by the Holy Spirit. The Puritan William Guthrie, in his classic work "The Christian's Great Interest," urged believers to build their assurance not on the shifting sands of their own feelings or achievements but on the solid rock of God's covenant promises.

Fourth, the covenant provides the foundation for Christian assurance. Because our salvation rests not on our own fluctuating performance but on God's unbreakable covenant promise, we can have confidence that we belong to Him and that He will never let us go. The Puritan David Clarkson preached a series of sermons on the covenant in which he reminded believers that God's faithfulness to His covenant does not depend upon their faithfulness to Him. Even when believers stumble and fall, the covenant stands firm — for it is founded on the blood of Christ, sealed by the Holy Spirit, and guaranteed by the oath of Almighty God. "For the mountains shall depart, and the hills be removed; but my kindness shall not depart from thee, neither shall the covenant of my peace be removed, saith the LORD that hath mercy on thee" (Isaiah 54:10, KJV).

Fifth, the covenant calls us to faithfulness. While the covenant of grace is not founded on our works, it does call us to live lives of grateful obedience. We are not saved by our works, but we are saved unto good works. "For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works, which God hath before ordained that we should walk in them" (Ephesians 2:10, KJV). The covenant of grace does not abolish the moral law — it writes the law on our hearts and empowers us to obey it from love rather than from fear.

Fifth, the covenant should shape our approach to family worship and child-rearing. The covenant of grace includes not only believers but their children. "For the promise is unto you, and to your children" (Acts 2:39, KJV). Puritan parents understood that their children were covenant children — born within the covenant community, entitled to the sign of the covenant (baptism), and called to embrace the covenant promises by faith. This gave them both urgency and hope in the task of raising their children in the nurture and admonition of the Lord.

--- Closing ---

The doctrine of the covenant reveals a God who binds Himself to His people — a God who makes promises and keeps them, who pledges His faithfulness to undeserving sinners, and who sends His own Son to secure the blessings of the covenant by His blood. This God is your God if you are in Christ. His covenant promises are your inheritance. His faithfulness is your security. And His grace will sustain you from now until you stand in His presence, where the covenant of grace will be fully and eternally consummated in the joy of the Lord. The Puritan John Flavel captured the wonder of the covenant when he observed that God has bound Himself by promise, sealed that promise with the blood of His Son, and confirmed it by the witness of His Spirit — so that believers might have strong consolation in every trial and unshakable confidence in every storm. The covenant is your anchor in the tempest. It is your fortress in the battle. It is your song in the night. "I will be their God, and they shall be my people" (Jeremiah 31:33, KJV).
