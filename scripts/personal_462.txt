PURITAN GOLD -- My Growth, Day 1
"The Pilgrim Sets Out — Bunyan's Pilgrim's Progress"
Reading Day — John Bunyan's Pilgrim's Progress, Part 1

---

Welcome. This month in your personal growth journey, we rotate through three modes of learning: Reading days, where we engage deeply with Puritan classics; Pastoral days, where we receive practical counsel for daily Christian living; and Theological days, where we explore the great doctrines of the faith. Today is a Reading day, and we begin with the most widely read Puritan work ever written: John Bunyan's Pilgrim's Progress.

Let us ground ourselves in Scripture. Hebrews 11:13-16 (KJV):

"These all died in faith, not having received the promises, but having seen them afar off, and were persuaded of them, and embraced them, and confessed that they were strangers and pilgrims on the earth. For they that say such things declare plainly that they seek a country. And truly, if they had been mindful of that country from whence they came out, they might have had opportunity to have returned. But now they desire a better country, that is, an heavenly: wherefore God is not ashamed to be called their God: for he hath prepared for them a city."

--- Teaching ---

John Bunyan (1628-1688) wrote The Pilgrim's Progress from a prison cell in Bedford, England. He was jailed for the crime of preaching without a license — a nonconformist pastor who chose imprisonment over silence. And from that cell, he produced the most influential allegory in the English language: the story of Christian, a man who flees the City of Destruction and journeys toward the Celestial City.

The opening scene is one of the most gripping in all of literature. A man stands in a field, clothed in rags, with a great burden upon his back and a book in his hand. He reads the book and weeps, crying out: "What shall I do?" This is the awakened sinner — the person who has read the Scripture, seen their own condition, and been overwhelmed by the weight of their guilt. The burden on Christian's back is the burden of sin, and no human effort can remove it.

Christian meets a man named Evangelist, who points him toward a wicket gate in the distance and says: "Flee from the wrath to come." Christian begins to run, and his neighbors — Obstinate and Pliable — try to stop him. Obstinate demands that he return to the comfortable life of the City of Destruction. Pliable, more sympathetic, decides to join him but quickly turns back when they fall into the Slough of Despond — a bog of discouragement and doubt that traps many pilgrims at the very beginning of their journey.

Bunyan's genius lies in his ability to make the spiritual life visible. The Slough of Despond is not merely a literary device; it is a real experience that every Christian knows. It is the discouragement that comes immediately after conversion — the realization that following Christ is harder than you expected, that the path is muddy, that progress is slow, and that the comforts you left behind are calling you back. Bunyan knew this slough personally. His own conversion was agonizing — years of doubt, despair, and spiritual torment before he found assurance.

Christian emerges from the Slough with the help of a character named Help. This is no small detail. Bunyan believed that the Christian life is not meant to be lived alone. We need the help of others — pastors, friends, fellow pilgrims — to pull us out of the bogs that threaten to swallow us. The Puritan vision of the Christian life was intensely communal. While personal faith is essential, solitary faith is dangerous.

The next stage of Christian's journey brings him to the house of Mr. Worldly Wiseman, who offers him an easier path — a detour to the village of Morality, where the burden of sin can be addressed through good behavior and self-improvement rather than through the cross. This is one of Bunyan's sharpest warnings. The gospel of self-improvement is not the Gospel of Christ. The way of morality without grace is a dead end that leaves the burden firmly on your back. Only at the cross is the burden removed.

And so Christian comes, at last, to the cross. Bunyan describes the moment with beautiful simplicity: "He ran thus till he came at a place somewhat ascending, and upon that place stood a Cross, and a little below, in the bottom, a sepulchre. So I saw in my dream, that just as Christian came up with the Cross, his burden loosed from off his shoulders, and fell from off his back, and began to tumble, and so continued to do, till it came to the mouth of the sepulchre, where it fell in, and I saw it no more."

The burden falls. The guilt is gone. The chains are broken. And Christian stands free — weeping, laughing, leaping for joy. This is the heart of the Gospel: not self-improvement but divine deliverance. Not morality but grace. Not your effort but Christ's sacrifice.

The Puritans returned to this scene again and again in their devotional lives. Jonathan Edwards called it "the most affecting picture of justification by faith in all of English literature." Richard Baxter, though he sometimes disagreed with Bunyan on theological details, admitted that "the scene at the cross in Pilgrim's Progress has brought more souls to understand free grace than a thousand sermons."

As you begin this month of growth, let Bunyan's pilgrim remind you of where you started: burdened, weeping, desperate. And let the cross remind you of what God has done: He took the burden off your back and buried it in a tomb where you will never see it again.

--- Closing ---

The journey is long, but the burden is gone. You walk not under condemnation but under grace. Let that truth sustain you today and every day of this month. Lord, thank You for the cross. Thank You for the moment when the burden fell. Keep us on the path, and bring us safely to the Celestial City. Amen.
