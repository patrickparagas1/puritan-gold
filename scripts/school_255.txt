Welcome back, students. Today we complete our study of the first tablet of the law with the fourth commandment — the commandment that sanctifies time itself and reveals that rest is not laziness but worship. The fourth commandment stands at the hinge between our duties toward God and our duties toward our neighbor, and it is perhaps the most countercultural commandment for our age — an age that worships productivity, fears silence, and has all but forgotten the meaning of holy rest. Let us listen carefully to what God says.

--- Lesson ---

Part One: The Commandment in Full

"Remember the sabbath day, to keep it holy. Six days shalt thou labour, and do all thy work: but the seventh day is the sabbath of the Lord thy God: in it thou shalt not do any work, thou, nor thy son, nor thy daughter, thy manservant, nor thy maidservant, nor thy cattle, nor thy stranger that is within thy gates: for in six days the Lord made heaven and earth, the sea, and all that in them is, and rested the seventh day: wherefore the Lord blessed the sabbath day, and hallowed it" (Exodus 20:8-11).

The fourth commandment is the longest of the ten, and its length reflects its complexity and importance. It is unique among the commandments in several ways. It is the only commandment that begins with the word "Remember" — suggesting that it was a principle already known before Sinai, now being formally reaffirmed. It is the only commandment stated primarily in positive form — not "thou shalt not" but "remember... to keep it holy." And it is the only commandment that provides its own theological rationale within its own text — grounding the Sabbath in God's own pattern of work and rest at creation.

The Sabbath commandment also has the broadest scope of application of any commandment. It applies not only to the individual but to the entire household — sons, daughters, servants, strangers, and even animals. It is, as we shall see, a commandment with profound implications for social justice, for the ordering of the family, and for the spiritual health of both individuals and communities.

Part Two: The Theological Foundation — God's Rest at Creation

The theological foundation of the Sabbath is God's own rest at the conclusion of creation. "For in six days the Lord made heaven and earth, the sea, and all that in them is, and rested the seventh day: wherefore the Lord blessed the sabbath day, and hallowed it" (Exodus 20:11).

Genesis chapter 2, verses 1 through 3, records the event that lies behind this commandment: "Thus the heavens and the earth were finished, and all the host of them. And on the seventh day God ended his work which he had made; and he rested on the seventh day from all his work which he had made. And God blessed the seventh day, and sanctified it: because that in it he had rested from all his work which God created and made."

God rested on the seventh day — not because He was tired, for "the everlasting God, the Lord, the Creator of the ends of the earth, fainteth not, neither is weary" (Isaiah 40:28). He rested because His work was complete, and He took delight in what He had made. His rest was not the rest of exhaustion but the rest of satisfaction — the joyful contemplation of a work perfectly accomplished.

The Sabbath commandment invites us to enter into God's pattern — to work purposefully for six days and then to rest, to reflect, to worship, and to enjoy the goodness of God's creation and the riches of His grace. The pattern of six days of labor and one day of rest is woven into the very fabric of creation. It is not a cultural convention imposed by one civilization upon another. It is a creation ordinance — established by God at the beginning of the world, before there was a Jewish nation, before there was a Mosaic covenant, before there was a specific people to whom the law was given.

This is why the commandment says "Remember" — not "institute" or "begin" but "remember." The Sabbath principle was already in place. It needed only to be recalled, formalized, and incorporated into the covenant life of the people of God.

Part Three: The Sabbath Before Sinai — Evidence of Its Antiquity

The antiquity of the Sabbath principle is confirmed by several indicators in Scripture. Before the Ten Commandments were given at Sinai, God had already established the Sabbath pattern in connection with the gathering of manna. In Exodus chapter 16, God instructed the Israelites to gather manna for six days and to rest on the seventh: "Six days ye shall gather it; but on the seventh day, which is the sabbath, in it there shall be none" (Exodus 16:26). When some of the people went out on the seventh day to gather manna and found none, the Lord said to Moses: "How long refuse ye to keep my commandments and my laws?" (Exodus 16:28) — indicating that the Sabbath was already an established commandment before Sinai.

The creation narrative itself establishes the Sabbath pattern before any law is given. God's act of resting on the seventh day and blessing it and sanctifying it (Genesis 2:3) is the foundational act that gives the Sabbath its meaning. The German Reformer Martin Luther, and later the Puritans, both recognized that the Sabbath is a creation ordinance — an institution that belongs not merely to the Mosaic economy but to the permanent moral order established by God at the beginning of the world.

The early church fathers confirmed this understanding. Irenaeus of Lyons, writing around 180 A.D., taught that the Sabbath was instituted at creation for the benefit of all humanity, not merely for the Jewish people. Athanasius, the great defender of Trinitarian orthodoxy at the Council of Nicaea in 325 A.D., wrote that the Sabbath was a gift of God to the human race, given for rest, worship, and the renewal of the soul.

Part Four: The Change from Seventh Day to First Day — The Lord's Day

The Puritans held the Sabbath — which they observed on the Lord's Day, Sunday, in honor of Christ's resurrection — in the highest regard. But the change from the seventh day (Saturday) to the first day (Sunday) requires explanation.

The New Testament provides clear evidence that the early church gathered for worship on the first day of the week. In Acts chapter 20, verse 7, we read: "And upon the first day of the week, when the disciples came together to break bread, Paul preached unto them." In 1 Corinthians chapter 16, verse 2, Paul instructs the Corinthian church: "Upon the first day of the week let every one of you lay by him in store, as God hath prospered him." And in Revelation chapter 1, verse 10, John writes: "I was in the Spirit on the Lord's day" — using a title for Sunday that was already established in the apostolic church.

The reason for this change is the resurrection of Jesus Christ. Christ rose from the dead on the first day of the week (Matthew 28:1; Mark 16:2; Luke 24:1; John 20:1). His first appearances to the disciples were on the first day (John 20:19). His second appearance to the gathered disciples was one week later — again on the first day (John 20:26). The outpouring of the Holy Spirit at Pentecost occurred on the first day of the week. From the earliest days of the church, Christians gathered on the first day to celebrate the resurrection — the great event that inaugurated the new creation, the new covenant, and the new age of the Spirit.

The Westminster Confession of Faith, Chapter 21, Section 7, states: "As it is the law of nature, that, in general, a due proportion of time be set apart for the worship of God; so, in His Word, by a positive, moral, and perpetual commandment binding all men in all ages, He hath particularly appointed one day in seven, for a Sabbath, to be kept holy unto Him: which, from the beginning of the world to the resurrection of Christ, was the last day of the week; and, from the resurrection of Christ, was changed into the first day of the week, which, in Scripture, is called the Lord's Day, and is to be continued to the end of the world, as the Christian Sabbath."

The key point is this: the Sabbath principle — one day in seven set apart for worship and rest — is a permanent moral obligation. What changed was the specific day, not the principle. The seventh day pointed backward to the first creation; the first day points forward to the new creation inaugurated by the resurrection of Christ.

Part Five: The Three Essential Aspects of Sabbath Observance

The Puritans identified three essential aspects of Sabbath observance, all grounded in Scripture and all directed toward the spiritual flourishing of the believer and the community.

The first aspect is rest from ordinary labor. The commandment is explicit: "In it thou shalt not do any work." This is not a prohibition of all activity but a prohibition of ordinary, everyday, income-producing labor — what the Puritans called "worldly employments and recreations." The purpose is to free the mind and body from the relentless demands of the workweek so that the soul can attend to higher things.

The Puritans made exceptions for works of necessity (caring for the sick, feeding livestock, responding to emergencies) and works of mercy (visiting the poor, relieving suffering, ministering to those in distress), following Jesus's own example and teaching. When the Pharisees criticized Jesus for healing on the Sabbath, He replied: "The sabbath was made for man, and not man for the sabbath" (Mark 2:27). And again: "It is lawful to do well on the sabbath days" (Matthew 12:12). The Sabbath rest is not a legalistic straightjacket that forbids all activity. It is a gracious liberation from the tyranny of work so that the soul can worship, rest, and be renewed.

Nicholas Bownd, the Puritan minister whose treatise "The Doctrine of the Sabbath," published in 1595 and expanded in 1606, was the most influential English work on the subject in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, developed detailed guidance for what activities were appropriate and inappropriate for the Lord's Day. Bownd's work was controversial in his own time — King James I issued the "Book of Sports" in 1618, explicitly authorizing recreational activities on Sundays in direct opposition to the Puritan position — but it shaped Puritan and Presbyterian Sabbath practice for generations.

Bownd taught that the Sabbath rest should extend not only to the head of the household but to every member — including servants, apprentices, and laborers. No one should be compelled to work on the Lord's Day. This had practical implications that were genuinely revolutionary in an age when masters often required their servants to work seven days a week without respite.

The second aspect is public worship. The Lord's Day is, first and foremost, the day for gathered worship — for hearing the Word preached, for singing psalms and hymns, for prayer, for the administration of the sacraments, and for the fellowship of the saints. The Puritans considered attendance at public worship not merely a recommendation but a divine command. To neglect public worship when health and circumstances permitted was to violate the fourth commandment directly.

The writer of Hebrews exhorted the early Christians: "Not forsaking the assembling of ourselves together, as the manner of some is; but exhorting one another: and so much the more, as ye see the day approaching" (Hebrews 10:25). The Puritans took this exhortation with absolute seriousness. They gathered for morning worship and often for afternoon worship as well, with sermons that could last an hour or more — sermons that the congregations received eagerly, understanding them as the primary means by which God nourished their souls and built up His church.

The Puritan commitment to preaching on the Lord's Day was extraordinary. Ministers such as Richard Baxter, who pastored the parish of Kidderminster from 1641 to 1660, devoted the better part of each week to preparing their Sunday sermons. Baxter's preaching was so powerful and his pastoral care so comprehensive that the entire character of the town was transformed. When he arrived, the parish was largely irreligious; by the time he left, the church could not hold the number of people who wished to attend. Baxter attributed this transformation not to his own abilities but to the faithful preaching of God's Word on the Lord's Day — the day that God had set apart for the nourishment of His people.

The third aspect is private and family devotion. Beyond public worship, the Sabbath is a day for extended Bible reading, catechetical instruction, spiritual conversation, and prayer — activities that the pressures of the workweek often crowd out. The Puritans saw the Lord's Day as the family's weekly opportunity for deepened spiritual engagement — a day when fathers could catechize their children, when families could discuss the morning's sermon, when individuals could read devotional works and meditate on Scripture at greater length than the busy weekdays permitted.

Matthew Henry, whose father Philip Henry was himself a distinguished Puritan minister ejected from his pulpit in 1662, wrote extensively about the Lord's Day as a day for family worship. In his "Directions for Daily Communion with God," Henry urged families to make the most of the Sabbath hours — rising early for private prayer, gathering for family worship before the public service, discussing the sermon over the midday meal, reading Scripture together in the afternoon, and closing the day with prayer and thanksgiving. The Lord's Day, for the Henry family and for Puritan families across England and New England, was not a day of joyless restriction but a day of spiritual abundance — the best day of the week.

Part Six: The Sabbath and Social Justice

The fourth commandment also has a social justice dimension that is often overlooked but was deeply important to the Puritans. Notice that the rest mandated by the commandment extends to everyone in the household — "thou, nor thy son, nor thy daughter, thy manservant, nor thy maidservant, nor thy cattle, nor thy stranger that is within thy gates."

No one is to be exploited by working without rest. The Sabbath principle is God's declaration that human beings are more than economic units of production. They are bearers of His image, created for worship and fellowship, not merely for labor. Every person — regardless of social status, economic position, or national origin — has the God-given right to rest.

This had radical implications in the ancient world, where slavery was universal and the labor of servants was often unceasing. The fourth commandment declared that even servants and strangers — people at the very bottom of the social hierarchy — had the right to one day's rest in every seven. This was unprecedented in the ancient Near East. No other legal code in the surrounding cultures guaranteed a day of rest for all members of society, including servants, foreigners, and even animals.

The Puritans recognized the ongoing relevance of this principle. In their own context, they advocated for laws that protected the Lord's Day from commercial activity and enforced the right of laborers to rest. They opposed the opening of markets, the conducting of business, and the performance of unnecessary labor on Sundays — not because they were killjoys but because they believed that every human being, from the wealthiest merchant to the poorest servant, had the right to a day of worship and rest.

In the broader sweep of history, the Sabbath principle has been one of the most powerful forces for the protection of labor rights. The five-day workweek, the concept of "the weekend," the labor laws that guarantee days off — all of these have their roots, directly or indirectly, in the fourth commandment's insistence that human beings are not machines and that work must be interrupted by rest. A society that abolishes the Sabbath — that demands seven-day-a-week productivity from its workers — is a society that has forgotten what human beings are for.

Part Seven: The Sabbath as a Sign of the Covenant

In Exodus chapter 31, God identifies the Sabbath as a sign of the covenant between Himself and His people: "Verily my sabbaths ye shall keep: for it is a sign between me and you throughout your generations; that ye may know that I am the Lord that doth sanctify you... It is a sign between me and the children of Israel for ever: for in six days the Lord made heaven and earth, and on the seventh day he rested, and was refreshed" (Exodus 31:13, 17).

A "sign" in the biblical sense is not merely a symbol or a decoration. It is a visible, tangible marker of a spiritual reality. The rainbow was a sign of God's covenant with Noah. Circumcision was a sign of God's covenant with Abraham. And the Sabbath was a sign of God's covenant with Israel — a weekly reminder that they belonged to the God who had created the world, redeemed them from slavery, and sanctified them as His own people.

For the Christian under the new covenant, the Lord's Day continues to function as a covenant sign — a weekly declaration that we belong to the risen Christ, that we live not by bread alone but by every word that proceeds from the mouth of God, and that our identity is rooted not in our productivity but in our relationship with the God who made us, saved us, and sustains us.

Jonathan Edwards, the great Puritan-influenced theologian of the eighteenth century who served as pastor in Northampton, Massachusetts, from 1727 to 1750 and later as president of the College of New Jersey (now Princeton University), preached a series of sermons on the perpetuity and change of the Sabbath. Edwards argued that the Christian Sabbath — the Lord's Day — is one of the most precious ordinances of the new covenant, given for the spiritual enrichment of believers and the corporate edification of the church. He taught that the way a Christian observes the Lord's Day is a reliable indicator of the state of his soul. A believer who loves the Sabbath — who looks forward to it, prepares for it, guards it, and makes the most of it — is a believer whose heart is oriented toward God. A believer who neglects the Sabbath, resents it, or fills it with worldly pursuits is a believer whose heart has drifted from its true center.

Part Eight: Objections to the Sabbath — Ancient and Modern

The opponents of the Sabbath, both in the Puritans' time and in ours, have raised several objections that deserve honest consideration.

The first objection is that the Sabbath was a ceremonial law unique to Israel, not a moral law binding on all people. The Puritans responded by pointing to the creation foundation of the Sabbath (Genesis 2:1-3), which predates the Mosaic economy by many centuries. They also pointed to the inclusion of the Sabbath commandment in the moral law — the Ten Commandments written on stone by the finger of God — rather than in the ceremonial or judicial codes. The Sabbath is categorized with "Thou shalt not kill" and "Thou shalt not steal," not with the regulations about sacrifices and dietary laws.

The second objection is that the Sabbath is a legalistic imposition that restricts Christian freedom. The Puritans saw it differently. They saw the Sabbath as one of God's greatest gifts — a gift of time, of rest, of worship, and of spiritual renewal. Isaiah expressed this perspective beautifully: "If thou turn away thy foot from the sabbath, from doing thy pleasure on my holy day; and call the sabbath a delight, the holy of the Lord, honourable; and shalt honour him, not doing thine own ways, nor finding thine own pleasure, nor speaking thine own words: then shalt thou delight thyself in the Lord; and I will cause thee to ride upon the high places of the earth, and feed thee with the heritage of Jacob thy father: for the mouth of the Lord hath spoken it" (Isaiah 58:13-14). Those who call the Sabbath a delight will themselves delight in the Lord. The Sabbath is not a burden to be endured but a blessing to be embraced.

The third objection, raised by some interpreters of Paul's epistles, is that Paul abolished the Sabbath in passages like Colossians 2:16-17: "Let no man therefore judge you in meat, or in drink, or in respect of an holyday, or of the new moon, or of the sabbath days: which are a shadow of things to come; but the body is of Christ." The Puritans responded that Paul was addressing the ceremonial sabbaths of the Mosaic calendar — the special Sabbaths associated with festivals like Passover, the Feast of Tabernacles, and the Year of Jubilee — not the weekly Sabbath of the fourth commandment. The weekly Sabbath, grounded in creation and included in the moral law, belongs to a different category entirely.

Part Nine: The Sabbath and the Coming Rest — Hebrews Chapter 4

The writer of Hebrews provides the deepest theological reflection on the Sabbath in the entire New Testament. In Hebrews chapter 4, the Sabbath is presented as a type — a pointer toward a greater reality: the eternal rest that awaits the people of God.

"There remaineth therefore a rest to the people of God. For he that is entered into his rest, he also hath ceased from his own works, as God did from his. Let us labour therefore to enter into that rest, lest any man fall after the same example of unbelief" (Hebrews 4:9-11).

The weekly Sabbath is a foretaste of the eternal Sabbath — the everlasting rest in which God's people will cease from the toil, the struggle, the suffering, and the sin of this present age and will enter into the fullness of joy in the presence of God. Every Lord's Day is a rehearsal for eternity — a weekly anticipation of the day when "God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away" (Revelation 21:4).

The Puritans treasured this eschatological dimension of the Sabbath. They saw each Lord's Day as a window into heaven — a day when the cares of the world could be set aside and the glories of the world to come could be contemplated. Richard Baxter, in his devotional masterpiece "The Saints' Everlasting Rest," published in 1650, urged Christians to use the Lord's Day as an occasion for heavenly meditation — for lifting the mind and heart above the concerns of earth and fixing them on the eternal rest that Christ has prepared for His people.

Baxter wrote that the discipline of heavenly meditation on the Lord's Day is one of the most powerful means of grace available to the Christian. It weans the heart from the world, strengthens faith in the unseen realities, kindles love for Christ, and produces a deep, settled peace that the troubles of the week cannot destroy. The Sabbath, in Baxter's understanding, is not merely a day of physical rest but a day of spiritual transportation — a weekly journey from the valley of earthly labor to the mountaintop of heavenly contemplation.

Part Ten: Christ and the Sabbath

No study of the fourth commandment is complete without examining Jesus Christ's relationship to the Sabbath. During His earthly ministry, Jesus engaged in frequent controversies with the Pharisees over Sabbath observance. These controversies are profoundly instructive, for they reveal both the correct and incorrect ways of understanding the fourth commandment.

The Pharisees had surrounded the Sabbath commandment with an elaborate system of man-made rules — the Mishnaic tractate Shabbat eventually catalogued thirty-nine categories of prohibited work, each with numerous subcategories and case-by-case rulings. They debated whether tying a knot was "work," whether writing two letters of the alphabet constituted a violation, and whether carrying a burden included wearing a prosthetic tooth on the Sabbath. This system, developed with sincere intentions, had the effect of turning the Sabbath from a day of joyful rest into a day of anxious rule-keeping.

Jesus challenged this legalistic distortion of the Sabbath with repeated acts of healing on the Sabbath day. He healed the man with the withered hand (Mark 3:1-6). He healed the woman who had been bent over for eighteen years (Luke 13:10-17). He healed the man born blind (John 9:1-14). He healed the man at the pool of Bethesda who had been ill for thirty-eight years (John 5:1-18). In each case, the Pharisees objected, and in each case, Jesus responded with a defense of Sabbath mercy.

His most important statements about the Sabbath include: "The sabbath was made for man, and not man for the sabbath" (Mark 2:27) — affirming that the Sabbath is a gift for human benefit, not a burden for human oppression. And: "The Son of man is Lord also of the sabbath" (Mark 2:28) — claiming divine authority over the Sabbath as its Creator and rightful Interpreter. And: "My Father worketh hitherto, and I work" (John 5:17) — identifying His works of mercy on the Sabbath with the Father's own ongoing work of sustaining and redeeming His creation.

The Puritans drew an important lesson from Jesus's Sabbath controversies: the fourth commandment requires not merely the cessation of labor but the active pursuit of worship, mercy, and spiritual good. A Sabbath spent in idle inactivity, without worship or works of mercy, is not a properly kept Sabbath. The day is to be filled — not with worldly business but with spiritual activity: worship, prayer, Scripture reading, acts of compassion, and the joyful fellowship of the saints.

Jesus also fulfilled the Sabbath in a deeper theological sense. He is the true Sabbath rest — the One in whom believers find rest from the burden of sin, rest from the futile attempt to earn salvation by their own works, and rest in the finished work of the cross. "Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest. Take my yoke upon you, and learn of me; for I am meek and lowly in heart: and ye shall find rest unto your souls" (Matthew 11:28-29). The weekly Sabbath points to Christ as the source of all true rest. He is the substance of which the Sabbath is the shadow.

The Puritan theologian John Owen, in his "Exercitations on the Epistle to the Hebrews," developed this Christological understanding of the Sabbath with remarkable depth. Owen taught that Christ's resurrection on the first day of the week was not merely a historical event but a theological declaration — the announcement that the old creation's work of redemption was complete and that the new creation had dawned. Just as God rested on the seventh day because the work of the first creation was finished, so Christ rested in the tomb on the seventh day and rose on the first day because the work of the new creation — the redemption of His people — was accomplished. Owen wrote that when Christ rose from the dead, He entered into His rest, and all who believe in Him are invited to enter that rest with Him. The weekly observance of the Lord's Day is therefore not merely a commandment to be obeyed but a gospel to be believed — a weekly proclamation that in Christ, the great work is done, and the weary soul may rest.

Thomas Watson, in his exposition of the Ten Commandments, offered this vivid metaphor: "The Sabbath is God's market-day for the soul." On the other six days, the Christian labors in the fields of earthly vocation; on the Sabbath, the Christian comes to market to trade earthly weariness for heavenly provision. Watson urged believers to approach the Lord's Day not with reluctance but with the eagerness of a hungry man approaching a feast — for the Sabbath, rightly observed, is indeed a feast for the soul, spread by the hand of God Himself.

Part Eleven: The Sabbath in Puritan New England

The Puritan colonies of New England provide one of the most thoroughgoing attempts in Western history to implement fourth-commandment observance in an entire society. The Puritans who settled Massachusetts Bay in 1630, Connecticut in 1636, and New Haven in 1638 made Sabbath observance a cornerstone of their civil and religious life.

The Massachusetts General Court passed laws requiring attendance at public worship and prohibiting unnecessary labor, travel, and recreation on the Lord's Day. The New Haven Colony's legal code was even more specific, forbidding cooking, cleaning, and any form of worldly conversation on Sunday. Violators were subject to fines and, in extreme cases, public censure.

These laws reflected the Puritans' deep conviction that the fourth commandment was binding not merely on individuals but on the community as a whole. They believed that a society that honored the Lord's Day would prosper under God's blessing, while a society that profaned it would incur His judgment. This was not mere theory — they took seriously the biblical warnings against Sabbath-breaking, such as Nehemiah's stern action against those who traded on the Sabbath in Jerusalem (Nehemiah 13:15-22) and Jeremiah's prophecy that the gates of Jerusalem would be consumed by fire if the people did not keep the Sabbath holy (Jeremiah 17:27).

The New England Sabbath began at sunset on Saturday evening and continued until sunset on Sunday evening — following the biblical pattern of "the evening and the morning" that marks each day of creation. Families prepared for the Sabbath on Saturday afternoon, completing their domestic tasks and turning their hearts toward the worship that would commence with the setting of the sun. Sunday morning was devoted to public worship, which typically included a lengthy sermon, corporate prayer, psalm-singing, and the reading of Scripture. A midday meal was followed by a second service in the afternoon. The evening was spent in family devotions, catechetical instruction, and spiritual conversation.

While modern critics have sometimes caricatured the Puritan Sabbath as a joyless day of restriction, the Puritans themselves experienced it as a day of profound spiritual refreshment. Cotton Mather, the prolific New England minister who served Boston's North Church from 1685 to 1728, wrote that the Lord's Day was the "queen of days" — the day he most anticipated and most enjoyed. His diary records the delight he took in preaching, prayer, and the fellowship of the saints on the Lord's Day, as well as the spiritual renewal that the day provided for the demanding work of ministry.

Samuel Sewall, a Puritan judge and diarist in Boston whose diary provides one of the most detailed records of daily life in colonial New England, recorded his family's Sabbath observance with evident affection. The Sewall family attended morning and afternoon worship, discussed the sermons over meals, read Scripture together, and closed the day with prayer and singing. For Sewall and his contemporaries, the Sabbath was not a burden but a joy — the highlight of the weekly cycle.

Part Twelve: The Fourth Commandment in Connection with the Other Commandments

The fourth commandment occupies a strategic position in the Decalogue. It is the last commandment on the first tablet — the tablet devoted to our duties toward God — and it stands as the hinge between our vertical relationship with God and our horizontal relationships with one another.

In one sense, the fourth commandment summarizes the entire first tablet. It is in the keeping of the Sabbath that we demonstrate most practically our commitment to the first three commandments. On the Sabbath, we worship the true God alone (first commandment). On the Sabbath, we worship Him according to His Word (second commandment). On the Sabbath, we hallow His name in public and private worship (third commandment). The Sabbath is, as it were, the weekly expression of all that the first tablet requires.

The fourth commandment also lays the foundation for the second tablet. A society that keeps the Sabbath is a society that honors authority (fifth commandment), because it submits to God's ordering of time. It is a society that values human life (sixth commandment), because it refuses to treat people as mere instruments of production. It is a society that protects the family (seventh commandment), because it provides time for the nurture of marital and familial bonds. It is a society that promotes justice (eighth commandment), because it guarantees rest for every member, regardless of social status. And it is a society that upholds truth (ninth commandment), because it acknowledges the truth about human nature — that we are made for God and cannot flourish without regular, dedicated time for worship and spiritual renewal.

The Puritans understood this interconnection with remarkable clarity. They saw the Sabbath not as an isolated commandment but as the keystone of the entire moral arch — the commandment that holds all the others together by providing the regular, weekly opportunity for the spiritual renewal upon which all obedience depends. A Christian who neglects the Sabbath will inevitably find the other commandments more difficult to keep, because the soul has been deprived of the nourishment that the Sabbath provides. A Christian who keeps the Sabbath faithfully will find the other commandments more natural and more joyful, because the soul has been fed, refreshed, and strengthened by regular communion with God.

Part Thirteen: Practical Application — Sabbath-Keeping in Our Hyperconnected Age

In our hyperconnected, always-on, relentlessly productive culture, the Sabbath principle is more needed than ever. We live in an age that never stops — an age of 24/7 commerce, constant connectivity, incessant notifications, and the expectation that every waking moment must be filled with activity. We are busier, more distracted, and more exhausted than any previous generation — and the consequences for our spiritual, mental, and physical health are severe.

The fourth commandment offers a radical alternative. It says: Stop. Rest. Worship. Remember who you are and whose you are. You are not a machine. You are not an economic unit. You are a bearer of God's image, created for worship and fellowship, and you need a day when the screens go dark, when the emails stop, when the calendar empties, and when the soul has room to breathe.

Practically, observing the Lord's Day in our age requires intentional planning and determined resistance to the cultural current. Here are several suggestions drawn from the Puritan tradition.

First, prepare for the Sabbath the day before. The Puritans, following the Jewish practice of Sabbath preparation, often used Saturday evening to prepare for Sunday — completing necessary tasks, laying out clothing, preparing food, and turning the heart toward the worship that would come in the morning. This preparation helps ensure that the Lord's Day begins in peace rather than in the frantic rush of last-minute activity.

Second, prioritize public worship above all else. Make attendance at the gathering of God's people on the Lord's Day the non-negotiable center of the day. Everything else — meals, recreation, social activities — should be arranged around the worship service, not the other way around. The Puritans would be astonished at the modern practice of skipping church for sports games, family outings, or simple convenience.

Third, set aside extended time for family worship and Scripture reading. Use the Lord's Day as an opportunity to go deeper into God's Word than the busy weekdays permit. Read a chapter or two aloud as a family. Discuss the morning's sermon. Catechize younger children. Pray together with greater length and depth than the compressed devotions of Monday through Saturday allow.

Fourth, embrace rest as an act of faith. In a culture that measures human worth by productivity, the decision to rest is a radical statement of trust — a declaration that our value does not depend on what we produce but on the God who created us and redeemed us. Rest says: God is sovereign. He can sustain the world without my help for one day. I can stop working because He never stops working on my behalf.

Fifth, protect the day from digital intrusion. Consider turning off devices, avoiding social media, and limiting screen time on the Lord's Day. The constant flow of information, entertainment, and distraction that our devices provide is one of the greatest enemies of the Sabbath rest. A day unplugged from the digital world and plugged into the spiritual world is a day that nourishes the soul in ways that no amount of scrolling can replicate.

--- Closing ---

The Sabbath is God's weekly reminder that we are not defined by our productivity but by His love. It is His gift of time — time to worship, time to rest, time to be renewed, and time to remember that this world is not our final home. In an age of restless striving and constant noise, the fourth commandment speaks with the quiet authority of the Creator: Be still, and know that I am God.

As Richard Baxter wrote, the Lord's Day is the market day of the soul — the day when we lay in the provisions of grace that will sustain us through the week ahead. Let us not neglect this blessed market. Let us come to it with eager hearts, open hands, and expectant faith, knowing that the God who blessed and hallowed the seventh day at creation continues to bless and hallow the first day of the week for all who observe it in spirit and in truth.

Rest in Him, dear students. Rest in the finished work of Christ. Rest in the promise of eternal Sabbath. And let the Lord's Day be, as the Puritans loved to say, the best day of all the week. As the prophet Isaiah declared with tender authority: "In returning and rest shall ye be saved; in quietness and in confidence shall be your strength" (Isaiah 30:15). The God who commands rest also promises blessing to those who heed His command. May we be numbered among those faithful ones who call the Sabbath a delight. Until next time.
