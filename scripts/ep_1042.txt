PURITAN GOLD -- Melchizedek: Complete Summary
A Full Overview of the Biblical Theology of Melchizedek


Welcome to Puritan Gold. This episode is a complete summary of our eight-part study of Melchizedek — the mysterious priest-king who appears in Scripture as one of the most profound types of Jesus Christ. If you have listened to the full series, this will serve as a thorough review that ties every thread together. If you are new to this study, this episode will give you a comprehensive overview of who Melchizedek was, every key passage of Scripture that concerns him, the typological connections to Christ, and the insights of the great Puritan theologians.

Let us begin with the question: who was Melchizedek?

Melchizedek first appears in Genesis chapter 14. The patriarch Abraham — still called Abram at that point — had just conducted a daring military rescue. His nephew Lot had been captured when a coalition of four eastern kings, led by Chedorlaomer king of Elam, invaded the region around Sodom and Gomorrah. Abraham armed 318 trained servants, pursued the invaders, defeated them in a night raid, and recovered Lot along with all the captives and goods.

As Abraham returned from this victory, two figures came out to meet him. One was the king of Sodom, who offered Abraham the spoils of war. The other was Melchizedek, king of Salem, priest of the most high God. The contrast between these two figures is striking and deliberate. The king of Sodom represents the world's offer — take the goods, enrich yourself through alliance with wickedness. Melchizedek represents God's provision — bread and wine, blessing, and the acknowledgment that all victory comes from the most high God, possessor of heaven and earth.

Melchizedek performed three priestly actions. He brought forth bread and wine — refreshment for the weary warrior, and a foreshadowing of the elements Christ would later institute at the Lord's Supper. He blessed Abraham, pronouncing a twofold benediction: blessed be Abraham of the most high God, and blessed be the most high God who delivered Abraham's enemies into his hand. And Abraham gave him tithes of all — a voluntary act of worship acknowledging Melchizedek's superior priestly authority.

The name Melchizedek itself is significant. It is a compound of two Hebrew words — melek, meaning king, and tsedek, meaning righteousness. He is the King of Righteousness. And he is king of Salem, from the Hebrew shalom, meaning peace. He is the King of Peace. The writer of Hebrews notes the order: first King of Righteousness, then King of Peace. This reflects the order of the gospel. There can be no peace with God until righteousness is established. Christ first satisfies the demands of divine justice, and only then does He bestow peace upon His people.

After Genesis 14, Melchizedek disappears from the biblical record for approximately one thousand years. He is not mentioned in the books of Moses. He is not mentioned in Joshua, Judges, Samuel, or Kings. The silence is vast and deliberate. And then, in Psalm 110, the silence breaks with thunderous force.

Psalm 110 is a psalm of David, and it is the most quoted psalm in the entire New Testament. In verse 1, the LORD — Jehovah the Father — speaks to David's Lord — the Messiah — and says: "Sit thou at my right hand, until I make thine enemies thy footstool." This establishes the Messiah's kingship and enthronement. In verse 3, the Messiah's people are described as willing volunteers in the day of His power, coming in the beauties of holiness like the dew of the morning — a picture of effectual calling, where the Spirit of God makes the hearts of the elect willing and eager.

And then comes verse 4, the verse that changes everything: "The LORD hath sworn, and will not repent, Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek." God takes an oath — the Almighty, who cannot lie, who needs no oath because His bare word is infinitely reliable, nevertheless condescends to swear. He will not repent, He will not change His mind. The Messiah is a priest — not after the order of Aaron, not of the tribe of Levi, but after the order of Melchizedek. And this priesthood is forever.

The implications are enormous. Under the old covenant, the offices of king and priest were strictly separated. The tribe of Judah provided the kings. The tribe of Levi provided the priests. When King Saul dared to offer sacrifice, his kingdom was torn from him. When King Uzziah entered the temple to burn incense, God struck him with leprosy. Yet here, David's Lord is declared to be both king and priest. This can only be possible if His priesthood is of a completely different order — the order of Melchizedek, where kingship and priesthood are united in one person.

The New Testament exposition of these Old Testament foundations is found in the epistle to the Hebrews, chapters 5 through 7. The writer of Hebrews builds the most sustained theological argument in the New Testament about the priesthood of Christ, and the Melchizedek typology is its backbone.

In Hebrews 5, the writer establishes the qualifications of a true high priest. He must be taken from among men. He must be able to have compassion on sinners. He must be called by God, not self-appointed. Christ meets every qualification. He was made flesh and dwelt among us. He suffered in every way that we suffer, learning obedience through the things He endured. He was called by God with the words of Psalm 2 — "Thou art my Son" — and Psalm 110 — "Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek." And having been perfected through suffering, He became the author of eternal salvation.

In Hebrews 6:13-20, the writer connects the oath of God to the believer's assurance. God confirmed His promise to Abraham by an oath, swearing by Himself because He could swear by no greater. In the same way, God confirmed Christ's priesthood by an oath. The result for believers is two immutable things — God's promise and God's oath — which provide a strong consolation and a hope that serves as an anchor of the soul, sure and steadfast, entering into that within the veil, where Christ has gone as our forerunner.

In Hebrews 7:1-3, the writer formally introduces Melchizedek, interpreting his names (King of Righteousness, King of Peace) and noting his extraordinary presentation in Scripture: without father, without mother, without genealogy, having neither beginning of days nor end of life, but made like unto the Son of God, abiding a priest continually. The Reformed and Puritan consensus is that these descriptions refer to Melchizedek as he appears in the biblical record. The Holy Spirit deliberately omitted his genealogy from Genesis so that he might serve as a more perfect type of Christ, who truly is eternal.

In Hebrews 7:4-10, the writer proves Melchizedek's superiority to Abraham and Levi through two arguments. First, Abraham paid tithes to Melchizedek — the lesser paying honor to the greater. Second, Melchizedek blessed Abraham — and without all contradiction, the less is blessed of the better. Furthermore, Levi himself, who would later receive tithes from the Israelites, was present in Abraham's loins when Abraham paid tithes to Melchizedek. Therefore Levi himself acknowledged Melchizedek's superiority.

In Hebrews 7:11-17, the writer draws the decisive conclusion. If the Levitical priesthood could bring perfection — complete access to God, permanent forgiveness, cleansed conscience — then why would God promise another priest of a different order? The very existence of Psalm 110:4 proves the Levitical system was insufficient. And a change of priesthood demands a change of law. The entire Mosaic ceremonial system was tied to the Levitical priesthood. When Christ arose as a priest after the order of Melchizedek — from the tribe of Judah, not Levi — the old legal framework was set aside. Christ's priesthood rests not on the law of a carnal commandment — on physical descent and Mosaic regulation — but on the power of an endless life.

In Hebrews 7:18-28, the writer reaches the summit. The former commandment is disannulled for its weakness. A better hope is brought in, by which we draw near to God. Christ is made the surety — the guarantor — of a better covenant. His priesthood is unchangeable because He continues forever. And then comes the crowning declaration: "Wherefore he is able also to save them to the uttermost that come unto God by him, seeing he ever liveth to make intercession for them." To the uttermost — completely, permanently, to the very end. He saves because He lives. He intercedes because He reigns. He is holy, harmless, undefiled, separate from sinners, made higher than the heavens. He offered Himself once — not repeatedly, not annually, but once for all. And the word of the oath makes Him, the Son, consecrated for evermore.

Now, what did the great Puritan theologians draw from all of this?

John Owen, in his monumental commentary on Hebrews, emphasized three excellencies of Christ's Melchizedekian priesthood. First, the union of king and priest in one person — a union impossible under the old covenant but perfectly realized in the sinless Son of God. Second, the perpetuity of the priesthood — Christ lives forever, therefore His intercession never ceases, therefore the salvation of His people is eternally secure. Third, the oath of God — which places the unchangeable nature of God Himself behind the appointment of Christ, making it the most secure reality in the universe.

Thomas Goodwin focused on the experiential comfort of this doctrine. He urged believers tormented by guilt and accusation to look upward to the throne of grace, where Christ sits as a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek. If His priesthood is eternal, then His intercession for the troubled saint is eternal. If His sacrifice was once for all, then the sins that burden the conscience are answered by a sufficient atonement. Goodwin also saw in the bread and wine of Genesis 14 a picture of Christ's ongoing provision for His weary people — meeting them with spiritual sustenance after every battle.

Matthew Henry brought his characteristically warm pastoral touch, emphasizing the practical implications. Christ as our Melchizedekian Priest is everything the Levitical priests were not — sinless, immortal, offering His own blood, serving in the true heavenly tabernacle. Henry also drew from Abraham's tithe a lesson about Christian giving: it is the joyful acknowledgment that all we have belongs to God and that Christ our Priest-King has authority over every dimension of our lives.

Stephen Charnock connected the doctrine to divine immutability. Because God does not change, His oath does not change. Because His oath does not change, Christ's priesthood does not change. Because Christ's priesthood does not change, the salvation of His people does not change. Charnock built a golden chain of immutability from the nature of God to the security of the believer.

And Richard Sibbes, the gentle pastor, saw in the union of king and priest the combination of power and tenderness that sinners need. A king without priestly compassion might crush us. A priest without kingly power might pity us but lack the authority to deliver us. But Christ is both — mighty enough to save and compassionate enough to weep with us in our weakness.

Let us gather these threads into a single tapestry. Melchizedek appears in Genesis 14 as a historical figure — king of Salem, priest of the most high God — who blessed Abraham, received his tithe, and brought forth bread and wine. He appears in Psalm 110 as a prophetic category — the order of an eternal priesthood established by the oath of God. He appears in Hebrews 5 through 7 as the typological key that unlocks the glory of Christ's priestly ministry — superior to Abraham, superior to Levi, unchangeable, eternal, and able to save to the uttermost.

The Old Testament types reinforce the theme. Salem is Jerusalem — the city of the cross. The separation of king and priest under the law pointed to the inadequacy of any merely human arrangement. Zechariah's vision of the Branch who would be a priest upon his throne pointed forward to Christ. And the bread and wine foreshadowed the communion table where Christ feeds His church with Himself.

The doctrine of Melchizedek, then, is not an obscure theological curiosity. It is the doctrine of the priesthood of Jesus Christ — the doctrine upon which our access to God, our assurance of salvation, and our hope of glory all depend. Without a priest, we have no mediator. Without an eternal priest, we have no permanent standing before God. Without a priest established by oath, we have no unshakeable security. But God has given us all of this in His Son — our Melchizedek, our King of Righteousness, our King of Peace, our High Priest forever.

He is able to save to the uttermost. He ever lives to make intercession. He is consecrated for evermore.

Come to God by Him. Amen.
