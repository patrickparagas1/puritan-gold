PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 8
"Of Christ the Mediator"
The Westminster Standards — Lesson 8


Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we come to the very heart of the Christian faith — the doctrine of Christ the Mediator. Everything we have studied so far has been leading to this moment. The doctrine of God's decree established that God had an eternal plan of salvation. The doctrine of creation showed us the world that God made and the humanity He placed at its pinnacle. The doctrine of the fall revealed the catastrophe of sin and the ruin of the human race. The doctrine of the covenant showed us that God, in His mercy, established a covenant of grace with fallen sinners. But all of these doctrines point forward to one Person — the Lord Jesus Christ, the eternal Son of God, who became man in order to accomplish the salvation that sinners could never accomplish for themselves. He is the Mediator of the covenant of grace, the Redeemer of God's elect, and the central figure of all Scripture and all history. Without Him, there is no salvation, no hope, and no future for the human race. With Him, there is forgiveness of sins, reconciliation with God, and eternal life. Let us pray and begin.

Lord Jesus Christ, Thou art the Mediator between God and men, the One who bridges the infinite chasm between a holy God and sinful creatures. As we study Thy person and Thy work today, give us eyes to see Thy glory — the glory of the only begotten of the Father, full of grace and truth. Draw our hearts to Thee with cords of love, and deepen our understanding of what it cost Thee to save us. In Thy most precious name, Amen.


Part One: The Need for a Mediator

Before we examine who the Mediator is and what He has done, we must understand why a Mediator is needed. The answer lies in the nature of God and the condition of man.

God is infinitely holy — "of purer eyes than to behold evil, and canst not look on iniquity" (Habakkuk 1:13, KJV). His holiness is not merely a quality that He possesses; it is the essence of His being. He is "the Holy One of Israel" (Isaiah 1:4, KJV), and everything about Him — His justice, His truth, His love, His power — is characterized by perfect, unblemished holiness. God cannot compromise with sin, overlook sin, or pretend that sin does not exist. His justice demands that every transgression of His law be punished with the penalty it deserves.

Man, on the other hand, is thoroughly sinful. As we studied in our lesson on the fall, every faculty of human nature has been corrupted by sin. We are guilty of Adam's transgression, corrupted in our whole nature, and incapable of any spiritual good. We cannot earn God's favor by our own efforts, satisfy His justice by our own suffering, or restore ourselves to fellowship with Him by our own power. "There is none righteous, no, not one" (Romans 3:10, KJV).

The result is an impassable gulf between God and man — a gulf created by human sin and maintained by divine justice. Sinful creatures cannot approach a holy God. And a holy God cannot simply overlook the sins of His creatures without violating His own justice. Something must be done to bridge this gulf, to satisfy divine justice, and to reconcile God and man. That something — or rather, that Someone — is the Mediator, the Lord Jesus Christ.

The apostle Paul states this truth with crystalline clarity: "For there is one God, and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus; Who gave himself a ransom for all" (1 Timothy 2:5-6, KJV). A mediator is one who stands between two estranged parties and brings them together. Christ stands between a holy God and sinful humanity, representing God to man and man to God, reconciling the two through His own person and work.

Part Two: The Person of the Mediator — True God

The Westminster Confession, Chapter 8, presents the doctrine of Christ the Mediator with great care and precision. The Shorter Catechism, Question 21, asks: "Who is the Redeemer of God's elect?" and answers: "The only Redeemer of God's elect is the Lord Jesus Christ, who, being the eternal Son of God, became man, and so was, and continueth to be, God and man in two distinct natures, and one Person, for ever."

Let us examine each element of this confession.

"The Lord Jesus Christ, being the eternal Son of God..." Christ did not begin to exist at Bethlehem. He is the eternal Son of God — the second Person of the Trinity — who has existed from all eternity in perfect communion with the Father and the Holy Spirit. "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. The same was in the beginning with God" (John 1:1-2, KJV). Christ is truly, fully, and eternally God — possessing the same divine nature, the same divine attributes, and the same divine glory as the Father and the Spirit.

The deity of Christ is essential to His work as Mediator. Only God can bear the infinite weight of divine wrath against sin. Only God can offer a sacrifice of infinite value. Only God can satisfy infinite justice. Only God can reveal God perfectly to man. Only God can give eternal life. If Christ were merely a creature — however exalted — His work could never accomplish what sinners need. But because He is the eternal Son of God, His person gives infinite value to His work, and His sacrifice is sufficient to save all who believe in Him.

The Confession, Chapter 8, Section 2, describes Christ's divine nature: "The Son of God, the second Person of the Trinity, being very and eternal God, of one substance, and equal with the Father..." The language is drawn from the ancient creeds of the church — Nicaea, Constantinople, and Chalcedon — and it affirms without qualification that Christ is "very God of very God," sharing the same divine essence with the Father and the Spirit.

The Puritan John Owen, who wrote more extensively on the person and work of Christ than perhaps any other Puritan theologian, argued that the glory of Christ's divine nature is the foundation of all Christian hope. In his devotional masterpiece "The Glory of Christ," Owen urged believers to meditate constantly upon the deity of the Savior — to consider that the One who hung upon the cross was not merely a good man or a noble martyr but the infinite, eternal, almighty God. This, Owen argued, is what gives the cross its saving power and the gospel its life-transforming message.

Part Three: The Person of the Mediator — True Man

The Mediator must also be truly human. The Confession continues: Christ "did, when the fullness of time was come, take upon him man's nature, with all the essential properties and common infirmities thereof; yet without sin."

This is the doctrine of the incarnation — the central miracle of the Christian faith. The eternal Son of God, without ceasing to be God, took upon Himself a genuine human nature — a human body, a human soul, human emotions, human experiences, and all the common infirmities of our fallen condition (hunger, thirst, weariness, pain, grief) — yet without sin. "And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us, (and we beheld his glory, the glory as of the only begotten of the Father,) full of grace and truth" (John 1:14, KJV).

The humanity of Christ is as essential to His mediatorial work as His deity. The Confession explains why: Christ took upon Himself human nature so that He might represent us before God, obey the law on our behalf, suffer the penalty that our sins deserved, and sympathize with us in our weaknesses. "For we have not an high priest which cannot be touched with the feeling of our infirmities; but was in all points tempted like as we are, yet without sin" (Hebrews 4:15, KJV).

The Shorter Catechism, Question 22, asks: "How did Christ, being the Son of God, become man?" and answers: "Christ, the Son of God, became man, by taking to himself a true body, and a reasonable soul, being conceived by the power of the Holy Ghost, in the womb of the Virgin Mary, and born of her, yet without sin." The virgin birth is the divinely appointed means by which the eternal Son entered the human race without inheriting the guilt and corruption of Adam's sin. He was born of a woman — truly human. He was conceived by the Holy Spirit — without original sin. He was the second Adam — the beginning of a new humanity.

The Puritan Thomas Goodwin wrote with great tenderness about the humanity of Christ. Goodwin emphasized that Christ did not merely assume a human nature as a garment to be worn temporarily and then discarded. He became truly human — He thought human thoughts, felt human emotions, endured human suffering, and experienced human death. And He remains truly human forever. The risen, glorified Christ who sits at the right hand of the Father is still the God-man — possessing both a divine and a human nature in one Person for all eternity. This means that believers have a High Priest in heaven who genuinely understands their struggles, their sorrows, and their temptations — because He has experienced them all Himself.

Part Four: The Virgin Birth and the Sinlessness of Christ

The Confession teaches that Christ was "conceived by the power of the Holy Ghost, in the womb of the Virgin Mary." The virgin birth is not merely a miraculous sign — it is a theological necessity. Through the virgin birth, the eternal Son of God entered the human race while avoiding the guilt and corruption of Adam's sin. Because Christ was not descended from Adam by ordinary generation, He did not inherit Adam's guilt. Because He was conceived by the Holy Spirit, His human nature was free from original sin. He was born holy — "that holy thing which shall be born of thee shall be called the Son of God" (Luke 1:35, KJV).

The sinlessness of Christ is essential to His mediatorial work. A sinful mediator could not atone for the sins of others, for he would need atonement for his own sins. But Christ was "holy, harmless, undefiled, separate from sinners" (Hebrews 7:26, KJV). He lived an entire human life without committing a single sin — in thought, word, or deed. He was tempted in all points as we are, yet He never yielded. His obedience was perfect, and His sacrifice was spotless. "Who did no sin, neither was guile found in his mouth" (1 Peter 2:22, KJV).

Part Five: The Two Natures in One Person

The Confession affirms that Christ is "God and man in two distinct natures, and one Person, for ever." This is the doctrine of the hypostatic union — the teaching that the divine nature and the human nature are united in the one Person of Christ without confusion, without change, without division, and without separation. The two natures are distinct — the divine nature remains fully divine, and the human nature remains fully human. They are not mixed, blended, or confused with each other. But they are inseparably united in the one Person of the Son of God — never to be divided or separated.

This doctrine was defined at the Council of Chalcedon in AD 451, and the Westminster divines embraced it without hesitation or qualification. The Chalcedonian definition was formulated to guard against four errors. First, it rejected the error of Arianism, which denied the full deity of Christ. Second, it rejected the error of Apollinarianism, which denied the full humanity of Christ. Third, it rejected the error of Nestorianism, which divided Christ into two persons. Fourth, it rejected the error of Eutychianism, which confused the two natures into one.

The Puritan Stephen Charnock reflected at length upon the wonder of the hypostatic union. Charnock marveled that in the one Person of Christ, infinity and finitude coexist — eternity and time, omnipotence and weakness, omniscience and learning, the Creator and the creature. This is a mystery that surpasses all human comprehension. We can confess it, we can believe it, we can worship the Christ in whom it is realized — but we cannot fully comprehend it. The incarnation is the supreme mystery of the Christian faith — a truth before which angels bow in wonder and theologians confess their limitations.

The practical significance of the hypostatic union is immense. Because Christ is one Person with two natures, every action He performs as Mediator has both divine and human dimensions. When Christ dies on the cross, it is a human death — for God cannot die. But it is the death of a divine Person — and therefore it has infinite value. When Christ obeys the law, it is genuine human obedience — rendered by a man in our place. But it is the obedience of the eternal Son — and therefore it perfectly satisfies the demands of divine justice. The Puritan John Owen expressed this by saying that the divine nature gives infinite worth and merit to all that Christ does in His human nature, making His obedience and sacrifice sufficient to save all the elect.

Part Five: The Three Offices of Christ

The Westminster Standards present Christ's mediatorial work under the framework of three offices — Prophet, Priest, and King. The Shorter Catechism, Question 23, states: "Christ, as our Redeemer, executeth the offices of a prophet, of a priest, and of a king, both in his estate of humiliation and exaltation."

This threefold office is rooted in the Old Testament. In ancient Israel, prophets, priests, and kings were all anointed with oil as a sign of their divine appointment. Christ — whose title "Messiah" or "Christ" means "the Anointed One" — unites all three offices in His own Person. He is the Prophet who reveals God's will, the Priest who atones for sin, and the King who governs all things.

The Shorter Catechism, Question 24, describes Christ's prophetic office: "Christ executeth the office of a prophet, in revealing to us, by his Word and Spirit, the will of God for our salvation." Moses foretold the coming of a great Prophet: "The LORD thy God will raise up unto thee a Prophet from the midst of thee, of thy brethren, like unto me; unto him ye shall hearken" (Deuteronomy 18:15, KJV). Christ is that Prophet — the final, definitive revelation of God's will. He taught with an authority that surpassed all the prophets who came before Him: "Never man spake like this man" (John 7:46, KJV). And He continues to teach through His Word and Spirit — illuminating the minds of believers, guiding the church into truth, and making known the things that belong to our salvation.

The Puritan William Perkins taught that Christ's prophetic office includes both external and internal dimensions. Externally, Christ teaches through the Scriptures and through the preaching of the Word. Internally, Christ teaches through the illumination of the Holy Spirit, who opens the eyes of the mind and the ears of the heart to receive and understand divine truth. Without the Spirit's internal work, the external Word falls on deaf ears and blind eyes. But when the Spirit accompanies the Word, the result is saving knowledge — a knowledge that transforms the mind, captures the heart, and redirects the life.

The Shorter Catechism, Question 25, describes Christ's priestly office: "Christ executeth the office of a priest, in his once offering up of himself a sacrifice to satisfy divine justice, and reconcile us to God, and in making continual intercession for us." The priestly work of Christ has two parts — His sacrifice and His intercession.

Christ's sacrifice is the centerpiece of the gospel. On the cross, Christ offered Himself as the perfect, once-for-all sacrifice for sin. He bore our guilt, endured our punishment, and satisfied the demands of divine justice on our behalf. "But this man, after he had offered one sacrifice for sins for ever, sat down on the right hand of God" (Hebrews 10:12, KJV). His sacrifice does not need to be repeated — it was complete, sufficient, and final. The debt has been paid. The penalty has been borne. The justice of God has been fully satisfied.

Christ's intercession is the ongoing application of His sacrifice. Having offered Himself on the cross, Christ now sits at the right hand of the Father, where He continually pleads the merits of His sacrifice on behalf of His people. "He ever liveth to make intercession for them" (Hebrews 7:25, KJV). When believers sin, they have an advocate with the Father — "Jesus Christ the righteous" — who pleads their cause and ensures their forgiveness (1 John 2:1, KJV). The Puritan Thomas Goodwin described Christ's intercession as the believer's greatest comfort in times of failure and weakness. When we fall into sin, we do not lose our Mediator. When we fail, He does not fail us. His intercession secures our perseverance and guarantees that we will never be cast out of God's favor.

The Shorter Catechism, Question 26, describes Christ's kingly office: "Christ executeth the office of a king, in subduing us to himself, in ruling and defending us, and in restraining and conquering all his and our enemies." Christ is King — not merely in a spiritual or metaphorical sense, but in the fullest and most absolute sense. He rules over all creation with sovereign authority. He governs His church with wisdom and love. He restrains and overcomes all the forces that oppose His kingdom. And He will one day bring all things under His feet — every enemy defeated, every rival overthrown, every knee bowed before His throne.

"The LORD said unto my Lord, Sit thou at my right hand, until I make thine enemies thy footstool" (Psalm 110:1, KJV). Christ's kingdom is not of this world — it is not a political kingdom established by military force. But it is a real kingdom — a kingdom of grace that is advancing through the world, conquering hearts by the gospel, transforming lives by the Spirit, and moving inexorably toward the day when Christ will return in glory to consummate His kingdom and reign forever and ever.

Part Six: Christ's Obedience — Active and Passive

Before we examine the atonement in its fullness, we must understand the critical distinction between Christ's active and passive obedience — a distinction that the Puritans regarded as essential to a proper understanding of the gospel.

Christ's active obedience refers to His perfect keeping of the law of God throughout His entire earthly life. From His birth to His death, Christ obeyed every commandment of God's moral law — perfectly, fully, and without the slightest deviation. He loved God with all His heart, soul, mind, and strength. He loved His neighbor as Himself. He honored His parents. He spoke only truth. He coveted nothing. He was angry only with righteous anger. He fulfilled every requirement of the law that Adam — and we — failed to keep. "For as by one man's disobedience many were made sinners, so by the obedience of one shall many be made righteous" (Romans 5:19, KJV).

This active obedience was necessary because the covenant of works required not merely the avoidance of sin but the positive performance of righteousness. It was not enough for the Mediator merely to pay the penalty for sin. He also had to provide the righteousness that the covenant demanded. God's law does not merely say "thou shalt not" — it also says "thou shalt." And Christ fulfilled both the negative prohibitions and the positive requirements of the law on behalf of His people.

Christ's passive obedience refers to His suffering and death — His bearing of the penalty that our sins deserved. "Surely he hath borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows: yet we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted. But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed" (Isaiah 53:4-5, KJV). On the cross, Christ endured the wrath of God against sin — not His own sin, for He had none, but our sin, which was imputed to Him. He suffered the full penalty of the broken covenant of works — the penalty of death in all its dimensions: physical, spiritual, and eternal.

Together, Christ's active and passive obedience constitute what the Puritans called the "double imputation" of the gospel. Our sin is imputed to Christ — He bears its penalty on the cross. And Christ's righteousness is imputed to us — we receive the benefit of His perfect obedience. We give Him our rags, and He gives us His robes. We give Him our guilt, and He gives us His righteousness. "For he hath made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin; that we might be made the righteousness of God in him" (2 Corinthians 5:21, KJV).

The Puritan Robert Traill, in his sermons on justification, insisted that both aspects of Christ's obedience are essential to the believer's standing before God. Without Christ's passive obedience, we would still be under condemnation for our sins. Without Christ's active obedience, we would still lack the positive righteousness that God's law requires. But through both together, the believer is fully and eternally accepted before God — pardoned of all guilt and clothed in perfect righteousness.

Part Seven: The Atonement of Christ

The Westminster Confession, Chapter 8, Section 5, provides one of the most carefully worded statements on the atonement in all of Reformed theology: "The Lord Jesus, by his perfect obedience and sacrifice of himself, which he through the eternal Spirit once offered up unto God, hath fully satisfied the justice of his Father; and purchased not only reconciliation, but an everlasting inheritance in the kingdom of heaven, for all those whom the Father hath given unto him."

Several truths demand our attention. First, Christ's atoning work includes both His "perfect obedience" and His "sacrifice of himself." Theologians distinguish between Christ's active obedience — His perfect keeping of the law throughout His entire life — and His passive obedience — His suffering and death on the cross. Both are essential. Christ's active obedience provides the positive righteousness that is credited to believers. Christ's passive obedience pays the penalty for sin that believers deserve. Together, they constitute a complete atonement — addressing both the guilt of sin (which demands punishment) and the requirement of righteousness (which demands perfect obedience).

The Puritan John Owen, in his monumental work "The Death of Death in the Death of Christ," developed the Reformed doctrine of the atonement with unparalleled rigor. Owen argued that Christ's atoning work was designed by the Father, accomplished by the Son, and applied by the Spirit for a specific people — the elect. The atonement is not merely a general provision that makes salvation possible for anyone who happens to believe. It is a particular, effectual, and definitive act that actually accomplishes salvation for all those whom the Father has given to the Son. "I lay down my life for the sheep" (John 10:15, KJV). Christ died not merely to make salvation available but to secure salvation actually — to purchase, procure, and guarantee the eternal redemption of His people.

Second, Christ's sacrifice "hath fully satisfied the justice of his Father." This is the doctrine of penal substitutionary atonement — the teaching that Christ bore the penalty of sin in the place of sinners, thereby satisfying the demands of God's justice. "He hath made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin; that we might be made the righteousness of God in him" (2 Corinthians 5:21, KJV). God's justice required that sin be punished. Christ bore that punishment on our behalf. And because the penalty has been fully paid, God can now be both "just, and the justifier of him which believeth in Jesus" (Romans 3:26, KJV).

Third, Christ's sacrifice purchased "not only reconciliation, but an everlasting inheritance." The atonement accomplishes more than mere pardon — it secures an eternal inheritance. Believers are not merely forgiven criminals; they are adopted heirs. They receive not only the removal of guilt but the gift of eternal glory. "And if children, then heirs; heirs of God, and joint-heirs with Christ" (Romans 8:17, KJV).

Part Seven: The Humiliation of Christ

The Westminster Standards organize Christ's mediatorial work into two great states — His humiliation and His exaltation. The Shorter Catechism, Question 27, asks: "Wherein did Christ's humiliation consist?" and answers: "Christ's humiliation consisted in his being born, and that in a low condition, made under the law, undergoing the miseries of this life, the wrath of God, and the cursed death of the cross; in being buried, and continuing under the power of death for a time."

The Larger Catechism, Question 46, expands on this with magnificent detail. Christ was humiliated in His conception and birth — the eternal Son of God was conceived in the womb of a humble virgin and born in a stable among animals. He was humiliated in His life — He endured poverty, rejection, persecution, and opposition from those He came to save. He was humiliated in His suffering — He endured the agony of Gethsemane, the betrayal of Judas, the abandonment of His disciples, the mockery of His enemies, the scourging, the crown of thorns, and the nails of the cross. He was humiliated in His death — He died the most shameful death known to the ancient world, bearing the curse of God upon Himself. "Cursed is every one that hangeth on a tree" (Galatians 3:13, KJV). And He was humiliated in His burial — His body was laid in a borrowed tomb, and He remained under the power of death for three days.

The Puritan Isaac Ambrose, in his devotional work "Looking Unto Jesus," traced every stage of Christ's humiliation with painstaking detail and deep devotional feeling. Ambrose urged believers to meditate upon the sufferings of Christ — not merely as historical events but as acts of love performed for their sake. The Son of God was born in a manger that we might be received into a mansion. He was made under the law that we might be freed from the law's condemnation. He endured the wrath of God that we might enjoy the favor of God. He died the cursed death of the cross that we might inherit the eternal blessedness of heaven.

Part Eight: The Exaltation of Christ

The Shorter Catechism, Question 28, asks: "Wherein consisteth Christ's exaltation?" and answers: "Christ's exaltation consisteth in his rising again from the dead on the third day, in ascending up into heaven, in sitting at the right hand of God the Father, and in coming to judge the world at the last day."

Christ's exaltation is the divine vindication of His mediatorial work. Having accomplished redemption by His death, Christ was raised from the grave in triumphant glory — declaring that the penalty of sin has been paid, that the power of death has been broken, and that the work of salvation is complete. "He is not here: for he is risen, as he said" (Matthew 28:6, KJV). The resurrection is the Father's public endorsement of the Son's sacrifice — His declaration that the debt has been paid in full and that the Mediator's work has been accepted.

Christ ascended into heaven, where He took His seat at the right hand of the Father — the place of supreme honor, authority, and power. The Confession, Chapter 8, Section 4, teaches that Christ is now "at the right hand of God, making intercession" for His people. This is not a position of passive rest but of active authority. From His heavenly throne, Christ governs the universe, rules His church, intercedes for His people, and directs the course of history toward its appointed consummation.

Christ's session at the right hand of the Father is the present reality that sustains the church in every age. The Puritan David Clarkson preached a remarkable series of sermons on the heavenly session of Christ, arguing that the believer's security rests not on anything within himself but on the exalted Christ who reigns above. Every trial, every temptation, every persecution that the church endures on earth is overseen by a sovereign King in heaven who is working all things according to His perfect will. "He must reign, till he hath put all enemies under his feet" (1 Corinthians 15:25, KJV). The enemies of the church may rage, the gates of hell may threaten, but the King of kings sits enthroned in unshakable authority — and His kingdom shall have no end.

And Christ will come again. He will return "in like manner" as He ascended — visibly, bodily, and in glory — to judge the living and the dead, to reward His faithful servants, and to usher in the eternal kingdom. "For the Lord himself shall descend from heaven with a shout, with the voice of the archangel, and with the trump of God: and the dead in Christ shall rise first" (1 Thessalonians 4:16, KJV). This is the hope of the church — the blessed hope of Christ's return, when every wrong will be righted, every tear will be dried, and every enemy of God will be vanquished forever.

Part Ten: The Offices of Christ in His Humiliation and Exaltation

The Shorter Catechism teaches that Christ exercises all three of His offices — Prophet, Priest, and King — "both in his estate of humiliation and exaltation." This is an important qualification that deserves careful attention.

As Prophet in His humiliation, Christ taught God's truth directly during His earthly ministry — in the Sermon on the Mount, in His parables, in His conversations with disciples and opponents, and in His final discourses before the cross. As Prophet in His exaltation, Christ continues to teach through His Word and Spirit — illuminating the minds of believers, guiding the church, and opening the Scriptures to those who seek Him.

As Priest in His humiliation, Christ offered Himself as the sacrifice for sin upon the cross — the great act of atonement that paid the penalty for human rebellion and satisfied the justice of God. As Priest in His exaltation, Christ intercedes for His people at the right hand of the Father — pleading the merits of His sacrifice and ensuring that every blessing of redemption is applied to every one of His sheep. "Wherefore he is able also to save them to the uttermost that come unto God by him, seeing he ever liveth to make intercession for them" (Hebrews 7:25, KJV).

As King in His humiliation, Christ established His kingdom through His teaching, miracles, and redemptive work — conquering the powers of darkness by His submission to the cross. "Having spoiled principalities and powers, he made a shew of them openly, triumphing over them in it" (Colossians 2:15, KJV). As King in His exaltation, Christ reigns over all things from His heavenly throne — governing the universe, ruling His church, restraining His enemies, and advancing His kingdom until the day when He shall return in glory.

The Puritan Thomas Manton preached extensively on the offices of Christ, arguing that each office addresses a specific aspect of the human condition resulting from the fall. As Prophet, Christ addresses our ignorance — for the fall darkened our minds, and we need a Teacher to restore the knowledge of God. As Priest, Christ addresses our guilt — for the fall made us liable to God's wrath, and we need a Sacrifice to bear our punishment and an Intercessor to plead our cause. As King, Christ addresses our bondage — for the fall enslaved us to sin, Satan, and death, and we need a mighty Deliverer to set us free. Together, the three offices provide a comprehensive salvation that meets every need of the fallen human soul.

Part Eleven: The Sufficiency and Efficacy of Christ's Work

The Confession affirms that Christ's mediatorial work is entirely sufficient and perfectly efficacious. It is sufficient — there is nothing lacking in what Christ has accomplished. His obedience is perfect. His sacrifice is complete. His intercession is unfailing. There is no sin so great that His blood cannot cover it, no sinner so lost that His grace cannot reach them, no need so deep that His provision cannot meet it.

And it is efficacious — it actually accomplishes what it intends. Christ did not merely make salvation possible; He actually saved His people. He did not merely open a door; He brought His sheep through it. He did not merely provide an opportunity; He secured an eternal redemption. "He shall see of the travail of his soul, and shall be satisfied" (Isaiah 53:11, KJV). Christ will not be disappointed in the result of His work. Every person for whom He died will be effectually called, justified, sanctified, and glorified.

The Puritan Richard Sibbes captured this confidence beautifully when he described Christ as "a bruised reed He will not break, and a smoking flax He will not quench." Even the weakest faith, even the faintest spark of spiritual life, is precious to Christ and will be sustained by Him. The Mediator does not lose those whom the Father has given Him. "All that the Father giveth me shall come to me; and him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out" (John 6:37, KJV).

Part Ten: Practical Applications

Several practical applications flow from the doctrine of Christ the Mediator.

First, Christ as Mediator is the foundation of all Christian confidence. We do not approach God on the basis of our own merit but on the basis of Christ's merit. We do not plead our own righteousness but His. We do not rely on our own strength but on His intercession. "Let us therefore come boldly unto the throne of grace, that we may obtain mercy, and find grace to help in time of need" (Hebrews 4:16, KJV).

Second, the three offices of Christ should shape our entire Christian life. As Prophet, Christ teaches us through His Word — we must be diligent students of Scripture. As Priest, Christ has made atonement for our sins — we must rest in His finished work and not attempt to add our own merits to it. As King, Christ rules over all things — we must submit to His authority in every area of life.

Third, the humiliation of Christ should produce in us a spirit of humble gratitude. When we consider what the Son of God endured for our salvation — the manger, the poverty, the rejection, the suffering, the cross — we should be overwhelmed by the depth of His love and the costliness of our redemption. "For ye know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, that, though he was rich, yet for your sakes he became poor, that ye through his poverty might be rich" (2 Corinthians 8:9, KJV).

Fourth, the exaltation of Christ should produce in us a spirit of joyful hope. Our Savior is not dead. He is risen. He is ascended. He is enthroned. He is interceding for us. And He is coming again. Whatever trials we face in this world, we face them in the knowledge that our Mediator reigns and that He will bring us safely home.

Fifth, the doctrine of Christ's atonement should fill us with evangelistic zeal. The gospel of Christ crucified and risen is the most glorious message in the universe. It is the power of God unto salvation for everyone who believes. We who have tasted the grace of the Mediator are commissioned to proclaim His grace to others — to tell sinners that there is a Savior who died for them, a Priest who intercedes for them, and a King who will receive them if they come to Him in faith and repentance. The Puritan Richard Baxter was consumed with this evangelistic passion. He wrote that no minister should rest as long as there are sinners within his reach who have not heard the gospel — for the blood of Christ is too precious to be left unproclaimed, and the souls of men are too valuable to be left unwarned.

Sixth, the sufficiency of Christ's work should cure us of all anxious self-reliance. We do not need to add anything to what Christ has done. We do not need to earn what He has freely given. We need only to trust Him — to rest upon His perfect work as the sole ground of our acceptance before God. The Puritans fought relentlessly against every form of works-righteousness — every attempt to supplement Christ's finished work with human merit, religious performance, or moral achievement. The Puritan Walter Marshall, in his classic work "The Gospel-Mystery of Sanctification," insisted that even our sanctification — our growth in holiness — must be grounded in Christ's finished work, not in our own efforts. We grow in holiness not by trying harder but by trusting more deeply in the Christ who has already accomplished everything necessary for our salvation. Our hope is built on nothing less than Jesus' blood and righteousness. We dare not trust the sweetest frame but wholly lean on Jesus' name.

Jesus Christ — the eternal Son of God become man, the Mediator of the covenant of grace, the Prophet who reveals God's truth, the Priest who atones for sin and intercedes for His people, the King who reigns over all things — is the center of the Christian faith and the hope of every believing soul. He humbled Himself to the dust of death that He might exalt us to the heights of glory. He bore the curse that we might receive the blessing. He died that we might live. Know Him. Trust Him. Love Him. Worship Him. For there is no other name under heaven given among men by which we must be saved. "Neither is there salvation in any other: for there is none other name under heaven given among men, whereby we must be saved" (Acts 4:12, KJV). The Puritan Thomas Brooks once wrote that Christ is the sun of the soul, the bread of life, the fountain of living water, the rock of ages, the door of heaven, the way to the Father, and the pearl of great price. He is everything that the human heart needs and everything that the human condition requires. In His person, we find the perfect union of God and man. In His work, we find the perfect solution to our sin. In His offices, we find the perfect answer to our ignorance, our guilt, and our bondage. Rest in Him alone. Until next time, students.
