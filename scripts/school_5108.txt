"Sanctification and Perseverance"
Westminster Standards — Lesson 8

Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we study the Westminster Standards' teaching on sanctification — the lifelong process of growing in holiness — and the perseverance of the saints. Let us pray and begin.

Holy Spirit, You are the agent of our sanctification. As we study Your transforming work, make us not merely informed students but willing participants in the process of becoming more like Christ. Amen.

--- Lesson ---

Justification changes our legal standing before God — we are declared righteous. But justification does not change our character. We are still the same people with the same sinful tendencies, the same bad habits, and the same spiritual weaknesses. Something more is needed: sanctification — the progressive transformation of the believer into the likeness of Christ.

The Shorter Catechism, Question 35, asks, "What is sanctification?" and answers: "Sanctification is the work of God's free grace, whereby we are renewed in the whole man after the image of God, and are enabled more and more to die unto sin, and live unto righteousness."

Several features of this definition deserve careful attention. First, sanctification is "the work of God's free grace." Just as we cannot justify ourselves, we cannot sanctify ourselves. The transformation of our character is ultimately the work of God's Spirit within us. This does not mean we are passive — we must actively cooperate with the Spirit through the means of grace (Scripture, prayer, worship, fellowship). But the power that produces change comes from God, not from us.

Second, sanctification involves renewal "in the whole man." The fall corrupted every part of our nature — mind, will, emotions, body. Sanctification addresses every part as well. Our minds are being renewed to think God's thoughts. Our wills are being strengthened to choose God's ways. Our emotions are being purified to love what God loves. Even our bodies are being offered as living sacrifices, holy and acceptable to God.

Third, sanctification involves two simultaneous movements: dying to sin and living to righteousness. The Puritans called these "mortification" (the killing of sin) and "vivification" (the bringing to life of godliness). The Christian life is not merely about stopping bad behavior. It is about cultivating good character. As we put off the old self with its sinful practices, we put on the new self, created in the image of God in true righteousness and holiness.

Fourth, sanctification is progressive — "more and more." Unlike justification, which is an instantaneous act, sanctification is a gradual process that unfolds over the entire course of the Christian life. There will be setbacks, periods of slow growth, and even seasons of spiritual struggle. But the overall direction of the believer's life is upward — toward greater holiness, greater love, greater conformity to Christ.

The Westminster Confession, Chapter 13, Section 2, provides a realistic picture of this process: "This sanctification is throughout in the whole man, yet imperfect in this life: there abideth still some remnants of corruption in every part, whence ariseth a continual and irreconcilable war, the flesh lusting against the Spirit, and the Spirit against the flesh." The Christian life is a battlefield. The old nature wars against the new. Sinful desires resist the Spirit's work. And this war will continue until the day we die or Christ returns.

But the Confession also provides assurance: "In which war, although the remaining corruption for a time may much prevail, yet, through the continual supply of strength from the sanctifying Spirit of Christ, the regenerate part doth overcome: and so the saints grow in grace, perfecting holiness in the fear of God." The battle is real, but the outcome is certain. Through the Spirit's power, the believer will ultimately overcome. Sin will not have the final word.

This leads naturally to the doctrine of perseverance. The Westminster Confession, Chapter 17, affirms: "They whom God hath accepted in his Beloved, effectually called and sanctified by his Spirit, can neither totally nor finally fall away from the state of grace; but shall certainly persevere therein to the end, and be eternally saved." This is one of the most comforting doctrines in the Reformed faith. True believers cannot lose their salvation. Not because their grip on God is so strong, but because God's grip on them is unbreakable.

The Shorter Catechism, Question 36, confirms: "The benefits which in this life do accompany or flow from justification, adoption, and sanctification, are, assurance of God's love, peace of conscience, joy in the Holy Ghost, increase of grace, and perseverance therein to the end." Perseverance is not merely a theological proposition. It is a lived experience — the believer's daily experience of God's sustaining grace.

The doctrine of perseverance does not encourage carelessness. The Confession warns that "they may, through the temptations of Satan and of the world, the prevalency of corruption remaining in them, and the neglect of the means of their preservation, fall into grievous sins; and for a time continue therein." Believers can and do fall into serious sin. But they will not remain there permanently. God will discipline them, restore them, and bring them safely home.

--- Review Questions ---

Review Question 1: What is sanctification, and how does it differ from justification?

Review Question 2: What does the Confession mean by the "continual and irreconcilable war" between the flesh and the Spirit?

Review Question 3: What does the doctrine of perseverance teach, and why is it comforting?

--- Activity ---

Design a two-column chart titled "Mortification and Vivification." In the left column, list five specific sins that Christians are called to "put to death." In the right column, list the corresponding virtues that Christians are called to "put on." For each pair, find a Scripture reference. Then write a prayer asking God for His sanctifying grace in one area where you personally need growth.

--- Closing ---

Sanctification is the lifelong adventure of becoming who you already are in Christ. It is hard work, but it is grace-powered work. And the God who began this good work in you will carry it on to completion. Until next time, students.
