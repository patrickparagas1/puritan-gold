Lesson 7: "John Calvin — The Glory of God in All Things"
Heroes of the Reformation


Good morning. Every day is a gift from God, and every lesson is an opportunity to see His glory more clearly. Welcome to the Puritan Academy. Let us open this day in prayer.

Father, we thank You for the privilege of education. Not everyone has the opportunity we have today. Help us to steward it well, to study with excellence, and to use what we learn in service of Your kingdom. In Jesus' name. Amen.

Let us begin with a time of prayerful reading, in the Puritan tradition.

Our Scripture for today is Romans 11:36; 1 Corinthians 10:31; Psalm 19:1:

"For of him, and through him, and to him, are all things: to whom be glory for ever. Amen. Whether therefore ye eat, or drink, or whatsoever ye do, do all to the glory of God. The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork."

Consider this: the God who flung the stars into space, who carved the mountains and filled the oceans, who designed the intricate beauty of every wildflower and the vast complexity of every galaxy — this God is also the God of your breakfast table, your daily work, your evening rest. There is no corner of your life where His glory does not shine. Every moment is an opportunity for worship. Every task is a sacred calling. You were made for this — to see His glory and to live for His glory. Let the magnificence of this purpose lift you above the smallness of self-centered living and into the vast, glorious, eternally satisfying life of living for God.

As we prepare for today's lesson, remember that everything we study flows from this one fountain: the Word of God. Our lesson today is part of Heroes of the Reformation, and it is titled: John Calvin — The Glory of God in All Things. Let us proceed with minds engaged and hearts open.


It is time for our main lesson. Give this your full attention.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes in Romans 11:36; 1 Corinthians 10:31; Psalm 19:1.

The glory of God is the ultimate purpose of all creation, all providence, and all redemption. To say that God does all things for His own glory is not to accuse Him of selfishness but to affirm that He is the highest good, the most beautiful reality, and the most worthy object of all praise. When God acts for His own glory, He is acting for the greatest possible good of all His creatures, because the glory of God is the fountain from which all blessings flow.

The apostle Paul captures this truth with one of the most sweeping statements in all of Scripture in Romans 11:36: "For of him, and through him, and to him, are all things: to whom be glory for ever. Amen." Three prepositions reveal the totality of God's involvement with all things. All things are "of him" — He is the source and origin. All things are "through him" — He is the sustainer and means. All things are "to him" — He is the goal and destination. Nothing exists that did not come from God, nothing is sustained apart from God, and nothing has any ultimate purpose other than the glory of God.

This verse comes at the conclusion of Paul's three-chapter exposition of God's sovereign purposes in history (Romans 9-11), in which he has traced the doctrines of election, the hardening of Israel, the ingrafting of the Gentiles, and the future restoration of Israel. After surveying these mysteries, Paul does not attempt to explain them fully but falls to his knees in worship: "O the depth of the riches both of the wisdom and knowledge of God! how unsearchable are his judgments, and his ways past finding out!" (Romans 11:33). The appropriate response to the sovereignty of God is not intellectual mastery but humble adoration.

In 1 Corinthians 10:31, Paul brings this cosmic truth down to the most mundane level of daily life: "Whether therefore ye eat, or drink, or whatsoever ye do, do all to the glory of God." If even eating and drinking — the most basic human activities — are to be done for God's glory, then nothing in life is religiously neutral. There is no secular space, no area of life that falls outside the lordship of Christ and the purpose of glorifying God. The student at his desk, the farmer in his field, the mother with her children, the merchant in his shop — all are called to live their ordinary lives as an act of worship, directing every thought, word, and deed toward the praise of God.

The Psalmist declares in Psalm 19:1, "The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork." The created world is a theatre of God's glory. Every star, every mountain, every ocean wave, every blade of grass is a testimony to the power, wisdom, and beauty of the Creator. Calvin, more than any other reformer, grasped the comprehensive scope of this truth. He taught that the entire created order — from the vastness of the galaxies to the intricate design of a flower — is a display of God's attributes designed to lead rational creatures to worship.

The concept of the glory of God pervades the entire Bible. God delivered Israel from Egypt "for his name's sake" (Psalm 106:8). He forgives sins "for his own name's sake" (1 John 2:12). He leads His people in right paths "for his name's sake" (Psalm 23:3). Christ came to earth so that "the Father may be glorified in the Son" (John 14:13). The Holy Spirit glorifies Christ (John 16:14). And in the eternal state, the glory of God illuminates the new creation: "And the city had no need of the sun, neither of the moon, to shine in it: for the glory of God did lighten it, and the Lamb is the light thereof" (Revelation 21:23).

Isaiah 43:7 reveals that human beings were created for this purpose: "Even every one that is called by my name: for I have created him for my glory, I have formed him; yea, I have made him." Our very existence is designed for the display of God's glory. To live for any other ultimate purpose — for self, for pleasure, for money, for fame — is to violate the fundamental purpose for which we were made. It is not merely sinful; it is absurd, like a fish trying to live on dry land.

The Westminster Shorter Catechism begins with the question, "What is the chief end of man?" and answers, "Man's chief end is to glorify God, and to enjoy him forever." Notice that glorifying God and enjoying God are joined together. They are not two separate activities but one. We glorify God most when we enjoy Him most, and we enjoy Him most when we see and savor His glory. The Christian life is not a joyless duty of trying to glorify a distant God but a joyful privilege of delighting in a glorious God. True religion is not the suppression of joy but the direction of joy toward its proper object.

This understanding has radical implications for every area of life. Education exists not merely for career preparation but for the cultivation of minds that can perceive and praise the wisdom of God in all disciplines. Art exists not merely for self-expression but for the creation of beauty that reflects the beauty of the Creator. Science exists not merely for technological advancement but for the discovery of the glory of God displayed in the natural world. Family exists not merely for human companionship but for the display of the love relationship between Christ and His church. Government exists not merely for social order but for the establishment of justice that reflects the justice of God. Every human institution and activity finds its true meaning only when it is understood in relation to the glory of God.

The doctrine of God's glory also shapes our understanding of suffering. If God does all things for His glory, then our trials and afflictions are not meaningless. They serve a purpose — to refine our faith, to display God's sustaining grace, to conform us to the image of Christ, and ultimately to enhance the eternal weight of glory that awaits us (2 Corinthians 4:17). The believer who understands that God's glory is the ultimate purpose of all things can endure hardship with patience and even with joy, knowing that "all things work together for good to them that love God" (Romans 8:28) — and the ultimate "good" is the glory of God and our participation in it.

This biblical foundation is essential. Without it, everything else we study today would be like a house built on sand. But with it, we stand on solid rock.


Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. John Calvin wrote extensively on this subject in "Institutes of the Christian Religion." The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life.

John Calvin (1509-1564) was the most systematic and influential theologian of the Protestant Reformation. His "Institutes of the Christian Religion," first published in 1536 and expanded through multiple editions until its final form in 1559, remains one of the greatest works of Christian theology ever written. Calvin's central theological conviction — the one that shaped everything else in his thought — was that the glory of God is the supreme purpose of all existence and that every area of human life must be brought under the lordship of Jesus Christ.

Calvin opens the Institutes with a statement that became the foundation of all Reformed theology: "Nearly all the wisdom we possess, that is to say, true and sound wisdom, consists of two parts: the knowledge of God and of ourselves." Calvin argued that these two types of knowledge are inseparable. We cannot truly know ourselves without knowing God (because we are made in His image and can only be understood in relation to Him), and we cannot truly know God without knowing ourselves (because our awareness of our own sinfulness and finitude drives us to seek the infinite, holy God). This dual knowledge is the beginning of all true wisdom and the foundation of all genuine piety.

Calvin's doctrine of the knowledge of God begins with what he calls the sensus divinitatis — the sense of divinity that God has implanted in every human heart. No one, Calvin argues, is truly an atheist. Even those who deny God's existence feel His reality pressing upon them. The testimony of creation confirms this inner sense: "Wherever you cast your eyes, there is no spot in the universe wherein you cannot discern at least some sparks of his glory." Calvin writes that the universe is like a magnificent theatre in which God displays His perfections for all to see. The heavens, the earth, the seas, the changing seasons, the human body — all declare the glory of their Maker.

However, Calvin was equally emphatic that natural revelation, though sufficient to leave all people without excuse (Romans 1:20), is insufficient to bring sinners to saving knowledge of God. Because of the fall, the human mind is so darkened that it distorts and suppresses the knowledge of God available in creation. Therefore, God has given a clearer revelation in His written Word — Scripture — which serves as "spectacles" that enable us to read the book of creation rightly. Without Scripture, we grope in darkness even with creation spread before us. With Scripture, we see God's glory blazing from every corner of the universe.

The Institutes are organized around the knowledge of God as Creator (Book 1), as Redeemer (Book 2), the manner in which we receive the grace of Christ — the work of the Holy Spirit (Book 3), and the external means by which God invites us into the fellowship of Christ and holds us therein — the church and sacraments (Book 4). This structure reveals Calvin's comprehensive vision: theology is not a narrow, specialized discipline but a framework for understanding everything — creation, redemption, the Christian life, and the church.

Calvin's influence on the Puritan movement was immense. The Puritans were, in many respects, Calvin's spiritual descendants. They embraced his emphasis on the sovereignty of God, the authority of Scripture, the centrality of preaching, and the necessity of applying the gospel to every area of life. The Westminster Confession of Faith and Catechisms are thoroughly Calvinist in their theology, and the great Puritan pastors — Owen, Goodwin, Watson, Baxter — all acknowledged their debt to Calvin's systematic theology.

Calvin's vision of the glory of God in all things produced a distinctive approach to Christian living that deeply shaped the Puritan ethos. Calvin taught that every legitimate human vocation is a calling from God and an arena for glorifying Him. The cobbler, the farmer, the magistrate, and the mother are all serving God in their callings just as much as the pastor or the professor. This doctrine of vocation transformed the Puritan understanding of work. Work was not merely a means of earning a living but a means of serving God and displaying His glory. The Puritans brought extraordinary diligence, integrity, and craftsmanship to their work because they understood that they were working not merely for human employers but for the Lord.

Calvin's Geneva became a model of what a city might look like when the lordship of Christ is taken seriously in every sphere. Calvin established an academy for education, reformed the city's laws to reflect biblical principles, organized systematic care for the poor, and created a consistory to oversee the spiritual welfare of the community. While Calvin's Geneva was far from perfect, it represented a genuine attempt to order all of life — personal, ecclesiastical, educational, political, and economic — under the authority of God's Word for God's glory.

The Puritan vision of the glory of God shaped their approach to education as well. They founded schools and colleges — Harvard, Yale, and Princeton all began as Puritan institutions — because they believed that the cultivation of the mind was essential for understanding God's revelation in both Scripture and creation. The Puritans were among the most highly educated people of their time, and their commitment to learning was driven not by intellectual pride but by the conviction that a well-trained mind is better equipped to perceive and proclaim the glory of God.

Calvin's teaching on the glory of God also produced a distinctive approach to worship. Calvin taught that worship must be regulated by the Word of God — that we are not free to worship God in whatever way seems good to us but only in the ways He has prescribed. This "regulative principle of worship" was embraced by the Puritans and enshrined in the Westminster Confession. Its purpose was to protect the glory of God in worship by ensuring that human inventions and traditions did not displace the divinely appointed elements of preaching, prayer, singing of psalms, reading of Scripture, and administration of the sacraments.

Calvin's legacy endures wherever Christians seek to bring every thought captive to the obedience of Christ, wherever believers pursue excellence in their callings for the glory of God, wherever churches worship according to the Word, and wherever the sovereignty and majesty of God are proclaimed as the central truths of the Christian faith. The Genevan Reformer pointed the church to the truth that has been the heartbeat of Reformed and Puritan Christianity: Soli Deo Gloria — to God alone be the glory.

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.


Let us now press deeper into the theme of God's glory by examining additional Scripture passages and drawing from Calvin's broader theological legacy.

The prophet Isaiah provides one of the most direct and comprehensive statements of God's jealousy for His own glory in Isaiah 42:8: "I am the LORD: that is my name: and my glory will I not give to another, neither my praise to graven images." God's glory is His alone. He will not share it with idols, with human achievement, with religious systems, or with any creature. This is not divine selfishness — it is divine truthfulness. God alone deserves all glory because God alone is the source, sustainer, and goal of all things. To give glory to anything other than God is not generosity — it is idolatry, a fundamental distortion of reality.

Isaiah 48:11 reinforces this with striking emphasis: "For mine own sake, even for mine own sake, will I do it: for how should my name be polluted? and I will not give my glory unto another." God acts "for mine own sake" — His own glory is the ultimate motive of all His actions. This includes His acts of creation (He made the world for His glory — Psalm 19:1), His acts of providence (He governs all things for His glory — Proverbs 16:4), His acts of judgment (He punishes sin for His glory — Romans 9:22-23), and His acts of redemption (He saves sinners for His glory — Ephesians 1:6, 12, 14). There is no act of God that does not ultimately serve the display and magnification of His own infinite perfections.

The apostle Paul, in his letter to the Colossians, provides a Christ-centered statement of this truth that is breathtaking in its scope. In Colossians 1:16-17, he writes of Christ: "For by him were all things created, that are in heaven, and that are in earth, visible and invisible, whether they be thrones, or dominions, or principalities, or powers: all things were created by him, and for him: And he is before all things, and by him all things consist." All things were created "by him" (He is the agent of creation) and "for him" (He is the purpose of creation). The universe exists for Christ's glory. Every star, every atom, every living creature exists to display the power, wisdom, and beauty of the eternal Son of God. And "by him all things consist" — He holds the universe together moment by moment by the word of His power.

Calvin's personal conversion experience shaped his understanding of God's glory. In the preface to his Commentary on the Psalms, Calvin describes his own conversion as a sudden, unexpected turning by which God "subdued and brought my mind to a teachable frame." Calvin had been devoted to the superstitions of the papacy and was deeply resistant to the Reformed faith. But God, Calvin writes, "by a sudden conversion subdued and brought my mind to a teachable frame, which was more hardened in such matters than might have been expected from one at my early period of life." Calvin's conversion was not the result of his own seeking but of God's sovereign action upon his heart — a personal experience of the irresistible grace he would later teach so powerfully.

This experience produced in Calvin a lifelong conviction that the glory of God is the only worthy purpose for human existence. Calvin's personal motto, which appears on many of his works, was a hand holding out a heart to God with the inscription: "My heart I give you, Lord, promptly and sincerely." This motto captures the essence of Calvin's piety: a whole-hearted, unreserved devotion to the glory of God in every area of life.

Calvin's doctrine of providence is intimately connected to his doctrine of God's glory. In the Institutes, Book 1, Chapters 16-18, Calvin teaches that God governs every event in the universe — from the fall of a sparrow to the rise and fall of empires — according to His own wise and sovereign counsel. Nothing happens by chance. Nothing is outside God's control. Even the sinful actions of human beings, while they are genuinely sinful and the actors are justly held responsible, serve God's ultimate purposes and contribute to the display of His glory. Calvin cites the story of Joseph, who said to his brothers, "Ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good" (Genesis 50:20) — the same act that was sinful from the brothers' perspective was purposeful from God's perspective.

This doctrine of meticulous providence gave Calvin and the Puritans extraordinary courage and composure in the face of adversity. If God governs all things for His glory, then no suffering is meaningless, no trial is wasted, and no situation is beyond redemption. Calvin himself faced enormous opposition — exile from Geneva in 1538, the death of his beloved wife Idelette in 1549, chronic illness throughout much of his life, and relentless attacks from both Catholic and Protestant opponents. Yet through it all, Calvin maintained a serene confidence that God was working all things for His glory and for the good of His people.

The Puritan divine Jonathan Edwards (1703-1758), though writing more than a century after Calvin, stands as the greatest theological heir of Calvin's vision of God's glory. In his treatise "The End for Which God Created the World," Edwards argues with philosophical rigor and poetic beauty that the ultimate purpose of creation is the display of God's glory and the communication of His goodness to His creatures. Edwards contends that God's glory and human happiness are not competing goals but converging ones — God is most glorified in us when we are most satisfied in Him. This insight, deeply Calvinist in its origin, provides the foundation for a vision of the Christian life that is at once supremely God-centered and genuinely joyful.

Edwards further argues that the glory of God is not a static attribute but a dynamic, overflowing fullness that God delights to share with His creatures. God created the world not because He needed anything — He is perfectly self-sufficient in His triune being — but because His goodness naturally overflows, like a fountain that pours forth water not because it lacks anything but because it is full. The creation is, as it were, the overflow of God's internal glory made external — a display of His perfections that invites rational creatures to behold, admire, and enjoy Him forever.

The Westminster Larger Catechism, Question 2, asks: "How doth it appear that there is a God?" The answer begins: "The very light of nature in man, and the works of God, declare plainly that there is a God." Calvin's teaching about the sensus divinitatis — the implanted sense of God in every human heart — and about the creation as a theatre of God's glory underlies this catechism answer. The evidence for God's existence is not hidden or obscure — it is blazingly obvious in the creation and in the human conscience. The problem is not a lack of evidence but a suppression of evidence by sinful hearts that do not want to acknowledge the God to whom they are accountable.


Before we move to our discussion questions, let us apply Calvin's vision of God's glory to our daily lives.

First, the doctrine of God's glory should transform the way you approach your daily work and studies. If everything is to be done for the glory of God (1 Corinthians 10:31), then there is no such thing as a menial or unimportant task. The student who studies diligently is glorifying God. The child who obeys his parents cheerfully is glorifying God. The worker who performs his tasks with integrity and excellence is glorifying God. Calvin's doctrine of vocation teaches that every legitimate calling is sacred when it is pursued as service to God.

Second, this doctrine should elevate your worship. When you come to worship, you are entering the presence of the God whose glory fills heaven and earth. Worship should not be casual, careless, or self-centered. It should be reverent, joyful, and God-centered — focused not on what you get out of it but on what God deserves to receive. Calvin's regulative principle of worship — that we should worship God only in the ways He has prescribed in His Word — protects the glory of God in worship by ensuring that human inventions do not displace divine ordinances.

Third, the glory of God should shape your response to suffering. If God works all things for His glory and for the good of His people, then even your most painful experiences have purpose and meaning. Suffering is not random. It is governed by a wise, loving, sovereign God who uses it to refine your faith, display His sustaining grace, and prepare you for an eternal weight of glory that far surpasses anything you endure in this life.

Fourth, Calvin's vision should produce in you a hunger to see God's glory in every area of life — in science, in art, in literature, in nature, in relationships, in work, in rest. Train your eyes to see what the Psalmist saw: "The heavens declare the glory of God." Train your heart to respond as Paul responded: "O the depth of the riches both of the wisdom and knowledge of God!" And let the Westminster Catechism's answer become your personal mission statement: your chief end is to glorify God and enjoy Him forever.

Fifth, let this doctrine guard you against the subtle idolatry of living for human approval. The Puritan Thomas Brooks, in his work "Precious Remedies Against Satan's Devices," warns that the desire for man's praise is one of the most dangerous snares the devil sets for the souls of believers. When we live for the glory of God, we are freed from the tyranny of human opinion. We no longer need the applause of the crowd because we have the approval of the King. As Paul writes in Galatians 1:10, "For do I now persuade men, or God? or do I seek to please men? for if I yet pleased men, I should not be the servant of Christ." Calvin himself experienced the sting of relentless criticism from his opponents, yet he pressed forward in his calling because his ultimate audience was not the citizens of Geneva but the God of heaven. Brooks observes that "the desire of man's glory is a gilded misery, a secret poison, a hidden plague — it is the most dangerous engineer of the soul." When you are tempted to seek the praise of men rather than the glory of God, remember that every human opinion is as fleeting as the morning mist, but the glory of God endures forever.

Sixth, the doctrine of God's glory should shape the way you think about eternity. The Puritan Richard Sibbes, in "The Bruised Reed," writes that the believer's ultimate destination is not merely heaven as a place but the full, unhindered vision of God's glory face to face. Sibbes observes that every experience of God's beauty in this life — every sunset that takes your breath away, every passage of Scripture that pierces your heart, every moment of worship when the Spirit lifts your soul — is merely a foretaste, a glimpse through a glass darkly, of the infinite glory that awaits you in eternity. Paul declares this hope in 2 Corinthians 3:18: "But we all, with open face beholding as in a glass the glory of the Lord, are changed into the same image from glory to glory, even as by the Spirit of the Lord." The Christian life is a journey from glory to glory — from the first faint glimpse of God's beauty at conversion to the ever-increasing brilliance of His presence in sanctification, and finally to the full, unclouded, eternal vision of His glory in the life to come. Let this hope sustain you in every trial, comfort you in every sorrow, and motivate you in every task. You were made for glory — not your own, but His. And one day, you will see it in full.


Discussion time. Engage with these questions carefully and honestly.

Discussion Question 1: Romans 11:36 says all things are 'of him, and through him, and to him.' How does this comprehensive statement shape our understanding of God's relationship to all of reality?

Discussion Question 2: Calvin taught that the universe is a 'theatre' of God's glory. What does this mean practically for how we approach subjects like science, art, and history?

Discussion Question 3: How does 1 Corinthians 10:31 transform our understanding of everyday activities like eating, working, and studying? What changes when we do everything 'to the glory of God'?

Discussion Question 4: Calvin argued that natural revelation is insufficient for saving knowledge of God and that we need Scripture as 'spectacles.' Why is creation alone not enough to bring sinners to salvation?

Discussion Question 5: The Westminster Catechism says our chief end is 'to glorify God and enjoy him forever.' How are glorifying God and enjoying God related? Can we do one without the other?

Take time with each question. The Puritans valued what they called "holy conference," the practice of discussing spiritual truths together. Do not settle for quick, surface answers. Push deeper. Ask follow-up questions. Listen carefully to one another.

Application is where truth becomes life. It is not enough to know what Romans 11:36; 1 Corinthians 10:31; Psalm 19:1 says. We must ask: how does this change the way I live today? How does this shape my thoughts, my words, my actions? The Puritans insisted that a truth that does not transform is a truth that has not been truly understood.


The best learning happens when we do something with what we have heard. Here is your activity.

Students will apply Calvin's vision of the glory of God to their own academic studies by creating a 'Soli Deo Gloria Curriculum Map.' Each student will take the subjects they are currently studying (Bible, history, literature, science, mathematics, art, music) and write a one-paragraph statement for each subject explaining how that discipline displays the glory of God. For example: 'Mathematics reveals the orderly, rational, and beautiful mind of God — the precision of geometry reflects His perfect design, the reliability of numbers reflects His faithfulness, and the infinite nature of some mathematical concepts points to His boundless wisdom.' After writing their paragraphs, students will present them to the class and discuss how a God-centered education differs from a secular one.

Materials needed: Lined paper or journals, list of current subjects for reference, Calvin's Institutes Book 1 Chapter 5 excerpts on creation as theatre of glory, presentation cards for classroom sharing, whiteboard for recording key themes across subjects

For advanced students, extend the curriculum map by adding a second paragraph to each subject explaining how a secular worldview approaches that same discipline differently from a God-centered worldview. For example, under Science, explain how a secular approach sees scientific discovery as an end in itself — knowledge for the sake of knowledge — while a God-centered approach sees scientific discovery as a means of perceiving and praising the wisdom of God displayed in creation. Under History, explain how a secular approach may see history as a random sequence of events driven by impersonal forces, while a God-centered approach sees history as the unfolding of God's sovereign purposes moving toward a divinely appointed goal. Under Literature, explain how a secular approach evaluates works of literature by purely aesthetic or political criteria, while a God-centered approach evaluates them by how truthfully they portray the human condition, the nature of good and evil, and the need for redemption.

This comparative exercise will sharpen your ability to think critically about the worldview assumptions that underlie every academic discipline. The Puritans understood that education is never neutral — it either moves the student toward God or away from Him. A curriculum map built on the principle of Soli Deo Gloria ensures that every subject points the student toward the glory of God and equips them to engage the world as disciples of Christ.

Students should conclude the activity by writing a brief personal statement — one or two paragraphs — explaining what it means to them personally to pursue all their studies for the glory of God. How does this purpose differ from studying merely for grades, career advancement, or personal fulfillment? How does it change your motivation, your diligence, and your attitude toward difficult subjects?

As you complete this activity, think about how it connects to what you studied in today's lesson. The goal is not just to do something, but to reinforce the truths you have been learning. The Puritans believed that education should engage the whole person: mind, heart, and hands.

Take your time with this activity. Quality matters more than speed. When you are finished, reflect on what you learned through the process of doing. Often we understand something far better after we have tried to put it into practice.


One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

John Calvin transformed Geneva, Switzerland from a politically unstable city into one of the most well-ordered and influential cities in Europe. Study the social, political, and educational reforms Calvin implemented: the founding of the Geneva Academy (1559), the consistory system for church discipline, provision for the poor, and the training of pastors who were sent throughout Europe. Compare Calvin's Geneva with other attempts to build a 'godly commonwealth,' such as the Puritan colonies in New England. What were the strengths and limitations of these efforts? Calvin's Geneva Academy, founded in 1559 under the leadership of Theodore Beza, became one of the most important educational institutions in the Protestant world. Students came from across Europe to study theology, classical languages, and the liberal arts under Reformed professors. Many of these students returned to their home countries as pastors, teachers, and leaders of Reformed movements — including John Knox, who brought Calvin's vision back to Scotland. The Academy was Calvin's lasting institutional legacy, and its influence on the spread of Reformed Christianity throughout Europe and eventually to the New World was immeasurable. Study also how Calvin's consistory — a body of pastors and elders responsible for church discipline — functioned as a mechanism for maintaining both doctrinal purity and moral accountability in the Genevan church. Compare this system with the elder-governed church structures that the Puritans later established in New England. How did Calvin's model of church governance influence the development of representative government and democratic institutions in the Western world?

Literature Connection:

The Institutes of the Christian Religion is not only a theological masterpiece but a literary one. Calvin's Latin and French prose is marked by clarity, elegance, and persuasive power. He draws on classical rhetoric, vivid metaphor, and biblical allusion throughout. Analyze Calvin's use of the 'spectacles' metaphor for Scripture and the 'theatre' metaphor for creation. How do these extended metaphors function as pedagogical tools? Compare Calvin's prose style with the more ornate style of his contemporaries. What are the advantages of clarity in theological writing?

Science Connection:

Calvin's teaching that the natural world is a display of God's glory provided a theological foundation for the scientific enterprise. Many early modern scientists — including Kepler, Boyle, and Newton — saw their work as 'thinking God's thoughts after Him.' Study how the Reformed conviction that God created an orderly, rational universe encouraged empirical investigation. Discuss the relationship between the doctrine of creation and the foundational assumptions of science: that the universe is orderly, that natural laws are consistent, and that the human mind is capable of understanding the natural world. These assumptions — which are essential for science to function — are difficult to justify within a purely materialistic worldview. Why should a universe that arose by chance be orderly? Why should natural laws be consistent across time and space? Why should the human brain, which evolved for survival rather than truth-seeking, be capable of understanding the deep structure of reality? The Christian doctrine of creation provides a rational foundation for all of these assumptions: the universe is orderly because it was designed by a rational God. Natural laws are consistent because they reflect the faithful character of a God who does not change. And the human mind can understand the natural world because both were made by the same Creator — we are, as Kepler said, "thinking God's thoughts after Him." Calvin's doctrine of creation as a theatre of God's glory thus provides not merely a theological motivation for scientific inquiry but an intellectual justification for its foundational assumptions.

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.

Additional Memory Verse:

"For of him, and through him, and to him, are all things: to whom be glory for ever. Amen." -- Romans 11:36

This verse is the most comprehensive single statement of God's relationship to all reality in the entire Bible. Three prepositions tell the whole story: all things are "of him" (He is the source), "through him" (He is the sustainer), and "to him" (He is the goal). Everything that exists came from God, is maintained by God, and is heading toward God. When you memorize this verse, you are memorizing the fundamental framework of reality itself — the answer to the question "Why does anything exist?" Everything exists for the glory of God. Let this verse reshape your entire worldview. There is no fact, no event, no experience that falls outside its scope. Everything — from the greatest galaxy to the smallest atom, from the most triumphant victory to the deepest suffering — is "of him, and through him, and to him." Soli Deo gloria.

Supplemental Reading Assignment:

For students who wish to go deeper, read the first chapter of Calvin's Institutes of the Christian Religion, where he discusses the dual knowledge of God and of ourselves. Calvin argues that we cannot truly know ourselves without knowing God (because we are made in His image) and we cannot truly know God without knowing ourselves (because our sinfulness drives us to seek His grace). This is one of the most profound opening statements in the history of theology, and it sets the framework for everything that follows in the Institutes. As you read, consider how Calvin's insight applies to your own experience: has your growing knowledge of God's glory deepened your understanding of your own sinfulness? And has your growing awareness of your own weakness increased your appreciation of God's grace? The two forms of knowledge — knowledge of God and knowledge of self — grow together and reinforce each other throughout the Christian life.
