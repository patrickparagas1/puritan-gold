PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"Whom Have I in Heaven but Thee?"
May 30, 2026

---

"Nevertheless I am continually with thee: thou hast holden me by my right hand. Thou shalt guide me with thy counsel, and afterward receive me to glory. Whom have I in heaven but thee? and there is none upon earth that I desire beside thee. My flesh and my heart faileth: but God is the strength of my heart, and my portion for ever." -- Psalm 73:23-28

We return today to the seventy-third psalm, but we come now to its summit, to those closing verses that rise above the turmoil and the doubt and the near-catastrophe of the earlier portions and arrive at a confession of faith so pure, so absolute, so all-consuming, that it stands as one of the highest peaks in the entire range of Old Testament devotion. Asaph began this psalm with his feet almost slipping, his faith almost failing, his heart almost overwhelmed by the apparent injustice of God's governance of the world. But he ends it here, with his feet on the rock, his faith soaring, and his heart overflowing with a love for God that has been tested in the fires of doubt and found to be genuine gold.

"Nevertheless I am continually with thee." This is the word that changes everything. Nevertheless. Despite my doubts. Despite my near-fall. Despite my foolish envy of the wicked and my bitter questioning of God's ways. Despite all of that, I am continually with thee. The relationship has not been broken. The covenant has not been dissolved. The bond between God and His child has held firm through the storm, and now, on the other side of the tempest, Asaph discovers that he is still in the grip of the Almighty.

"Thou hast holden me by my right hand." It was not Asaph who held on to God. It was God who held on to Asaph. This is one of the most comforting truths in all of Reformed theology. The perseverance of the saints is not grounded in the strength of the saints but in the faithfulness of God. If our continued relationship with God depended upon our ability to hold on to Him, we would all have been lost long ago. Our grip is feeble. Our faith wavers. Our love grows cold. Our devotion falters. But God's grip never loosens. His faithfulness never wavers. His love never grows cold. He has holden us by the right hand, and He will not let go.

"Thou shalt guide me with thy counsel, and afterward receive me to glory." Here is the entire trajectory of the believer's life, compressed into a single verse. Guidance now. Glory afterward. The path between the two may be long and winding, dark and difficult, filled with valleys and shadowed by mountains. But the beginning is certain, for God guides, and the ending is certain, for God receives into glory. The believer who walks by the counsel of God will arrive at the glory of God. There is no other possible outcome. The Shepherd who leads will bring His sheep safely home.

And now comes the great question, the question that is really not a question at all but a confession, a declaration, a triumphant shout of faith: "Whom have I in heaven but thee?" Asaph searches the highest heavens, that realm of perfect blessedness where angels worship and the saints rest from their labours, and he finds that even there, in the very paradise of God, there is nothing and no one that he desires apart from God Himself. Heaven without God would be no heaven at all. The streets of gold, the gates of pearl, the river of life, the tree of life, all of these are precious, but they are precious only because of the One who fills them with His presence. Take God out of heaven, and you have taken everything out of heaven.

"And there is none upon earth that I desire beside thee." Having searched the heavens, Asaph now searches the earth, and the verdict is the same. There is nothing in all the created world, no person, no pleasure, no possession, no achievement, no experience, that can satisfy the soul that has tasted the goodness of God. The man who has God has everything. The man who has everything except God has nothing. This is the arithmetic of the kingdom, and it is foolishness to the world but wisdom to the children of God.

John Owen, in his great work on communion with God, wrote that the enjoyment of God is the only happiness that can satisfy the soul of man. The enjoyment of other things may produce a kind of contentment for a time, but it is a contentment that always has an edge of unease, a whisper of insufficiency, a nagging awareness that something is still missing. Only when the soul rests in God, and in God alone, does it find that the restlessness is gone, the hunger is satisfied, the thirst is quenched, and the deepest longing of the human heart is at last fulfilled.

"My flesh and my heart faileth: but God is the strength of my heart, and my portion for ever." Here is the final and most searching test. The flesh fails. The body grows old, grows weak, grows sick, and at last grows cold in the embrace of death. The heart fails. The emotional and intellectual powers that once burned bright grow dim, falter, and give way. Everything that is natural, everything that is human, everything that belongs to this present world, fails in the end. But God does not fail. He is the strength of the failing heart. He is the portion of the failing flesh. And He is the portion not merely for a season, not merely for the years of this present life, but forever. When the flesh has returned to dust and the heart has beaten its last, God will still be the portion of His people, and they will dwell in His presence for all eternity.

"But it is good for me to draw near to God: I have put my trust in the Lord God, that I may declare all thy works." Let this be the settled conclusion of every believing heart. It is good to draw near to God. Not merely permissible, not merely advisable, but good, deeply and thoroughly and eternally good. And the man who draws near to God will never be disappointed, for he will find there a portion that nothing can diminish, a strength that nothing can exhaust, and a glory that nothing can fade. Amen.
