PURITAN GOLD -- Family Devotional, Day 6
"Speaking Life in the Home"
William Gouge — Domestical Duties


Good evening, dear family. Welcome back to Puritan Gold. Tonight we look at what William Gouge teaches about the words we speak to one another within the household. Few things shape a home more than the way its members talk to each other. Let us open our hearts to the Lord.

Let us pray. Heavenly Father, set a watch over our mouths tonight. Let the words of our mouths and the meditations of our hearts be acceptable in Your sight, O Lord, our strength and our redeemer. Teach us to speak life and grace within these walls. In Christ's name, Amen.

Our Scripture reading this evening comes from Ephesians 4:29-32. Let us hear the Word of the Lord:

"Let no corrupt communication proceed out of your mouth, but that which is good to the use of edifying, that it may minister grace unto the hearers. And grieve not the holy Spirit of God, whereby ye are sealed unto the day of redemption. Let all bitterness, and wrath, and anger, and clamour, and evil speaking, be put away from you, with all malice: And be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, even as God for Christ's sake hath forgiven you."

That is Ephesians 4:29-32. Let those words settle in your heart for a moment before we continue.


William Gouge understood that the health of a household can be measured by the words spoken within its walls. In "Of Domesticall Duties," he devotes careful attention to the manner of speech that should characterize every member of the Christian home. His teaching on this subject is both convicting and practical.

Gouge begins with the principle that all communication within the family should edify — that is, build up. Paul's command in Ephesians 4:29 is sweeping in its scope: "Let no corrupt communication proceed out of your mouth, but that which is good to the use of edifying, that it may minister grace unto the hearers." Every word spoken in the home should either instruct, encourage, comfort, or correct. Words that tear down — harsh criticism, bitter sarcasm, cutting remarks, angry outbursts — have no place in the Christian household. Such words grieve the Holy Spirit and wound the souls of those we are called to love.

Gouge addresses husbands first. He warns against the husband who speaks to his wife with contempt, who belittles her opinions, who corrects her publicly, or who uses harsh language when displeased. Such speech, Gouge argues, destroys the trust and intimacy that marriage is meant to produce. A husband's words should be like a stream of living water in the home — refreshing, nourishing, life-giving. He should praise his wife, encourage her labors, speak well of her before others, and express his love not only in actions but in words. Gouge notes that many men are generous with words of affirmation to strangers and colleagues but stingy with them toward their own wives, and this is a grave injustice.

To wives, Gouge counsels speech marked by respect and wisdom. The Proverbs 31 woman "openeth her mouth with wisdom; and in her tongue is the law of kindness." A wife's words carry enormous power in the home. She can build her husband's confidence or destroy it. She can create an atmosphere of peace or an atmosphere of strife. She can encourage her children or crush their spirits. Gouge urges wives to speak with kindness, to offer counsel with humility, and to avoid the nagging and contentious speech that Proverbs warns against repeatedly.

To parents, Gouge's counsel is especially practical. He insists that parents must speak to their children with both firmness and tenderness. Correction should be clear and direct, but never cruel. Instruction should be patient and repeated as often as necessary. And praise should be given generously when children do well. Gouge observes that many parents are quick to point out their children's failures but slow to acknowledge their successes, and this imbalance produces discouragement rather than growth.

Paul's command to "be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another" gives us the three marks of household speech: kindness, tenderheartedness, and forgiveness. Kindness means choosing words that are gentle and considerate. Tenderheartedness means being sensitive to the feelings and struggles of family members — speaking with awareness that every person in the home carries burdens we may not fully see. And forgiveness means refusing to hold past offenses over one another, refusing to bring up old wounds in new arguments, and extending to each other the same grace that God has extended to us in Christ.

Gouge writes that the family that practices these three habits of speech will find their home transformed. Where kindness reigns, bitterness cannot take root. Where tenderheartedness prevails, anger loses its power. Where forgiveness flows freely, old wounds heal and new trust grows. The Christian home should be the safest place on earth — a place where every member knows they will be spoken to with love, listened to with patience, and forgiven with grace.

Tonight, let each of us examine our own speech. Are our words building up or tearing down? Are we speaking life or sowing discord? The tongue is a small member, James tells us, but it can set a whole forest ablaze — or it can water a garden and make it flourish.


Let us pause and talk as a family. Here are tonight's discussion questions. Everyone gets a turn to share.

Question 1: Paul says our words should "minister grace unto the hearers." What does it feel like when someone speaks grace-filled words to you? Can you give an example?

Question 2: Gouge says many people are kinder with their words to strangers than to their own family. Why do you think this happens? How can we guard against it?

Question 3: What is one change each of us could make in the way we speak to one another this week?

Take a few minutes to talk about these questions together. Let everyone share, and listen to one another with patience and love.


Now, here is our memory verse for today. Let us say it together:

"Let no corrupt communication proceed out of your mouth, but that which is good to the use of edifying, that it may minister grace unto the hearers." -- Ephesians 4:29

Try to say it out loud three times right now. Then, before bed tonight, see if you can say it from memory.


Let us pray together. Lord, forgive us for the careless and hurtful words we have spoken in this home. Cleanse our lips and renew our hearts. Help us to speak only what is kind, true, and edifying. Let our home be a place where every member is built up by the words they hear. Make us a family that speaks life. In Jesus' name, Amen.

Sleep well tonight, knowing that God watches over your family.
