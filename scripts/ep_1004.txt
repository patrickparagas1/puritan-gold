PURITAN GOLD -- Study Topic: The Book of Sirach, Part 4 of 10
"The Lowly Heart Before God"
Sirach Chapters 4–5 — Walking in Humility and Patient Trust

---

Good evening, and welcome back to Puritan Gold. We continue our journey through the Book of Sirach, and tonight we arrive at a subject that lies at the very heart of the Christian life — humility. Chapters 4 and 5 of Sirach contain some of Ben Sira's most penetrating counsel on lowliness of heart, patience under trial, and the deadly danger of presumption. If the fear of the Lord is wisdom's crown, then humility is its garment — the visible clothing of the soul that walks rightly before God.

Let us read from Sirach chapter 3, verses 17 through 20, and then from chapters 4 and 5:

"My son, go on with thy business in meekness; so shalt thou be beloved of him that is approved. The greater thou art, the more humble thyself, and thou shalt find favour before the Lord. Many are in high place, and of renown: but mysteries are revealed unto the meek. For the power of the Lord is great, and he is honoured of the lowly."

And from Sirach 4:1-10:

"My son, defraud not the poor of his living, and make not the needy eyes to wait long. Make not an hungry soul sorrowful; neither provoke a man in his distress. Add not more trouble to an heart that is vexed; and defer not to give to him that is in need. Reject not the supplication of the afflicted; neither turn away thy face from a poor man. Turn not away thine eye from the needy, and give him none occasion to curse thee. For if he curse thee in the bitterness of his soul, his prayer shall be heard of him that made him. Get thyself the love of the congregation, and bow thy head to a great man. Let it not grieve thee to bow down thine ear to the poor, and give him a friendly answer with meekness. Deliver him that suffereth wrong from the hand of the oppressor; and be not fainthearted when thou sittest in judgment."

--- Teaching ---

The world of Ben Sira, like our own, was a world that rewarded the proud. In ancient Jerusalem, wealth, lineage, and political connection determined a man's standing. The wise teacher who ran his house of learning saw daily how ambition and self-promotion corrupted even promising students. And so he returned again and again to the countercultural virtue of humility.

"The greater thou art, the more humble thyself." This is a maxim that runs against every instinct of fallen human nature. The natural man reasons: the greater I become, the more I deserve to be served, the more I may demand deference from others. But the wisdom of God inverts this reasoning entirely. Our Lord Jesus Christ Himself taught this very principle: "Whosoever will be great among you, let him be your minister; And whosoever will be chief among you, let him be your servant: Even as the Son of man came not to be ministered unto, but to minister, and to give his life a ransom for many" (Matthew 20:26-28).

Ben Sira's counsel on humility is not abstract philosophy. It immediately becomes practical and concrete. The humble person does not defraud the poor or make the needy wait. The humble person does not add trouble to a vexed heart or turn away from a beggar's eyes. Humility, in Sirach's moral vision, is not merely a feeling of self-deprecation. It is an active posture of service toward those who are lower, weaker, and more vulnerable. True lowliness bows not only before God but before the least of God's creatures.

This connection between humility and care for the poor is deeply biblical. The Psalmist declares: "He raiseth up the poor out of the dust, and lifteth the needy out of the dunghill" (Psalm 113:7). Proverbs 14:31 warns: "He that oppresseth the poor reproacheth his Maker: but he that honoureth him hath mercy on the poor." And our Lord Himself identified so closely with the poor that He could say: "Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me" (Matthew 25:40). Ben Sira understood what the Puritans would later call "practical divinity" — theology that bears fruit in how we treat the person standing in front of us.

The Puritan Richard Sibbes wrote beautifully about humility in his classic work The Bruised Reed: "A humble soul is ambitious, but it is to be more holy, not to be more honored." Sibbes understood that humility is not the absence of ambition but its redirection. The humble man still strives, still labors, still exerts himself — but toward holiness rather than human recognition. Ben Sira would have agreed. He is not counseling laziness or passivity. He is counseling the redirection of the heart's energy from self-advancement to the service of God and neighbor.

Chapter 4 continues with a remarkable passage in which Wisdom herself speaks, promising to test those who follow her: "For at the first she will walk with him by crooked ways, and bring fear and dread upon him, and torment him with her discipline, until she may trust his soul, and try him by her laws. Then will she return the straight way unto him, and comfort him, and shew him her secrets."

Here is a theme we encountered in chapter 2 — the testing and refining of the faithful. Wisdom does not immediately reveal her treasures. She first leads the student through difficulty and discipline. She tests his patience, his faithfulness, his willingness to endure hardship for the sake of learning. Only after he has been proved does she open the treasury of her secrets.

The Puritans knew this experience well. John Bunyan's Pilgrim's Progress is, in many ways, a long meditation on this very principle. Christian does not walk a straight, easy road to the Celestial City. He passes through the Slough of Despond, the Valley of the Shadow of Death, Vanity Fair, and Doubting Castle. At every stage, wisdom — which is to say, divine providence — tests his soul. And at every stage, the humble pilgrim who endures is rewarded with deeper knowledge of God.

In chapter 5, Ben Sira turns to a specific form of pride that is particularly dangerous: presumption. "Set not thy heart upon thy goods, and say not, I have enough for my life. Follow not thine own mind and thy strength, to walk in the ways of thy heart: And say not, Who shall control me for my works? for the Lord will surely revenge thy pride."

Presumption is the sin of assuming that we are secure in our own strength. It is the rich fool of our Lord's parable, who said to his soul: "Soul, thou hast much goods laid up for many years; take thine ease, eat, drink, and be merry" (Luke 12:19). That very night, his soul was required of him. Ben Sira warns against this same folly. The man who says "I have enough" and "Who shall control me?" has forgotten that every breath he draws is a gift from the Almighty.

Ben Sira also addresses the presumption of delayed repentance: "Say not, I have sinned, and what harm hath happened unto me? for the Lord is longsuffering, he will in no wise let thee go. Concerning propitiation, be not without fear to add sin unto sin: And say not, His mercy is great; he will be pacified for the multitude of my sins." This is a warning against what the Puritans called "abusing the mercy of God." Thomas Watson devoted an entire chapter in The Doctrine of Repentance to this deadly error — the reasoning that says, "God is merciful, therefore I may sin freely." Watson called this "drawing poison from the sweetest flower." God's mercy is not a license to sin. It is an invitation to repent.

The chapter concludes with wisdom about speech: "Be swift to hear; and let thy life be sincere; and with patience give answer. If thou hast understanding, answer thy neighbour; if not, lay thy hand upon thy mouth. Honour and shame is in talk: and the tongue of man is his fall."

Here Ben Sira anticipates the counsel of the Apostle James: "Wherefore, my beloved brethren, let every man be swift to hear, slow to speak, slow to wrath" (James 1:19). The humble person listens before speaking. He does not rush to display his knowledge or his opinions. If he has understanding, he shares it. If not, he has the wisdom to remain silent. In a world of constant chatter, where every person feels entitled to broadcast every thought, this ancient counsel rings with startling relevance.

The common thread binding these chapters together is this: humility is the soil in which every other virtue grows. Without it, generosity becomes display. Without it, learning becomes arrogance. Without it, even religion becomes the worst form of pride — the Pharisee's prayer: "God, I thank thee, that I am not as other men." But with humility, the soul becomes fertile ground for the fear of the Lord, for patience, for mercy, for genuine wisdom.

--- Application ---

1. Practice downward service this week. Find someone who is lower than you in status, wealth, or strength, and serve them without announcement or expectation of return. Visit the sick. Listen to the lonely. Give to the poor. Let humility take the form of action.

2. Examine your speech. Are you swift to hear and slow to speak? Or do you rush to fill every silence with your own voice? This week, practice the discipline of listening. In at least one conversation each day, let the other person speak twice as long as you do.

3. Beware the presumption of delayed repentance. If there is a sin you have been tolerating because "God is merciful," repent of it today. Do not add sin unto sin. The mercy of God is meant to lead you to repentance, not to embolden you in transgression.

4. Memorize Sirach's golden maxim: "The greater thou art, the more humble thyself, and thou shalt find favour before the Lord." Let it challenge your ambition and reshape your understanding of true greatness.

--- Closing ---

Let us pray. O God of all grace, who resisteth the proud but giveth grace unto the humble, we confess our love of self-promotion and our impatience with lowly service. Strip from us the garments of pride and clothe us in the meekness of Christ, who, being in the form of God, thought it not robbery to be equal with God, but made himself of no reputation, and took upon him the form of a servant. Teach us to bow before Thee and before our neighbor, that in lowliness we may find the treasures of Thy wisdom. Through the same Jesus Christ, who humbled Himself unto death, even the death of the cross. Amen.

Go in peace, dear friend. Next time, we will turn to Sirach chapter 6 and the beautiful, searching subject of true friendship. Until then, walk humbly with your God, and He shall lift you up in due time.
