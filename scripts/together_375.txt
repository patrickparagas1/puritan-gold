PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 14
"I Sleep, but My Heart Waketh"
Song of Solomon 4:16-5:1 Review and Chapter 4 Summary — Guarding the Garden Together


Welcome, dear couple. Tonight we pause to gather the riches of this second week. We have journeyed through the bride's nighttime search, Solomon's glorious wedding procession, the bridegroom's detailed praise of his bride, the invitation to leave and cleave, the enclosed garden, and the divine blessing over marital intimacy. Tonight, let us draw these threads together with a passage that bridges what we have studied and what lies ahead.

Song of Solomon 4:16 and 5:1 (review):

"Awake, O north wind; and come, thou south; blow upon my garden, that the spices thereof may flow out. Let my beloved come into his garden, and eat his pleasant fruits. I am come into my garden, my sister, my spouse: I have gathered my myrrh with my spice; I have eaten my honeycomb with my honey; I have drunk my wine with my milk: eat, O friends; drink, yea, drink abundantly, O beloved."


This week has taken us deep into the heart of what the Puritans called "the mystery of conjugal love" — the profound, multidimensional reality of the marriage bond. Let us gather the wisdom of this week into a unified vision.

First, we learned from the bride's nighttime search that disconnection is inevitable but not permanent. Every marriage will have nights when the beloved seems absent. The question is whether you will rise and seek, or lie still and suffer. Richard Baxter wrote that the greatest enemy of marriage is not conflict but passivity — the refusal to pursue reconciliation when distance has set in. The bride teaches us to rise, to search, and to hold on when we find each other again.

Second, we learned from Solomon's wedding procession that the marriage covenant is both public and private. The chariot was gilded on the outside but "paved with love" on the inside. Your marriage is a testimony to the watching world, but its most important reality is invisible — the love that fills the interior when no one else is looking. William Gouge urged couples to tend the inside of their marriage with even more care than the outside.

Third, we learned from the bridegroom's detailed praise that specific admiration is the lifeblood of a thriving marriage. He did not offer generic flattery but particular, personal, attentive celebration of his bride's unique beauty. Thomas Gataker wrote that every spouse is a text to be studied, a landscape to be explored, a treasure to be discovered — not once but continually, throughout the whole of life.

Fourth, we learned from the invitation to "come with me from Lebanon" that marriage requires leaving old attachments in order to cleave fully to each other. The lions and leopards of Lebanon — old loyalties, family patterns, competing allegiances — must be left behind so that the new life of covenant love can flourish.

Fifth, we learned from the enclosed garden that the privacy of marriage is not a restriction but the very condition for abundance. When the walls are secure and the gate is guarded, everything within the garden flourishes — trust, vulnerability, physical intimacy, emotional depth, spiritual companionship. Break down the walls, and the garden is exposed to every passing threat.

Sixth, we learned from God's own voice in chapter five, verse one, that marital love is not merely permitted but extravagantly blessed. "Eat, O friends; drink abundantly, O beloved." The Creator delights in the love of His creatures. Your marriage is not a concession; it is a celebration.

The Puritans wove all of these threads into a single garment. They called marriage "the queen of all earthly relations" — not because it is the only important relationship, but because it is the one that most fully displays the Gospel of Christ's love for His church. When you pursue each other through dark nights, celebrate each other with specific praise, guard your privacy with fierce loyalty, and feast on each other's love with holy abandon, you are painting a portrait of the Gospel that the world desperately needs to see.

As you close this second week, take stock. Which of these truths has spoken most deeply to your marriage this week? Which one do you most need to put into practice? Do not try to apply everything at once. Choose one thread and weave it intentionally into the fabric of your marriage this week.


Turn to each other and consider these questions.

Question 1: Which teaching from this week has been most meaningful to your marriage? Why?

Question 2: Is there one specific change — one practical step — that you want to make together based on what you have heard this week? Name it and commit to it.

Question 3: As you enter the second half of the Song next week, what do you most hope God will speak to your marriage? Share that hope with each other now.


Tonight's prayer focus: Thank God for two weeks in the Song of Solomon together. Pray over each of the truths you have received — the courage to seek, the beauty of covenant, the power of praise, the grace of leaving and cleaving, the safety of the enclosed garden, and the abundance of God's blessing. Ask Him to root these truths deeply in your marriage.


You have gathered much this week, dear couple. Now let the north wind and the south wind blow upon your garden so that its spices flow out — not only for your own delight but for the glory of the God who planted it. Goodnight. Rest well together.
