PURITAN GOLD -- Family Devotional, Day 13
"The Grace of Forgiveness"
Matthew Henry — Commentary on Colossians


Good evening, family. There is no place God loves to meet His people more than when families gather in His name. Welcome to Puritan Gold. Let us open our Bibles and our hearts.

Let us begin in prayer. Lord Jesus, You said that where two or three are gathered in Your name, there You are in the midst of them. We claim that promise tonight as a family. Be here with us. Teach us. Shape us. We pray in Your holy name. Amen.

Every word of Scripture is breathed out by God. Tonight we receive His breath from Colossians 3:13:

"Forbearing one another, and forgiving one another, if any man have a quarrel against any: even as Christ forgave you, so also do ye."

That is Colossians 3:13. Let those words settle in your heart for a moment before we continue.


Few things are harder for a family — or for any human relationship — than forgiveness. But few things are more necessary. Paul writes in Colossians 3:13, 'Forbearing one another, and forgiving one another, if any man have a quarrel against any: even as Christ forgave you, so also do ye.'

Let us unpack this verse carefully. First, 'forbearing one another.' The word 'forbearing' means to put up with, to bear with, to be patient with. It acknowledges a basic reality: people are imperfect, and living together requires patience. In a family, there will be annoying habits, clashing personalities, and daily irritations. Forbearance is the grace to endure these without exploding or holding grudges.

Second, 'forgiving one another, if any man have a quarrel against any.' This goes beyond forbearance. Forbearance deals with minor irritations. Forgiveness deals with real offenses — times when someone genuinely hurts us. Paul does not say, 'Forgive if you feel like it' or 'Forgive if they apologize first.' He says to forgive, period.

And then comes the standard: 'even as Christ forgave you, so also do ye.' This is the game changer. The measure of our forgiveness is not human fairness but Christ's example. How did Christ forgive us? Freely. Fully. While we were still sinners (Romans 5:8). Without waiting for us to earn it. At great personal cost. If Christ forgave us like that, we are called to forgive others in the same spirit.

Jesus taught this explicitly in Matthew 18:21-22, when Peter asked, 'Lord, how oft shall my brother sin against me, and I forgive him? till seven times?' Peter thought he was being generous — the rabbis of his day taught that forgiving three times was sufficient. But Jesus said, 'I say not unto thee, Until seven times: but, Until seventy times seven.' In other words, forgiveness has no limit.

Jesus then told the parable of the unforgiving servant — a man who was forgiven a debt of ten thousand talents (an astronomical sum, equivalent to millions) but then refused to forgive a fellow servant who owed him a hundred pence (a trivial amount). The master's response was severe: 'Shouldest not thou also have had compassion on thy fellowservant, even as I had pity on thee?' When we consider how much God has forgiven us, how can we withhold forgiveness from others?

In Ephesians 4:32, Paul writes similarly, 'And be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, even as God for Christ's sake hath forgiven you.' Kindness, tenderheartedness, and forgiveness — these are the marks of a family transformed by the gospel.

Matthew Henry wrote that forgiveness is the very spirit of Christianity. He argued that if God, who has been infinitely offended by human sin, freely forgives all who come to Him through Christ, then we who have been so freely forgiven have no right to refuse forgiveness to others.

Henry applied this directly to family life. He observed that some of the deepest wounds happen within families, precisely because family members are so close. A harsh word from a parent, a betrayal by a sibling, a broken promise from a spouse — these cut deeply because they come from those we love most. And yet, Henry said, it is within the family that forgiveness must be practiced most diligently.

Henry also made an important distinction between forgiveness and trust. Forgiveness is releasing the debt — choosing not to hold the offense against the person. Trust must be rebuilt over time through changed behavior. You can forgive someone immediately while still taking wise steps to protect yourself from repeated harm. Forgiveness does not mean pretending the hurt never happened or allowing abuse to continue.

Henry counseled families to make forgiveness a daily practice. Do not let the sun go down on your anger (Ephesians 4:26). Clear the air before bedtime. Say 'I am sorry' and 'I forgive you' often. These are among the most powerful words a family can speak.

For our little ones: everyone makes mistakes. When someone says sorry, we can choose to forgive, just like God forgives us. For older children: is there someone you need to forgive? Holding onto anger is like carrying a heavy rock — it hurts you more than anyone else. For the whole family: let us create a culture of quick apology and ready forgiveness in our home.


One of the great blessings of the Christian tradition is that we stand on the shoulders of faithful saints who came before us.

Matthew Henry, in his work "Commentary on the Whole Bible," wrote these words:

"Forgiveness is the very spirit of Christianity. If God, who has been infinitely offended, forgives freely, how can we, who have been so freely forgiven, refuse to forgive one another?"

What Matthew Henry is reminding us is that the truths we have read tonight in Colossians 3:13 are not new. They have been treasured by faithful Christians for centuries. And they are just as true and just as needed in our family today as they were hundreds of years ago. Let us take these words to heart and let them shape the way we live together as a family.


Now we get to the best part: talking about God's Word together as a family. Here are tonight's questions.

Question 1: What does forgiveness mean? Is it easy or hard?

Question 2: How many times did Jesus say we should forgive someone?

Question 3: Why does Paul say we should forgive 'even as Christ forgave you'? How does knowing God forgave us help us forgive others?

Question 4: What is the difference between forgiving someone and trusting them? Can you forgive someone and still set healthy boundaries?

Take a few minutes to talk about these questions together. Let everyone share, and listen to one another with patience and love.


Now, here is our memory verse for today. Let us say it together:

"Even as Christ forgave you, so also do ye." -- Colossians 3:13b

Try to say it out loud three times right now. Then, before bed tonight, see if you can say it from memory. Tomorrow morning, try again. The more we hide God's Word in our hearts, the more it will guide us when we need it most.


Here is a practical way to live out tonight's devotional:

Do a 'Heavy Rock' exercise. Give each family member a heavy object to hold (a book, a water bottle, a rock). Have them hold it with an outstretched arm while you talk about how unforgiveness is like carrying a heavy weight. Then, when each person says 'I choose to forgive,' they set down the weight. Discuss how much lighter it feels to let go.


Gracious God, thank You for meeting with our family tonight. Your Word never returns void, and we trust that what we have heard will bear fruit in each of our lives. Strengthen our family bonds, deepen our faith, and make us a light in this world. Through Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.

May the peace of God guard your hearts and minds tonight. Goodnight, family.
