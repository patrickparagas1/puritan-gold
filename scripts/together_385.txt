PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 24
"Our Little Sister"
Song of Solomon 8:8-10 — Nurturing the Next Generation of Love


Welcome, dear couple. Tonight the Song turns outward from the couple themselves to consider the next generation. The bride's brothers speak about their little sister — a younger woman who is not yet of age. This passage is about the responsibility of mature love to nurture and protect emerging love.

Song of Solomon 8:8-10:

"We have a little sister, and she hath no breasts: what shall we do for our sister in the day when she shall be spoken for? If she be a wall, we will build upon her a palace of silver: and if she be a door, we will enclose her with boards of cedar. I am a wall, and my breasts like towers: then was I in his eyes as one that found favour."


"We have a little sister, and she hath no breasts." The brothers have a younger sister who is not yet mature — not yet ready for the responsibilities and intimacies of marriage. They ask a profoundly important question: "What shall we do for our sister in the day when she shall be spoken for?" How shall we prepare her? How shall we protect her? How shall we equip her for the day when she will be pursued by a suitor?

The Puritans took this question very seriously. They considered the preparation of children for marriage to be one of the most sacred duties of parents. William Gouge devoted entire chapters to the topic, arguing that parents must teach their children three things about marriage: its holiness — that marriage is a divine institution, not merely a social convention; its responsibilities — that marriage requires sacrifice, service, and self-denial; and its joys — that marriage, rightly entered and faithfully maintained, is the sweetest of all earthly relationships.

"If she be a wall, we will build upon her a palace of silver." If the little sister proves to be a wall — that is, if she is virtuous, firm in her boundaries, strong in her resolve — then they will build upon her strength. They will adorn her. They will add beauty to her virtue. The Puritans interpreted this as the encouragement and celebration of moral strength. When you see character in a young person, you invest in them. You build them up. You honor their integrity.

"And if she be a door, we will enclose her with boards of cedar." If she is a door — that is, if she is easily opened, vulnerable to flattery, weak in her boundaries — then they will protect her. They will enclose her with strong cedar boards. Not to imprison her, but to guard her until she is mature enough to guard herself. The Puritans saw both responses as expressions of love: building up the strong, and protecting the vulnerable.

"I am a wall, and my breasts like towers: then was I in his eyes as one that found favour." The bride responds with confidence. She was a wall — a woman of virtue, of strength, of firm boundaries. And because of that moral strength, she found favor in the eyes of her beloved. He was not attracted to weakness or compliance but to character. He loved her because she was strong, not despite it.

Richard Baxter wrote that the most attractive quality in a potential spouse is not beauty, wealth, or charm but godly character. He urged parents to teach their children to look for character first and everything else second. And he urged young people to build character in themselves, knowing that the one worth marrying is the one worth being worthy of.

Thomas Gataker applied this passage to married couples as well. He wrote that husbands and wives have a duty not only to each other and to their own children but to the wider community of young people around them. Your marriage is a model. Your love is a curriculum. Young couples are watching you and learning what marriage looks like from your example. Are you teaching them that marriage is a burden or a blessing? Are you modeling complaint or celebration? Are you building walls of virtue or leaving doors unprotected?

The Puritans also saw in this passage a principle of mentoring. Older couples have a responsibility to mentor younger couples — to share their wisdom, their mistakes, their hard-won insights. If your marriage has survived storms and come out stronger, that story is a gift that younger couples need to hear.

Tonight, consider your wider circle. Who are the "little sisters" and "little brothers" in your life — the young people, the engaged couples, the newlyweds who are looking to you for guidance? What are you teaching them by the quality of your love?


Turn to each other and consider these questions.

Question 1: What did your families of origin teach you about marriage — both good and bad? How have those lessons shaped your own marriage?

Question 2: Is your marriage modeling something worth imitating for the younger people around you — your children, your nieces and nephews, younger couples at church? What would you want them to learn?

Question 3: Is there a younger couple or a single person in your life who could benefit from your mentorship? How might you reach out to them?


Tonight's prayer focus: Pray for the next generation. Pray for your children, if you have them, that they would be walls of virtue when the day of courtship comes. Pray for young couples in your community, that they would find godly models of marriage. Pray that your own marriage would be a testimony worth imitating.


Your love is not just for you. It is a lighthouse for the next generation, a curriculum of grace, a living portrait of the Gospel. Shine brightly, dear couple. The little sisters and brothers are watching. Goodnight.
