PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 13
"No Graven Images: Worship in Spirit"
Christian Ethics & Moral Philosophy — Lesson 3


Welcome back, students. Today we study the second commandment — the commandment that governs not whom we worship but how we worship. If the first commandment addresses the object of worship — God alone — the second commandment addresses the manner of worship — according to God's Word alone. Together, these two commandments form the bedrock of all true religion. Let us give this commandment the careful and reverent attention it deserves.


Part One: The Commandment Stated and Explained

The second commandment declares: "Thou shalt not make unto thee any graven image, or any likeness of any thing that is in heaven above, or that is in the earth beneath, or that is in the water under the earth: thou shalt not bow down thyself to them, nor serve them: for I the Lord thy God am a jealous God, visiting the iniquity of the fathers upon the children unto the third and fourth generation of them that hate me; and shewing mercy unto thousands of them that love me, and keep my commandments" (Exodus 20:4-6).

This commandment is among the longest and most detailed of the ten, and its length is proportionate to its importance. Notice that God does not merely say "do not make images." He specifies with comprehensive thoroughness: no likeness of anything in heaven above, or in the earth beneath, or in the water under the earth. The scope is total. Nothing in the created order — no angel, no human figure, no animal, no fish, no celestial body — is to be fashioned into an object of worship or a representation of the divine.

The Westminster Larger Catechism, Question 108, identifies the duties required by the second commandment. These include "the receiving, observing, and keeping pure and entire, all such religious worship and ordinances as God hath instituted in his word; particularly prayer and thanksgiving in the name of Christ; the reading, preaching, and hearing of the word; the administration and receiving of the sacraments; church government and discipline; the ministry and maintenance thereof; religious fasting; swearing by the name of God, and vowing unto him: as also the disapproving, detesting, opposing, all false worship; and, according to each one's place and calling, removing it, and all monuments of idolatry."

The breadth of this exposition is remarkable. The second commandment is not merely a prohibition of carved idols. It is a comprehensive charter for the worship of the living God — a charter that both prescribes what is commanded and proscribes what is not commanded.

Part Two: The Distinction Between the First and Second Commandments

Before we proceed further, we must understand clearly the distinction between the first and second commandments, for they are often confused or conflated. The first commandment addresses the question: Whom shall we worship? The answer: God alone. The second commandment addresses the question: How shall we worship? The answer: according to God's Word alone.

The Roman Catholic tradition, following Augustine's numbering, combines the first and second commandments into one and divides the tenth commandment into two (coveting a neighbor's wife and coveting a neighbor's goods). The Reformed tradition, following the numbering of Josephus, Origen, and the majority of the church fathers, treats them as distinct commandments — and with good reason. The prohibition of false gods and the prohibition of false worship address fundamentally different aspects of our relationship with God.

A person may worship the true God in a false way — and this is precisely what the second commandment forbids. When Aaron fashioned the golden calf at the foot of Mount Sinai, he did not intend to introduce a new deity. He said to the people: "These be thy gods, O Israel, which brought thee up out of the land of Egypt" (Exodus 32:4), and he declared a feast "to the Lord" — using God's covenant name (Exodus 32:5). Aaron was attempting to worship the true God through a visible representation, an image. This is exactly what the second commandment prohibits. The sin of the golden calf was not primarily a first-commandment violation — the worship of a false god — but a second-commandment violation — the worship of the true God in a false way.

This distinction was enormously important to the Puritans and to the entire Reformed tradition. It means that sincerity of intention is not sufficient for acceptable worship. A worshiper may be entirely sincere in his devotion and entirely wrong in his method. What matters is not merely that we worship the right God but that we worship the right God in the right way — the way that He Himself has prescribed in His Word.

Part Three: The Regulative Principle of Worship

This brings us to one of the most distinctive and important principles of Reformed theology: the Regulative Principle of Worship. This principle holds that the acceptable way of worshiping the true God is instituted by God Himself and is limited by His own revealed will. We may worship God only in the ways that He has commanded in His Word. Whatever He has not commanded, we are not free to introduce.

This principle is distinguished from what is sometimes called the Normative Principle, which holds that we may do in worship anything that Scripture does not explicitly forbid. The difference between these two principles is significant. Under the Normative Principle, the question is: "Is this forbidden?" Under the Regulative Principle, the question is: "Is this commanded?" The Puritans, following Calvin and the Scottish Reformers, embraced the Regulative Principle and regarded it as the clear teaching of the second commandment.

The biblical basis for the Regulative Principle extends far beyond the second commandment itself. Throughout Scripture, God responds with severe judgment to unauthorized worship — worship that may have been well-intentioned but was not prescribed by His Word.

Consider Nadab and Abihu, the sons of Aaron, who "offered strange fire before the Lord, which he commanded them not" (Leviticus 10:1). God struck them dead on the spot. Their offering was not inherently sinful — fire was an element of authorized worship. But it was fire that God had not commanded, and that was sufficient to condemn it. Moses explained to the stunned Aaron: "This is it that the Lord spake, saying, I will be sanctified in them that come nigh me, and before all the people I will be glorified" (Leviticus 10:3).

Consider Uzzah, who reached out to steady the Ark of the Covenant when the oxen stumbled as the Ark was being transported to Jerusalem. "And the anger of the Lord was kindled against Uzzah; and God smote him there for his error; and there he died by the ark of God" (2 Samuel 6:7). Uzzah's intention was good — he was trying to prevent the Ark from falling. But God had commanded that the Ark be carried by Levites using poles, not transported on an ox cart. The method was wrong, even though the motive was right.

Consider King Saul, who offered a burnt offering at Gilgal instead of waiting for Samuel the priest. Samuel rebuked him with words that go to the heart of the Regulative Principle: "Hath the Lord as great delight in burnt offerings and sacrifices, as in obeying the voice of the Lord? Behold, to obey is better than sacrifice, and to hearken than the fat of rams. For rebellion is as the sin of witchcraft, and stubbornness is as iniquity and idolatry" (1 Samuel 15:22-23). Saul's sin was not that he worshiped a false god but that he worshiped the true God in an unauthorized manner.

Consider King Uzziah, who entered the temple to burn incense on the altar — a function reserved exclusively for the priests. "Then Uzziah was wroth, and had a censer in his hand to burn incense: and while he was wroth with the priests, the leprosy even rose up in his forehead before the priests in the house of the Lord" (2 Chronicles 26:19). God struck the king with leprosy for presuming to worship in a way that was not prescribed for him.

These examples, and many others throughout Scripture, establish a clear principle: God cares not only that we worship but how we worship. He has not left the manner of worship to human creativity or preference. He has prescribed the elements of acceptable worship in His Word, and He expects His people to adhere to that prescription with reverence and precision.

Part Four: The Puritan Application of the Regulative Principle

The Regulative Principle of Worship was central to the Puritan understanding of church life and was one of the primary sources of their conflict with the Church of England. The Puritans objected to vestments, altars, candles, incense, images, crucifixes, the sign of the cross in baptism, kneeling at communion, and elaborate ceremonies — not because these things were inherently evil but because they were human additions to the worship of God, additions not authorized by Scripture.

The conflict over worship was not a trivial matter of personal taste. It was a deeply principled disagreement about the nature of divine authority. Archbishop William Laud, who served as Archbishop of Canterbury from 1633 until his imprisonment in 1641 and execution in 1645, aggressively imposed liturgical conformity on the English church, requiring ceremonies and vestments that the Puritans considered unbiblical. Laud moved communion tables from the center of the church to the east end, installed altar rails, required ministers to wear surplices, and insisted on ceremonial practices drawn from the medieval Catholic tradition.

The Puritans resisted. Men like William Prynne, who was fined, imprisoned, and had his ears cut off for publishing a criticism of the theater and, by extension, of the court's entertainments; Henry Burton, who was pilloried and imprisoned for preaching against Laudian innovations; and John Bastwick, who suffered similar punishments — all endured severe persecution for their commitment to the Regulative Principle. They believed that the second commandment gave them no option: if God had not commanded a practice in worship, they could not in good conscience participate in it, regardless of what any human authority demanded.

The Westminster Assembly, meeting from 1643 to 1652, produced the Westminster Directory for Public Worship as a replacement for the Book of Common Prayer. The Directory was explicitly based on the Regulative Principle. It prescribed the elements of worship — reading of Scripture, preaching, prayer, singing of psalms, administration of the sacraments, and collection of offerings — while leaving the specific circumstances (the time of day, the length of the service, the particular psalms to be sung) to the prudential judgment of each congregation. The distinction between elements (prescribed by God) and circumstances (determined by prudence) was a crucial refinement that prevented the Regulative Principle from becoming an unworkable rigidity.

John Owen, who participated in the Westminster Assembly and later served as vice-chancellor of Oxford, defended the Regulative Principle with characteristic theological depth. In his treatise "A Discourse Concerning Liturgies," Owen argued that the simplicity of New Testament worship — preaching, prayer, singing, and the sacraments — was sufficient and beautiful precisely because it was ordained by God. To add to it was to imply that God's prescription was inadequate — that human wisdom could improve upon divine wisdom. Owen considered this a form of the sin of Nadab and Abihu: offering "strange fire" that the Lord had not commanded.

Part Five: The Danger of Images — Reducing the Infinite to the Finite

The second commandment also addresses a deeper spiritual danger: the danger of reducing God to a manageable, comprehensible image. An idol is always smaller than the reality it represents. When we make an image of God, we inevitably diminish Him. We make the infinite finite, the invisible visible, the incomprehensible comprehensible, the omnipresent local. And in doing so, we worship not the true God but our own imagination of God — which is no God at all.

John Calvin, in Book One, Chapter 11 of the "Institutes of the Christian Religion," developed this argument with great force. Calvin wrote that images not only fail to represent God accurately — they actively distort our understanding of who God is. A physical image gives the impression that God is material, when in fact He is spirit. It gives the impression that God is limited to a particular form, when in fact He is boundless. It gives the impression that God can be contained in a particular place, when in fact He fills heaven and earth.

The prophet Isaiah exposed the absurdity of image-worship with devastating irony. In Isaiah chapter 44, verses 14 through 17, the prophet describes a craftsman who cuts down a tree, uses half of it for fuel to cook his dinner and warm himself, and then carves the other half into a god before which he bows down and prays: "Deliver me; for thou art my god" (Isaiah 44:17). The same piece of wood that serves as firewood serves as a deity. The absurdity is almost comical — but Isaiah's point is deadly serious. An image-god is a god of our own making, a god under our control, a god that cannot see, hear, speak, or save.

This principle extends beyond physical images to mental images. The second commandment forbids not only the carving of statues but the construction of false conceptions of God in the mind. Calvin wrote that the human heart is a perpetual factory of idols — and this factory operates in the realm of ideas and imaginations as well as in the realm of wood and stone. When we conceive of God as indulgent and permissive, ignoring His holiness and justice, we have constructed a mental idol. When we conceive of God as distant and uncaring, ignoring His love and compassion, we have constructed a mental idol. When we conceive of God as merely a larger version of ourselves — subject to our limitations, sharing our prejudices, serving our agendas — we have constructed a mental idol. The second commandment calls us to smash these mental idols and to worship the God who reveals Himself in Scripture — the God who is holy, sovereign, just, merciful, and utterly beyond our ability to contain or control.

Part Six: Worship in Spirit and in Truth — The New Testament Fulfillment

Jesus Christ Himself provided the definitive commentary on the second commandment in His conversation with the Samaritan woman at Jacob's well, recorded in John chapter 4. The woman raised the question of the proper place of worship — whether in Jerusalem (as the Jews said) or on Mount Gerizim (as the Samaritans said). Jesus replied with words that transformed the entire framework of the discussion:

"Woman, believe me, the hour cometh, when ye shall neither in this mountain, nor yet at Jerusalem, worship the Father. Ye worship ye know not what: we know what we worship: for salvation is of the Jews. But the hour cometh, and now is, when the true worshippers shall worship the Father in spirit and in truth: for the Father seeketh such to worship him. God is a Spirit: and they that worship him must worship him in spirit and in truth" (John 4:21-24).

This passage is of immense importance for the second commandment. Jesus declares that the Father actively seeks worshipers — but not just any worshipers. He seeks those who worship "in spirit and in truth." Worship "in spirit" means worship that engages the inner person — the heart, the mind, the affections, the will — not merely the outward form. Worship "in truth" means worship that conforms to the truth of God's self-revelation — to who God actually is, as He has made Himself known in His Word and supremely in His Son.

The statement "God is a Spirit" is the theological foundation of the second commandment. Because God is spirit — not material, not physical, not confined to any form — He cannot be accurately represented by any material image. Every physical representation of God is a lie, because it attributes to God a material form that He does not possess. This is why the second commandment is not merely a preference but a necessity. Images of God are forbidden not because God is arbitrary but because images are inherently incapable of representing the God who is spirit.

The Puritans seized upon this passage as the charter for their entire approach to worship. They believed that New Testament worship, purified of all the accretions of medieval ceremony, was the purest expression of worship "in spirit and in truth." Simple, unadorned, Word-centered, Spirit-dependent worship — this was the Puritan ideal. Not worship that dazzled the eyes with visual splendor but worship that engaged the heart with divine truth. Not worship that relied on human artistry but worship that relied on the Holy Spirit's power. Not worship that appealed to the senses but worship that transformed the soul.

Part Seven: The Second Commandment and the History of the Church

The history of the church's relationship to images is complex and deeply instructive. For the first three centuries, the early church appears to have been overwhelmingly opposed to the use of images in worship. The early church fathers — Clement of Alexandria (c. 150-215 A.D.), Tertullian (c. 155-240 A.D.), Origen (c. 185-254 A.D.), and Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 260-340 A.D.) — all spoke against the use of images in Christian worship, regarding it as a pagan practice incompatible with biblical faith.

However, beginning in the fourth century, after Constantine legalized Christianity in 313 A.D. and the church gained imperial patronage, images gradually crept into Christian worship. By the seventh century, the veneration of icons — painted images of Christ, the Virgin Mary, and the saints — had become widespread in the Eastern church. This development provoked a fierce controversy known as the Iconoclastic Controversy, which raged from approximately 726 to 843 A.D.

The iconoclasts (image-breakers) argued that the veneration of icons violated the second commandment. Emperor Leo III ordered the destruction of religious images in 726 A.D., and his successors continued the policy. The iconodules (image-defenders) argued that the incarnation of Christ — God becoming man — had sanctified the use of images, since Christ Himself was "the image of the invisible God" (Colossians 1:15).

The Second Council of Nicaea in 787 A.D. ultimately ruled in favor of the veneration of icons, distinguishing between latria (the worship due to God alone) and dulia (the veneration or honor given to saints and their images). This distinction became central to both Eastern Orthodox and Roman Catholic theology of images.

The Reformers rejected this distinction as a sophistical evasion of the second commandment. Calvin argued that the common people, untrained in theological subtleties, inevitably confused veneration with worship, and that the practical effect of image-veneration was identical to the image-worship forbidden by the commandment. The Reformers in Zurich, led by Ulrich Zwingli, and later in Scotland, led by John Knox, ordered the removal of images from churches — sometimes with great violence, as in the iconoclastic riots that swept through the Netherlands in 1566, known as the Beeldenstorm.

The Puritans continued this reforming impulse in England. William Dowsing, appointed Parliamentary Commissioner for the destruction of superstitious images in East Anglia in 1643, kept a detailed journal of his work removing stained glass windows, statues, crucifixes, and other images from hundreds of churches. While later generations have criticized the destruction of artistic heritage, the Puritans acted from deep conviction: the second commandment, they believed, left them no choice. The worship of God in spirit and truth demanded the removal of all material representations of the divine.

Part Eight: The Puritan View of Religious Art and Ceremony

The Puritans' commitment to the second commandment raised difficult questions about the role of art in Christian life. Were all images forbidden? Were paintings of biblical scenes sinful? What about illustrations in Bible study materials? What about the visual beauty of creation itself?

The Puritans were careful to distinguish between images used as objects of worship and images used for other purposes. The second commandment does not forbid all art or all visual representation. It forbids the making of images for use in the worship of God — images that are bowed down to, served, venerated, or used as aids to devotion directed toward the divine. A painting of a landscape is not a violation of the second commandment. A sculpture of a historical figure is not a violation. Even a painting depicting a biblical scene, used as an educational tool rather than an object of worship, was generally not considered problematic — though the Puritans were cautious about images of Christ, recognizing that any attempt to depict His divine nature was inevitably reductive and misleading.

The key question was always the purpose and use of the image, not its mere existence. An image used in worship — set up in a church as a focus of devotion, kissed, knelt before, or addressed in prayer — violates the second commandment regardless of its artistic merit. An image used for education, historical record, or aesthetic enjoyment outside the context of worship does not fall under the same prohibition.

This distinction is important because it prevents the second commandment from being turned into a blanket condemnation of all visual beauty — a charge that has sometimes been leveled against the Puritans unfairly. The Puritans were not philistines. They appreciated beauty. They wrote magnificent poetry — John Milton's "Paradise Lost," published in 1667, is one of the greatest works of English literature ever produced. They valued the beauty of creation as a reflection of the glory of the Creator. But they drew a sharp line between aesthetic beauty and religious worship, insisting that the worship of God be governed by His Word rather than by human artistic sensibility.

The question of ceremony was equally important. The Puritans distinguished between "circumstances" of worship — things necessary for the decent and orderly conduct of any public gathering, such as the time of meeting, the arrangement of seating, and the volume of singing — and "elements" of worship — the specific acts that constitute worship itself, such as preaching, prayer, singing, and the sacraments. Circumstances could be determined by prudential judgment, as long as they were consistent with the general rules of Scripture. Elements could only be determined by divine prescription. A church might decide to meet at ten o'clock in the morning rather than at noon — that is a circumstance. But a church could not decide to add a dance performance or a dramatic reenactment to the worship service as an element of worship — that would be a human addition to what God has prescribed.

This distinction between elements and circumstances, articulated with particular clarity by George Gillespie in his "A Dispute Against the English Popish Ceremonies," published in 1637, became one of the foundational principles of Presbyterian worship. Gillespie, a Scottish minister who was one of the youngest and most brilliant members of the Westminster Assembly — he was only twenty-five when he was appointed as a commissioner — argued that the Church of England's imposition of ceremonies not found in Scripture was a violation of the second commandment and an infringement on the crown rights of King Jesus, who alone has the authority to determine how His church shall worship.

Part Nine: The Mercy Annexed to the Second Commandment

We must not overlook the remarkable promise attached to the second commandment: "shewing mercy unto thousands of them that love me, and keep my commandments" (Exodus 20:6). While the judgment for violating this commandment extends to the third and fourth generation, the mercy for keeping it extends to thousands. The disproportion is deliberate and glorious. God's mercy always exceeds His judgment. His grace always outweighs His wrath. His love always outlasts His discipline.

Thomas Watson expounded this promise with characteristic pastoral warmth. He wrote that God is more inclined to mercy than to judgment — that He delights in showing mercy and only reluctantly executes judgment. The thousands of generations of mercy promised to those who love God and keep His commandments is a reflection of God's own nature, for "the Lord, the Lord God, merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abundant in goodness and truth, keeping mercy for thousands, forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin" (Exodus 34:6-7).

But Watson also noted the condition attached to the promise: "them that love me, and keep my commandments." The mercy is not indiscriminate. It is covenant mercy — mercy shown to those who are in relationship with God, who love Him, and whose love is expressed in obedience to His commands. This is not salvation by works. The love that keeps God's commandments is itself a gift of grace, produced by the Holy Spirit in the hearts of believers. But it is a real love that produces real obedience — including obedience to the second commandment, the commandment that governs how we worship.

The connection between love and commandment-keeping is one that Jesus emphasized repeatedly: "If ye love me, keep my commandments" (John 14:15). "He that hath my commandments, and keepeth them, he it is that loveth me: and he that loveth me shall be loved of my Father, and I will love him, and will manifest myself to him" (John 14:21). "If ye keep my commandments, ye shall abide in my love; even as I have kept my Father's commandments, and abide in his love" (John 15:10). Love and obedience are inseparable in the Christian life. A love that does not obey is not genuine love. And obedience that does not spring from love is not genuine obedience. The second commandment, with its annexed promise of mercy, calls us to worship God out of love — the kind of love that delights in doing what He commands and refrains from doing what He has not commanded.

Part Ten: The Spirituality of the Second Commandment

Like every other commandment, the second commandment has a spiritual dimension that reaches beyond outward behavior to the inner attitudes of the heart. The Puritans recognized that the second commandment addresses not only what we do in formal worship services but how we think about God at all times.

Every false conception of God is, in a sense, a mental idol — a graven image carved not in wood or stone but in the workshop of the imagination. When we think of God as less holy than He actually is, we have made a false image. When we think of God as less sovereign than He actually is, we have made a false image. When we think of God as more tolerant of sin than He actually is, we have made a false image. When we think of God as less loving, less just, less faithful, less powerful, or less wise than He has revealed Himself to be in Scripture, we have fashioned an idol in our minds and are worshiping a god of our own making.

This is why the Puritans placed such enormous emphasis on the study of Scripture and on sound, accurate, reverent theology. They understood that right worship depends on right knowledge. We cannot worship God in truth if we do not know the truth about God. And we cannot know the truth about God unless we study His self-revelation in Scripture with diligence, humility, and the illuminating help of the Holy Spirit.

Stephen Charnock, in his great work on the divine attributes, warned against the tendency to conceive of God by analogy with ourselves — to project our own qualities, preferences, and limitations onto the divine nature. He wrote that God is infinitely above all human comprehension, and that our highest and best thoughts about God fall infinitely short of the reality. This should produce in us not despair but humility — a willingness to let God define Himself rather than presuming to define Him according to our own imaginations.

The second commandment, in its spiritual application, calls us to constant theological vigilance — to the ongoing discipline of testing our conceptions of God against the standard of His Word and correcting them wherever they fall short. It calls us to what the Puritans called "right thoughts of God" — thoughts that are formed not by our wishes, our fears, or our cultural assumptions, but by the revelation of God in Scripture and in Christ.

Part Eleven: Christ and the Second Commandment

As with the first commandment, the second commandment finds its ultimate fulfillment in the person of Jesus Christ. Christ is "the image of the invisible God" (Colossians 1:15) — not a human-made image but a divinely given image, the perfect representation of the Father. "He that hath seen me hath seen the Father," Jesus said to Philip (John 14:9). In Christ, the invisible God has made Himself visible — not through a carved idol but through the incarnation of the eternal Son.

This has profound implications for the second commandment. We do not need images, icons, statues, or human inventions to know what God is like. God has given us something infinitely better: His own Son. "God, who at sundry times and in divers manners spake in time past unto the fathers by the prophets, hath in these last days spoken unto us by his Son, whom he hath appointed heir of all things, by whom also he made the worlds; who being the brightness of his glory, and the express image of his person, and upholding all things by the word of his power, when he had by himself purged our sins, sat down on the right hand of the Majesty on high" (Hebrews 1:1-3).

Christ also perfectly modeled second-commandment obedience in His own worship of the Father. Throughout His earthly ministry, Jesus worshiped according to the Father's will, not according to human tradition. He cleansed the temple — twice — driving out those who had corrupted God's house of prayer with commercial activity (John 2:13-17; Matthew 21:12-13). He criticized the Pharisees for elevating human traditions above the commandments of God: "Full well ye reject the commandment of God, that ye may keep your own tradition" (Mark 7:9). He submitted perfectly to the Father's will in all things, including the manner of His worship and the conduct of His ministry.

For the Christian, the second commandment is fulfilled in Christ and through Christ. We worship the Father through the Son, by the power of the Holy Spirit, according to the truth of God's Word. This Trinitarian pattern of worship — access to the Father through the mediation of the Son by the agency of the Spirit — is the heart of worship "in spirit and in truth." It requires no images, no human inventions, no visual aids. It requires only the living Word of God, the indwelling Spirit of God, and the mediating Son of God. This is the simplicity and glory of Christian worship, and this is what the second commandment protects.

Part Twelve: Practical Application for Today

How does the second commandment apply to Christians today? We may not be tempted to bow before carved idols, but the principles of this commandment speak powerfully to our present situation in several important ways.

First, the second commandment reminds us that God alone has the right to determine how He is worshiped. In an age of consumer-driven worship — where churches compete for attendance by offering the most entertaining experience — the second commandment calls us back to the simple, biblical pattern. The question is not "What kind of worship do I prefer?" but "What kind of worship has God prescribed?" The elements of biblical worship — the reading and preaching of God's Word, prayer, the singing of praise, the administration of the sacraments, and the giving of offerings — are sufficient for the nourishment of the soul and the glory of God. Human additions, however creative and well-intentioned, risk the sin of Nadab and Abihu: offering strange fire that the Lord has not commanded.

The Puritan Richard Greenham, who served as rector of Dry Drayton near Cambridge from 1570 to 1591 and was renowned for his pastoral compassion and practical wisdom, taught that worship should be measured not by its appeal to the worshiper but by its faithfulness to the Word of God. He warned that when churches begin to evaluate worship by asking "Did the people enjoy it?" rather than "Did God command it?", they have already begun the slide toward the self-worship that the second commandment was designed to prevent. Worship is not entertainment. Worship is not self-expression. Worship is the offering of praise, prayer, and attention to the God who has revealed Himself in His Word and who has prescribed the manner in which He will be honored.

Second, the second commandment warns us against reducing God to a comfortable image. Our culture is relentlessly producing false images of God — a God who endorses our political agenda, a God who affirms every lifestyle choice without question, a God who exists to serve our desires, a God who is too kind to judge and too indulgent to command. These cultural images of God are as much idols as any statue of Baal, and the second commandment calls us to reject them and to seek the God who reveals Himself in Scripture — even when that God challenges our assumptions, contradicts our preferences, and calls us to repentance.

J.I. Packer, the modern Reformed theologian who stood firmly in the Puritan tradition and whose book "Knowing God" published in 1973 has introduced millions of readers to the riches of Puritan theology, devoted an entire chapter to the second commandment. Packer argued that the greatest danger facing the modern church is not the worship of physical idols but the worship of mental idols — false images of God constructed from our own wishes and cultural assumptions rather than from the revelation of Scripture. He wrote that we must let God be God — that we must come to Scripture not to find a God who confirms what we already believe but to discover the God who actually exists, even if that God is more holy, more demanding, more gracious, and more glorious than we had imagined.

Third, the second commandment points us to the sufficiency of Christ as the image of God. "Who is the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of every creature" (Colossians 1:15). "Who being the brightness of his glory, and the express image of his person" (Hebrews 1:3). We do not need carved images, painted icons, or human inventions to know what God is like. We have Christ. He is the perfect, divinely authorized representation of the Father. To know Christ — through His Word, by His Spirit — is to know the invisible God. This is worship in spirit and in truth.

Fourth, the second commandment calls us to examine the worship practices of our own churches and families with honest and searching eyes. Are we worshiping according to the prescriptions of God's Word, or have we introduced elements that, however appealing, lack biblical warrant? Are we placing our confidence in the right methods and means — the preaching of the Word, the administration of the sacraments, the prayers of the saints — or are we substituting human inventions in the hope that they will be more effective? The second commandment calls us to trust God's wisdom over our own, His methods over our innovations, His sufficiency over our creativity. This is not a call to coldness or deadness in worship. It is a call to spiritual vitality rooted in divine truth — a worship that burns with holy fire, but fire that God Himself has kindled.

As we conclude our study of the second commandment, let us remember the words of the prophet Isaiah, who was given a vision of the worship of heaven itself: "In the year that king Uzziah died I saw also the Lord sitting upon a throne, high and lifted up, and his train filled the temple. Above it stood the seraphims: each one had six wings; with twain he covered his face, and with twain he covered his feet, and with twain he did fly. And one cried unto another, and said, Holy, holy, holy, is the Lord of hosts: the whole earth is full of his glory" (Isaiah 6:1-3).

In heaven, there are no images. There are no human inventions. There are no creative innovations. There is only the unveiled glory of the living God, worshiped by beings who cover their faces in reverent awe. The second commandment is God's gracious provision for our worship on earth — a safeguard that keeps us from the folly of our own imaginations and directs us to the only worship that is worthy of the God who fills heaven and earth with His glory.

The second commandment is God's declaration that He will be worshiped on His terms, not ours. It is a commandment born of love — the love of a God who knows that only true worship can satisfy the human soul, and who protects us from the impoverishment of worshiping our own inventions instead of the living God. Let us worship Him in spirit and in truth, with reverence and godly fear, laying aside all human additions and resting in the beautiful simplicity of what He has commanded. As Watson wrote, God's worship is like a garden enclosed — its beauty is preserved by the walls that protect it from intrusion. The second commandment is that protective wall. Let us honor it with grateful and obedient hearts, trusting that the God who prescribed our worship also empowers it by His Spirit. Until next time, students.
