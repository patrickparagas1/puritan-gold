PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Doctrine Deep-Dive, Day 15
"The Mystery of Providence"
Providence Explored Through the Puritans


Good evening, student of the faith. Tonight we engage in one of the noblest pursuits of the Christian life: the study of doctrine. The Puritans did not shy away from deep theology. They embraced it, because they knew that right thinking about God leads to right living before God.

Tonight we draw from the work of John Flavel, specifically from "The Mystery of Providence." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Romans 8:28:

"And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


The doctrine of divine providence is the teaching that God governs all things — every event, every circumstance, every detail of creation and history — according to His eternal purpose and for the ultimate good of His people. It is one of the most comforting and yet most challenging doctrines in the Christian faith: comforting because it assures us that nothing happens by accident, challenging because it requires us to trust God when His ways are inscrutable.

John Flavel's 'The Mystery of Providence' is the definitive Puritan work on this doctrine. Flavel wrote it as a practical guide for believers who need to learn how to read the hand of God in the events of their lives. Flavel was no armchair theologian; he had personally experienced the death of loved ones, the loss of his pulpit during the Great Ejection of 1662, and the perils of secret ministry under government persecution. His writing on providence is forged in the furnace of personal suffering.

Flavel begins by defining providence. It is, he writes, the outward execution of God's eternal decree — the unfolding in time of what God determined in eternity. Providence is not fate, for fate is blind and impersonal. Providence is the governance of a personal, wise, and loving God who directs all things toward a purpose. It is not chance, for chance implies randomness and lack of design. Every event, from the fall of a sparrow to the rise and fall of empires, is under the meticulous direction of the Almighty.

Flavel identifies several characteristics of divine providence. First, it is universal in scope. Nothing falls outside its reach. Not the weather, not the economy, not the decisions of kings, not the seemingly insignificant events of daily life. Flavel writes that the very hairs of our head are numbered, and not one falls without the Father's knowledge and consent. This universality is essential: if even one event were outside God's control, then nothing could be guaranteed, for that one uncontrolled event might unravel the entire plan.

Second, Flavel argues that providence is wise. God does not merely control events; He orders them with infinite wisdom toward infinitely good ends. The events that seem most random, most painful, most inexplicable are woven into a tapestry that will one day reveal breathtaking beauty. Flavel writes that the darkest threads in the tapestry of providence are as necessary to the final design as the brightest. Without them, the picture would be incomplete.

Third, Flavel teaches that providence is particular. God's care extends not merely to the human race in general but to each individual believer. He orders the details of your life — your birth, your family, your education, your vocation, your trials, your blessings — with the same care and precision with which He orders the movements of the stars. Flavel invites believers to review their personal histories and to trace the hand of God in the events that have shaped their lives. What seemed at the time like coincidence, misfortune, or random circumstance will often, in retrospect, reveal the fingerprints of a wise and loving Providence.

The Puritan Stephen Charnock adds depth to Flavel's practical treatment by grounding providence in the attributes of God. In his work on the divine attributes, Charnock argues that God's providence flows necessarily from His nature. Because God is omniscient, He knows all events. Because He is omnipotent, He controls all events. Because He is wise, He orders all events purposefully. Because He is good, He directs all events toward the ultimate good of His creatures. Providence is not an arbitrary exercise of power; it is the expression of a perfectly wise, perfectly good, perfectly powerful God.

Flavel's most searching application of this doctrine is directed at the believer who is suffering. When trials come — and they will come — the doctrine of providence provides a foundation for endurance. You are not the victim of blind forces. You are in the hands of a Father who loves you, a Savior who intercedes for you, and a Spirit who sustains you. The trial has a purpose, even if that purpose is hidden. The pain has a point, even if the point will only be clear in eternity.

Flavel also addresses the relationship between providence and prayer. He argues that prayer is not opposed to providence but is one of the chief instruments through which providence operates. God has decreed not only the ends but also the means, and prayer is among the means He has appointed. The man who says, 'If God has already determined what will happen, why should I pray?' has misunderstood both prayer and providence. God has determined that certain blessings will come through prayer, and apart from prayer they will not come. Providence and prayer are partners, not rivals.

Flavel closes with a practical exhortation: keep a journal of providence. Record the events of your life — the unexpected blessings, the painful trials, the surprising turns — and review them regularly. You will begin to see patterns. You will begin to recognize God's hand. And your faith will be strengthened for whatever lies ahead.


Let us pause and reflect. Consider these questions in the quiet of your heart.

Question 1: When unexpected trials come into my life, is my first instinct to trust God's providence or to doubt His goodness?

Question 2: Can I trace specific events in my personal history where God's providential hand was clearly at work, even though I could not see it at the time?

Question 3: How does the doctrine of particular providence — that God cares about the details of my specific life — change the way I face daily decisions and anxieties?

Question 4: Have I cultivated the habit of noting God's providences in my life, or do I let them pass unrecognized?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Sovereign Lord, Ruler of heaven and earth, I thank You that nothing in my life is random, nothing is wasted, and nothing is outside Your control. When I cannot understand Your ways, help me to trust Your character. When the threads of my life seem dark and tangled, remind me that You are weaving a tapestry of breathtaking beauty. Teach me to trace Your hand in the events of my days and to rest in Your wise and loving governance. Through Christ my Lord, Amen.
