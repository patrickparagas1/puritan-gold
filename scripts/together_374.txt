PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 13
"I Am Come into My Garden"
Song of Solomon 5:1 — The Consummation and the Divine Blessing

---

Welcome, dear couple. Tonight we linger on a single verse — one of the most remarkable in the entire Bible. It is the moment of consummation, and it includes a voice that changes everything. Listen carefully.

Song of Solomon 5:1:

"I am come into my garden, my sister, my spouse: I have gathered my myrrh with my spice; I have eaten my honeycomb with my honey; I have drunk my wine with my milk: eat, O friends; drink, yea, drink abundantly, O beloved."

--- Teaching ---

"I am come into my garden, my sister, my spouse." The bride invited him in chapter four: "Let my beloved come into his garden, and eat his pleasant fruits." Now he has come. The invitation has been accepted. The garden has been entered. This is the consummation of their love — the moment when the enclosed garden is opened to the one who has the right to enter.

The Puritans spoke about marital consummation with a directness that often surprises modern readers. They did not treat the physical union of husband and wife as shameful or merely tolerable. They celebrated it as a gift from God — a means of grace within the marriage covenant. William Gouge wrote that "the marriage bed is undefiled" and that husbands and wives should approach physical intimacy with joy, gratitude, and freedom. Thomas Gataker called the marital union "the crown of all earthly comforts" — the deepest expression of the covenant bond.

"I have gathered my myrrh with my spice; I have eaten my honeycomb with my honey; I have drunk my wine with my milk." The language is one of abundant, unhurried enjoyment. He has gathered, eaten, and drunk. There is no haste here, no selfishness, no taking without savoring. This is the picture of mutual, generous, patient intimacy — the kind that the Puritans commended to married couples.

Richard Baxter wrote that physical intimacy in marriage should be characterized by three things: mutuality — both partners giving and receiving with equal attention to each other's needs; tenderness — approaching each other with gentleness, never with demand or coercion; and gratitude — recognizing that the gift of physical union is a grace from God, not a right to be seized.

But the most remarkable part of this verse is the final sentence: "Eat, O friends; drink, yea, drink abundantly, O beloved." Who speaks these words? Throughout the Song, only two voices have been heard — the bride and the bridegroom. This third voice is different. Most commentators, including the Puritans, identified this as the voice of God Himself.

Consider what this means. God speaks over the consummation of this marriage and says: "Eat. Drink abundantly." He does not say, "That is enough." He does not say, "Be moderate." He says, "Drink abundantly." The God who created marriage, who designed the covenant, who ordained the physical union, speaks a word of extravagant blessing over the couple's intimacy. He approves. He celebrates. He encourages abundance.

This is profoundly important for married couples to hear. Many Christians carry guilt or shame about physical intimacy — the residue of a culture that has distorted God's good gift. The Song of Solomon, with God's own voice of blessing, dismantles that shame. Your physical union is not tolerated by God; it is celebrated by Him. He looks upon your love and says, "Drink abundantly."

Matthew Henry, the great Puritan commentator, wrote on this verse that "Christ is pleased when believers feast upon the provisions of His grace, and He is equally pleased when married couples feast upon the provisions of marital love." The God who sets a table of grace in the spiritual life sets a table of abundance in the married life. Both tables are His gift. Both tables invite feasting, not nibbling.

The Puritans also drew from this verse a lesson about generosity in marriage. "Eat, O friends" — they are called friends. Physical intimacy is not a transaction; it is a feast shared between friends. The best marriages are those where husband and wife are not just lovers but genuine friends — companions who delight in each other's company, who share inside jokes, who prefer each other's presence to anyone else's. When friendship is the foundation, intimacy becomes its natural, joyful expression.

Tonight, receive the blessing of this verse over your marriage. God Himself is speaking to you: Eat. Drink. Drink abundantly. Let there be no shame, no holding back, no meagerness in your love for each other.

--- Reflection Prompts ---

Turn to each other and consider these questions.

Question 1: Do you carry any guilt, shame, or hesitation about the physical dimension of your marriage? How does hearing God's voice of blessing — "Drink abundantly" — speak to that?

Question 2: The bridegroom's enjoyment is unhurried and generous. How can you cultivate more patience, tenderness, and mutual attention in your physical intimacy?

Question 3: Are you friends as well as lovers? What is the state of your friendship right now — your shared laughter, your inside jokes, your delight in each other's company?

--- Prayer Focus ---

Tonight's prayer focus: Thank God for the gift of physical and emotional intimacy in marriage. Ask Him to remove any shame or guilt that does not belong and to replace it with the freedom and joy that He intends. Pray that your love would be abundant, generous, and richly blessed.

--- Closing ---

God Himself speaks over your love tonight: Eat, O friends. Drink abundantly, O beloved. Receive that blessing. Rest in that freedom. Goodnight, dear couple.
