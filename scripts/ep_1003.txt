PURITAN GOLD -- Study Topic: The Book of Sirach, Part 3 of 10
"A Son's Duty, A Father's Blessing"
Sirach Chapter 3 — The Sacred Duties Owed to Father and Mother

---

Good evening, dear friend, and welcome once more to Puritan Gold. In our last session, we explored the magnificent opening hymn of Sirach on the fear of the Lord. Tonight we turn to chapter 3, where Ben Sira takes up one of the most tender and searching subjects in all of Scripture — the duty of children to honor their parents. I ask you to open your heart as well as your mind, for this teaching reaches into the most intimate chambers of family life.

Let us read from Sirach chapter 3, verses 1 through 16:

"Hear me your father, O children, and do thereafter, that ye may be safe. For the Lord hath given the father honour over the children, and hath confirmed the authority of the mother over the sons. Whoso honoureth his father maketh an atonement for his sins: and he that honoureth his mother is as one that layeth up treasure. Whoso honoureth his father shall have joy of his own children; and when he maketh his prayer, he shall be heard. He that honoureth his father shall have a long life; and he that is obedient unto the Lord shall be a comfort to his mother. My son, help thy father in his age, and grieve him not as long as he liveth. And if his understanding fail, have patience with him; and despise him not when thou art in thy full strength. For the relieving of thy father shall not be forgotten: and instead of sins it shall be added to build thee up. In the day of thine affliction it shall be remembered; thy sins also shall melt away, as the ice in the fair warm weather. He that forsaketh his father is as a blasphemer; and he that angereth his mother is cursed of God."

--- Teaching ---

The fifth commandment stands at a remarkable position in the Decalogue. It is the hinge between our duties to God and our duties to our neighbor. "Honour thy father and thy mother: that thy days may be long upon the land which the Lord thy God giveth thee" (Exodus 20:12). It is the first commandment with promise, as the Apostle Paul notes in Ephesians 6:2. And it is a commandment that does not expire when a child reaches adulthood. Ben Sira understood this, and his exposition in chapter 3 is among the most developed treatments of filial piety in all of ancient literature.

Notice first how Ben Sira grounds parental honor in divine authority. "The Lord hath given the father honour over the children, and hath confirmed the authority of the mother over the sons." This is not a social convention or a cultural preference. It is a divine ordinance. When a child honors his parents, he is honoring God's own arrangement. When he dishonors them, he is rebelling against the order of creation itself. The Puritan William Gouge, in his great treatise Of Domestical Duties, made this same argument at length. Parents stand in the place of God to their children, he wrote, not because parents are divine, but because God has delegated to them a sacred trust.

Ben Sira then unfolds a remarkable series of blessings attached to filial honor. He who honors his father makes atonement for sin. He who honors his mother stores up treasure. He shall have joy in his own children. His prayers shall be heard. He shall have long life. These are not magical promises — Ben Sira is not trading in superstition. He is articulating the same principle that Solomon taught in Proverbs: that obedience to God's moral order brings blessing, while rebellion brings curse. The universe is morally ordered because a moral God created it. When we align our lives with His design, we experience the fruit of righteousness. When we violate it, we reap the whirlwind.

But it is the second half of this passage that I find most searching and most needed in our age. Ben Sira turns his attention to the care of aging parents: "My son, help thy father in his age, and grieve him not as long as he liveth. And if his understanding fail, have patience with him; and despise him not when thou art in thy full strength."

Here is counsel that strikes close to home. It is one thing to honor your parents when you are young and dependent upon them. It is quite another to honor them when the roles have reversed — when they have grown old and feeble, when their minds have begun to fail, when they have become, in the eyes of the world, a burden. Ben Sira says plainly: even then, especially then, honor them. Have patience. Do not despise them.

The Hebrew and Greek world of Ben Sira's day was not so different from our own in this regard. The temptation to discard the elderly, to warehouse them out of sight, to resent the inconvenience of their declining years — this temptation is as old as fallen human nature. And Ben Sira confronts it with the full weight of divine authority. To forsake your father, he says, is blasphemy. To provoke your mother to anger is to invite the curse of God. These are not gentle words. They are meant to shock us into seriousness.

The Puritans took the fifth commandment with profound gravity. The Westminster Larger Catechism devotes several detailed questions to the duties of children toward parents, including "bearing with their infirmities, and covering them in love." Richard Baxter in The Christian Directory wrote at length about the obligation of adult children to support aging parents, both materially and emotionally. He argued that the child who neglects an aged parent sins against nature, against gratitude, and against the express command of God. "Nature teacheth the young stork to feed the old one," Baxter wrote, "and shall Christians be below the storks?"

There is a beautiful symmetry here that we should not miss. When you were an infant, your parents bore with your weakness, your inability to feed yourself, your lack of understanding. They did not despise you in your helplessness. They cared for you with tenderness, often at great personal cost and with many sleepless nights. Now, when the wheel of life has turned and they have entered a second helplessness, the debt of love calls you to return that same tenderness. This is not merely duty — it is the natural fruit of a grateful heart.

Ben Sira also makes a striking theological claim: "The relieving of thy father shall not be forgotten: and instead of sins it shall be added to build thee up." We must be careful here not to read this as a doctrine of salvation by works. Ben Sira is writing within the covenantal framework of Israel, where obedience to God's commands brings blessing within the covenant community. His point is pastoral, not systematic. He is saying that God notices when you care for your aging father. God remembers acts of filial love. In the day of your own affliction, that faithfulness will not be overlooked.

Our Lord Jesus Christ Himself underscored the gravity of the fifth commandment when He rebuked the Pharisees for their tradition of Corban. In Mark 7:10-13, He said: "For Moses said, Honour thy father and thy mother; and, Whoso curseth father or mother, let him die the death: But ye say, If a man shall say to his father or mother, It is Corban, that is to say, a gift, by whatsoever thou mightest be profited by me; he shall be free. And ye suffer him no more to do ought for his father or his mother; Making the word of God of none effect through your tradition." Christ was clear: no religious excuse can discharge the duty of caring for your parents.

The latter portion of Sirach 3 turns to the virtue of humility, and we will address that theme more fully in our next session. But even here, the connection is instructive. The proud man thinks himself too important to serve his aging parents. The humble man counts it a privilege. Humility and filial piety are, in Ben Sira's moral universe, deeply intertwined. The one who bows low before God will not think it beneath him to bow low in service to his father and mother.

Let me close the teaching portion with this thought. The Puritan John Flavel once observed that "God often visits the sins of ungrateful children upon them in their own old age." There is a sobering principle of sowing and reaping at work in the fifth commandment. The way you treat your parents in their twilight years may well be the pattern your own children follow when you reach yours. But beyond this prudential concern, there is a deeper motivation: love. We honor our parents not merely because God commands it, not merely because blessing attaches to obedience, but because love — genuine, Spirit-wrought, covenantal love — compels us. They gave us life. They bore our weaknesses. They prayed over us. The least we can do is hold their hand when the shadows lengthen.

--- Application ---

1. If your parents are living, examine your conduct toward them this week. Do you honor them in word and deed? Do you call, visit, and listen with patience? If there has been distance or neglect, take a step toward restoration today.

2. If your parents are aging or infirm, ask God for the grace of patient tenderness. "If his understanding fail, have patience with him." This is one of the most Christlike acts of love you will ever perform. It is costly, unglamorous, and deeply beautiful.

3. If your parents have passed from this life, honor their memory by living out the godly principles they taught you. Speak well of them. Pass their faith to the next generation. The fifth commandment does not end at the grave.

4. Teach your own children by example. The most powerful lesson in filial piety is not a sermon — it is watching you care for your own parents with gentleness and respect. Your children are watching. Let them see the fifth commandment lived out in your home.

--- Closing ---

Let us pray. Gracious Father, Thou who hast set the solitary in families and ordained the bond between parent and child, we confess that we have too often taken this sacred relationship for granted. Forgive us for impatience, for neglect, for the pride that chafes at serving those who served us first. Give us tender hearts and willing hands to honor our fathers and mothers as Thou hast commanded. May the love of Christ constrain us, and may our homes be places where the fifth commandment is written not on stone but on the heart. Through Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.

Go in peace, dear friend. In our next session we will turn to Sirach chapters 4 and 5 and the beautiful subject of humility and patience before God. Until then, honor those whom God has placed above you, and the Lord shall bless the work of your hands.
