PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 13
"Redeeming the Time Together"
Ephesians 5:15-16 — Communication and Holy Living


Good evening, dear husband and wife. Tonight we turn to a subject that affects every marriage in every generation, but perhaps no generation more than our own. The subject is time, how we spend it, how we waste it, and how we can redeem it for the glory of God and the good of our marriage.

Let us read our passage. Ephesians 5:15-16 (KJV):

"See then that ye walk circumspectly, not as fools, but as wise, Redeeming the time, because the days are evil."


Paul urges the Ephesian believers to walk circumspectly. The word means carefully, precisely, with full awareness of their surroundings. It is the opposite of carelessness, of sleepwalking through life, of letting the days pass without thought or intention. And the particular focus of this careful walking is the redemption of time.

To redeem means to buy back, to rescue from waste, to make the most of what would otherwise be lost. Paul says we must redeem the time because the days are evil. The days are evil not merely because the world is full of sin, but because time itself is a wasting resource. Every hour that passes is an hour that will never return. Every evening you spend together is an evening you will never have again. The wise couple treats time as the precious, non-renewable gift that it is.

William Gouge was deeply concerned about the way families spent their time. He observed that many godly couples who would never waste their money thought nothing of wasting hours, even entire evenings, in idle pursuits that did nothing to strengthen their marriage, their family, or their walk with God. Gouge urged couples to take an honest inventory of their time. How many hours each week do you spend in meaningful conversation? How many hours in prayer together? How many hours in the study of God's Word? And how many hours in activities that, while not sinful, produce no lasting fruit?

This is not a call to eliminate rest or recreation. The Puritans were not the dour killjoys they are often portrayed as being. They believed in Sabbath rest, in family enjoyment, in the lawful pleasures of married life. But they insisted that these pleasures be governed by wisdom rather than by impulse. The wise couple plans its time. The wise couple protects its time together from the endless intrusions of a demanding world. The wise couple says no to good things in order to say yes to the best things.

Richard Baxter offered counsel that is strikingly relevant to our own age. He warned against allowing outside obligations and amusements to crowd out the time a husband and wife need with each other. A man who gives his best hours to his work and his worst hours to his wife has his priorities disordered. A woman who pours all her energy into social obligations and has nothing left for her husband has forgotten where her first duty lies. Baxter counseled couples to guard their evenings jealously, to set aside time for conversation, for prayer, for reading, and for the simple pleasure of being together without distraction.

Consider your own marriage in this light. Are you redeeming the time, or are you letting it slip through your fingers? Do you protect time for each other, or do you give the scraps of your day to the most important relationship in your earthly life? The Puritans would have been horrified by the modern habit of sitting in the same room while each person stares at a separate screen. They believed that a husband and wife should be present to each other, not merely physically but mentally and emotionally.

Dear couple, the days are evil, and the days are short. You do not know how many evenings you will have together. Do not waste them. Redeem them. Use them wisely. Let every evening be an investment in a marriage that will bear fruit for generations. Turn off the distractions. Look into each other's eyes. Speak of things that matter. Pray together. Read the Word together. Hold each other. These are the investments that yield eternal dividends.


Question 1: If we honestly evaluated how we spend our evenings, would we say we are redeeming the time or wasting it?

Question 2: What distractions or time-wasters do we need to remove or reduce in order to invest more in our marriage?

Question 3: What is one specific time commitment we can make to each other this week, a time that is protected and set apart for us?


Almighty God, Thou who art the Lord of time, forgive us for the hours we have wasted and the evenings we have squandered. Teach us to number our days that we may apply our hearts unto wisdom. Help us to redeem the time Thou hast given us, not squandering it on empty pursuits but investing it in our marriage, our family, and our walk with Thee. Give us the courage to say no to the world's demands and yes to the things that truly matter. May every evening we share together be a gift offered back to Thee with gratitude and purpose. In Jesus' name, Amen.
