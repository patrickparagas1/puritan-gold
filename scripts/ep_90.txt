PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"The Lord Is My Rock"
May 29, 2026

---

"I will love thee, O Lord, my strength. The Lord is my rock, and my fortress, and my deliverer; my God, my strength, in whom I will trust; my buckler, and the horn of my salvation, and my high tower. I will call upon the Lord, who is worthy to be praised: so shall I be saved from mine enemies. The sorrows of death compassed me, and the floods of ungodly men made me afraid. The sorrows of hell compassed me about: the snares of death prevented me. In my distress I called upon the Lord, and cried unto my God: he heard my voice out of his temple, and my cry came before him, even into his ears." -- Psalm 18:1-6

This psalm is David's great thanksgiving for deliverance, composed at the end of his long conflict with Saul and with all the enemies who had sought his life. It is one of the longest psalms in the Psalter, and also one of the most dramatic, filled with vivid descriptions of divine intervention that read like an eyewitness account of God descending from heaven to rescue His beleaguered servant. And it begins with one of the most tender and personal declarations of love to be found anywhere in the writings of David: "I will love thee, O Lord, my strength."

The Hebrew word for "love" here is a rare and intensely personal word that carries the sense of deep, visceral affection, the kind of love a mother has for her child, the kind of love that arises from the most intimate bonds of relationship. David does not merely respect God, or honour God, or obey God. He loves God. And his love is not a vague, general sentiment. It is rooted in specific, concrete experience. He loves God because God has been his strength, his rock, his fortress, his deliverer, his buckler, the horn of his salvation, and his high tower.

Consider this remarkable accumulation of metaphors. Each one describes a different aspect of God's protection, and together they paint a picture of security so complete that no enemy, no danger, no threat of any kind can penetrate it. A rock speaks of stability, of something immovable and unshakeable amid the shifting sands of circumstance. A fortress speaks of protection, of walls so thick and gates so strong that no invading army can breach them. A deliverer speaks of rescue, of a mighty champion who comes to the aid of the helpless. A buckler speaks of a shield that intercepts the arrows of the enemy before they can find their mark. The horn of salvation speaks of strength and victory, for the horn was a symbol of power in the ancient world. And a high tower speaks of elevation, of being lifted above the reach of one's adversaries.

David had experienced all of these aspects of God's protection during his years of fleeing from Saul. He had hidden in the rocks of the wilderness, and God had been his rock. He had taken refuge in the strongholds of En-gedi and Masada, and God had been his fortress. He had been surrounded by enemies on every side, and God had been his deliverer. He had faced the sword and the spear, and God had been his buckler. He had fought against overwhelming odds, and God had been the horn of his salvation. He had been hunted like a partridge on the mountains, and God had been his high tower.

"The sorrows of death compassed me, and the floods of ungodly men made me afraid." David does not pretend that he was never frightened. He does not claim that his faith was always steady, his courage always unshaken. He was afraid. The sorrows of death surrounded him like the waves of a storm-tossed sea. The floods of ungodly men swept toward him with terrible force. He felt the cold grip of death upon his heart and the hot breath of his enemies upon his neck. And in that extremity, he did the one thing that a believing soul can do when all human resources have been exhausted: "In my distress I called upon the Lord, and cried unto my God."

This is the simplicity of faith. When the situation is desperate, the remedy is simple: call upon God. Not with elaborate prayers or polished phrases, but with the raw, unvarnished cry of a soul in distress. David cried. He did not compose. He did not theologize. He did not analyze. He cried. And his cry, rough and inarticulate as it may have been, was heard in the temple of heaven and came before God even into His ears.

And then the response of God is described in language of such sublime and terrible majesty that it defies all comparison. "Then the earth shook and trembled; the foundations also of the hills moved and were shaken, because he was wroth." God came down to deliver His servant, and the entire creation convulsed at His approach. The earth quaked. The mountains shook. Smoke went up from His nostrils, and fire from His mouth. He bowed the heavens and came down, riding upon a cherub and flying upon the wings of the wind. He made darkness His secret place and pavilioned Himself in the thick clouds of the sky.

This is not mythology. This is theological poetry of the highest order, describing in the language of theophany, of divine appearance, the reality of God's intervention in the life of His servant. When God acts to deliver His people, the whole creation feels the impact. The forces of nature are His instruments. The elements of the universe are His servants. And no power, whether visible or invisible, whether earthly or demonic, can withstand the hand of the Almighty when He stretches it forth on behalf of those who call upon His name.

"He sent from above, he took me, he drew me out of many waters." Out of the flood that threatened to overwhelm him, David was drawn forth by the hand of God. Not by his own swimming. Not by his own strength. But by the sovereign, gracious, irresistible power of the God who is worthy to be praised. And this is the testimony of every redeemed soul. We were drowning, and He drew us out. We were falling, and He caught us. We were dying, and He gave us life. "The Lord is my rock." May He be yours as well. Amen.
