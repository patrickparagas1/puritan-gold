PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Pastoral Encouragement, Day 20
"Loving Her as Christ Loved the Church"
Puritan Counsel on Sacrificial Marriage


Welcome, dear listener. The Puritans were master pastors. They knew how to comfort the afflicted and afflict the comfortable. Tonight, in that tradition, we bring a word of pastoral encouragement and challenge.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Ephesians 5:28-29:

"So ought men to love their wives as their own bodies. He that loveth his wife loveth himself. For no man ever yet hated his own flesh; but nourisheth and cherisheth it, even as the Lord the church."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


Paul's instruction in Ephesians 5 sets the highest possible standard for a husband's love: the self-giving love of Christ for His church. This is not a love that seeks its own comfort; it is a love that seeks the good of the beloved at any cost. Thomas Watson, in his various works on Christian duty, applies this standard with both tenderness and searching precision.

Watson writes that a husband's love for his wife should be 'like Christ's love to the church: holy, spiritual, and inviolable.' He identifies several characteristics of this love.

First, it is a love that nourishes. Paul uses the word 'nourisheth,' which means to provide for, to feed, to sustain. Watson applies this not merely to material provision (food, shelter, clothing) but to emotional and spiritual provision. The husband who provides financially but withholds affection, encouragement, and spiritual leadership is not fully nourishing his wife. She needs more than a roof over her head; she needs a husband who feeds her soul.

Second, it is a love that cherishes. The word 'cherisheth' carries the image of a bird warming its young under its wings. It means to create an atmosphere of warmth, safety, and tender care. Watson writes that the husband who cherishes his wife makes her feel valued, protected, and treasured. She does not live in fear of his criticism or indifference; she rests in the security of his love.

Third, it is a love that sanctifies. Paul says that Christ loved the church 'that he might sanctify and cleanse it.' Watson applies this to the husband's calling: your love for your wife should make her more holy. This does not mean acting as her judge or her moral policeman. It means creating an environment in which godliness can flourish: leading family worship, praying with her, encouraging her spiritual gifts, and modeling repentance when you fail.

Watson identifies several common failures in husbandly love. The first is harshness. Some husbands confuse leadership with domination. They rule their homes with an iron hand, demanding obedience through intimidation rather than earning respect through love. Watson writes that 'the husband who governs by fear rather than love has forgotten that he is a picture of Christ, not a picture of a tyrant.'

The second failure is neglect. Many husbands do not actively mistreat their wives; they simply ignore them. They give their best energy to work, their remaining energy to hobbies, and leave nothing for their marriage. Watson warns that neglect is a form of unlove as damaging as cruelty: 'The wife who is neglected starves in spirit, even if she is fed in body.'

The third failure is comparison. Some husbands compare their wives unfavorably to other women — whether to women in their social circle, to idealized images in media, or to a romanticized memory of their wife in earlier years. Watson calls this a form of infidelity of the heart: 'The husband who wishes his wife were someone else has already broken faith with her.'

Richard Baxter adds practical counsel: show your love in ways your wife can perceive. Do not assume that providing financially is sufficient demonstration of love. Some wives need words of affirmation. Some need physical affection. Some need quality time. Some need acts of service. Learn what communicates love to your specific wife, and practice it consistently.

Baxter also counsels husbands to ask their wives how they are doing — not as a formality, but as a genuine inquiry. 'The husband who does not know the state of his wife's heart,' Baxter writes, 'is a negligent shepherd who does not know the condition of his flock.'

The standard is Christ. He did not wait for the church to earn His love; He gave it freely. He did not love the church because she was lovely; He loved her and made her lovely. He did not seek His own comfort; He laid down His life. This is your calling. It is impossible in your own strength, which is exactly why you need the same Spirit who empowered Christ to empower you.


Reflection is where truth becomes personal. Consider these questions carefully.

Question 1: Am I nourishing and cherishing my wife in the ways she most needs, or am I defining love on my own terms?

Question 2: Which of Watson's common failures — harshness, neglect, or comparison — do I most need to repent of?

Question 3: If my wife were honest, what would she say she needs most from me right now?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Lord Jesus, You loved the church and gave Yourself for her. I confess that my love for my wife falls far short of that standard. Teach me to nourish, cherish, and sanctify her by Your grace. Help me to love not as I think she should be loved, but as she needs to be loved. Amen.

May the Lord guard your mind and your heart tonight. Until we meet again.
