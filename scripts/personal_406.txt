PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Doctrine Deep-Dive, Day 6
"Common Grace and Saving Grace"
The Two Graces Explored Through the Puritans


Welcome, friend. The Puritans called doctrine the bones of the body of divinity. Without strong bones, the body collapses. Tonight we strengthen those bones as we study a vital truth together.

Tonight we draw from the work of Multiple (Calvin, Edwards, Manton), specifically from "Various works on the doctrine of grace." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Matthew 5:45:

"That ye may be the children of your Father which is in heaven: for he maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, and sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


One of the most important distinctions in Reformed theology is the distinction between common grace and saving grace. Understanding this distinction helps us make sense of the world around us: why unregenerate people can be kind, creative, and morally upright, yet still stand under the judgment of God; and why the saving grace of God in Christ is so utterly different from every other blessing God bestows.

Common grace is the term theologians use to describe God's non-saving goodness toward all people without distinction. Jesus Himself teaches this in Matthew 5:45: God 'maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, and sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust.' This is grace because no one deserves any good thing from God; all humanity stands under the curse of the fall. Yet God, in His kindness, restrains sin, preserves order, bestows talents, enables civilization, and gives temporal blessings to believer and unbeliever alike.

John Calvin, though not a Puritan himself, laid much of the groundwork that the Puritans built upon. In his 'Institutes of the Christian Religion,' Calvin writes of the 'general grace of God' by which He 'restrains the perverseness of our nature from breaking forth into action.' Without this restraining grace, Calvin argues, human society would be impossible. Every man would give full vent to his depravity, and the world would descend into utter chaos. That it has not done so is evidence not of human goodness but of divine restraint.

Calvin further argues that the gifts of intellect, art, science, and moral virtue in unregenerate people are expressions of common grace. When an unbelieving scientist discovers a truth about the natural world, he does so because God has given him the faculty of reason. When an unbelieving artist creates a work of beauty, he does so because God has endowed him with creative capacity. These gifts do not earn merit before God, and they do not indicate saving grace, but they are nonetheless genuine gifts of God's goodness.

Jonathan Edwards brings a sharper edge to the distinction. In his sermon 'Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God' and in his theological treatise 'Religious Affections,' Edwards is concerned to show that common grace, however genuine, is radically insufficient for salvation. Common grace restrains sin but does not remove it. It improves behavior but does not change the heart. It enables outward morality but does not produce saving faith. Edwards warns against confusing the effects of common grace with the evidences of saving grace. A man may be moral, charitable, and even religious, yet remain an unconverted sinner if his heart has not been transformed by the sovereign, regenerating work of the Holy Spirit.

Edwards identifies what he calls 'truly gracious affections' — the marks of saving grace that distinguish the regenerate from the merely moral. These include: a spiritual sense of the excellency and beauty of divine things (not merely intellectual assent), a love for God for His own sake (not merely for His gifts), a hatred of sin as sin (not merely as inconvenience), and a practice of holiness that flows from a changed nature (not merely from fear or self-interest).

Thomas Manton, the great Puritan preacher, adds pastoral application. In his sermons on Jude, Manton warns that one of the great dangers of the church is the confusion of common grace with saving grace. People sit in pews, hear sermons, participate in sacraments, and even experience emotional responses to the gospel, yet are never truly converted. Manton writes that the difference between common and saving grace is the difference between ice that melts in the sun and is soon frozen again, and ice that is melted by fire and transformed permanently into water. Common grace produces temporary change; saving grace produces permanent transformation.

What does this mean for the man who wants to grow in his understanding of God's ways? First, it means gratitude. Every good thing you enjoy — health, food, friendship, beauty, order — is an expression of God's common grace. Thank Him for these gifts, and do not take them for granted. Second, it means humility. The morality you see in your unbelieving neighbor is a gift of God, not a sign that he does not need a Savior. Third, it means urgency. Common grace is not saving grace. The restraining hand of God does not guarantee the regenerating work of God. Press into the means of grace — the Word, prayer, the sacraments, the fellowship of saints — for it is through these means that saving grace is ordinarily applied.


Reflection is where truth becomes personal. Consider these questions carefully.

Question 1: Do I recognize and give thanks for God's common grace in my daily life — in the food I eat, the order of society, the beauty of creation?

Question 2: Am I in danger of confusing common grace with saving grace in my own life or in my assessment of others?

Question 3: How does the distinction between common and saving grace increase my sense of the preciousness of the gospel?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Lord, I thank You for Your common grace that sustains me and all people daily. I thank You even more for Your saving grace that has changed my heart and given me new life. Help me to never confuse the two, and to treasure the gospel above every other gift. In Christ, Amen.

May the Lord guard your mind and your heart tonight. Until we meet again.
