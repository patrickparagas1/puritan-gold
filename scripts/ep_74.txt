PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"How Amiable Are Thy Tabernacles"
May 13, 2026

---

"How amiable are thy tabernacles, O Lord of hosts! My soul longeth, yea, even fainteth for the courts of the Lord: my heart and my flesh crieth out for the living God. Yea, the sparrow hath found an house, and the swallow a nest for herself, where she may lay her young, even thine altars, O Lord of hosts, my King, and my God. Blessed are they that dwell in thy house: they will be still praising thee." -- Psalm 84:1-4

The sons of Korah, who composed this psalm, were the doorkeepers of the temple. They stood at the threshold of the house of God day after day, watching the worshippers come and go, hearing the songs of praise, witnessing the sacrifices, breathing the incense. And from that lowly station they composed one of the most beautiful and heartfelt expressions of love for the presence of God to be found anywhere in sacred literature. Their psalm teaches us that it is not the prominence of our position but the sincerity of our devotion that determines the quality of our worship.

"How amiable are thy tabernacles, O Lord of hosts!" The word "amiable" means lovely, desirable, worthy of deep affection. The psalmist looks upon the house of God and sees not a mere building of stone and timber, but a place radiant with the presence of the living God. And his soul responds with a longing so intense that it borders on physical pain. "My soul longeth, yea, even fainteth for the courts of the Lord." This is not the dutiful attendance of a man who goes to church because custom demands it. This is the passionate hunger of a soul that has tasted the sweetness of God's presence and cannot bear to be separated from it.

"My heart and my flesh crieth out for the living God." Observe that this desire engages the whole man, heart and flesh together. It is not merely a spiritual exercise, detached from the realities of bodily existence. The psalmist's very flesh cries out for God, as a parched throat cries out for water, as empty lungs cry out for air. This kind of desire cannot be manufactured by religious technique or worked up by emotional manipulation. It is the spontaneous overflow of a heart that has been awakened by the Holy Spirit to the surpassing value of knowing God.

And then comes one of the most tender images in all the Psalms. "Yea, the sparrow hath found an house, and the swallow a nest for herself, where she may lay her young, even thine altars, O Lord of hosts, my King, and my God." The sparrow and the swallow, those commonest and most insignificant of birds, have found a home in the very precincts of the temple. They nest among the rafters above the altar of God. And the psalmist, far from shooing them away as a nuisance, sees in their presence a parable of his own deepest desire. Even the birds have found a dwelling place near God's altar. How much more does the believing soul long to find its home there!

Matthew Henry, that prince of commentators, observed that the sparrow is one of the most despised of creatures. It has no beautiful plumage, no melodious song, no special usefulness to man. And yet it is welcome in the house of God. What comfort this gives to the humblest believer! You may feel yourself as insignificant as a sparrow, as overlooked and as ordinary as the commonest bird that flies. But in the house of God, you have a home. At the altar of God, you have a nest. You are welcome there, not because of your own beauty or importance, but because the Lord of hosts delights to receive the smallest and the lowest of His creatures into His presence.

"Blessed are they that dwell in thy house: they will be still praising thee." Here is the blessedness of continual communion with God. Those who dwell in His house do not praise Him once and then fall silent. They are still praising, always praising, ever finding new reasons for gratitude and adoration. For the God who dwells in His temple is an infinite God, and the exploration of His perfections is an occupation that eternity itself shall not exhaust. The angels who have stood before His throne for ages uncounted have not yet sounded the depths of His glory. And the saints who have been in heaven for millennia have not yet run out of reasons to cry, "Holy, holy, holy, is the Lord of hosts!"

"Blessed is the man whose strength is in thee; in whose heart are the ways of them. Who passing through the valley of Baca make it a well; the rain also filleth the pools." The valley of Baca is the valley of weeping, a dry and desolate place through which the pilgrims had to pass on their way to the temple in Jerusalem. But the pilgrims whose strength is in God do not merely endure this valley. They transform it. They make it a well. Their faith turns the desert into a garden. Their hope makes springs to flow in the wilderness.

What a picture this is of the Christian's journey through this world! This present life is, for every child of God, a valley of Baca, a valley of tears. We weep over our sins. We weep over our losses. We weep over the sorrows of a fallen world. But the believer who draws his strength from God does not merely survive the valley. He digs wells in it. He discovers springs of living water that the world cannot see. His afflictions become the very means by which God brings refreshment to his own soul and to the souls of others. The rain fills the pools, and what was dry becomes fruitful, and what was barren becomes a source of life.

"For a day in thy courts is better than a thousand. I had rather be a doorkeeper in the house of my God, than to dwell in the tents of wickedness." Here speaks the doorkeeper of the temple, the lowliest of all the servants of God's house. His position was humble. His duties were menial. He did not stand in the inner sanctuary. He did not offer the great sacrifices. He did not pronounce the priestly blessing. He simply opened and closed the door. But he counted that single day at the threshold of God's house more precious than a thousand days spent in the most sumptuous tents of the ungodly.

This is the value system of heaven, and it stands in absolute opposition to the value system of the world. The world measures blessedness by wealth, by status, by comfort, by power. The kingdom of God measures blessedness by proximity to the presence of God. And by that measure, the poorest, humblest, most overlooked saint who lives in daily communion with the Almighty is richer than all the kings and princes of the earth combined. "For the Lord God is a sun and shield: the Lord will give grace and glory: no good thing will he withhold from them that walk uprightly." Amen.
