PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Guided Puritan Reading, Day 7
"Christ's Pity Greater Than His Anger"
Thomas Goodwin — The Heart of Christ, Chapter 2


Welcome. Tonight we read together. Not quickly, not casually, but with the kind of attentive, worshipful reading that the Puritans practiced every day. Open your heart as we open this text.

Tonight we draw from the work of Thomas Goodwin, specifically from "The Heart of Christ in Heaven Towards Sinners on Earth." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Isaiah 54:7-8:

"For a small moment have I forsaken thee; but with great mercies will I gather thee. In a little wrath I hid my face from thee for a moment; but with everlasting kindness will I have mercy on thee, saith the LORD thy Redeemer."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


Goodwin now presses deeper into the disposition of Christ's heart by addressing a question that troubles many believers: when I sin, does Christ's anger outweigh His pity? Is He more inclined toward judgment or toward mercy? Goodwin's answer, grounded in careful exegesis and rich pastoral experience, is emphatic: Christ's pity toward His people always outweighs His displeasure at their sin.

This is not to say that Christ is indifferent to sin. Goodwin is careful to maintain the holiness of Christ. Sin grieves Him. It dishonors His name. It wounds the relationship between Himself and His people. Christ is not a permissive friend who shrugs at transgression. But — and here is the crucial point — His grief over sin is always accompanied by, and subordinated to, a greater grief over the misery that sin produces in His beloved ones. He hates the sin, but He pities the sinner. And the pity is always greater than the displeasure.

Goodwin draws this principle from the prophetic Scriptures. In Isaiah 54, God compares His momentary wrath to His everlasting kindness. The wrath is real but temporary; the kindness is real and eternal. Goodwin argues that this is not merely an Old Testament principle superseded by the New; it is a revelation of the very heart of God, fully manifested in the Person of Christ. Christ disciplines His people, but His discipline is always restorative, never destructive. It aims at their good, not their ruin.

Goodwin illustrates this with the analogy of a mother and her sick child. The mother may give the child bitter medicine. The child may cry and struggle. But the mother's heart is not hardened by the child's suffering; it is broken by it. She gives the medicine not because she enjoys the child's pain but because she loves the child too much to allow the disease to continue. So it is with Christ. When He chastens His people — through conviction of sin, loss of comfort, or painful providences — His heart is full of pity even as His hand administers the discipline.

Goodwin then makes a point of extraordinary pastoral significance. He argues that Christ is more provoked to pity by our sins than He is provoked to anger by them. This sounds almost scandalous, but Goodwin defends it carefully. When a believer sins, Christ sees not merely the transgression but the underlying weakness, the power of temptation, the remaining corruption that the believer is fighting against. He sees the whole picture, and the whole picture moves Him to compassion. This does not mean He excuses the sin; it means He responds to it with redeeming pity rather than condemning wrath.

Goodwin supports this with the example of Peter's denial. When Peter denied Christ three times, Christ did not respond with cold fury. He turned and looked at Peter — and that look was not a look of condemnation but of heartbroken love. It was a look that said, 'I knew this would happen. I have already prayed for you. And I will restore you.' Peter went out and wept bitterly, not because he saw anger in Christ's eyes but because he saw love that he knew he did not deserve.

Goodwin also addresses the believer's experience of spiritual desertion — those seasons when God seems absent, when prayer feels empty, when the soul is shrouded in darkness. Goodwin argues that even in these seasons, Christ's heart has not changed. The sun is still shining even when clouds obscure it. Christ may withdraw the sense of His presence for wise and loving reasons — to deepen dependence, to expose hidden idols, to intensify longing for Him — but He never withdraws His actual presence or His actual love. The believer in spiritual darkness is still held in the hand of a compassionate Savior.

What does this mean for you today? It means that your worst day as a Christian — the day of your most shameful failure, your most grievous sin, your most painful doubt — is still a day when Christ's heart is more inclined toward pity than toward anger. This does not give license to sin; it gives courage to repent. If Christ's heart were hard, we would have reason to despair. But because His heart is tender, we have reason to run to Him, not away from Him, when we have fallen.

Goodwin makes one further observation that is worth carrying with you throughout this day. He notes that Christ's discipline of His people is always proportioned to their ability to bear it. The Father does not lay upon His children more than they can endure. He knows their frame; He remembers that they are dust. And when the discipline has accomplished its purpose, He restores the comfort of His presence with a tenderness that makes the former darkness seem like the shadow that sets off the brilliance of the returning light. The God who hides His face for a moment gathers His people with everlasting kindness.


Now let us turn the lens inward. What is God saying to you through tonight's study?

Question 1: When I sin, do I instinctively run to Christ or away from Him? What does this reveal about my understanding of His heart?

Question 2: Have I experienced seasons of spiritual darkness, and did I interpret them as evidence of Christ's anger or as a means of His deeper work in my soul?

Question 3: How does the truth that Christ's pity is greater than His anger change the way I approach repentance?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Lord Jesus, I confess that I have often imagined You to be angry when in truth You were full of pity. When I fall, give me the grace to run to You rather than from You. Let the knowledge of Your tender heart be the ground of my repentance and the fuel of my hope. I come to You today with all my failures, trusting in Your everlasting kindness. Amen.

Press on, dear saint. The race is worth running, and the prize is Christ Himself.
