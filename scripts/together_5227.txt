PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 27
"Charm Is Deceitful, Beauty Is Vain"
Proverbs 31:30-31 — The Virtuous Wife


Good evening, dear husband and wife. Tonight we come to the climax of the Proverbs 31 portrait. After describing the virtuous woman's industry, wisdom, generosity, and faithfulness, Solomon now reveals the one quality that underlies and sustains all the rest. Without it, everything else would be hollow. With it, everything else shines.

Let us read our passage. Proverbs 31:30-31 (KJV):

"Favour is deceitful, and beauty is vain: but a woman that feareth the LORD, she shall be praised. Give her of the fruit of her hands; and let her own works praise her in the gates."


Solomon's words cut against the grain of every age, including our own. Favour is deceitful. The word "favour" here refers to charm, to outward grace and social attractiveness. It is deceitful because it can mask a hollow interior. A charming woman may lack character. A socially graceful woman may be inwardly corrupt. Charm promises much but delivers little of lasting value. It fades with familiarity, and it cannot sustain a marriage through the trials that every couple must face.

Beauty is vain. This does not mean that beauty is sinful or that physical appearance does not matter. God created beauty and called it good. But beauty is vain in the sense that it is fleeting, temporary, and insufficient as a foundation for a marriage or a life. The beauty that captivates at twenty will change at forty and fade at sixty. The husband who married for beauty alone will find himself married to a stranger when that beauty changes. The wife who relies on her appearance for her sense of worth will face a crisis when the mirror tells a different story than it once did.

But a woman that feareth the Lord, she shall be praised. Here is the foundation. Here is the bedrock. The fear of the Lord is the one quality that does not fade with time, that does not deceive with false promises, that does not fail when tested. The woman who fears the Lord possesses something that no amount of charm or beauty can provide: a character rooted in eternal reality. Her fear of God shapes her speech, her work, her relationships, and her priorities. It makes her wise. It makes her kind. It makes her faithful. And it makes her worthy of the praise that Solomon describes.

William Gouge urged both husbands and wives to evaluate their priorities in the light of this verse. Husbands, he asked, what do you most value in your wife? Is it her appearance, her social skills, her ability to impress your friends? Or is it her fear of the Lord, her devotion to Scripture, her faithfulness in prayer, her integrity of character? The husband who values the fear of the Lord above all other qualities in his wife has his priorities rightly ordered. The husband who values charm and beauty above character is building on sand.

For wives, Gouge was equally direct. Where do you invest your greatest energy? In cultivating your appearance or in cultivating your soul? In keeping up with fashion or in keeping up with God's Word? There is nothing wrong with caring for your body and your appearance. The Puritans were not opposed to beauty or to taking reasonable care of one's physical self. But they insisted that the inner life must take priority over the outer. The woman whose primary pursuit is the fear of the Lord will find that she possesses a beauty that time cannot diminish and that draws people to her not because of how she looks but because of who she is.

Richard Baxter observed that the beauty of a godly character actually increases with age. While physical beauty peaks and declines, the beauty of a life lived in the fear of the Lord deepens and ripens over the decades. The older woman who has walked with God for fifty years possesses a radiance that no young face can match. Her wrinkles are testimonies of faithfulness. Her gray hair is a crown of glory. Her gentleness, her wisdom, her patience have been refined by years of communion with God. This is the beauty that matters most, and it is the beauty that will be praised.

The final verse adds a lovely touch: "Give her of the fruit of her hands; and let her own works praise her in the gates." The virtuous woman does not need someone else to manufacture a reputation for her. Her works speak for themselves. Her faithfulness is its own testimony. The life she has lived is the evidence of her character, and it proclaims her worth in the most public of places.


Question 1: What do we value most in each other? Are our priorities aligned with what Scripture says matters most?

Question 2: How can we guard against the world's emphasis on outward appearance and cultivate a deeper appreciation for inner character?

Question 3: In what ways is the fear of the Lord visible in our daily life together? How can we strengthen it?


O Lord, who seest not as man seeth, for man looketh on the outward appearance, but Thou lookest on the heart, teach us to value what Thou dost value. Deliver us from the deceitfulness of charm and the vanity of beauty. Root our marriage in the fear of the Lord, that we might build a life together that endures when all that is superficial has faded away. May the wife in this marriage be praised for her reverence of Thee, and may the husband honor her for the qualities that truly matter. Grant us both a deepening fear of Thy holy name, that our marriage might bring glory to Thee forever. In Jesus' name, Amen.
