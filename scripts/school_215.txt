Lesson 5: "The Fountain of Life — Daily Dependence"
The Work of Christ


Good morning, students. Welcome to the Puritan Academy. Let us begin this day as the Puritans did, by acknowledging God as the source of all knowledge and wisdom. Before we open our textbooks, let us open our hearts.

Almighty God, the fountain of all wisdom, we ask You to bless our studies today. Open our minds to understand, our hearts to receive, and our wills to obey the truth. Grant us diligence, humility, and a spirit of inquiry that honors You. Through Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.

Let us meditate on a portion of Scripture before we begin our main lesson.

Our Scripture for today is John 4:13-14:

"Jesus answered and said unto her, Whosoever drinketh of this water shall thirst again: But whosoever drinketh of the water that I shall give him shall never thirst; but the water that I shall give him shall be in him a well of water springing up into everlasting life."

Come to the fountain this morning. Do not try to face this day in the strength of yesterday's grace. Come fresh to Christ. Draw deeply from the well of salvation. Drink of the water that will never run dry. Whatever you need today — cleansing from sin, refreshment for weariness, sustenance for the journey, satisfaction for the longings of your heart — it is all found in Christ. He is the fountain of life. With Him is fullness of joy. At His right hand are pleasures forevermore. Do not go to broken cisterns that can hold no water. Come to the living fountain. Drink freely. Drink deeply. And you shall never, ever thirst again.

As we prepare for today's lesson, remember that everything we study flows from this one fountain: the Word of God. Our lesson today is part of The Work of Christ, and it is titled: The Fountain of Life — Daily Dependence. Let us proceed with minds engaged and hearts open.


Now let us turn to our main lesson for today.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes in John 4:13-14.

The image of Christ as the fountain of life runs throughout Scripture like a crystal stream, nourishing every part of the biblical landscape. From the rivers of Eden to the river of life in the New Jerusalem, the Bible consistently presents God — and specifically Christ — as the source of all spiritual life, refreshment, and sustenance. John 4:13-14 records Jesus' conversation with the Samaritan woman at Jacob's well, where He reveals Himself as the fountain that satisfies the deepest thirst of the human soul.

"Whosoever drinketh of this water shall thirst again." Jesus begins with a statement of the inadequacy of all earthly sources of satisfaction. The water in Jacob's well was real water — good, clean, refreshing. But it could not permanently satisfy. Whoever drank would thirst again within hours. This is a parable of all earthly pleasures, possessions, and achievements. They provide temporary refreshment but never permanent satisfaction. The pursuit of happiness through earthly means is like drinking salt water — the more you drink, the thirstier you become. Augustine expressed this truth memorably: "Thou hast made us for Thyself, O Lord, and our hearts are restless until they rest in Thee."

"But whosoever drinketh of the water that I shall give him shall never thirst." The contrast is absolute. Earthly water provides temporary relief; Christ's water provides permanent satisfaction. The word "never" in the Greek is emphatic — "shall by no means, not ever, under any circumstances thirst." This does not mean that the believer never desires more of Christ (indeed, the more we drink, the more we desire), but that the deepest thirst of the soul — the longing for meaning, purpose, acceptance, love, and God Himself — is permanently and completely satisfied in Christ.

"But the water that I shall give him shall be in him a well of water springing up into everlasting life." The water Christ gives is not merely a drink that satisfies momentarily but an internal spring — a living fountain — that wells up continuously from within the believer. This is the indwelling presence of the Holy Spirit (John 7:38-39), who is the living water that Christ gives to all who believe in Him. This spring is inexhaustible, unfailing, and eternal — it springs up "into everlasting life."

Psalm 36:7-9 celebrates God as the fountain of all life and goodness: "How excellent is thy lovingkindness, O God! therefore the children of men put their trust under the shadow of thy wings. They shall be abundantly satisfied with the fatness of thy house; and thou shalt make them drink of the river of thy pleasures. For with thee is the fountain of life: in thy light shall we see light." The phrase "fountain of life" expresses the truth that all life — physical and spiritual — originates in God and flows from Him. Apart from God, there is no life, no joy, no satisfaction. With God, there is abundance, pleasure, and light. The "river of thy pleasures" is not a metaphor for sensual indulgence but for the deep, pure, overflowing joy that comes from knowing and fellowshipping with God.

Revelation 22:1-2 completes the biblical arc of the fountain motif: "And he shewed me a pure river of water of life, clear as crystal, proceeding out of the throne of God and of the Lamb. In the midst of the street of it, and on either side of the river, was there the tree of life, which bare twelve manner of fruits, and yielded her fruit every month: and the leaves of the tree were for the healing of the nations." In the eternal city, the river of life flows from the throne of God and the Lamb. There is no temple, no mediating institution, no barrier between God and His people. The fountain flows freely and openly, and all who dwell in the city drink from it forever.

Isaiah 12:3 captures the joyful experience of drawing from this fountain: "Therefore with joy shall ye draw water out of the wells of salvation." There is joy in this drawing — not drudgery, not duty, but delight. The wells of salvation are not dry or distant; they are full, accessible, and life-giving. Every act of prayer, every reading of Scripture, every moment of worship is a drawing from these wells. And the water never runs out.

The practical message of these passages is daily dependence upon Christ. Just as the physical body needs daily water to survive, the spiritual life needs daily refreshment from the fountain of Christ. We cannot live on yesterday's grace. We cannot sustain ourselves on past spiritual experiences. We need fresh water every day — fresh communion with Christ, fresh reading of His Word, fresh fellowship with His people, fresh surrender to His will. Jeremiah 2:13 records God's lament over Israel's departure from Him: "For my people have committed two evils; they have forsaken me the fountain of living waters, and hewed them out cisterns, broken cisterns, that can hold no water." The tragedy of the human condition is not merely that we sin but that we abandon the inexhaustible fountain of living water for broken cisterns of our own making — cisterns that crack and leak and leave us empty.

This biblical foundation is essential. Without it, everything else we study today would be like a house built on sand. But with it, we stand on solid rock.


Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. John Flavel wrote extensively on this subject in "The Fountain of Life." The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life.

John Flavel titled his masterwork on the person and work of Christ "The Fountain of Life," drawing from the rich biblical imagery of Christ as the source of all spiritual life and refreshment. For Flavel, this was not merely a convenient title but a theological statement of the highest order. Christ is the fountain — the origin, the source, the spring — from which all the blessings of salvation flow to the souls of the redeemed.

Flavel opened his work with a meditation on the inexhaustible sufficiency of Christ. He observed that earthly fountains, no matter how abundant, can eventually be drained or polluted. But the fountain of Christ's grace is infinite and eternal. It cannot be exhausted by the demands of millions of thirsty souls, nor can it be contaminated by the sins of those who come to drink. Flavel wrote: "Christ is an ever-flowing, ever-full, ever-free fountain of grace. All the saints that have ever drawn from this fountain have not diminished it by a single drop. And all the sins that have been washed in this fountain have not polluted it by a single stain."

Flavel explored the richness of the fountain metaphor with great pastoral skill. He observed that a fountain provides four essential services. First, it cleanses. Just as water washes away dirt and impurity, so the blood of Christ washes away sin. Zechariah 13:1 prophesied, "In that day there shall be a fountain opened to the house of David and to the inhabitants of Jerusalem for sin and for uncleanness." This fountain was opened at Calvary, when the blood and water flowed from Christ's pierced side (John 19:34). Every sin — past, present, and future — can be cleansed by this fountain.

Second, a fountain refreshes. The weary traveler who finds a spring of cool water in the desert is revived and strengthened. So the weary believer who draws near to Christ in prayer and worship is refreshed and renewed. Flavel noted that many Christians live in a state of spiritual dehydration — dry, tired, and listless — not because the fountain has run dry but because they have neglected to drink. The remedy for spiritual dryness is not more activity but more communion with Christ.

Third, a fountain sustains. Water is essential for life — without it, every living thing perishes. In the same way, spiritual life depends entirely upon the continuous supply of grace from Christ. The believer who neglects communion with Christ will not lose his salvation (for the fountain never fails), but he will lose his vitality, his joy, and his fruitfulness. Daily dependence upon Christ is not optional but essential.

Fourth, a fountain satisfies. Nothing quenches thirst like pure, fresh water. And nothing satisfies the soul like the grace of Christ. Flavel urged his hearers to stop trying to satisfy their souls with earthly substitutes — wealth, pleasure, fame, relationships — and to come to the only fountain that can truly satisfy. He echoed the invitation of Isaiah 55:1: "Ho, every one that thirsteth, come ye to the waters, and he that hath no money; come ye, buy, and eat; yea, come, buy wine and milk without money and without price."

Flavel devoted considerable attention to the means by which believers draw from the fountain of Christ. He identified several primary means of grace. Prayer is the bucket with which we draw water from the well. Scripture reading is the channel through which the fountain flows to us. Fellowship with other believers is the communal pool where we drink together. The Lord's Supper is the special feast where the fountain is set before us in visible, tangible form. Preaching is the public announcement that the fountain is open and all are invited to drink.

Flavel was also concerned to address those who felt unworthy to come to the fountain. He observed that many sensitive believers hold back from Christ because they feel their sins are too great, their faith is too weak, or their repentance is too shallow. To these struggling souls, Flavel offered words of tender encouragement. He reminded them that the invitation is to "whosoever" — not to the strong, the worthy, or the righteous, but to anyone who thirsts. The only qualification for coming to the fountain is thirst — a sense of need, a recognition of emptiness, a desire for what only Christ can give. Flavel wrote: "Christ does not invite the full but the thirsty. He does not call the righteous but sinners. If you feel your need of Christ, you are welcome. If you thirst for His grace, you may drink freely. The fountain is open, and no one who comes will be turned away."

The Puritan emphasis on daily dependence upon Christ was not mere theory but lived practice. The Puritans structured their daily lives around communion with Christ. They rose early for morning devotions, spent time in prayer and Scripture before beginning the day's work, paused at midday for reflection, and concluded the day with evening worship and self-examination. This rhythm of daily drawing from the fountain of Christ gave their lives a depth, stability, and joy that was evident to all who knew them.

Flavel's own life illustrated this principle. Despite persecution, poverty, and the loss of his first wife and several children, Flavel maintained a consistent, joyful devotion to Christ that astonished his contemporaries. When asked the secret of his spiritual vitality, Flavel pointed to the fountain: "I know no other source of strength, comfort, or joy than Jesus Christ. He is my fountain, and I drink from Him every day. Without Him, I would be the most miserable of men. With Him, I am the richest.'

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.


Let us now press deeper into the doctrine of Christ as the fountain of life, examining additional Scripture and Puritan insights that will enrich our understanding.

The Westminster Shorter Catechism, Question 1, asks: "What is the chief end of man?" The answer is: "Man's chief end is to glorify God, and to enjoy him for ever." Notice the word "enjoy." The fountain of life is not merely a doctrine to be studied but a reality to be experienced. God does not merely command our obedience; He invites our enjoyment. The fountain metaphor speaks of satisfaction, delight, refreshment, and pleasure. Psalm 16:11 declares: "Thou wilt shew me the path of life: in thy presence is fulness of joy; at thy right hand there are pleasures for evermore." The Christian life is not a grim march through a barren wilderness but a joyful pilgrimage to an overflowing fountain. The Puritans, contrary to popular caricature, were deeply committed to the enjoyment of God. They knew that the soul was made for God and that nothing less than God Himself can satisfy the deepest longings of the human heart.

Thomas Goodwin, the great Puritan theologian, explored the fountain of life metaphor with characteristic depth in his treatise "Christ Set Forth." Goodwin taught that Christ is not merely one fountain among many but the only fountain that can truly satisfy the human soul. All other fountains — wealth, pleasure, fame, relationships, achievement — are "broken cisterns that can hold no water" (Jeremiah 2:13). They promise satisfaction but deliver disappointment. They offer refreshment but leave the soul thirstier than before. Only Christ, the fountain of living water, can quench the soul's deepest thirst because only Christ can give what the soul truly needs: forgiveness for guilt, cleansing for defilement, strength for weakness, comfort for sorrow, and the presence of God Himself for the terrible emptiness that sin has created.

Goodwin distinguished between three kinds of spiritual thirst that drive the soul to the fountain of Christ. First, there is the thirst of conviction — the awareness of sin and the desperate need for forgiveness. This is the thirst that drove the prodigal son back to his father's house and that brought the publican to the temple to cry, "God be merciful to me a sinner" (Luke 18:13). Second, there is the thirst of communion — the desire for a deeper, richer experience of God's presence and love. This is the thirst of David when he wrote, "As the hart panteth after the water brooks, so panteth my soul after thee, O God. My soul thirsteth for God, for the living God: when shall I come and appear before God?" (Psalm 42:1-2). Third, there is the thirst of consummation — the longing for the day when all things will be made new and the fountain of life will flow without any barrier or interruption forever. This is the thirst that makes the believer cry with the apostle John, "Even so, come, Lord Jesus" (Revelation 22:20).

Consider the remarkable passage in Ezekiel 47:1-12, where the prophet sees a river flowing from the temple that grows deeper and wider as it flows, bringing life wherever it goes: "And it shall come to pass, that every thing that liveth, which moveth, whithersoever the rivers shall come, shall live: and there shall be a very great multitude of fish, because these waters shall come thither: for they shall be healed; and every thing shall live whither the river cometh" (Ezekiel 47:9). This vision looks forward to the life-giving power of Christ flowing from the throne of God to bring healing and renewal to a broken world. The river that begins as a trickle at the temple threshold becomes an uncrossable torrent — a picture of the ever-increasing fullness of Christ's grace that flows to His people in deeper and richer measures as they draw near to Him. The Puritans loved this passage because it assured them that the fountain of Christ is not static but dynamic — it does not merely drip; it flows, and it grows mightier with every passing generation until it covers the earth.

The prophet Joel also speaks of the fountain in connection with the last days: "And it shall come to pass in that day, that the mountains shall drop down new wine, and the hills shall flow with milk, and all the rivers of Judah shall flow with waters, and a fountain shall come forth of the house of the LORD, and shall water the valley of Shittim" (Joel 3:18). The valley of Shittim — a barren, dry valley — is watered by the fountain that comes from the Lord's house. This is a picture of the gospel: wherever the fountain of Christ's grace flows, barren lives are made fruitful, dry souls are refreshed, and dead hearts are brought to life.

Consider also the words of Jesus in John 7:37-39: "In the last day, that great day of the feast, Jesus stood and cried, saying, If any man thirst, let him come unto me, and drink. He that believeth on me, as the scripture hath said, out of his belly shall flow rivers of living water. (But this spake he of the Spirit, which they that believe on him should receive.)" John explains that the living water Jesus promised is the Holy Spirit Himself. The Holy Spirit is the fountain within the fountain — the divine Person who indwells the believer and makes the grace of Christ a living, flowing reality in the heart. Without the Spirit, we might know about the fountain of life intellectually but never experience it personally. It is the Spirit who takes the things of Christ and makes them real to us (John 16:14). He is the one who causes us to taste and see that the Lord is good (Psalm 34:8). He is the divine wellspring within who transforms the doctrine of Christ's sufficiency from abstract theology into lived experience.

The Puritan Richard Baxter, author of "The Saints' Everlasting Rest" and one of the most influential pastoral writers of the seventeenth century, also wrote movingly about Christ as the soul's fountain. Baxter, who suffered greatly from chronic illness throughout his life, found in Christ a sustaining fountain that compensated for every physical weakness and earthly deprivation. Baxter taught his readers to practice what he called "heavenly meditation" — the deliberate, daily exercise of fixing the mind on Christ and the glories of heaven. He argued that the believer who cultivates the habit of heavenly meditation will find that the fountain of Christ flows more abundantly in his experience than in the experience of one who merely reads about it. Meditation is the means by which the water of life percolates from the head to the heart, from doctrine to experience, from knowledge to joy.

Samuel Rutherford, writing from his exile in Aberdeen, expressed the believer's experience of the fountain of Christ with incomparable beauty. Rutherford was a man who knew suffering — he lost his wife and all of his children, was stripped of his pastoral charge, and was banished from the people he loved. Yet his letters overflow with the joy of a man who has drunk deeply from the fountain of life. Rutherford wrote: "I have been to the bottom of my Lord's well and have found that there is no bottom to it. The deeper I drink, the sweeter the water. The more I taste of Christ, the more I thirst for Him. He is a fountain without a shore, a sea without a bottom, a sun without a setting. I would not exchange one hour of communion with Christ for all the kingdoms of this world."


Let us now consider how the truth of Christ as the fountain of life should shape our daily practice.

First, this truth should establish in us a habit of morning devotion. Just as the body needs water upon waking, the soul needs the refreshment of Christ at the beginning of each day. The Puritans rose early — often before dawn — to spend time in prayer and Scripture before the demands of the day could crowd out the voice of God. They understood that the soul that starts the day at the fountain will have strength for whatever the day brings. Make it your practice to drink from the fountain of Christ before you drink anything else — before you check your responsibilities, before you begin your work, before you engage with the world. Come to Christ first, and everything else will be sanctified by His presence.

Second, this truth should rescue us from the tyranny of broken cisterns. Every human being is thirsty for something — for love, for approval, for significance, for pleasure, for security. The great tragedy of the human condition is that we go to the wrong places to satisfy these thirsts. We go to entertainment when we should go to worship. We go to social approval when we should go to divine acceptance. We go to material possessions when we should go to spiritual riches. Jeremiah's indictment of Israel applies to every generation: "My people have committed two evils; they have forsaken me the fountain of living waters, and hewed them out cisterns, broken cisterns, that can hold no water" (Jeremiah 2:13). The Puritan answer to this universal problem is simple and profound: come back to the fountain. Stop trying to fill the infinite emptiness of your soul with finite things. Come to Christ. He alone satisfies.

Third, this truth should make us generous toward others. A fountain does not keep its water to itself; it overflows and gives life to everything around it. Jesus said, "He that believeth on me, as the scripture hath said, out of his belly shall flow rivers of living water" (John 7:38). The believer who drinks deeply from the fountain of Christ becomes a fountain himself — a channel through which the grace, love, and truth of Christ flow to others. The Puritans were remarkably generous — with their time, their resources, their hospitality, and their spiritual counsel — because they understood that they were stewards of an overflowing fountain. Share what you have received. Give as you have been given to. Let the living water of Christ flow through you to a thirsty world.

Fourth, this truth should sustain us in times of spiritual dryness. Every believer goes through seasons when God seems distant, when prayer feels empty, when the Bible seems dry, and when the fountain appears to have run out. The Puritans called these seasons "desertions" and wrote extensively about how to endure them. Their counsel was consistent: do not abandon the means of grace. Keep reading the Bible, even when it feels lifeless. Keep praying, even when heaven seems silent. Keep gathering with God's people, even when you would rather be alone. The fountain has not dried up. It cannot dry up. It is fed by the infinite grace of an unchanging God. Your feelings may fluctuate, but the fountain flows on. Wait for the Lord. He will refresh you in His time.


Now let us pause and consider what we have learned. Discussion is where understanding deepens.

Discussion Question 1: Jesus said that whoever drinks the water He gives will 'never thirst' (John 4:14). What does this mean in light of the fact that Christians still desire more of Christ?

Discussion Question 2: Jeremiah 2:13 describes the tragedy of forsaking the 'fountain of living waters' for 'broken cisterns.' What are some 'broken cisterns' that people turn to instead of Christ?

Discussion Question 3: Flavel identified four services a fountain provides: cleansing, refreshing, sustaining, and satisfying. Which of these do you most need from Christ today?

Discussion Question 4: How do the means of grace — prayer, Scripture, fellowship, the Lord's Supper, preaching — function as channels through which we draw from the fountain of Christ?

Discussion Question 5: Why do many Christians live in a state of spiritual dryness? What practical steps can we take to drink more deeply from the fountain of life?

Take time with each question. The Puritans valued what they called "holy conference," the practice of discussing spiritual truths together. Do not settle for quick, surface answers. Push deeper. Ask follow-up questions. Listen carefully to one another.

Application is where truth becomes life. It is not enough to know what John 4:13-14 says. We must ask: how does this change the way I live today? How does this shape my thoughts, my words, my actions? The Puritans insisted that a truth that does not transform is a truth that has not been truly understood.


Now we move to our afternoon activity, where we put truth into practice.

Students will create a comprehensive 'Fountain of Life' study guide that synthesizes all five lessons on the work of Christ. The study guide should include: (1) a section on Christ as Prophet with key Scriptures and Flavel's insights, (2) a section on Christ as Priest with the comparison chart from Lesson 12, (3) a section on Christ as King with the implications for daily life, (4) a section on Union with Christ with the vine and branches imagery, and (5) a section on the Fountain of Life with the four services of the fountain. Each section should include memory verses, key definitions, and personal application points. This study guide serves as a review of the entire unit and as a resource students can keep for future reference.

Materials needed: Notebooks or blank booklets, pens, colored pencils for illustrations and highlighting, Bibles, notes from previous lessons in this unit

As a supplementary activity, students should create a "Broken Cisterns vs. Living Fountain" comparison chart. On one side of the page, list at least six "broken cisterns" that people commonly turn to instead of Christ (such as material possessions, entertainment, social media approval, romantic relationships, academic achievement, or physical appearance). For each, explain why it ultimately fails to satisfy the soul — why it is a cistern that "can hold no water." On the other side, describe how Christ as the fountain of living water meets the legitimate need that each broken cistern fails to meet. For example, people seek approval from peers because they need to feel valued and accepted; Christ meets this need perfectly because He accepts us fully and unconditionally in the beloved (Ephesians 1:6). People seek entertainment because they need joy and refreshment; Christ provides a joy that is deeper and more lasting than any entertainment the world can offer (Psalm 16:11). This exercise helps students identify the specific broken cisterns in their own lives and redirects their thirst toward the only fountain that can truly satisfy.

Additionally, students should read and meditate on Psalm 63:1-8, which is David's song of longing for God written during a time of exile in the wilderness. Note how David describes his thirst for God (v. 1), his satisfaction in God (v. 5), and his security in God (v. 8). Write a personal psalm of at least eight lines expressing your own thirst for God, your gratitude for His provision, and your confidence in His sufficiency. The Puritans frequently practiced this kind of devotional composition, following the model of the biblical psalms to give voice to their deepest spiritual experiences.

As you complete this activity, think about how it connects to what you studied in today's lesson. The goal is not just to do something, but to reinforce the truths you have been learning. The Puritans believed that education should engage the whole person: mind, heart, and hands.

Take your time with this activity. Quality matters more than speed. When you are finished, reflect on what you learned through the process of doing. Often we understand something far better after we have tried to put it into practice.


One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

Water has been one of the most important resources in human history. Ancient civilizations arose around water sources — the Nile, the Tigris and Euphrates, the Indus, the Yellow River. Roman aqueducts were engineering marvels that brought water over great distances to sustain cities. Study how the control and distribution of water has shaped human civilization and how the biblical metaphor of God as the "fountain of living waters" would have resonated powerfully with people in the arid Near East. In ancient Palestine, where much of the biblical narrative takes place, water was scarce and precious. The difference between a living spring and a dry cistern was literally the difference between life and death. The inhabitants of Judah and Galilee understood viscerally what it meant to depend on a fountain for survival. They knew the terror of drought and the jubilation of rain. When Jesus stood up at the Feast of Tabernacles — a harvest festival that included elaborate water ceremonies commemorating God's provision in the wilderness — and cried, "If any man thirst, let him come unto me, and drink" (John 7:37), His audience understood exactly what He was claiming. He was claiming to be the fulfillment of everything that the water ceremonies symbolized — the living water that God had promised His people, the fountain that would never fail. Study the historical context of the Feast of Tabernacles and the water ceremonies associated with it, and discuss how this context enriches our understanding of Jesus' declaration. Study also how the Puritans themselves experienced dependence on God's provision in their settlement of New England, where harsh winters and uncertain harvests taught them to look to the Lord as their daily fountain of sustenance.

Literature Connection:

Water imagery is one of the most universal and powerful symbol systems in world literature. From Homer's wine-dark sea to Coleridge's Ancient Mariner dying of thirst surrounded by water to Eliot's "The Waste Land" longing for rain, writers have used water to symbolize life, renewal, purification, and hope. Study how the biblical use of water imagery — from the rivers of Eden to the river of life in Revelation — provides the archetypal foundation for these literary treatments. John Bunyan, in "The Pilgrim's Progress," describes several scenes involving water that carry deep spiritual significance. Christian passes through the River of Death at the end of his journey, and the depth of the water corresponds to his faith — when his faith is strong, the water is shallow; when his faith wavers, the water rises. George Herbert, the great devotional poet, wrote of God as a fountain in his poem "The Pulley," which imagines God bestowing all His blessings upon humanity but withholding rest, so that weariness might drive the soul to its Maker. The Puritan hymn writer Anne Bradstreet, the first published poet in colonial America, also employed water imagery in her devotional poetry to express her dependence upon God's sustaining grace. Study how these literary works use water as a metaphor for divine grace, spiritual refreshment, and the soul's dependence upon God, and discuss how the biblical fountain of life provides the ultimate source of these literary traditions.

Science Connection:

Water is uniquely essential to life on earth. Study the remarkable properties of water that make it indispensable for biological processes: its role as a universal solvent, its unusual density behavior (ice floats, allowing aquatic life to survive winter), its high heat capacity (stabilizing climate), and its role in photosynthesis, cellular respiration, and DNA replication. These extraordinary properties of water reflect the design of a Creator who ordained water as the sustainer of physical life, just as He ordained Christ as the sustainer of spiritual life. Consider the hydrological cycle: water evaporates from the oceans, forms clouds, falls as rain, flows through rivers and streams, nourishes the earth, and returns to the oceans to begin the cycle again. This continuous cycle of giving, receiving, and giving again mirrors the spiritual economy of grace: God gives grace to us through Christ, we receive it by faith, and we pass it on to others through love and service. The water cycle also illustrates the concept of daily dependence — just as the earth needs continual cycles of rain, the soul needs continual refreshment from the fountain of Christ. A single rainfall does not sustain the earth forever; neither does a single spiritual experience sustain the soul forever. We need daily, repeated, ongoing communion with the fountain of life.

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.


Let us continue to mine the depths of this glorious doctrine with further Scripture and the testimony of the faithful Puritans who walked closely with the Fountain of Life.

Consider the beautiful words of Psalm 87:7: "As well the singers as the players on instruments shall be there: all my springs are in thee." The psalmist declares that all his springs — all his sources of refreshment, joy, strength, and vitality — are found in God alone. This is a comprehensive statement of dependence. Not some springs, not most springs, but all springs. Every good thing, every moment of comfort, every flash of insight, every season of fruitfulness finds its origin in God. The believer who grasps this truth will never look elsewhere for what only God can provide. He will stop digging broken cisterns because he has found the living spring. He will cease wandering in the desert because he has discovered the oasis that never dries.

The prophet Zechariah spoke of the fountain with particular reference to the cleansing power of Christ's blood: "In that day there shall be a fountain opened to the house of David and to the inhabitants of Jerusalem for sin and for uncleanness" (Zechariah 13:1). This prophecy looks forward to the cross of Christ, where the fountain was opened once and for all. The blood that flowed from Christ's wounds is the cleansing water of this fountain. It washes away not only the guilt of sin but the defilement of sin — the stain, the pollution, the corruption that sin leaves upon the soul. William Cowper's great hymn captures this truth with magnificent simplicity when it speaks of a fountain filled with blood drawn from Immanuel's veins, where sinners plunged beneath that flood lose all their guilty stains. Every believer, from the greatest saint to the most struggling sinner, needs this fountain daily. We do not outgrow our need for cleansing. We do not advance beyond our need for the blood of Christ. Every day, we come to the fountain afresh, and every day, its waters are sufficient to wash us clean.

Thomas Watson, one of the most beloved Puritan pastors, wrote extensively about the believer's daily dependence upon Christ. In his work "The Lord's Prayer," Watson observed that the petition "Give us this day our daily bread" teaches us the principle of daily dependence not only for physical sustenance but for spiritual sustenance as well. Watson wrote: "God will have us come to Him day by day, that we may feel our dependence upon Him. He does not give us grace in bulk, as a merchant might fill a warehouse with goods for the entire year. Rather, He gives us grace as a father gives bread to his children — one day at a time, fresh every morning, so that we must return to Him each day with empty hands and hungry hearts." This insight is profoundly practical. We cannot stockpile grace. We cannot store up spiritual strength for future use. We must come to the fountain every single day, acknowledging our emptiness and receiving His fullness afresh.

Watson also taught that daily dependence upon Christ is not a sign of weakness but a mark of wisdom. He observed that the manna God provided for Israel in the wilderness could not be stored overnight — it would breed worms and stink (Exodus 16:20). Only the manna gathered on the sixth day for the Sabbath was preserved by God's special providence. Watson drew the spiritual parallel: "As the manna must be gathered fresh each morning, so must the grace of Christ be sought fresh each day. Yesterday's grace will not sustain today's trials. Last week's communion with God will not carry you through this week's temptations. You must go to the fountain daily, or you will find yourself spiritually famished." This is why the Puritans were so insistent upon morning devotions. They understood that the soul needs daily nourishment just as the body does, and that the fountain of Christ is the only source of that nourishment.

John Owen, whom many regard as the greatest theological mind among the Puritans, explored the fountain of life doctrine with his characteristic precision in his treatise "The Glory of Christ." Owen taught that the believer's contemplation of Christ — His person, His work, His offices, His glory — is the primary means by which the soul is sustained and refreshed. Owen wrote: "The steady contemplation of the glory of Christ will carry us through all difficulties, trials, and temptations. For in beholding His glory, we are changed into the same image from glory to glory, as by the Spirit of the Lord." Owen drew this insight from 2 Corinthians 3:18: "But we all, with open face beholding as in a glass the glory of the Lord, are changed into the same image from glory to glory, even as by the Spirit of the Lord." The fountain of life is not merely a source of comfort; it is a source of transformation. As we drink from the fountain — as we contemplate Christ, meditate upon His Word, commune with Him in prayer — we are progressively changed into His likeness. The water of life does not merely quench our thirst; it transforms our nature.

Owen also addressed the question of why believers sometimes experience spiritual dryness even when they are faithful in the means of grace. He taught that God sometimes withdraws the felt sense of His presence in order to deepen the believer's longing, strengthen the believer's faith, and prepare the believer for a richer experience of His grace. Owen compared this to a father who briefly hides from a small child during a game — not to abandon the child but to increase the child's desire and delight in finding the father. Owen wrote: "When God seems to withdraw from us, He does not withdraw His love, only the sensible manifestations of it. His purpose is not to punish but to purify — to wean us from dependence upon feelings and to establish us in dependence upon His unfailing Word and unchanging character."

Richard Sibbes, known as "the sweet dropper" for his gentle, pastoral preaching, wrote beautifully about Christ as the fountain in his work "The Bruised Reed and Smoking Flax." Sibbes was particularly concerned to encourage weak and struggling believers — those whose faith was as feeble as a bruised reed and whose devotion was as faint as smoking flax. Sibbes taught that Christ does not break the bruised reed or quench the smoking flax (Isaiah 42:3; Matthew 12:20). Instead, He tenderly nourishes even the weakest faith and fans even the faintest spark of grace into a flame. Sibbes wrote: "Christ's work is to bring those that are bruised to a happy condition. For bruising makes way for healing. And those that are broken, He binds up. Those that are weak, He strengthens. Those that are cast down, He lifts up. Come to Him, then, you who are bruised and broken. Come to the fountain, you who are weak and weary. He will not turn you away. He will not despise your smallness. He will receive you tenderly, heal you gently, and sustain you faithfully until the day of your perfection."

This is one of the most precious truths about the fountain of life: it is accessible to the weakest believer. You do not need great faith to come to the fountain. You do not need great holiness or great knowledge or great spiritual accomplishment. You need only thirst. You need only come. The invitation is universal and unconditional: "Ho, every one that thirsteth, come ye to the waters" (Isaiah 55:1). "If any man thirst, let him come unto me, and drink" (John 7:37). "And whosoever will, let him take the water of life freely" (Revelation 22:17).

Jeremiah Burroughs, another faithful Puritan pastor, explored the theme of daily dependence in his classic work "The Rare Jewel of Christian Contentment." Burroughs taught that contentment is the fruit of daily drinking from the fountain of Christ. The discontented soul is a soul that has wandered from the fountain and is trying to satisfy its thirst with broken cisterns. The contented soul is one that has learned, through daily communion with Christ, that He is sufficient for every need, every circumstance, and every trial. Burroughs wrote: "Christian contentment is that sweet, inward, quiet, gracious frame of spirit, which freely submits to and delights in God's wise and fatherly disposal in every condition." This contentment does not come from favorable circumstances but from the fountain of Christ, who satisfies the soul regardless of outward conditions.

Burroughs distinguished between the contentment that the world offers and the contentment that Christ provides. The world's contentment depends on getting what you want — it is contentment by addition. Christ's contentment depends on wanting what you have — it is contentment by subtraction. When you have Christ, you have everything you need, because Christ is the fountain of all good things. When you lack Christ, you lack everything, no matter how much the world gives you. Burroughs observed that the apostle Paul exemplified this fountain-fed contentment when he wrote from prison: "I have learned, in whatsoever state I am, therewith to be content" (Philippians 4:11). Paul had learned the secret of daily dependence upon the fountain of Christ, and this made him content in every condition — whether in abundance or in want, whether in freedom or in chains.

Consider also the words of the psalmist in Psalm 46:4: "There is a river, the streams whereof shall make glad the city of God, the holy place of the tabernacles of the most High." Even in the midst of cosmic upheaval — when the mountains are shaking and the waters are roaring (Psalm 46:2-3) — there is a river that brings gladness to the people of God. This river flows from the throne of God and represents the continuous, uninterrupted supply of grace that sustains the church in every age. The world may be in turmoil. Kingdoms may rise and fall. Wars may rage and plagues may spread. But the river of God flows on, and those who dwell beside it are sustained, refreshed, and made glad.

Thomas Brooks, the Puritan author of "Precious Remedies against Satan's Devices," applied the fountain metaphor to the believer's warfare against temptation. Brooks taught that the primary reason believers fall into sin is that they have neglected the fountain. When the soul is well-watered with the grace of Christ — through prayer, Scripture reading, meditation, and fellowship — it is resistant to temptation. But when the soul is dry and parched — when communion with Christ has been neglected — it becomes vulnerable to the slightest temptation, just as a dry forest is vulnerable to the smallest spark. Brooks wrote: "Keep your soul well-watered with the grace of Christ, and the fires of temptation will be unable to take hold. But let your soul grow dry through neglect of the means of grace, and even a small temptation will set your whole life ablaze." This is profoundly practical counsel. The best defense against temptation is not greater willpower but greater communion with Christ. Come to the fountain daily, drink deeply, and you will find that the fires of temptation lose their power.

Jonathan Edwards, the great Puritan theologian of colonial New England, preached a series of sermons on the fountain of divine love that flows from the heart of God to the souls of the redeemed. Edwards taught that God is an infinite fountain of love, goodness, and beauty, and that the purpose of creation and redemption is to display the overflowing fullness of this fountain. Edwards wrote: "God is glorified not only by His glory being seen, but by its being rejoiced in. When those that see it delight in it, God is more glorified than if they only see it. God made the world that He might communicate His fullness — that the streams of His goodness might flow forth. And the way this fullness is communicated is by the creature beholding it, and rejoicing in it, and being satisfied by it." For Edwards, the fountain of life is not merely a metaphor; it is the central reality of the universe. God is the fountain, creation is the stream, and the destiny of the redeemed is to drink from this fountain forever.

Edwards also explored the eschatological dimension of the fountain — the future, eternal enjoyment of God that awaits the redeemed in heaven. Edwards taught that the fountain of God's love and glory is inexhaustible and that the saints will spend all eternity exploring its depths without ever reaching the bottom. Edwards wrote with characteristic precision: "The enjoyment of God by the saints in heaven will be progressive — they will be ever discovering new beauties, new glories, new dimensions of God's infinite perfection. The fountain will never run dry, and the saints will never tire of drinking from it. Their capacity for joy will increase forever, and as their capacity increases, so will their enjoyment. Eternity will not be long enough to exhaust the fountain of God's infinite goodness."

This eschatological hope gives us strength for the present. We drink from the fountain now by faith, in the midst of a fallen world, through imperfect means of grace. But one day, we will drink from the fountain by sight, in the midst of a perfected creation, without any barrier between our souls and the infinite God who is our source, our sustenance, and our supreme delight. As Revelation 21:6 promises: "I will give unto him that is athirst of the fountain of the water of life freely." And Revelation 22:17 extends the invitation one final time: "And the Spirit and the bride say, Come. And let him that heareth say, Come. And let him that is athirst come. And whosoever will, let him take the water of life freely."

Let us also reflect upon the practical rhythm of daily dependence as the Puritans practiced it. The Puritans divided their day into three seasons of devotion. In the morning, they drew from the fountain through prayer and Scripture reading, preparing their souls for the challenges of the day ahead. At midday, they paused to refresh themselves at the fountain, reviewing the morning's meditation and lifting brief prayers for strength and guidance. In the evening, they returned to the fountain for a longer season of self-examination, thanksgiving, and contemplation of God's faithfulness throughout the day. This threefold rhythm ensured that the soul was never far from the fountain and that the believer lived in a state of continual dependence upon Christ. David practiced this same rhythm: "Evening, and morning, and at noon, will I pray, and cry aloud: and he shall hear my voice" (Psalm 55:17). Daniel also maintained this discipline, even when it cost him a night in the lions' den: "He kneeled upon his knees three times a day, and prayed, and gave thanks before his God, as he did aforetime" (Daniel 6:10).

The fountain of life doctrine is not merely one truth among many. It is the central truth from which all other truths flow. Christ is the fountain of our justification — in Him we are declared righteous. Christ is the fountain of our sanctification — by His Spirit we are made holy. Christ is the fountain of our adoption — through Him we are brought into God's family. Christ is the fountain of our perseverance — by His power we are kept from falling. Christ is the fountain of our glorification — in His presence we will be perfected forever. Every blessing, every grace, every spiritual gift flows from this one inexhaustible fountain. And therefore, every day, in every circumstance, through every trial and every triumph, we must come back to the fountain, drink deeply, and acknowledge our absolute, total, daily dependence upon Jesus Christ, the Fountain of Life.

Closing Reflection:

As we bring this lesson to a close, let us hear once more the invitation of Christ the fountain: "If any man thirst, let him come unto me, and drink" (John 7:37). This invitation stands open today, tomorrow, and forever. It is not limited to the worthy or the strong or the spiritually accomplished. It is addressed to the thirsty — to anyone who feels the emptiness, the dryness, the aching need that nothing in this world can fill. Come to Christ. Drink from the fountain that never runs dry. And discover for yourself the truth that has sustained millions of believers through twenty centuries of joy and sorrow, triumph and trial: Christ is enough. He is more than enough. He is the fountain of life, and with Him is fullness of joy forever.

The great Puritan pastor John Owen, reflecting on the sufficiency of Christ, wrote with this magnificent simplicity: "In Christ there is a fullness of all that we stand in need of. There is a fullness of merit to procure our pardon, a fullness of Spirit to sanctify our natures, a fullness of grace to adorn our souls, a fullness of truth to guide our understandings, a fullness of power to subdue our enemies, and a fullness of glory to reward our sufferings. All this fullness is in Christ, and all this fullness is for us — freely, fully, and forever." May this fullness be your daily experience and your eternal inheritance. Drink deeply. Drink often. And never, ever stop coming back to the fountain, for it is inexhaustible, unfailing, and free.
