PURITAN GOLD -- Study Topic: The Book of Enoch, Part 8 of 10
"The Dream Visions (1 Enoch 83-90)"
The Book of Enoch — Animal Apocalypse and Israel's history

---

Grace and peace to you, beloved. Today we come to one of the most creative and imaginative sections of the Book of Enoch — the Dream Visions found in chapters 83 through 90. This section contains two visions that Enoch purportedly received before his marriage, and the second of them — known as the Animal Apocalypse — is one of the most remarkable pieces of Jewish apocalyptic literature ever composed.

Let us read from Ezekiel 34:30-31:

"Thus shall they know that I the LORD their God am with them, and that they, even the house of Israel, are my people, saith the Lord GOD. And ye my flock, the flock of my pasture, are men, and I am your God, saith the Lord GOD."

--- Teaching ---

The first Dream Vision, contained in chapters 83 and 84, is relatively brief. Enoch sees a vision of the earth being swallowed up in a great abyss — a premonition of the flood that will come in the days of Noah. The vision is terrifying, and Enoch responds with prayer, pleading with God to preserve a remnant upon the earth. His grandfather Mahalalel counsels him to pray, and Enoch's prayer is a beautiful expression of faith in God's sovereignty even in the face of impending judgment.

This pattern — vision of judgment followed by intercessory prayer — is deeply biblical. Abraham interceded for Sodom (Genesis 18:23-32). Moses interceded for Israel after the golden calf (Exodus 32:11-14). Daniel interceded for his people in exile (Daniel 9:3-19). The great figures of Scripture do not merely observe God's judgments from a distance; they enter into them through prayer, pleading for mercy on behalf of those under threat. The Enochic tradition places Enoch squarely in this prophetic tradition of intercession.

The second Dream Vision — the Animal Apocalypse in chapters 85 through 90 — is a far more elaborate composition. It retells the entire history of the world, from Adam to the messianic age, using an allegorical system in which human beings and nations are represented by animals. This type of allegorical history was not unique to 1 Enoch; it finds parallels in the canonical Scriptures as well. Ezekiel 34 uses the imagery of sheep and shepherds to describe Israel and its leaders. Daniel's visions employ a ram, a goat, and other beasts to represent successive empires. Our Lord Himself used the image of sheep and goats in His parable of the final judgment.

In the Animal Apocalypse, the patriarchs are represented as bulls. Adam is a white bull. Cain and Abel are a black bull and a red calf, respectively. The narrative follows the biblical story closely: the murder of Abel, the line of Seth, the corruption of the antediluvian world, and the flood. Noah is a white bull who is saved in an ark while the rest of the world perishes.

After the flood, the narrative shifts. The patriarchs from Abraham onward are represented as sheep, and Israel becomes a flock under God's care. This is a natural development of the biblical metaphor. The Lord is a shepherd and Israel is His flock — an image that pervades the Psalms, the Prophets, and the teaching of our Lord: "I am the good shepherd: the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep" (John 10:11).

The history of Israel is told with remarkable fidelity to the biblical account, though in compressed allegorical form. Moses is a sheep who is elevated to become a man — a unique transformation that symbolizes his special role as mediator between God and His people. The conquest of Canaan, the period of the judges, the monarchy of David and Solomon, the division of the kingdom, the exile, and the return are all depicted through the imagery of sheep, their enemies (represented as wolves, lions, eagles, and other predators), and the angelic shepherds whom God appoints to oversee His flock.

One of the most theologically significant elements of the Animal Apocalypse is its treatment of the angelic shepherds. God, angry with His flock for their unfaithfulness, hands them over to seventy shepherds — angelic beings tasked with disciplining Israel. However, these shepherds exceed their mandate, destroying more of the flock than God had decreed. This detail illuminates a profound theological truth: God uses secondary agents to accomplish His purposes, but those agents bear responsibility for their own actions. The Babylonians were God's instrument for judging Israel, yet they were judged in turn for their cruelty (Isaiah 10:5-12, Habakkuk 1-2). The seventy shepherds of the Animal Apocalypse will themselves face judgment for their excessive violence.

This principle of divine sovereignty operating through morally accountable agents is fundamental to Reformed theology. The Westminster Confession of Faith states that "God, from all eternity, did, by the most wise and holy counsel of His own will, freely, and unchangeably ordain whatsoever comes to pass: yet so, as thereby neither is God the author of sin, nor is violence offered to the will of the creatures." The Animal Apocalypse, in its own way, grapples with this same mystery — the mystery of how God governs history through agents who are themselves responsible for their choices.

The vision reaches its climax with the appearance of a great horned sheep — generally understood as a symbol of Judas Maccabeus, the Jewish hero who led the revolt against the Seleucid Empire in the second century before Christ. The Maccabean revolt was a defining moment in Second Temple Judaism, and many scholars believe the Animal Apocalypse was written during or shortly after this period. The great horned sheep fights valiantly against the enemies of the flock, and God Himself intervenes to bring final deliverance.

The apocalypse concludes with a vision of the new Jerusalem. A new house is built — greater and more glorious than the first — and all the scattered sheep are gathered into it. The nations are converted and join the flock. And then, in the closing verses, a white bull is born — a return to the original symbolism of the patriarchal period. This white bull represents the Messiah, the one who will restore humanity to its original glory. All the animals of the earth become white bulls — humanity is transformed and renewed, returning to the state of primordial purity.

For the Christian reader, this vision of cosmic restoration points unmistakably toward the new creation promised in Christ. "Behold, I make all things new" (Revelation 21:5). The Messiah is not merely a political deliverer but the agent of universal transformation — the one through whom all creation is renewed and restored to its original purpose.

--- Application ---

1. See in the history of Israel the pattern of God's dealings with all His people: faithfulness, rebellion, judgment, and restoration. God does not abandon His flock, even when they wander. He disciplines in order to restore.

2. Trust in God's sovereign governance of history. The rise and fall of empires, the movements of nations, the great events of human history — all are under His hand. No earthly power operates independently of His decree.

3. Hold accountable those who abuse their authority. The angelic shepherds who exceeded their mandate faced judgment. So too will every leader — in church, in state, in family — who misuses the authority God has given.

4. Look forward to the new creation with confidence. The vision of the white bull — humanity restored to its original glory — is not a fantasy but a promise. In Christ, all things will be made new.

--- Closing ---

O Great Shepherd of the sheep, who hast watched over Thy flock through all the ages of history — through rebellion and exile, through judgment and restoration — we praise Thee for Thy faithfulness. Thou hast never abandoned Thy people. Thou hast disciplined us in love and restored us in mercy. Bring us at last to that new Jerusalem where Thy flock shall dwell in safety forever, through Jesus Christ, the Lamb who was slain. Amen.

Until we gather again, beloved, may the Good Shepherd guide your steps and keep you in His fold. Grace and peace be with you.
