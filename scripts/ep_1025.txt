PURITAN GOLD -- Study Topic: The Book of Enoch, Part 5 of 10
"The Parables of Enoch (1 Enoch 37-44)"
The Book of Enoch — The Son of Man and messianic prophecy

---

Welcome, beloved friends, to what may be the most theologically significant portion of our study. Today we turn to the Parables of Enoch — also called the Similitudes — found in chapters 37 through 71 of 1 Enoch. In this episode, we shall examine chapters 37 through 44, which introduce one of the most remarkable figures in all of Jewish apocalyptic literature: the Son of Man.

Let us begin with the words of our Lord Jesus Christ from Matthew 26:64:

"Jesus saith unto him, Thou hast said: nevertheless I say unto you, Hereafter shall ye see the Son of man sitting on the right hand of power, and coming in the clouds of heaven."

--- Teaching ---

The Parables of Enoch occupy a unique place in the study of Jewish messianic expectation. Unlike the other sections of 1 Enoch, no fragments of the Parables have been found among the Dead Sea Scrolls, leading some scholars to date them to the first century before or after Christ. Others maintain an earlier date. The question of dating is important because it bears upon the relationship between the Parables and the New Testament — but regardless of the precise chronology, the theological connections are profound and illuminating.

The Parables are introduced as "the second vision of wisdom" which Enoch received. They are structured as three parables or extended visions, each revealing aspects of God's plan for the final judgment and the vindication of the righteous. At the center of these visions stands a figure of surpassing glory and authority: the Son of Man, the Righteous One, the Elect One, the Anointed One.

The title "Son of Man" immediately recalls the vision of Daniel 7:13-14: "I saw in the night visions, and, behold, one like the Son of man came with the clouds of heaven, and came to the Ancient of days, and they brought him near before him. And there was given him dominion, and glory, and a kingdom, that all people, nations, and languages, should serve him: his dominion is an everlasting dominion, which shall not pass away, and his kingdom that which shall not be destroyed."

In the Book of Daniel, the "Son of man" appears as a heavenly figure — one who approaches the Ancient of Days and receives universal, everlasting dominion. The Parables of Enoch take this Danielic vision and develop it with extraordinary richness. The Son of Man in the Parables is not merely a figure in a single vision but a central character in an extended drama of judgment and salvation.

In 1 Enoch chapter 46, the Son of Man is described as having a countenance "full of graciousness, like one of the holy angels." He is seated beside the "Head of Days" — a clear parallel to Daniel's "Ancient of Days" — and he possesses authority to reveal "all the treasures of that which is hidden." Here is a figure who is both human in appearance and heavenly in authority — one who shares the throne of God and possesses divine knowledge.

Chapter 48 is particularly remarkable. The Son of Man is described as one who was "chosen and hidden before Him, before the creation of the world and for evermore." His name was "named before the Lord of Spirits" before the stars were made. He is "the light of the Gentiles" and "the hope of those who are troubled of heart." Compare this with the language of the New Testament: Christ was "foreordained before the foundation of the world" (1 Peter 1:20); He is "a light to lighten the Gentiles, and the glory of thy people Israel" (Luke 2:32); He is the "hope of glory" (Colossians 1:27).

The parallels are striking, and they raise an important question: Was the author of the Parables drawing upon a tradition that also informed the New Testament writers, or was he writing after the coming of Christ, under Christian influence? Scholars disagree, and certainty is not possible. What we can say is that the Parables of Enoch demonstrate that the concept of a preexistent, heavenly messianic figure was present in Jewish thought — a concept that the New Testament writers would have recognized and employed.

The Son of Man in the Parables is given four primary roles. First, he is the Judge. In chapter 38, the righteous pray for the Son of Man to appear and execute judgment upon the wicked. In chapter 41, Enoch sees the Son of Man sitting on the throne of glory, judging the deeds of the holy ones in heaven. Second, he is the Revealer of secrets. He possesses the wisdom of God and makes known the hidden things. Third, he is the Vindicator of the righteous. The oppressed and persecuted people of God look to him for deliverance. Fourth, he is the Destroyer of the wicked. Kings and the mighty who have oppressed the righteous will fall before him in terror.

Every one of these roles finds its fulfillment in Christ. Our Lord is the Judge to whom the Father has committed all judgment (John 5:22). He is the one in whom "are hid all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge" (Colossians 2:3). He is the Vindicator who shall wipe away every tear from the eyes of His people (Revelation 21:4). He is the one before whom every knee shall bow and every tongue confess (Philippians 2:10-11).

Chapters 38 through 44 also contain vivid descriptions of the fate of the wicked. The kings and the mighty who have denied the Lord of Spirits and His Anointed shall be driven from the presence of the Son of Man. Their faces shall be "filled with darkness and shame," and they shall have "no one to deliver them." The language is sobering and intended to be so. The certainty of divine judgment is a truth that runs throughout both the canonical and extra-canonical literature of this period.

The Puritan John Owen, in his magnificent work on the person of Christ, emphasized that the titles and offices of the Messiah were progressively revealed throughout the Old Testament and intertestamental periods. The full glory of Christ could not be comprehended from any single text, whether canonical or extra-canonical, but required the cumulative witness of prophecy, type, and vision. The Parables of Enoch, while not inspired Scripture, represent a significant chapter in this unfolding revelation — a witness from within Second Temple Judaism to the expectation of a divine-human deliverer who would judge the world in righteousness and establish God's kingdom forever.

What is most important for us as Christian readers is that these expectations were not disappointed. The Son of Man did come. He came not first in clouds of glory but in the humility of a manger, to live and die for sinners. And He shall come again — this time in the very glory that the Parables envision — to judge the living and the dead and to bring in the everlasting kingdom.

--- Application ---

1. Worship Christ as the Son of Man who fulfills all messianic expectation. The hopes and visions of the ages find their "Yes" and "Amen" in Him. Let your faith rest not in human speculation but in the revealed Christ of Scripture.

2. Take comfort that the Judge of all the earth is also your Savior. The Son of Man who sits upon the throne of judgment is the same Jesus who died for your sins. If God is for us, who can be against us?

3. Let the hope of Christ's return shape your daily life. The Parables call for patient endurance from the righteous in the face of present suffering. So too does the New Testament: "Be patient therefore, brethren, unto the coming of the Lord" (James 5:7).

4. Remember that Christ was chosen before the foundation of the world. Your salvation is not an afterthought or a contingency plan. It is the eternal purpose of God, decreed before the stars were made.

--- Closing ---

Lord Jesus Christ, Thou Son of Man and Son of God, chosen before the foundation of the world and revealed in the fullness of time — we worship Thee. Thou art the Judge, the Revealer, the Vindicator, and the King. Strengthen our faith as we await Thy glorious return, and keep us faithful until that day. For Thine is the kingdom and the power and the glory, forever and ever. Amen.

Go in peace, beloved, and let the hope of Christ's coming fill your hearts with joy until we meet again.
