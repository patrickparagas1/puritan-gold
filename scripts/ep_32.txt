PURITAN GOLD -- Study Devotional, Day 1
Series: Romans
"The Gospel of God"
Paul's greeting and the revelation of God's righteousness by faith

---

Good evening, and welcome to our study devotional from Puritan Gold. We are continuing our journey through one of the most important books in all of Scripture. The Puritans treasured careful, verse-by-verse study, and tonight we follow in their footsteps.

We are studying the book of Romans together. Tonight's lesson is titled "The Gospel of God." Each session builds on what came before, so if you have been following along, you will see how tonight's passage connects to the larger argument of the book.

--- Scripture Reading ---

Let us read our passage for tonight. It comes from Romans 1:1-17:

"Paul, a servant of Jesus Christ, called to be an apostle, separated unto the gospel of God, (Which he had promised afore by his prophets in the holy scriptures,) Concerning his Son Jesus Christ our Lord, which was made of the seed of David according to the flesh; And declared to be the Son of God with power, according to the spirit of holiness, by the resurrection from the dead... For I am not ashamed of the gospel of Christ: for it is the power of God unto salvation to every one that believeth; to the Jew first, and also to the Greek. For therein is the righteousness of God revealed from faith to faith: as it is written, The just shall live by faith."

Read that passage again to yourself. Mark any words or phrases that stand out. The Puritans read with a pen in hand, always ready to note what God was showing them.

--- Key Verse ---

Let us focus on one verse that captures the heart of tonight's passage.

"For I am not ashamed of the gospel of Christ: for it is the power of God unto salvation to every one that believeth; to the Jew first, and also to the Greek." -- Romans 1:16

This verse is the lens through which we will examine the rest of tonight's passage. Keep it in the front of your mind as we proceed.

--- Exposition ---

Now let us move into the exposition of this passage.

Martyn Lloyd-Jones offers valuable commentary on this passage. The Puritans were expositors of the highest order, and their insights remain remarkably fresh.

Martyn Lloyd-Jones devoted years of Friday evening lectures to Romans at Westminster Chapel, and he regarded these opening seventeen verses as containing the whole of the Christian gospel in seed form. Lloyd-Jones insisted that verse 16 is the heartbeat of the entire epistle: the gospel is not advice, not a philosophy, not a moral code, but 'the power of God unto salvation.' He drew careful attention to the word 'power' (Greek: dunamis), noting that it is not merely a message about power but is itself the very instrument through which God saves sinners. The gospel does not merely describe what God has done; it actually accomplishes what it declares.

Lloyd-Jones further emphasized that verse 17 is the theological key that unlocks everything that follows in Romans. The phrase 'the righteousness of God' does not refer merely to an attribute of God's character, but to a righteousness that God provides and imputes to the believing sinner. This was the very truth that broke open the Reformation when Luther grasped it. Lloyd-Jones followed the Reformers in reading 'from faith to faith' as meaning that this righteousness is received entirely by faith, from first to last, with no admixture of human works or merit.

He also stressed Paul's self-designation as 'a servant of Jesus Christ.' The Greek word doulos means a bond-slave, one who has no rights of his own and exists entirely for the master's purposes. Lloyd-Jones saw in this Paul's utter submission to Christ and argued that every genuine Christian must come to this same place of surrender. There is no gospel without lordship, and no lordship without the gospel. The two are inseparable. This opening salvo sets the tone for everything Romans will teach: God is sovereign, man is helpless, and salvation comes entirely by grace through faith in the Lord Jesus Christ.

The epistle to the Romans is the most systematic and comprehensive statement of Christian theology in the entire Bible. Paul wrote it from Corinth around 57 AD, addressing a church he had never visited, laying out the gospel with a logical precision that has shaped Christian thought for two millennia. These opening seventeen verses function as a preface to the entire letter, and they contain in compressed form every major theme Paul will develop.

Paul begins by identifying himself as 'a servant of Jesus Christ, called to be an apostle, separated unto the gospel of God.' Notice three things about his self-description. First, he is a doulos, a bond-slave. The man who had once been a Pharisee of the Pharisees, a Hebrew of the Hebrews, now counts all his former privileges as loss for the excellency of knowing Christ (Philippians 3:7-8). Second, he is 'called' — his apostleship is not self-appointed but divinely commissioned. Third, he is 'separated unto the gospel of God.' From his mother's womb, God had set him apart for this very purpose (Galatians 1:15).

The gospel Paul preaches is not a novelty. It was 'promised afore by his prophets in the holy scriptures.' The Old Testament is a gospel book. Isaiah spoke of the suffering servant who would bear our iniquities (Isaiah 53). Jeremiah prophesied a new covenant written on the heart (Jeremiah 31:31-34). David foresaw the resurrection of the Holy One who would not see corruption (Psalm 16:10). The gospel has deep roots in the whole counsel of God.

This gospel concerns 'his Son Jesus Christ our Lord.' Paul presents Christ in His full identity. According to the flesh, He was 'made of the seed of David,' fulfilling the covenant promise that David's throne would be established forever (2 Samuel 7:12-13). But He was also 'declared to be the Son of God with power, according to the spirit of holiness, by the resurrection from the dead.' The resurrection is the great vindication. Jesus claimed to be the Son of God, and God proved it by raising Him from the dead. As Peter declared at Pentecost, 'God hath made that same Jesus, whom ye have crucified, both Lord and Christ' (Acts 2:36).

In verses 8 through 15, Paul expresses his deep desire to visit the Roman believers. He thanks God for their faith, prays for them constantly, and longs to impart some spiritual gift to strengthen them. Notice his pastoral heart — this is not a cold theologian writing abstract doctrine but a shepherd yearning over souls. He desires mutual encouragement, 'that I may be comforted together with you by the mutual faith both of you and me.' Even an apostle needs fellowship.

Then comes the thunderclap of verse 16: 'For I am not ashamed of the gospel of Christ: for it is the power of God unto salvation to every one that believeth; to the Jew first, and also to the Greek.' Why would Paul need to say he is not ashamed? Because the gospel is scandalous. In sophisticated Rome, with its philosophy, power, and culture, the message of a crucified Jewish carpenter seemed foolishness. Paul himself acknowledged this in 1 Corinthians 1:23: 'We preach Christ crucified, unto the Jews a stumblingblock, and unto the Greeks foolishness.' Yet this despised message is nothing less than the power of God unto salvation.

The word 'power' here is dunamis, from which we get dynamite. But the gospel is not merely explosive; it is effective. It actually accomplishes salvation. It is not a potential power waiting to be activated by human effort; it is the operative power of God that creates faith, regenerates the dead heart, and brings sinners into right standing with the Almighty. And it is offered 'to every one that believeth' — Jew and Gentile alike. The ground is level at the foot of the cross.

Verse 17 is the thesis statement of the entire epistle: 'For therein is the righteousness of God revealed from faith to faith: as it is written, The just shall live by faith.' This verse changed the world when Martin Luther grasped its meaning. The 'righteousness of God' is not His punitive justice that condemns sinners, but His saving righteousness that He freely gives to those who believe. It is an alien righteousness — not our own, but Christ's — credited to our account by faith alone.

The quotation is from Habakkuk 2:4, a text that appears three times in the New Testament (here, Galatians 3:11, and Hebrews 10:38). Each usage emphasizes a different word: Romans stresses 'the just,' Galatians stresses 'by faith,' and Hebrews stresses 'shall live.' Together they proclaim that the one who is righteous by faith shall live — now and forever.

The phrase 'from faith to faith' has been interpreted various ways. Some take it as from the faith of the Old Testament saints to the faith of New Testament believers, showing continuity in God's saving method. Others read it as faith from beginning to end — the righteous life starts in faith and continues in faith. Calvin understood it as growing faith, from one degree to another. However we read it, the emphasis is unmistakable: faith is the sole instrument by which we receive God's righteousness. It is not faith plus works, faith plus circumcision, faith plus law-keeping. It is faith alone, sola fide, from start to finish.

This is the gospel Paul will spend sixteen chapters unpacking. He will show that all are under sin (chapters 1-3), that justification is by faith alone (chapters 3-5), that the justified are freed from sin's dominion (chapters 6-8), that God's sovereign purposes include both Jew and Gentile (chapters 9-11), and that the redeemed are called to live transformed lives (chapters 12-16). It is the most magnificent theological argument ever written, and it all begins here with one glorious declaration: the just shall live by faith.

--- Application ---

Now let us consider: how does this truth apply to our lives? The Puritans never separated doctrine from practice. Every truth they studied was immediately applied.

How does this opening passage of Romans apply to your daily life? Consider these specific steps.

First, examine your identity. Paul called himself a servant of Jesus Christ before he mentioned his apostleship. In our culture of self-promotion and personal branding, we are tempted to lead with our accomplishments. But the highest honor any human can possess is to be a bond-slave of Jesus Christ. This week, begin each morning by consciously acknowledging your identity: you belong to Christ, not to yourself. Let that truth shape every decision you make — at work, in your family, in how you spend your time and money.

Second, are you ashamed of the gospel? Perhaps not overtly, but functionally. Do you avoid spiritual conversations at work? Do you water down the message when speaking with educated or sophisticated people? Paul was writing to the capital of the world's greatest empire, and he declared without hesitation that the gospel is the power of God. Identify one person in your life who needs to hear the gospel this week, and resolve to share it with them — not with clever arguments but with simple, bold declaration of what God has done in Christ.

Third, memorize Romans 1:16-17 this week. These verses are the foundation of everything that follows. Write them on a card, put them on your phone's lock screen, repeat them in your prayers. Let the truth of justification by faith alone sink deep into your bones. When guilt accuses you, when your failures haunt you, when Satan whispers that you are not good enough, answer him with this: 'The just shall live by faith.' Your standing before God rests not on your performance but on the finished work of Christ, received by faith alone.

Fourth, pray for boldness. Paul asked the Ephesians to pray 'that utterance may be given unto me, that I may open my mouth boldly, to make known the mystery of the gospel' (Ephesians 6:19). If an apostle needed prayer for boldness, how much more do we? Ask God to remove the fear of man and replace it with a holy confidence in His power to save.

Write down one concrete action you will take this week in response to tonight's study. Do not let this truth remain theoretical. The Puritans measured the success of a sermon not by how much the hearer learned but by how much the hearer changed.

--- Closing ---

Almighty God, we stand in awe of Your gospel — the power of God unto salvation. Forgive us for the times we have been ashamed of Your truth. Grant us the boldness of Paul, the faith of Abraham, and the humility of bond-servants. Root us deeply in the righteousness that comes by faith alone in Christ alone. Let the truth of Romans transform our minds and set our hearts ablaze. May we never tire of this glorious message. In the name of Jesus Christ our Lord, Amen.

Until next time, keep studying. The Word of God will never run dry.
