SEGMENT 1: MORNING DEVOTION
----------------------------------------

Welcome to today's lesson. The Puritans were some of the most learned men and women of their age, and yet they counted all their learning as nothing compared to knowing Christ. Let us follow their example today.

God of all knowledge, we begin with You. Without Your light, even the brightest minds walk in darkness. Shine Your truth into our studies today. Help us to love learning and to learn in love. For the glory of Your name. Amen.

Let us read a passage slowly, letting each word settle into our minds.

Our Scripture for today is Hebrews 11:6; James 1:5-8; Mark 11:22-24; 1 John 5:14-15:

"But without faith it is impossible to please him: for he that cometh to God must believe that he is, and that he is a rewarder of them that diligently seek him. If any of you lack wisdom, let him ask of God, that giveth to all men liberally, and upbraideth not; and it shall be given him. But let him ask in faith, nothing wavering. For he that wavereth is like a wave of the sea driven with the wind and tossed. For let not that man think that he shall receive any thing of the Lord. A double minded man is unstable in all his ways. And Jesus answering saith unto them, Have faith in God. For verily I say unto you, That whosoever shall say unto this mountain, Be thou removed, and be thou cast into the sea; and shall not doubt in his heart, but shall believe that those things which he saith shall come to pass; he shall have whatsoever he saith. Therefore I say unto you, What things soever ye desire, when ye pray, believe that ye receive them, and ye shall have them. And this is the confidence that we have in him, that, if we ask any thing according to his will, he heareth us: And if we know that he hear us, whatsoever we ask, we know that we have the petitions that we desired of him."

When you pray today, pause before you begin and remind yourself of who you are addressing. You are not casting words into an empty void. You are not performing a ritual before an indifferent deity. You are speaking to the living God — the One who flung the stars into space, who knows the number of hairs on your head, who loved you before the foundation of the world and gave His only Son to redeem you. This God invites you to ask. This God promises to hear. This God delights to give good gifts to His children. Come to Him now with faith — not faith in your own worthiness, but faith in His faithfulness. Bring your greatest need, your deepest longing, your most impossible situation, and lay it before the throne of grace. He is able to do exceeding abundantly above all that you ask or think. Believe it. Pray it. Expect it.

As we prepare for today's lesson, remember that everything we study flows from this one fountain: the Word of God. Our lesson today is part of The Life of Prayer, and it is titled: Praying with Faith — Expecting God to Answer. Let us proceed with minds engaged and hearts open.

SEGMENT 2: MAIN LESSON PART 1 -- BIBLICAL FOUNDATION
----------------------------------------

Let us proceed to the main body of today's teaching.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes in Hebrews 11:6; James 1:5-8; Mark 11:22-24; 1 John 5:14-15.

The writer to the Hebrews makes a declaration that should arrest the attention of every person who has ever bowed a knee in prayer: "Without faith it is impossible to please him: for he that cometh to God must believe that he is, and that he is a rewarder of them that diligently seek him" (Hebrews 11:6). This verse establishes two foundational requirements for all who approach God. First, we must believe that He exists — that the God to whom we pray is real, personal, and present. Second, we must believe that He is a rewarder — that He is not indifferent to those who seek Him but actively, generously, faithfully responds to those who come to Him in sincerity.

These two requirements are not merely intellectual acknowledgments but deep convictions of the heart that shape the entire character of our prayer life. To believe that God is means to believe that behind the silence of heaven there is a living Person who hears every word, sees every tear, and knows every need. To believe that He is a rewarder means to come expecting something — not demanding, not dictating, but genuinely expecting that the God who invites us to pray will not send us away empty-handed.

The apostle James develops this theme with great practical force. Writing to believers who need wisdom for the trials they face, he gives this remarkable promise: "If any of you lack wisdom, let him ask of God, that giveth to all men liberally, and upbraideth not; and it shall be given him" (James 1:5). Notice the generosity embedded in this promise. God gives "liberally" — not grudgingly, not sparingly, not conditionally, but with the overflowing abundance of infinite resources. And He gives "upbraideth not" — without reproach, without scolding, without making us feel foolish for asking. What an encouragement to come boldly to the throne of grace!

But James immediately adds a condition that cuts to the heart of our prayer failures: "But let him ask in faith, nothing wavering. For he that wavereth is like a wave of the sea driven with the wind and tossed. For let not that man think that he shall receive any thing of the Lord. A double minded man is unstable in all his ways" (James 1:6-8). The wavering person is compared to an ocean wave — constantly in motion, never settled, driven by whatever wind happens to be blowing. One moment he believes God will answer; the next moment he doubts. One day he prays with confidence; the next day he wonders if anyone is listening. James says bluntly that such a person should not expect to receive anything from the Lord. Double-mindedness and effective prayer are mutually exclusive.

This does not mean that we must achieve some state of perfect, unwavering certainty before God will hear us. The father of the demon-possessed boy cried out, "Lord, I believe; help thou mine unbelief" (Mark 9:24), and Christ honored that honest, struggling faith. The faith that pleases God is not faith that never struggles but faith that, in the midst of its struggles, still clings to God and His promises. It is the faith that says, "Even though I cannot see the answer, I believe that God is faithful. Even though my feelings waver, I will anchor my soul to His Word."

Our Lord Jesus Christ gave perhaps the most astonishing promise about faith and prayer in Mark 11:22-24: "Have faith in God. For verily I say unto you, That whosoever shall say unto this mountain, Be thou removed, and be thou cast into the sea; and shall not doubt in his heart, but shall believe that those things which he saith shall come to pass; he shall have whatsoever he saith. Therefore I say unto you, What things soever ye desire, when ye pray, believe that ye receive them, and ye shall have them." Mountains being cast into the sea — this is the language of impossibility overcome by faith. Christ is not teaching us to use prayer as a magical formula for getting whatever we want. He is teaching us that the God to whom we pray is the God who created mountains and seas, and nothing is too hard for Him.

The key phrase is "have faith in God" — literally, "have the faith of God" or "have God's faithfulness." Our faith is not faith in our own faith. It is not faith in the power of positive thinking. It is faith in God — in His character, His promises, His power, and His goodness. When we pray with faith, we are not generating some mysterious spiritual energy through the intensity of our belief. We are simply taking God at His word, trusting that what He has promised He is able and willing to perform.

The apostle John provides the essential qualifier that prevents us from turning prayer into a wish-fulfillment machine: "And this is the confidence that we have in him, that, if we ask any thing according to his will, he heareth us: And if we know that he hear us, whatsoever we ask, we know that we have the petitions that we desired of him" (1 John 5:14-15). The phrase "according to his will" is the governing principle. Faith in prayer is not the arrogant insistence that God must do what we want. It is the humble confidence that God will do what He has promised. Our prayers are to be shaped by His will, informed by His Word, and submitted to His wisdom.

This means that praying with faith requires knowing God's will, and we learn God's will primarily through His Word. The promises of Scripture become the foundation of our prayers. When God promises to forgive the sins of those who confess them (1 John 1:9), we can pray for forgiveness with absolute confidence. When God promises to supply all our needs according to His riches in glory (Philippians 4:19), we can bring our needs to Him with full assurance. When God promises that all things work together for good to those who love Him (Romans 8:28), we can face our trials with the confidence that He is working even when we cannot see it.

Praying with faith, then, is not a technique to be mastered but a relationship to be cultivated. The more we know God through His Word, the more we understand His will. The more we understand His will, the more confidently we pray. The more confidently we pray, the more we see God at work. And the more we see God at work, the deeper our faith grows. This is the virtuous cycle of the prayer life — faith feeding prayer, and prayer feeding faith, in an ever-deepening spiral of communion with the living God.

Let us therefore come to prayer not as beggars hoping for scraps but as children approaching a generous Father, not as strangers petitioning a distant king but as the beloved of God drawing near to the One who delights to give good gifts to those who ask Him. Let us cast aside our doubts, anchor our souls to His promises, and pray with the expectant faith that honors God and moves His mighty hand.

This biblical foundation is essential. Without it, everything else we study today would be like a house built on sand. But with it, we stand on solid rock.

SEGMENT 3: MAIN LESSON PART 2 -- PURITAN TEACHING
----------------------------------------

Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. John Flavel wrote extensively on this subject in "The Mystery of Providence." The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life.

John Flavel (1627-1691) was a Presbyterian minister in Dartmouth, England, whose pastoral writings combined deep theological insight with warm, practical application. His work "The Mystery of Providence" is a masterpiece of Puritan devotional literature, tracing the hand of God through the circumstances of daily life and teaching believers to see divine faithfulness in every event. Flavel's treatment of faith in prayer is intimately connected to his understanding of providence — for if God governs all things, then prayer offered in faith has a guaranteed audience with the Sovereign of the universe.

Flavel begins his teaching on faith and prayer by establishing that faith is not a human achievement but a divine gift. He writes that the ability to believe God's promises and to approach Him with confidence is itself the work of the Holy Spirit in the soul. Left to ourselves, we would never pray with faith because we would never believe that the infinite, holy God of the universe would concern Himself with the petitions of sinful creatures. But the Spirit opens our eyes to see the promises of God, warms our hearts to embrace those promises, and strengthens our wills to act upon them. Faith is always and entirely a gift of grace.

Yet Flavel is equally insistent that faith must be exercised and cultivated. He uses the analogy of a muscle that grows stronger with use and atrophies with neglect. A believer who never ventures anything on God's promises will never experience the joy of seeing those promises fulfilled. A Christian who always plays it safe, who never prays bold prayers, who never asks for things that only God can provide — such a Christian will have a thin, anemic faith that provides little comfort in the day of trial. Flavel urges believers to stretch their faith by bringing great requests to a great God.

Flavel identifies the promises of God as the food upon which faith feeds. He writes that every promise in Scripture is a blank check signed by the God who cannot lie, and faith is the hand that presents that check at the bank of heaven. But we must know the promises in order to claim them. This is why Flavel emphasizes the necessity of daily Scripture reading and meditation. The believer who stores God's promises in his memory has an inexhaustible supply of fuel for his prayers. When he is sick, he remembers, "I am the LORD that healeth thee" (Exodus 15:26). When he is afraid, he recalls, "Fear thou not; for I am with thee" (Isaiah 41:10). When he is in need, he claims, "My God shall supply all your need according to his riches in glory by Christ Jesus" (Philippians 4:19).

Flavel addresses one of the great obstacles to praying with faith: the memory of past sins and failures. Many believers, he observes, come to prayer weighed down by guilt and shame, feeling that they have forfeited the right to ask God for anything. To these struggling souls, Flavel points to the character of God as described in James 1:5 — He gives "liberally, and upbraideth not." God does not keep a record of our past failures to throw in our face when we come to Him with new requests. He does not say, "How dare you ask for anything after what you did yesterday?" The blood of Christ has removed the barrier, and the Father receives us with open arms, not because we deserve it, but because Christ deserves it on our behalf.

Flavel is particularly helpful on the distinction between faith and presumption. Presumption claims what God has not promised. Faith claims what God has promised. Presumption demands that God serve our will. Faith submits to God's will and trusts His wisdom. Presumption is rooted in self-confidence. Faith is rooted in God-confidence. Flavel warns that we must never confuse bold faith with arrogant presumption, and the way to tell the difference is to ask whether our request is grounded in the promises of God's Word or merely in the desires of our own heart.

One of Flavel's most valuable contributions is his teaching on how to handle unanswered prayer without losing faith. He argues that God always answers the prayers of His children, but He does so in one of three ways: He says yes, He says no, or He says wait. When God says yes, our faith is confirmed. When God says no, our faith is tested — but Flavel assures us that every divine "no" is itself a gift, because God, who sees the end from the beginning, knows that what we asked for would have harmed us. When God says wait, our faith is refined, as we learn to trust His timing as well as His power.

Flavel draws extensively on the doctrine of providence to strengthen the believer's faith in prayer. If God governs every atom in the universe, if not a sparrow falls without His knowledge, if the hairs of our head are numbered — then how can we doubt that He hears and governs the petitions of His own children? The God who controls the movements of galaxies is the same God who invites us to call Him Father. The God who holds the ocean in the hollow of His hand is the same God who promises to supply all our needs. Faith in prayer is simply the application of our faith in God's sovereign providence to the specific circumstances of our lives.

Flavel also addresses the corporate dimension of faith in prayer. He observes that when believers pray together in faith, there is a multiplication of spiritual power. He cites the promise of Christ: "If two of you shall agree on earth as touching any thing that they shall ask, it shall be done for them of my Father which is in heaven" (Matthew 18:19). The agreement spoken of here is not merely verbal agreement but agreement of heart and faith — a shared conviction that God is able and willing to do what is being asked. Flavel encourages churches to cultivate this kind of united, faith-filled prayer, arguing that many blessings are withheld from the church not because God is unwilling to give them but because the church is too timid to ask for them.

Flavel concludes his treatment of faith and prayer with a meditation on the faithfulness of God across history. He surveys the great prayers of Scripture — Abraham's intercession for Sodom, Moses's plea for Israel, Elijah's prayer on Mount Carmel, Hezekiah's prayer against Sennacherib, Daniel's prayer in the lions' den — and in every case he finds the same pattern: faith in God's promise, persistence in prayer, and faithful divine response. The God who answered then is the same God who hears us now. His arm has not shortened, His ear has not grown heavy, His love has not diminished. He is the same yesterday, today, and forever.

The practical application of Flavel's teaching is this: come to prayer expecting God to act. Do not pray with the half-hearted resignation that says nothing will change. Do not pray with the superstitious hope that repeating the right words will magically produce results. Pray with the settled conviction that the God who made heaven and earth, who raised Jesus from the dead, who has promised never to leave or forsake His children — this God hears you, loves you, and will answer you according to His infinite wisdom and unfailing faithfulness.

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.

SEGMENT 4: DISCUSSION AND APPLICATION
----------------------------------------

Let us consider together what these truths mean for our lives.

Discussion Question 1: Hebrews 11:6 says we must believe that God 'is a rewarder of them that diligently seek him.' How does believing God is a rewarder shape the way we pray?

Discussion Question 2: James compares the doubting person to a wave of the sea. What does this image teach us about the instability that comes from wavering faith?

Discussion Question 3: Flavel distinguishes between faith and presumption. How can we tell the difference in our own prayer lives? What are practical tests?

Discussion Question 4: How does the doctrine of God's providence strengthen our faith in prayer? If God controls all things, what does that mean for our confidence when we pray?

Discussion Question 5: Flavel says God answers every prayer with 'yes,' 'no,' or 'wait.' How should we respond when the answer is 'no' or 'wait' without losing faith?

Take time with each question. The Puritans valued what they called "holy conference," the practice of discussing spiritual truths together. Do not settle for quick, surface answers. Push deeper. Ask follow-up questions. Listen carefully to one another.

Application is where truth becomes life. It is not enough to know what Hebrews 11:6; James 1:5-8; Mark 11:22-24; 1 John 5:14-15 says. We must ask: how does this change the way I live today? How does this shape my thoughts, my words, my actions? The Puritans insisted that a truth that does not transform is a truth that has not been truly understood.

SEGMENT 5: AFTERNOON ACTIVITY
----------------------------------------

Let us transition from study to practice. Here is your afternoon activity.

Students will create a 'Promises Prayer Book' — a personal collection of God's promises organized by category for use in prayer. Using a blank journal or stapled booklet, students will create sections for common prayer needs: (1) Forgiveness and Cleansing, (2) Guidance and Wisdom, (3) Provision and Daily Needs, (4) Protection and Safety, (5) Comfort in Suffering, (6) Strength for Service, (7) Salvation of Others. Under each category, students will write out at least three Scripture promises in full (KJV), noting the reference. They will then write a brief model prayer based on each promise, demonstrating how to 'pray the promises.' The completed book becomes a permanent prayer resource that grounds their faith in God's Word rather than their own feelings.

Materials needed: Blank journals or stapled booklets of lined paper, concordance or topical Bible for finding promises, colored tabs or sticky notes for section dividers, pens, printed list of suggested promise passages organized by category

As you complete this activity, think about how it connects to what you studied in today's lesson. The goal is not just to do something, but to reinforce the truths you have been learning. The Puritans believed that education should engage the whole person: mind, heart, and hands.

Take your time with this activity. Quality matters more than speed. When you are finished, reflect on what you learned through the process of doing. Often we understand something far better after we have tried to put it into practice.

SEGMENT 6: CROSS-SUBJECT CONNECTIONS
----------------------------------------

One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

John Flavel ministered during the Great Ejection of 1662, when over 2,000 Puritan ministers were expelled from their pulpits by the Act of Uniformity. Flavel continued preaching in secret, sometimes in forests and on beaches to avoid arrest. Study the persecution of nonconformists under Charles II and how this experience of suffering deepened the Puritans' dependence on prayer and their confidence in divine providence. How did faith in God's promises sustain persecuted believers during this dark period of English church history?

Literature Connection:

Flavel's writing excels in the use of analogy and illustration drawn from everyday life — sailing, farming, commerce, medicine. Identify and analyze the analogies Flavel uses to explain abstract theological concepts. How effective are they in making doctrine accessible? Write your own extended analogy explaining the concept of faith in prayer, drawing from a field you know well (sports, music, technology, etc.). What makes an analogy effective in theological writing?

Science Connection:

The scientific method itself requires a form of faith — the confidence that the universe operates according to consistent, discoverable laws. Scientists formulate hypotheses (statements of expectation) and design experiments to test them, trusting that nature will respond in predictable ways. Compare this scientific confidence in natural law with the believer's confidence in God's promises. How are they similar? How are they fundamentally different? Consider the concept of 'reproducibility' in science and 'faithfulness' in theology.

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.

SEGMENT 7: EVENING REVIEW AND MEMORY WORK
----------------------------------------

We have reached the final segment of today's lesson. The Puritans placed great value on review and memorization. They knew that what is not reviewed is soon forgotten. Let us take time now to consolidate what we have learned.

Review Questions:

Review Question 1: What two things must a person believe about God according to Hebrews 11:6?

Review Question 2: What does James 1:5 say about the character of God when He gives in response to prayer?

Review Question 3: According to 1 John 5:14-15, what is the essential condition for confident prayer?

Review Question 4: How does Flavel use the doctrine of providence to strengthen faith in prayer?

Review Question 5: What is the difference between faith and presumption according to Flavel's teaching?

Write out your answers to these questions. Use complete sentences and support your answers with what you learned in today's lesson. If you are unsure of an answer, go back and review that section of the lesson.

Memory Verse:

"But without faith it is impossible to please him: for he that cometh to God must believe that he is, and that he is a rewarder of them that diligently seek him." -- Hebrews 11:6

Read this verse aloud five times. Then close your eyes and try to recite it from memory. Write it out by hand on a card or in your journal. The Puritans memorized vast portions of Scripture, and it was one of the great sources of their spiritual strength. Even one verse, truly hidden in the heart, can sustain you in times of trial.

We have covered much ground today. Take time this evening to reflect on what stood out to you most. Talk about it with your family. Write it in your journal. Let these truths take root. Until next time, students.
