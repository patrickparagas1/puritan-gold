"And Jacob was left alone; and there wrestled a man with him until the breaking of the day. And he said, Let me go, for the day breaketh. And he said, I will not let thee go, except thou bless me. And he said unto him, What is thy name? And he said, Jacob. Are there prayers you have stopped praying? Petitions you once brought fervently before God but have quietly abandoned because the answer seemed too long in coming? Consider Jacob, who wrestled all night and would not let go. Consider the widow, who came to the judge again and again and again. Consider Epaphras, who labored fervently in prayer as though his very life depended on it. God has not forgotten your prayers. Not one tear you have shed in His presence has been lost. Not one cry of your heart has gone unheard. The delay is not a denial. Take up those abandoned prayers again. Return to the throne of grace with fresh determination and ancient faith. Say with Jacob: I will not let thee go, except thou bless me. The God who wrestles with you is the God who longs to bless you.

As we prepare for today's lesson, remember that everything we study flows from this one fountain: the Word of God. Our lesson today is part of The Life of Prayer, and it is titled: Wrestling in Prayer — Perseverance. Let us proceed with minds engaged and hearts open.


Now we come to the heart of today's curriculum.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes in Genesis 32:24-30; Luke 18:1-8; Colossians 4:12; Romans 15:30.

One of the most striking and mysterious episodes in all of Scripture is Jacob's wrestling with the Angel of the Lord at the brook Jabbok, recorded in Genesis 32. This event is far more than a physical struggle — it is the supreme Old Testament portrait of what it means to wrestle in prayer, to persevere before God until the blessing comes. Jacob was alone, afraid, facing the prospect of meeting his estranged brother Esau the next morning, and in that dark night of the soul, God met him in the form of a man and engaged him in a contest that would change his name and his destiny forever.

The text says that Jacob "was left alone; and there wrestled a man with him until the breaking of the day" (Genesis 32:24). The wrestling lasted all night long. There was no quick resolution, no immediate answer. Jacob clung to this mysterious figure through the long hours of darkness, refusing to release his grip even when the angel dislocated his hip. The pain must have been excruciating — a hip wrenched from its socket — yet Jacob held on. And from his lips came those immortal words that summarize the essence of persevering prayer: "I will not let thee go, except thou bless me" (Genesis 32:26).

This is the spirit that God honors in prayer. Not the casual, half-hearted petition that we forget before we rise from our knees, but the desperate, tenacious clinging to God that refuses to take no for an answer. Jacob was not wrestling against God in rebellion; he was wrestling with God in faith. He believed that the God who had appeared to him was able to bless him, and he was determined to receive that blessing or perish in the attempt. The result was transformative: "Thy name shall be called no more Jacob, but Israel: for as a prince hast thou power with God and with men, and hast prevailed" (Genesis 32:28). The name Israel means "he who strives with God" or "prince with God," and it became the name of God's chosen nation.

Notice that Jacob prevailed — but he prevailed as a cripple. He walked away from Peniel limping, and he limped for the rest of his life. This is a profound spiritual lesson. God answers persevering prayer, but He often answers it in ways that humble us and remind us of our dependence upon Him. The blessing came with a wound. The victory came with a limp. So it is in the life of prayer: God gives us what we ask, but He also breaks us of our self-sufficiency in the process.

Our Lord Jesus Christ reinforced this principle of perseverance in prayer through the parable of the persistent widow in Luke 18:1-8. Luke introduces this parable with an explicit statement of its purpose: Christ "spake a parable unto them to this end, that men ought always to pray, and not to faint" (Luke 18:1). The word "faint" means to grow weary, to lose heart, to give up. Christ knows that His disciples will be tempted to stop praying when answers are delayed, and so He tells this story to strengthen their resolve.

The parable presents an unjust judge who "feared not God, neither regarded man" — a thoroughly corrupt official with no sense of accountability to heaven or earth. A poor widow comes to him repeatedly, seeking justice against her adversary. The judge ignores her. She comes again. He ignores her again. But she persists, and eventually the judge relents — not out of righteousness or compassion, but simply to end her persistent demands.

Christ's argument runs from the lesser to the greater. If even an unjust, godless judge will eventually answer persistent pleading, "shall not God avenge his own elect, which cry day and night unto him, though he bear long with them?" (Luke 18:7). The contrast is total. God is not unjust but righteous. He is not indifferent but compassionate. The widow was a stranger to the judge; believers are God's own elect, His beloved children. If persistence prevailed even under those unfavorable conditions, how much more will persevering prayer prevail with a loving heavenly Father?

Yet Christ ends the parable with a searching question: "Nevertheless when the Son of man cometh, shall he find faith on the earth?" (Luke 18:8). The question implies that persevering prayer is rare because persevering faith is rare. It is easy to pray once and give up. It is easy to conclude that God has said no when He has merely said "wait." The faith that continues to knock when the door remains shut, that continues to seek when nothing has been found, that continues to ask when silence is the only reply — this is the faith that moves mountains and receives the blessing of Israel.

The apostle Paul uses the language of struggle and labor when describing the prayer life of believers. He describes Epaphras as "always labouring fervently for you in prayers" (Colossians 4:12). The Greek word translated "labouring fervently" is agonizomenos, from which we derive the English word "agony." Prayer, for Epaphras, was not a leisurely activity but an agonizing labor. He agonized for the Colossian believers, striving that they might "stand perfect and complete in all the will of God." This is intercessory prayer at its most intense — prayer that costs something, prayer that drains you, prayer that wrestles and labors and refuses to rest until the work is done.

Paul himself sought this kind of prayer support, asking the Romans to "strive together with me in your prayers to God for me" (Romans 15:30). The word "strive together" is synagonisasthai — to agonize alongside, to wrestle together. Paul saw prayer as a team effort, a corporate struggle in which believers joined their strength together to prevail before the throne of grace. Prayer is not a solitary enterprise alone; it is the church militant on its knees, waging war against the powers of darkness through intercession.

The practical lesson for us is clear: do not give up in prayer. When God delays, He is not denying. When heaven seems silent, it is not empty. When the answer tarries, faith must hold on. Like Jacob, we must say, "I will not let thee go, except thou bless me." Like the widow, we must come again and again and again. Like Epaphras, we must labor fervently, agonizing before God for the things that matter most. And like Paul, we must call upon our brothers and sisters to strive together with us. For God has promised: "Ask, and it shall be given you; seek, and ye shall find; knock, and it shall be opened unto you" (Matthew 7:7). The present tense in the original Greek means keep asking, keep seeking, keep knocking. Perseverance in prayer is not an option for the believer — it is a command.

The prophet Daniel provides yet another magnificent example of persevering prayer. In Daniel 10, we read that Daniel set himself to pray and fast for three full weeks — twenty-one days of sustained, agonizing intercession. When the angel finally appeared to him, the heavenly messenger revealed something astonishing: "Fear not, Daniel: for from the first day that thou didst set thine heart to understand, and to chasten thyself before thy God, thy words were heard, and I am come for thy words. But the prince of the kingdom of Persia withstood me one and twenty days" (Daniel 10:12-13). Daniel's prayer was heard from the very first day, but the answer was delayed for three weeks because of spiritual warfare in the heavenly realm. This passage pulls back the curtain on the invisible dimension of prayer, revealing that our prayers engage cosmic forces and that delays may have nothing to do with God's unwillingness and everything to do with the spiritual battle being waged on our behalf.

The psalmist David repeatedly models persevering prayer in the Psalms. In Psalm 13, he cries out, "How long wilt thou forget me, O LORD? for ever? how long wilt thou hide thy face from me?" (Psalm 13:1). The repeated cry of "how long" is the cry of a soul that has been waiting, that feels forgotten, that is tempted to give up — and yet continues to pray. David does not stop at his complaint. By the end of the psalm, he declares, "But I have trusted in thy mercy; my heart shall rejoice in thy salvation. I will sing unto the LORD, because he hath dealt bountifully with me" (Psalm 13:5-6). This is the pattern of persevering prayer: honest anguish that does not pretend, combined with stubborn faith that will not let go, leading ultimately to praise that trusts God even before the answer arrives.

In the book of Revelation, we are given a remarkable glimpse of how God regards the accumulated prayers of His saints: "And another angel came and stood at the altar, having a golden censer; and there was given unto him much incense, that he should offer it with the prayers of all saints upon the golden altar which was before the throne" (Revelation 8:3). The prayers of the saints are not lost. They are not forgotten. They rise before the throne of God like incense, collected and honored and answered in God's perfect timing. Every prayer you have ever prayed in faith is preserved in heaven, and not one of them will return void.

This biblical foundation is essential. Without it, everything else we study today would be like a house built on sand. But with it, we stand on solid rock.


Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. John Owen wrote extensively on this subject in "A Discourse on the Work of the Holy Spirit in Prayer." The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life.

John Owen (1616-1683), the greatest of the Puritan theologians, devoted careful attention to the work of the Holy Spirit in prayer in his comprehensive treatise on the Spirit's operations. Owen understood that true prayer is never merely a human activity — it is a divine-human cooperation in which the Holy Spirit enables, directs, and empowers the believer's petitions. But Owen was equally insistent that the Spirit's work in prayer does not render human effort unnecessary. Rather, the Spirit's assistance is precisely what makes genuine spiritual effort possible, including the effort of perseverance.

Owen begins his treatment of persevering prayer by establishing that all true prayer originates with the Holy Spirit. He writes that the Spirit is called "the spirit of grace and of supplications" (Zechariah 12:10) because He is the author and sustainer of every genuine prayer that rises from the human heart. Without the Spirit's work, our prayers would be nothing more than empty words — formal exercises devoid of spiritual power. But when the Spirit moves in the soul, He creates desires that align with the will of God, and those desires become the fuel that sustains prayer through long seasons of waiting and apparent silence.

Owen identifies several reasons why God delays answering prayer, and each reason illuminates why perseverance is both necessary and beneficial. First, God delays to test and strengthen our faith. Faith that has never been tested is faith that has never been proven. When God withholds the answer, He is not punishing us but training us. He is building in us a deeper, more resilient trust that can withstand the storms of life. Owen compares this to the process by which a muscle grows stronger through resistance — the very difficulty of persevering prayer is what makes our faith grow.

Second, Owen teaches that God delays to wean us from creature-dependence and drive us to greater dependence upon Himself. When our prayers are answered immediately, we are tempted to treat God as a vending machine — insert the prayer, receive the blessing. But when the answer is delayed, we are forced to examine our own hearts, to search out hidden sins, to deepen our understanding of God's character, and to learn what it truly means to trust Him when we cannot see His hand. Owen writes that the season of waiting is often more spiritually profitable than the moment of receiving, because it is in the waiting that our souls are most deeply shaped.

Third, Owen observes that God sometimes delays to increase the value of the blessing when it finally comes. A gift that is received after long yearning and desperate pleading is treasured far more than one that drops into our laps without effort. When Hannah received Samuel after years of barren weeping, her gratitude was so overwhelming that she dedicated the child entirely to God's service. When the church in Acts prayed earnestly for Peter's release from prison, their joy at his deliverance was all the greater because of the intensity of their intercession. Owen argues that God is so generous that He not only gives us what we ask for but arranges the timing so that the giving produces maximum joy and gratitude.

Owen is particularly insightful on the relationship between the Spirit's work and human perseverance. He rejects both extremes. On one hand, he opposes those who teach that human effort in prayer is unnecessary because the Spirit does everything — a view that leads to spiritual passivity and neglect of the means of grace. On the other hand, he opposes those who teach that perseverance in prayer is entirely a matter of human willpower and discipline — a view that leads to Pharisaic self-righteousness. Owen's position is that the Holy Spirit produces in us both the desire and the ability to persevere, but He does so in a way that engages our faculties rather than bypassing them. We truly pray, we truly persevere, we truly wrestle — but we do so because the Spirit is at work in us, enabling us to do what we could never do in our own strength.

Owen draws a vivid analogy from the story of Jacob at Jabbok. He notes that Jacob's wrestling was genuine — the struggle was real, the pain was real, the determination was real. But Jacob was not wrestling against God; he was wrestling with God, and it was God who had initiated the encounter. The angel could have defeated Jacob at any moment — as demonstrated by the single touch that dislocated his hip — but He chose to let Jacob prevail. This, Owen argues, is a picture of all true persevering prayer. God creates the conditions that drive us to pray. God sustains us in the struggle. And God, in His sovereign mercy, allows us to prevail — not because our prayer overcomes His reluctance, but because His grace works through our persistence to accomplish His eternal purpose.

Owen also addresses the practical challenge of maintaining perseverance when the spirit is willing but the flesh is weak. He counsels believers to establish regular habits of prayer — set times and set places — because discipline provides the framework within which the Spirit works. He warns against waiting for feelings before praying, arguing that we should pray whether we feel like it or not, and that the act of faithful obedience often kindles the affection that was absent at the beginning. He encourages the use of Scripture in prayer, particularly the Psalms, because the words of God sustain the spirit when our own words fail.

Owen further teaches that persevering prayer should be accompanied by the use of all legitimate means. If we pray for holiness, we must also read the Word, attend worship, and avoid temptation. If we pray for the conversion of a friend, we must also share the gospel, live a faithful witness, and invite them to hear the preaching. Prayer and means go hand in hand, and to neglect the means while praying for the end is to tempt God rather than trust Him.

The pastoral heart of Owen shines through in his treatment of discouraged believers who feel that their prayers are ineffective. He assures them that no prayer offered in faith is ever lost. Every sincere petition is recorded in heaven, received by the Father through the mediation of Christ, and answered according to infinite wisdom. The answer may not come in the form we expect, or at the time we choose, but it will come. Owen writes that the prayers of the saints are like seeds planted in the ground — they may lie dormant for a season, but they will eventually spring up and bear fruit, sometimes long after the one who planted them has gone to glory.

Owen concludes by pointing to the supreme example of persevering prayer: the Lord Jesus Christ Himself. In the Garden of Gethsemane, Christ prayed three times with such intensity that His sweat became "as it were great drops of blood" (Luke 22:44). He persevered through the darkest night of human history, and His prayer was heard — not in the removal of the cup, but in the strength to drink it. If the Son of God persevered in prayer, how much more must we, who have so much less strength and so much more need? Owen exhorts us to fix our eyes on Christ, the author and finisher of our faith, and to run with patience the race that is set before us, knowing that He who began a good work in us will complete it at the day of Jesus Christ.

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.

Let us press deeper into the theology of persevering prayer by examining additional Scripture and Puritan wisdom on this vital subject.

The prophet Habakkuk provides a powerful example of wrestling with God in prayer. Faced with the injustice of his own nation and the terrifying prospect of Babylonian invasion, Habakkuk cries out to God with raw honesty: "O LORD, how long shall I cry, and thou wilt not hear! even cry out unto thee of violence, and thou wilt not save!" (Habakkuk 1:2). Habakkuk is not politely requesting information — he is wrestling with God, pressing Him for an answer, refusing to accept silence as a final word. And God does not rebuke Habakkuk for his boldness. Instead, He answers him, revealing His sovereign purposes and calling the prophet to faith: "The just shall live by his faith" (Habakkuk 2:4). Habakkuk's persistent, honest, even agonized prayer was met not with divine displeasure but with divine revelation.

Consider also the example of Hannah in 1 Samuel 1. Year after year, Hannah endured the pain of barrenness while her rival Peninnah provoked and taunted her. Yet Hannah did not give up. She "was in bitterness of soul, and prayed unto the LORD, and wept sore" (1 Samuel 1:10). Her prayer was so intense that the priest Eli thought she was drunk. But Hannah was not drunk — she was pouring out her soul before God with a desperation born of years of unfulfilled longing. And God heard her. Samuel was born, and through him, the nation of Israel was transformed. The lesson is unmistakable: the prayers that change history are prayers that refuse to stop, prayers that persist through years of waiting and weeping.

The Puritan pastor Thomas Goodwin (1600-1680) wrote penetrating words about the nature of persevering prayer. Goodwin, who served as president of Magdalen College, Oxford, under Oliver Cromwell and was one of the most brilliant theologians of the Puritan era, taught that God deliberately delays His answers to cultivate in His children the graces that only waiting can produce. Goodwin observes that the farmer who plants a seed must wait patiently for the harvest. The seed does not spring up overnight. It lies hidden in the ground, invisible to the eye, apparently dead and useless. But unseen processes are at work beneath the surface — the seed is germinating, taking root, sending out shoots. So it is with our prayers. When God delays, He is not idle. He is at work behind the scenes, orchestrating circumstances, preparing hearts, advancing His purposes in ways we cannot see. The delay is not wasted time; it is working time.

Goodwin also teaches that persevering prayer deepens our knowledge of God in ways that immediate answers never could. When God answers quickly, we rejoice and move on. But when God delays, we are driven to study His character more deeply, to search His promises more diligently, to examine our own hearts more honestly, and to cling to His faithfulness with greater tenacity. The season of waiting becomes a school of theology, where we learn lessons about God's wisdom, patience, and sovereign love that we could learn nowhere else.

The apostle Paul himself provides a striking example of persevering prayer in 2 Corinthians 12:7-10. He describes a "thorn in the flesh" — some unnamed affliction that caused him great distress. "For this thing I besought the Lord thrice, that it might depart from me" (2 Corinthians 12:8). Paul prayed repeatedly, earnestly, desperately for relief. And the answer came — but not the answer Paul expected: "My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness" (2 Corinthians 12:9). God said no to Paul's request but yes to his deeper need. Paul wanted removal of the thorn; God gave him something better — sufficient grace and perfected power in the midst of weakness. This teaches us that persevering prayer does not always result in getting what we asked for. Sometimes it results in getting something far greater — a deeper experience of God's grace, a more profound understanding of His purposes, and a closer conformity to the image of Christ.

William Bridge (1600-1671), another prominent Puritan divine and one of the five dissenting brethren at the Westminster Assembly, wrote a remarkable work entitled "A Lifting Up for the Downcast," in which he addresses believers who have grown discouraged in prayer. Bridge counsels the disheartened saint to distinguish between God's silence and God's refusal. Silence does not mean no. A father who does not answer his child's request immediately is not rejecting the child — he may be waiting for the right moment, preparing a better gift, or teaching the child valuable lessons through the discipline of patience. Bridge writes that many of the greatest blessings God gives are blessings that come after extended seasons of prayer, and that the waiting itself is part of the gift.

Bridge was educated at Emmanuel College, Cambridge — that great nursery of Puritan ministers — and served in several pastoral positions before being silenced by Archbishop Laud for his nonconformist views. He fled to the Netherlands, where he ministered to English congregations in Rotterdam, before returning to England during the Civil War. His experience of exile and persecution gave his writing on prayer a depth and authenticity that could not have come from a comfortable study. He knew what it meant to pray in the dark, to wrestle with God when the heavens seemed silent, and to persevere when every earthly support had been removed.


Before we proceed to our afternoon activity, let us consider how to apply the truths of persevering prayer to our daily lives.

First, make a list of your "long-term prayers" — the requests you have been bringing to God for weeks, months, or even years without apparent answer. Write them down. Date them. Do not let them fade from your prayer life simply because the answer has been delayed. Return to them regularly with fresh faith and renewed determination. Remember Jacob: "I will not let thee go, except thou bless me."

Second, when you are tempted to stop praying about a matter, ask yourself: has God specifically said no? If not, keep praying. The absence of an answer is not a denial. It may be a divine invitation to dig deeper, to pray more fervently, and to exercise greater faith.

Third, study the examples of persevering prayer in Scripture — Jacob, Hannah, Elijah, Daniel, the persistent widow — and let their stories strengthen your resolve. When your own faith falters, lean on the faith of those who have gone before you. Their stories are recorded in Scripture precisely for this purpose: "For whatsoever things were written aforetime were written for our learning, that we through patience and comfort of the scriptures might have hope" (Romans 15:4).

Fourth, enlist others to pray with you. Paul asked the Romans to "strive together" with him in prayer. There is strength in corporate intercession that exceeds what any individual can generate alone. Share your prayer burdens with trusted brothers and sisters in Christ and commit to praying together until the answer comes.

Fifth, in seasons of prolonged waiting, discipline yourself to give thanks for what God has already done while you wait for what He has not yet done. Thanksgiving keeps the heart from bitterness, maintains a right perspective on God's goodness, and demonstrates the very faith that honors God and moves His hand.

Students will conduct a 'Prayer Perseverance Study' by examining five biblical examples of persevering prayer: (1) Jacob wrestling at Jabbok (Genesis 32), (2) Hannah praying for a son (1 Samuel 1), (3) Elijah praying for rain (1 Kings 18:41-46), (4) Daniel's three-week prayer and fasting (Daniel 10:1-14), (5) the early church praying for Peter's release (Acts 12:1-17). Students will then write a one-page essay connecting these examples to Owen's teaching on why God delays answers to prayer.

Materials needed: Large index cards or card stock, colored pens, printed Scripture passages for each case study, copies of Owen's teaching on prayer delays, lined paper for essay, poster board for displaying completed case studies

As you complete this activity, think about how it connects to what you studied in today's lesson. The goal is not just to do something, but to reinforce the truths you have been learning. The Puritans believed that education should engage the whole person: mind, heart, and hands.

Take your time with this activity. Quality matters more than speed. When you are finished, reflect on what you learned through the process of doing. Often we understand something far better after we have tried to put it into practice.


One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

John Owen served as chaplain to Oliver Cromwell and later as Dean of Christ Church, Oxford, making him one of the most influential figures in Puritan England. During the English Civil War and Interregnum (1642-1660), Owen witnessed firsthand the power of corporate prayer as armies, parliaments, and churches devoted themselves to days of fasting and prayer before critical decisions and battles. Study how the practice of designated prayer days shaped English political and military history during this turbulent period.

Literature Connection:

Owen's prose style is notoriously dense and complex, requiring careful, patient reading — qualities that mirror his teaching on perseverance. Analyze a passage from Owen's work and identify his rhetorical strategies: how does he use Scripture, logical argument, analogy, and pastoral exhortation? Compare his style with John Bunyan's much simpler prose. Consider how different writing styles serve different audiences and purposes in communicating the same theological truths.

Science Connection:

The concept of perseverance has parallels in scientific methodology. Great scientific discoveries often require years or decades of persistent inquiry. Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin only after years of patient observation. Marie Curie's isolation of radium required processing tons of pitchblende over four years of exhausting labor. Study the scientific method and the role of persistence in scientific breakthroughs, drawing parallels to the spiritual discipline of persevering prayer.

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.

Memory Verse:

"And he said, I will not let thee go, except thou bless me." -- Genesis 32:26

Read this verse aloud five times. Then close your eyes and try to recite it from memory. Write it out by hand on a card or in your journal. The Puritans memorized vast portions of Scripture, and it was one of the great sources of their spiritual strength. Even one verse, truly hidden in the heart, can sustain you in times of trial.

Additional Memory Passage:

"And he spake a parable unto them to this end, that men ought always to pray, and not to faint." -- Luke 18:1

This verse from our Lord Jesus Christ states the purpose of persevering prayer with unmistakable clarity. We ought always to pray. We ought never to faint — never to grow weary, never to lose heart, never to give up. Commit this verse to memory alongside Genesis 32:26, and let them serve as twin anchors for your soul when the temptation comes to abandon your prayers. The God who spoke these words through His Son is the God who hears your prayers and will answer them in His perfect time.

Summary of Key Truths:

Let us gather the central lessons from today's study. First, persevering prayer is modeled throughout Scripture — by Jacob at Jabbok, by the persistent widow in Christ's parable, by Epaphras in his agonizing intercession, and by Paul in his call for believers to strive together in prayer. Second, God honors the prayer that will not let go. Jacob's immortal words — "I will not let thee go, except thou bless me" — capture the essence of the faith that prevails. Third, God delays His answers not to punish but to train, to deepen our faith, to increase our dependence on Him, and to magnify the blessing when it finally comes. Fourth, the Holy Spirit is the One who empowers our perseverance — apart from His enabling, we would never have the strength to continue praying through the long nights of waiting. Fifth, persevering prayer often brings unexpected answers. Paul asked for the removal of his thorn; God gave him sufficient grace. Hannah asked for a son; God gave her a prophet who would reshape the nation. The answers that come through perseverance are often greater than anything we originally requested.

Let these truths steel your resolve. Whatever you are praying for, do not give up. Whatever battle you are fighting in prayer, do not surrender. The God who wrestled with Jacob at Jabbok is the same God who meets you in your prayer closet. He is not reluctant to bless you — He is preparing you to receive a blessing greater than you can imagine. As Daniel's experience teaches us, the very moment you begin to pray, heaven responds — even when you cannot see the response for weeks or months or years. Your prayers are heard. Your prayers are honored. Your prayers are stored before the throne of God like precious incense. Hold on. Press in. Keep praying. The morning is coming.

Today's lesson is complete. You have done excellent work. As you go about the rest of your evening, meditate on your memory verse and review the key points. A diligent student stores up knowledge for the future. May the God who never slumbers and never sleeps watch over your prayers and bring them to fruition in His perfect time.
