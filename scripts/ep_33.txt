PURITAN GOLD -- Study Devotional, Day 2
Series: Romans
"The Wrath of God Revealed"
Mankind's suppression of truth and the downward spiral of sin


Welcome, friend. Tonight we open our Bibles again for another session of deep, prayerful study. The Puritans believed that the richest treasures of Scripture are found not on the surface but beneath it, by those willing to dig.

We are studying the book of Romans together. Tonight's lesson is titled "The Wrath of God Revealed." Each session builds on what came before, so if you have been following along, you will see how tonight's passage connects to the larger argument of the book.


Let us read our passage for tonight. It comes from Romans 1:18-32:

"For the wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness and unrighteousness of men, who hold the truth in unrighteousness; Because that which may be known of God is manifest in them; for God hath shewed it unto them. For the invisible things of him from the creation of the world are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made, even his eternal power and Godhead; so that they are without excuse: Because that, when they knew God, they glorified him not as God, neither were thankful; but became vain in their imaginations, and their foolish heart was darkened. Professing themselves to be wise, they became fools, And changed the glory of the uncorruptible God into an image made like to corruptible man, and to birds, and fourfooted beasts, and creeping things. Wherefore God also gave them up to uncleanness through the lusts of their own hearts... Who knowing the judgment of God, that they which commit such things are worthy of death, not only do the same, but have pleasure in them that do them."

Read that passage again to yourself. Mark any words or phrases that stand out. The Puritans read with a pen in hand, always ready to note what God was showing them.


Within tonight's passage, one verse stands out as a key that unlocks the rest.

"For the invisible things of him from the creation of the world are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made, even his eternal power and Godhead; so that they are without excuse." -- Romans 1:20

This verse is the lens through which we will examine the rest of tonight's passage. Keep it in the front of your mind as we proceed.


With our key verse before us, let us examine the broader teaching.

John Owen offers valuable commentary on this passage. The Puritans were expositors of the highest order, and their insights remain remarkably fresh.

John Owen, in his extensive writings on sin and temptation, provides profound commentary on the mechanism Paul describes in this passage. Owen understood that sin is not merely the breaking of rules but a fundamental disorder of the soul, a turning away from the Creator to the creature. In his treatise 'Of the Mortification of Sin in Believers,' Owen argued that indwelling sin possesses a law-like power that relentlessly draws the soul downward unless it is actively opposed by the Spirit of God.

Owen gave particular attention to the threefold 'God gave them up' in this passage (verses 24, 26, 28). He saw this as the most terrifying judgment imaginable — not fire from heaven, not plague, not exile, but God removing His restraining grace and allowing sinners to follow their own desires to their logical and devastating conclusion. Owen called this judicial abandonment, and he warned that it is the ultimate expression of divine wrath in this present age. When God gives a person over to their sin, the sinner may feel more free than ever, but in reality they are plunging deeper into bondage.

Owen also stressed what he called the 'deceitfulness of sin,' drawing from Hebrews 3:13. Sin never presents itself honestly. It promises liberty and delivers slavery. It promises satisfaction and delivers emptiness. It promises wisdom and delivers folly. This is precisely what Paul describes: 'Professing themselves to be wise, they became fools.' Owen counseled believers to watch the first movements of sin in the heart, for what begins as a small inclination, if left unchecked, can become an overwhelming compulsion. The downward spiral Paul traces — from ingratitude to idolatry to moral degradation — is not merely a description of pagan Rome but a warning to every human heart that refuses to glorify God and give Him thanks.

Having declared the glorious gospel of righteousness by faith, Paul now turns to demonstrate why that gospel is so desperately needed. Romans 1:18-32 is one of the most sobering passages in all of Scripture, presenting a devastating indictment of human rebellion against God. Before Paul can explain the cure, he must diagnose the disease.

Verse 18 begins with a parallel to verse 17. In verse 17, the righteousness of God is revealed in the gospel. In verse 18, the wrath of God is revealed from heaven. These are not contradictory but complementary truths. The God who saves is also the God who judges. His wrath is not arbitrary anger or capricious fury; it is His holy, righteous, and settled opposition to all that violates His character. It is revealed 'against all ungodliness and unrighteousness of men.' Ungodliness refers to sins against the first table of the law — failure to love and honor God. Unrighteousness refers to sins against the second table — failure to love neighbor. Together they encompass the totality of human rebellion.

But notice the critical phrase: men 'who hold the truth in unrighteousness.' The word 'hold' here is katecho, which means to suppress, to hold down, to restrain. Humanity's problem is not ignorance but suppression. People do not fail to know God; they actively push down the truth about God because it confronts their desire to live autonomously.

Verses 19-20 establish what theologians call general revelation or natural theology. God has made Himself known through creation. 'The invisible things of him from the creation of the world are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made, even his eternal power and Godhead.' This is a remarkable statement. The invisible attributes of God — His eternal power, His divine nature — are made visible through the created order. The heavens declare the glory of God (Psalm 19:1). The complexity of a single cell, the vastness of the galaxies, the precision of natural laws — all these testify to an intelligent, powerful, eternal Creator.

The result is devastating: 'so that they are without excuse.' No one can stand before God and claim ignorance. The evidence is overwhelming and universally accessible. As David wrote, 'The fool hath said in his heart, There is no God' (Psalm 14:1). It is not an intellectual conclusion but a moral rebellion — a refusal of the heart, not a failure of the mind.

Verses 21-23 trace the downward trajectory that begins when humans refuse to acknowledge God. It follows a precise logical sequence. First, 'when they knew God, they glorified him not as God.' They had knowledge but refused worship. Second, 'neither were thankful.' Ingratitude is the root of all sin. When we cease to recognize that every good thing comes from God (James 1:17), we begin to claim credit for ourselves. Third, they 'became vain in their imaginations, and their foolish heart was darkened.' The mind that rejects God does not remain neutral; it fills with vanity, emptiness, futile speculation. Fourth, 'professing themselves to be wise, they became fools.' This is the great irony of human rebellion — the more humanity boasts of its intellectual achievements apart from God, the more foolish it becomes. Fifth, they 'changed the glory of the uncorruptible God into an image made like to corruptible man, and to birds, and fourfooted beasts, and creeping things.' Idolatry is always a downgrade. Man was made in the image of God, but sinners remake God in the image of man — or worse, of animals.

This is not merely ancient history. Modern idolatry takes different forms — the worship of self, of money, of pleasure, of political power, of technology — but the dynamic is identical. Whenever we place anything in the position that belongs to God alone, we are exchanging His glory for a lie.

Then comes the most terrifying phrase in the passage, repeated three times with escalating intensity. In verse 24: 'Wherefore God also gave them up to uncleanness through the lusts of their own hearts.' In verse 26: 'For this cause God gave them up unto vile affections.' In verse 28: 'God gave them over to a reprobate mind.' This is judicial abandonment — God's wrath expressed not through lightning bolts but through letting sinners have what they want. C.S. Lewis captured this truth: 'The doors of hell are locked on the inside.' God's judgment is to say, in effect, 'Thy will be done.'

The passage culminates in verses 29-31 with a devastating catalog of sins that characterize humanity under divine abandonment: fornication, wickedness, covetousness, maliciousness, envy, murder, debate, deceit, malignity, whispering, backbiting, hatred of God, despite, pride, boasting, invention of evil things, disobedience to parents, covenant-breaking, lack of natural affection, implacability, and mercilessness. This is not a description of only the worst of sinners; it is a portrait of the human condition apart from God's grace.

Verse 32 delivers the final blow: 'Who knowing the judgment of God, that they which commit such things are worthy of death, not only do the same, but have pleasure in them that do them.' The ultimate depth of depravity is not merely to sin but to celebrate sin, to approve of evil, to take pleasure in the rebellion of others. When a society reaches this point — where wickedness is applauded and righteousness is mocked — it has reached the bottom of the spiral Paul describes.

This passage is essential for understanding the gospel. Until we see the depth of human depravity, we cannot appreciate the height of divine grace. Paul is building his case like a prosecutor before a jury. Every mouth must be stopped, and all the world must become guilty before God (Romans 3:19), so that the free gift of righteousness by faith might shine in its full glory.


Application is where truth meets life. Let us consider what this passage demands of us.

This passage confronts us with uncomfortable truths about the human condition, including our own hearts. Here are specific applications.

First, practice gratitude deliberately. Paul identifies ingratitude as the first step in the downward spiral: they 'glorified him not as God, neither were thankful.' Begin and end each day by naming specific things for which you are thankful. Not vague, general thankfulness, but particular mercies — the breath in your lungs, the food on your table, the grace that saved your soul. Ingratitude is the soil in which every other sin grows. Pull it up by the roots through intentional, daily thanksgiving.

Second, examine your idols. We may not bow to statues, but we all have functional idols — things we look to for security, identity, satisfaction, or meaning apart from God. Is it your career? Your children? Your reputation? Financial security? Your phone? Ask yourself: what would devastate me if it were taken away? That reveals where your worship truly lies. Bring those idols before the Lord in prayer and ask Him to dethrone them.

Third, guard against the celebration of sin. Verse 32 warns about those who 'have pleasure in them that do them.' In our media-saturated age, we are constantly exposed to entertainment that glamorizes what God condemns. Be discerning about what you watch, read, and listen to. Ask: does this content encourage me to applaud what God hates? Make intentional choices to fill your mind with 'whatsoever things are true, whatsoever things are honest, whatsoever things are just, whatsoever things are pure' (Philippians 4:8).

Fourth, let this passage drive you to compassion, not condemnation. Paul writes this not with glee but with grief. He will later express his willingness to be accursed for the sake of his countrymen (Romans 9:3). When we see the wreckage of sin in others' lives, our response should not be self-righteous judgment but heartbroken intercession. 'But for the grace of God, there go I.' Pray for those trapped in the spiral Paul describes.

Write down one concrete action you will take this week in response to tonight's study. Do not let this truth remain theoretical. The Puritans measured the success of a sermon not by how much the hearer learned but by how much the hearer changed.


Holy God, we tremble before Your righteous wrath against sin. We confess that we too have suppressed truth, failed to give You thanks, and exchanged Your glory for lesser things. Have mercy on us. Open our eyes to the idols we cherish. Guard our hearts from the downward spiral of ingratitude and rebellion. Fill us with gratitude that overflows into worship. Give us compassion for a world lost in darkness. And thank You that where sin abounded, grace did much more abound. Through Jesus Christ our Lord, Amen.

Goodnight. May the truths you have studied tonight take deep root in your heart.
