PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"The Lord Is My Shepherd"
May 3, 2026

---

"The Lord is my shepherd; I shall not want. He maketh me to lie down in green pastures: he leadeth me beside the still waters. He restoreth my soul: he leadeth me in the paths of righteousness for his name's sake. Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil: for thou art with me; thy rod and thy staff they comfort me." -- Psalm 23:1-4

Of all the psalms that David wrote, none has been so universally loved, so frequently quoted, so deeply treasured by the people of God in every age, as this the twenty-third. It has been the companion of saints upon their deathbeds and the comfort of pilgrims in the darkest valleys of their earthly journey. It has been whispered by martyrs at the stake and sung by missionaries in far-off lands. And the reason for its enduring power is simply this: it speaks of a relationship so intimate, so personal, so tender, that every believing soul can say with David, "The Lord is my shepherd."

Observe that David does not say, "The Lord is a shepherd," as though speaking of some abstract doctrine. Nor does he say, "The Lord is the shepherd of His people," as though content to lose himself in the crowd. He says, "The Lord is my shepherd." Here is the very essence of saving faith: the personal appropriation of Christ. It is one thing to believe that God is merciful. The devils believe that, and tremble. It is quite another thing to say, with the full assurance of the Holy Spirit bearing witness with your spirit, "He is my God, my shepherd, my portion, my all." Thomas Goodwin, that eminent divine of the Puritan age, once observed that a man may believe all the articles of the Christian faith and yet perish, if he does not personally close with Christ and rest upon Him alone for salvation. It is the possessive pronoun that makes all the difference. My shepherd.

And what follows from this blessed relationship? "I shall not want." Here is a declaration of absolute sufficiency. The sheep that belongs to this Shepherd shall lack nothing that is truly needful for its welfare. Not merely the necessities of the body, though these are included, but the far deeper needs of the soul: pardon for sin, peace with God, strength for the journey, comfort in affliction, guidance in perplexity, and glory at the end. He who has the Lord for his shepherd possesses in that one relationship all that his nature can ever require, whether in this world or in the world to come.

"He maketh me to lie down in green pastures." The sheep does not find the green pastures by its own cleverness or by its own wandering. The Shepherd leads it there. And notice that He makes the sheep to lie down. A sheep will not lie down when it is hungry, or when it is frightened, or when it is tormented by insects, or when there is discord within the flock. The good Shepherd so provides for His sheep, so protects them, so tends to their every need, that they are at last able to lie down in perfect contentment and rest. Oh, what a picture this is of the soul that has found its rest in Christ! The anxieties that once tormented it are stilled. The fears that once drove it from place to place are quieted. The restless hunger that once sent it searching in every direction for satisfaction is at last appeased. Christ has made the soul to lie down in the green pastures of His Word, and there it feeds and is at peace.

"He leadeth me beside the still waters." Not the raging torrents, not the thundering cataracts, but the still waters, the waters of quietness. Sheep are timid creatures and will not drink from rushing streams, for the current frightens them and the noise drives them away. So the Shepherd leads His flock to where the waters are gentle, where they can drink without fear. The Holy Spirit, in applying the Word of God to the believer's heart, does not overwhelm the soul with more than it can bear. He leads gently, gradually, tenderly, giving grace according to the need of the hour.

"He restoreth my soul." What a needful ministry this is! For the sheep of Christ's flock are prone to wander. They stray from the path. They fall into ditches. They entangle themselves in thorns. They lose their way and lie down in exhaustion, unable to find their way back to the fold. But the good Shepherd does not abandon His wandering sheep. He goes after it, finds it, lifts it upon His shoulders, and carries it home. He restores its soul. He revives its drooping graces. He rekindles its dying faith. He brings it back to the green pastures and the still waters from which it had foolishly departed.

And then comes the verse that has sustained more dying saints than perhaps any other passage in Holy Scripture: "Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil: for thou art with me." Notice the careful language of the Spirit. It is the valley of the shadow of death, not death itself. A shadow may terrify, but it cannot harm. The shadow of a sword cannot cut. The shadow of a serpent cannot bite. And the shadow of death cannot destroy the soul that is kept by the power of God. Moreover, the believer walks through this valley. He does not remain in it. It is a passage, not a destination. On the other side of the valley lies the house of the Lord, where the redeemed shall dwell forever.

"For thou art with me." Here is the secret of the believer's courage in the face of death. It is not that he is naturally brave, or that he has schooled himself by philosophy to meet his end with stoic composure. It is that the Shepherd is with him. The same Shepherd who led him through every trial of life accompanies him through the last great trial of death. The rod of protection and the staff of guidance are still in the Shepherd's hand. The presence that sustained him in the green pastures sustains him in the dark valley. And as Samuel Rutherford, that saintly Scottish divine, wrote from his place of exile, the very worst that can be said of death is that it is the passage from the lower pastures to the upper, from the green fields of earth to the golden streets of glory, from the company of the Shepherd's flock below to the company of the Shepherd Himself in the house not made with hands, eternal in the heavens.

"Surely goodness and mercy shall follow me all the days of my life: and I will dwell in the house of the Lord for ever." Let every sheep of Christ's fold take heart from these immortal words. Your Shepherd has never lost a single one of His own. His goodness and mercy are not before you only, but behind you also, guarding your steps, covering your failures, making up every deficiency. And the end of the journey is not the grave, but the Father's house, where you shall dwell in the presence of your Shepherd forever and ever. Amen.
