PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 2
"Dark but Lovely"
Song of Solomon 1:5-8 — Beauty Beyond the Surface

---

Welcome back, dear couple. Last night we heard the bride's opening declaration of love. Tonight she reveals something deeply personal — an insecurity about her appearance, and the beloved's response that transforms it. Settle in together. Let God's Word speak to the tender places in your hearts.

Song of Solomon 1:5-8:

"I am black, but comely, O ye daughters of Jerusalem, as the tents of Kedar, as the curtains of Solomon. Look not upon me, because I am black, because the sun hath looked upon me: my mother's children were angry with me; they made me the keeper of the vineyards; but mine own vineyard have I not kept. Tell me, O thou whom my soul loveth, where thou feedest, where thou makest thy flock to rest at noon: for why should I be as one that turneth aside by the flocks of thy companions?"

--- Teaching ---

There is a stunning vulnerability in these words. The bride stands before the daughters of Jerusalem — the other women, the watching world — and confesses what she perceives as her flaw. "I am black, but comely." She has been darkened by the sun because she was forced to labor in her family's vineyards. She did not have the luxury of sheltered, pampered beauty. Life was hard. Work was demanding. And now, standing before her beloved king, she feels the sting of comparison.

Every marriage contains moments like this. Moments when one spouse feels inadequate, unworthy, or diminished — whether by past wounds, family dysfunction, the comparison trap of our age, or simply the wear and tear of years. The bride's confession is achingly honest: "My mother's children were angry with me." There is family conflict behind her insecurity. There is a history of being treated as less than. There is neglect — "mine own vineyard have I not kept." She has spent so much time serving others' demands that she has neglected her own soul, her own beauty, her own flourishing.

The Puritans understood this dynamic well. Richard Baxter, in his Christian Directory, wrote extensively about how past wounds and family dysfunction can follow a person into marriage. He counseled husbands and wives to be gentle with each other's histories — to recognize that every spouse brings into the marriage a story that includes pain, rejection, and unhealed places. Your task as a spouse is not to fix your partner's past but to create a present so full of grace that the past begins to lose its power.

Notice something crucial: the bride says "I am black, but comely." Not "I am black and therefore ugly." She holds both truths together. Yes, she has been weathered by life. Yes, she bears the marks of hardship. But she is still beautiful. The Puritans would say this is the posture of every believer before Christ: we are sinners, but justified. We are broken, but beloved. We are flawed, but beautiful in the eyes of the One who loves us.

William Gouge taught that one of the primary duties of a husband is to build up his wife's sense of her own worth — not with flattery but with genuine, consistent affirmation that flows from the heart. And the duty of a wife toward her husband is the same: to see him not as the world sees him but as God sees him — chosen, loved, and called to a noble purpose.

"Tell me, O thou whom my soul loveth." Even in her insecurity, the bride reaches for her beloved. She does not withdraw into self-pity. She does not isolate. She turns toward the one who loves her and asks for his presence. This is the key to navigating vulnerability in marriage: turn toward each other, not away. When you feel inadequate, do not retreat into silence or defensiveness. Reach for your spouse. Say, as the bride does, "Tell me where you are. I need to be near you."

The phrase "whom my soul loveth" tells us something about the depth of this love. It is not merely physical attraction. It is not infatuation. It is soul-love — the kind of attachment that goes beyond the surface into the deepest places of personhood. The Puritans called this "soul-union" and considered it the highest form of human intimacy. Thomas Gataker wrote that when two souls are knit together by covenant love, there is a union that nothing in this world can sever.

Tonight, take an honest look at the insecurities and vulnerabilities that exist in your marriage. Not to dwell on them, but to bring them into the light. The bride did not hide her darkness. She named it. And in naming it, she made space for her beloved to respond with love. Do the same tonight. Name what is hard. Name what makes you feel inadequate. And then listen — really listen — as your spouse responds not with judgment but with grace.

--- Reflection Prompts ---

It is time for reflection. Turn to each other and consider these questions together.

Question 1: Are there insecurities or past wounds that you have never fully shared with your spouse? Tonight might be the night to name one — not to solve it, but to let your beloved see it.

Question 2: How do you respond when your spouse is vulnerable with you? Do you move toward them with gentleness, or does discomfort cause you to pull away?

Question 3: The bride says she neglected her "own vineyard." Are there ways you have neglected your own soul-care — your own spiritual, emotional, or physical health — because you were busy tending everyone else's demands?

--- Prayer Focus ---

Tonight's prayer focus: Pray for healing in the tender, wounded places of your marriage. Ask God to give each of you the courage to be vulnerable and the grace to receive vulnerability with kindness. Pray over each other's insecurities — not to fix them, but to cover them with the love of Christ.

--- Closing ---

You are dark, perhaps, in the eyes of the world — weathered by life, worn by labor, marked by struggle. But you are comely in the eyes of the One who loves you. And in each other's arms, that is enough. Go in peace. Goodnight.
