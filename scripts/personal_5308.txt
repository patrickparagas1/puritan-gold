PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Guided Puritan Reading, Day 8
"All Things for Good: Affliction"
Thomas Watson — All Things for Good


Good evening, friend. Tonight we turn to one of the most beloved Puritan works ever written: Thomas Watson's "All Things Work Together for Good to Them That Love God." If you have ever suffered — and you have — this book was written for you.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Romans 8:28:

"And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


Thomas Watson was a London pastor who was ejected from his pulpit on St. Bartholomew's Day, 1662, when the Act of Uniformity forced nearly two thousand Puritan ministers out of the Church of England. Watson knew suffering firsthand. He knew what it was to lose his living, his public ministry, and his security for the sake of conscience. And yet, from this experience, he produced a work of extraordinary comfort and depth.

Watson begins by explaining the scope of the promise. Paul does not say that some things work together for good, or that pleasant things work together for good. He says "all things." Watson takes this comprehensive word and presses it to its fullest meaning. All things includes afflictions. All things includes temptations. All things includes desertions — those dark nights when God seems to have withdrawn His presence. All things includes even the sins of the believer, not in the sense that sin is good, but in the sense that God, in His infinite wisdom, can bring good out of evil.

Watson is careful to specify who this promise belongs to: "them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose." This is not a universal promise for all people. It is a particular promise for the people of God. Watson writes that the wicked have no claim upon this text. Their afflictions are not working for their good but for their judgment. But for the believer — the one who loves God and has been called by His grace — every trial, every sorrow, every loss is being woven into a tapestry of good that will one day be fully revealed.

Watson then devotes a substantial portion of his treatise to explaining how afflictions work for the believer's good. He identifies several specific goods that flow from suffering.

First, affliction works for the believer's good by revealing sin. Watson writes that prosperity often conceals sin the way a calm sea hides rocks beneath the surface. Affliction lowers the waterline and reveals what was always there. The man who is patient in prosperity may discover impatience in adversity. The woman who is generous when she has abundance may discover covetousness when she has little. This revelation is painful but necessary. You cannot mortify a sin you do not see.

Second, affliction works for the believer's good by weaning him from the world. Watson observes that the human heart is naturally addicted to earthly comforts. We cling to health, wealth, reputation, and relationships as though they were our final good. Affliction loosens our grip. It teaches us, sometimes gently and sometimes with great severity, that this world is not our home and its treasures are not our portion. Watson writes that God sometimes takes away our earthly comforts so that we might learn to find our comfort in Him alone.

Third, affliction works for the believer's good by conforming him to the image of Christ. Christ was a man of sorrows and acquainted with grief. The believer who suffers is walking in the footsteps of his Master. Watson writes that affliction is the chisel by which God shapes the believer into the likeness of His Son. The process is painful, but the result is glorious. "For whom the Lord loveth he chasteneth, and scourgeth every son whom he receiveth" (Hebrews 12:6).

Fourth, affliction produces prayer. Watson notes that believers often pray most fervently when they are suffering most deeply. Jonah prayed from the belly of the fish. David prayed from the cave at Adullam. The church prayed when Peter was imprisoned. Affliction drives us to our knees, and there we find God waiting for us with more grace than we knew we needed.

Fifth, affliction works for the believer's good by preparing him for glory. Watson develops a beautiful analogy: just as gold is refined by fire, the believer is refined by suffering. The dross of worldliness, self-reliance, and pride is burned away, and what remains is pure faith, tested and proven. Peter writes of this in his first epistle: "That the trial of your faith, being much more precious than of gold that perisheth, though it be tried with fire, might be found unto praise and honour and glory at the appearing of Jesus Christ" (1 Peter 1:7).

Watson closes his treatment of affliction with a pastoral exhortation. He urges the suffering believer not to judge God's love by his present circumstances but by the cross. If God did not spare His own Son but delivered Him up for us all, how shall He not with Him also freely give us all things? (Romans 8:32). The God who gave you His Son will not waste your suffering. Every tear is counted. Every sorrow is measured. And all things — yes, all things — are working together for your eternal good.


Take these questions with you into the quiet of the evening.

Question 1: Am I able to see past my present suffering to the good that God may be working through it?

Question 2: Has affliction revealed any hidden sin in my heart that prosperity had concealed?

Question 3: How has suffering deepened my dependence on God and loosened my grip on this world?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


O Lord, we believe Thy promise that all things work together for good. Strengthen our faith when suffering obscures Thy purposes. Teach us to trust Thy wisdom when we cannot trace Thy hand. Let our afflictions produce the peaceable fruit of righteousness. Through Christ, who suffered for us, Amen.

Goodnight, friend. The God who works all things for good is watching over you tonight.
