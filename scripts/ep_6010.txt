PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"My God, My God, Why Hast Thou Forsaken Me?"
June 10, 2026

---

"My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? why art thou so far from helping me, and from the words of my roaring? O my God, I cry in the daytime, but thou hearest not; and in the night season, and am not silent. But thou art holy, O thou that inhabitest the praises of Israel. Our fathers trusted in thee: they trusted, and thou didst deliver them." -- Psalm 22:1-4

No psalm in all the Psalter reaches such depths of anguish or scales such heights of glory as this, the great Messianic psalm that our Lord Jesus Christ quoted from the cross in the darkest hour the universe has ever known. "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?" These words, wrung from the lips of the dying Saviour as He hung between heaven and earth, bearing in His own body the sins of His people, are the most terrible and the most wonderful words ever spoken by human tongue. They are terrible because they reveal the full horror of what it cost the Son of God to redeem fallen humanity. And they are wonderful because they assure us that the price has been fully paid and that no child of God shall ever be truly forsaken.

Spurgeon, who preached upon this psalm with tears streaming down his face, declared that no human mind can fully fathom the meaning of Christ's cry of dereliction. We can approach it with reverence. We can bow before it in wonder. We can feel something of its terrible weight pressing upon our hearts. But we cannot comprehend it, for it speaks of a suffering that is unique in the history of the universe -- the suffering of the infinite Son of God enduring the wrath of His Father against sin, not for His own transgressions, for He had none, but for the transgressions of those whom He came to save.

"Why hast thou forsaken me?" The question is real, not rhetorical. In the economy of redemption, the Father did truly turn His face away from the Son in the hour of His atoning work. Not because the Father ceased to love the Son -- that is impossible, for the love between the persons of the Trinity is eternal and unchangeable. But the Son had taken upon Himself the guilt of His people, and that guilt demanded the full outpouring of divine justice. As the sin-bearer, He stood in the place of sinners, and He received what sinners deserved: the terrible experience of being cut off from the felt presence of God.

John Owen, in his great work on the death of Christ, laboured to show that the sufferings of our Lord upon the cross were not merely physical but spiritual, and that the spiritual sufferings infinitely exceeded the physical. The nails, the thorns, the scourging, the thirst -- these were grievous indeed, but they were as nothing compared to the anguish of a holy soul enduring the hiding of the Father's face. For what is hell but the eternal separation of the creature from the Creator? And Christ, in those dreadful hours upon the cross, experienced in His soul the equivalent of that separation, compressed into a finite space of time but infinite in its intensity, so that His people might never experience it at all.

"But thou art holy, O thou that inhabitest the praises of Israel." Even in the depths of His agony, the suffering Saviour does not accuse God of injustice. He affirms the holiness of God. He acknowledges that the God who has forsaken Him is righteous in doing so, for the sin that He bears deserves nothing less than the full weight of divine wrath. This is the marvel of the cross: that justice and mercy meet, that holiness and love embrace, that God punishes sin to the uttermost while simultaneously providing a way of escape for the sinner. The cross does not set aside the justice of God; it satisfies it. And the holy God who inhabits the praises of Israel is the same God who lays our sins upon His beloved Son and then raises Him from the dead as the proof that the sacrifice has been accepted and the debt has been paid.

"Our fathers trusted in thee: they trusted, and thou didst deliver them." In the midst of His suffering, Christ looks back upon the long history of God's faithfulness to His people. Abraham trusted, and God delivered him. Moses trusted, and God delivered him. David trusted, and God delivered him. Generation after generation of saints had committed their cause to the Lord and had found Him faithful. And now the Son of God Himself hangs upon the cross, trusting the Father in the darkest hour, waiting for the deliverance that will come on the third day when the stone is rolled away and the grave yields up its captive.

Thomas Goodwin meditated deeply upon the trust of Christ in His Father during the passion, and he concluded that it was the most extraordinary act of faith the world has ever seen. For Christ trusted when every sensible evidence of the Father's love had been withdrawn. He trusted when the cup of wrath was being pressed to His lips. He trusted when darkness covered the land and the earth shook and the veil of the temple was rent in two. And His trust was not disappointed, for the Father heard the cry of His Son and answered it with the resurrection, the ascension, and the exaltation to His own right hand in glory.

Let us then never forget the price that was paid for our redemption. When we are tempted to treat sin lightly, let us remember what it cost the Son of God to bear it. When we are tempted to doubt the love of God, let us look to the cross and see there the most unmistakable demonstration of love that the universe has ever witnessed. And when we ourselves walk through seasons of darkness and the felt absence of God, let us remember that our Saviour has gone before us into that darkness and has emerged victorious on the other side. Because He was forsaken, we shall never be. Because He endured the hiding of the Father's face, we shall behold that face with joy throughout eternity. Amen.
