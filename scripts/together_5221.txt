PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 21
"Resolving Disagreements"
Domestical Duties — Puritan Household Duties

---

Good evening, dear husband and wife. We close our third week with a subject that every couple knows all too well: disagreements. William Gouge understood that conflict is an inevitable part of married life. The question is not whether you will disagree, but how you will handle it when you do.

Let us read our passage. James 1:19-20 (KJV):

"Wherefore, my beloved brethren, let every man be swift to hear, slow to speak, slow to wrath: For the wrath of man worketh not the righteousness of God."

--- Teaching ---

William Gouge devoted an entire section of his Domestical Duties to the subject of disagreements between husband and wife. He began with a frank acknowledgment: even the most godly couples will disagree. Two people of different temperaments, different backgrounds, and different perspectives will inevitably see some things differently. This is not a sign of a bad marriage. It is a sign of a real one. The presence of disagreement is normal. The absence of any disagreement would be artificial and unhealthy.

What matters, Gouge taught, is how disagreements are handled. He offered several principles for godly conflict resolution that are as practical today as they were four hundred years ago.

The first principle is to be swift to hear. This is the hardest discipline in conflict. When we disagree, our natural instinct is to formulate our response while the other person is still speaking. We listen not to understand but to refute. Gouge counseled couples to discipline themselves to truly listen. Let your spouse finish speaking. Do not interrupt. Do not dismiss. Do not assume you already know what they are going to say. Ask clarifying questions. Repeat back what you have heard to make sure you understand correctly. Many disagreements dissolve when both parties feel genuinely heard.

The second principle is to be slow to speak. Do not say the first thing that comes to mind. In the heat of disagreement, the first thing that comes to mind is usually the most damaging. Pause. Think. Pray. Consider whether what you are about to say will move the conversation toward resolution or toward escalation. Gouge noted that a single careless word spoken in anger can cause more damage than a hundred kind words can repair.

The third principle is to be slow to wrath. Anger is the great destroyer of marital discussions. When anger takes control, reason departs. When voices rise, hearts close. The couple that allows anger to govern their disagreements will find that their conflicts produce only heat, never light. Gouge counseled couples to agree on a signal, a word or a gesture, that either spouse can use to pause a conversation when anger is rising. Walk away. Cool down. Return when you can speak with composure and clarity.

Gouge also identified several common errors in marital conflict. The first is generalizing. Phrases like "You always" and "You never" are almost always inaccurate and almost always inflammatory. Speak to the specific issue at hand, not to a sweeping pattern. The second is bringing up old offenses. Once an issue has been resolved and forgiven, it must not be introduced into a new disagreement. This is a violation of the forgiveness you have promised. The third is involving outsiders unnecessarily. Gouge urged couples to resolve their conflicts between themselves whenever possible, turning to a trusted pastor or counselor only when they have exhausted their own efforts.

Richard Baxter added a crucial dimension: remember that you are on the same team. In a disagreement, your spouse is not your enemy. The problem is your enemy. You and your spouse are partners fighting against the problem, not against each other. When you approach conflict with this mindset, everything changes. You stop trying to win and start trying to find a solution that is good for both of you and honoring to God.

Dear couple, when was your last disagreement? How did you handle it? Did you listen before speaking? Did you speak with restraint and respect? Did you resolve it with grace? If not, tonight is an opportunity to revisit it in a healthier way. And if there is a current disagreement simmering between you, use the principles we have studied tonight to address it with wisdom, patience, and love.

--- For Discussion ---

Question 1: When we disagree, are we more inclined to be swift to hear or swift to speak? How can we improve?

Question 2: Is there a current disagreement between us that we need to address with fresh wisdom and patience?

Question 3: What ground rules can we establish for how we handle conflict in the future, so that our disagreements are productive rather than destructive?

--- Prayer ---

O God of peace, we confess that we have not always handled our disagreements in a manner worthy of Thy name. We have been quick to speak and slow to listen. We have let anger govern our words. We have fought against each other instead of fighting together against the problem. Forgive us, Lord. Teach us the way of godly conflict resolution. Give us ears that hear, tongues that are slow to speak, and spirits that are slow to wrath. When we disagree, help us to remember that we are on the same team, united by Thy grace and bound by Thy love. May our conflicts produce not bitterness but growth, not division but deeper unity. Through Jesus Christ, the Prince of Peace, Amen.
