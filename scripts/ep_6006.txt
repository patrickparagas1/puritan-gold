PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"How Long Wilt Thou Forget Me, O Lord?"
June 6, 2026

---

"How long wilt thou forget me, O Lord? for ever? how long wilt thou hide thy face from me? How long shall I take counsel in my soul, having sorrow in my heart daily? how long shall mine enemy be exalted over me? Consider and hear me, O Lord my God: lighten mine eyes, lest I sleep the sleep of death." -- Psalm 13:1-3

Four times in the space of two short verses, David cries out "How long?" and in these repeated cries we hear the voice of a soul stretched almost to the breaking point by prolonged suffering and the apparent forgetfulness of God. This is no polite inquiry but the agonized plea of a man who has been waiting and waiting and waiting for deliverance, and who feels that he can bear the waiting no longer. And yet -- and here is the wonder of it -- he does not abandon his God. He questions, he pleads, he protests, but he does not turn away. The very intensity of his complaint is itself a proof of faith, for a man does not cry out to one in whom he has ceased to believe.

Spurgeon tenderly observed that this psalm is the cry of a believer in the dark, not the cry of an unbeliever in despair. There is a world of difference between the two. The unbeliever in despair has nowhere to turn. His suffering is meaningless, his pain is purposeless, and his future is without hope. But the believer in the dark is speaking to Someone. He is addressing his complaint to a personal God whom he knows to be good and faithful, even though present experience seems to contradict that knowledge. And this is precisely why the psalm begins with grief and ends with singing, for the man who takes his sorrow to God will always find, sooner or later, that God has an answer.

"How long wilt thou forget me, O Lord? for ever?" Now we know, from the teaching of Scripture itself, that God never truly forgets His children. "Can a woman forget her sucking child, that she should not have compassion on the son of her womb? Yea, they may forget, yet will I not forget thee," saith the Lord. But there is a felt forgetfulness, an experiential abandonment, that is every bit as painful as the real thing. When God withholds the sense of His presence, when the heavens are as brass and the prayers seem to bounce back from the ceiling, when the soul searches for the comfort of the Holy Spirit and finds only silence, the believer feels forgotten even though he is not. And it is no sin to tell God so, for David does exactly that, and the Holy Spirit saw fit to record his complaint in the inspired Word for the instruction and comfort of all subsequent generations of sufferers.

Richard Sibbes, that physician of the soul, taught that God sometimes withdraws the sense of His presence in order to teach His children the difference between the gift and the Giver. We are creatures prone to idolatry, and we can make an idol even of our spiritual comforts. When the joy of the Lord fills our hearts, we are tempted to rest in the joy rather than in the Lord who gives it. And so God, in His wise and loving discipline, occasionally removes the comfort while retaining the relationship, hides the sunshine while preserving the sun, that we might learn to trust Him for who He is and not merely for what He gives.

"How long shall I take counsel in my soul, having sorrow in my heart daily?" Here David describes the weary cycle of self-counsel that every sufferer knows. When God is silent, the soul begins to talk to itself. It turns the problem over and over, examines it from every angle, searches for some resolution, some comfort, some ray of hope -- and finds none. The mind becomes a prison, and sorrow the relentless jailer who never grants a moment's reprieve. Day after day the same dark thoughts circle round and round, and the heart grows heavier and heavier under the accumulated weight of unexplained affliction.

John Owen, in his treatise on the mortification of sin, teaches that prolonged inward sorrow, when it is not brought to the cross of Christ and left there, can become itself a species of spiritual bondage. The remedy is not to suppress the grief or to pretend that it does not exist, but to pour it out before God in honest and unreserved complaint, as David does here, and then to wait upon the Lord for the answer that will surely come. For God is not indifferent to the tears of His children. He has a bottle for every tear and a book for every sigh, and He will, in His own time, turn mourning into dancing and sorrow into joy.

"Consider and hear me, O Lord my God: lighten mine eyes, lest I sleep the sleep of death." Even in the depths of his anguish, David's prayer is full of faith. He calls God "my God," appropriating the covenant relationship even when it seems most unlikely. And his petition is specific and concrete: "Lighten mine eyes." He asks for the restoration of spiritual vision, for the lifting of the darkness that has settled upon his soul, for the return of that inward light which alone can dispel the shadows of despair. And he is right to ask for this, for the darkness of soul that comes from the felt absence of God is indeed a kind of death -- a death of joy, a death of hope, a death of spiritual vitality -- from which only God Himself can raise us.

And then comes the glorious turn, in verses five and six, which we must not overlook. "But I have trusted in thy mercy; my heart shall rejoice in thy salvation. I will sing unto the Lord, because he hath dealt bountifully with me." How is it possible that the same man who cried "How long?" should now be singing? How can sorrow so quickly give way to joy? The answer lies in the nature of faith itself. Faith does not wait for circumstances to change before it begins to praise. It lays hold upon the mercy of God in the very teeth of present suffering and declares, before the deliverance has come, that it will come. David's heart rejoices not because the trial is over but because the God who has dealt bountifully with him in the past will surely deal bountifully with him again. Amen.
