PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"Lord, Thou Hast Been Our Dwelling Place"
May 14, 2026

---

"Lord, thou hast been our dwelling place in all generations. Before the mountains were brought forth, or ever thou hadst formed the earth and the world, even from everlasting to everlasting, thou art God. Thou turnest man to destruction; and sayest, Return, ye children of men. For a thousand years in thy sight are but as yesterday when it is past, and as a watch in the night. So teach us to number our days, that we may apply our hearts unto wisdom." -- Psalm 90:1-4, 12

This is the only psalm attributed to Moses, the man of God, and it bears upon it the unmistakable marks of his long experience and his profound acquaintance with both the majesty of God and the frailty of man. Moses had watched an entire generation of Israelites perish in the wilderness. He had stood at the graves of six hundred thousand men who had come out of Egypt with high hopes and solemn promises, and who had died in the desert without ever setting foot in the Promised Land. And out of this long, melancholy experience, Moses composed a meditation on the brevity of human life and the eternity of God that has never been surpassed for its solemnity and its grandeur.

"Lord, thou hast been our dwelling place in all generations." The first word of this psalm is the most important. Lord. Not the mountains, which seem so permanent. Not the earth, which seems so solid. Not the generations of men, which come and go like waves upon the shore. But the Lord Himself is the dwelling place of His people. Everything else changes. The mountains crumble. The earth decays. The generations pass away. But God remains, the same unchanging, unfailing habitation of His children in every age. Abraham dwelt in God. Moses dwelt in God. David dwelt in God. And every believer in every century since has found in the eternal God a home that no earthquake can shake and no flood can wash away.

"Before the mountains were brought forth, or ever thou hadst formed the earth and the world, even from everlasting to everlasting, thou art God." Here Moses pushes back the curtain of time and peers into the infinite past, and what does he see? God. Before anything was, God was. Before the first mountain heaved itself up from the depths of the primordial deep, before the first star kindled its fire in the heavens, before the first angel spread its wings in the courts of glory, God was there, complete in Himself, lacking nothing, needing nothing, the self-existent, self-sufficient, eternal I AM.

And from that infinite past, Moses extends his gaze to the infinite future. "From everlasting to everlasting, thou art God." Not "thou wast God" or "thou wilt be God," but "thou art God." The present tense of eternal being. God does not become. God does not develop. God does not grow or diminish. He is. And what He is, He always has been and always shall be. This truth, which the theologians call the immutability of God, is the bedrock upon which every other comfort rests. If God could change, then His promises might fail. If God could diminish, then His power might prove insufficient. But because He is the same from everlasting to everlasting, every word He has spoken shall stand, and every purpose He has formed shall come to pass.

Against this backdrop of divine eternity, Moses now sets the picture of human frailty. "Thou turnest man to destruction; and sayest, Return, ye children of men." Man is dust, and to dust he returns. His life is like grass that springs up in the morning and by evening is cut down and withered. His years are threescore and ten, or if by reason of strength fourscore, and even those years are filled with labour and sorrow. We spend our years as a tale that is told, a story that begins and ends and is soon forgotten.

Moses does not paint this picture to drive us to despair but to drive us to wisdom. "So teach us to number our days, that we may apply our hearts unto wisdom." This is the great practical lesson of the psalm. Teach us, Lord, to reckon with the brevity of our lives, not as a morbid exercise in pessimism, but as a spur to wise and godly living. The man who truly grasps the shortness of his time upon this earth will not waste his days in trivial pursuits. He will not fritter away his hours in the accumulation of things that perish. He will invest his brief allotment of days in the things that endure: the knowledge of God, the service of Christ, the building of His kingdom, the nurture of his own soul and the souls of those entrusted to his care.

The Puritans were masters of this art of numbering their days. They kept before their minds the constant awareness that life is short and eternity is long, and this awareness gave to their lives a seriousness, a depth, and a purposefulness that our frivolous age would do well to recover. Richard Baxter, in his great work on the Reformed Pastor, confessed that he preached as a dying man to dying men, and that the nearness of death kept his preaching urgent and his living earnest. Jonathan Edwards resolved, in his famous list of resolutions, to live each day as though it were his last, and to do nothing that he would be ashamed to do if he knew the trumpet of the last judgment were about to sound.

"O satisfy us early with thy mercy; that we may rejoice and be glad all our days." Here Moses turns from the contemplation of human frailty to a plea for divine satisfaction. If our days are few, then let them be full. If our years are brief, then let them be blessed. Satisfy us, Lord, not with the fleeting pleasures of this world, but with Thy mercy, Thy lovingkindness, Thy faithful covenant love. A single day lived in the conscious enjoyment of God's mercy is worth more than a thousand years of godless prosperity. And a life that is short but saturated with the goodness of God is infinitely richer than a life that is long but empty of His presence.

"And let the beauty of the Lord our God be upon us: and establish thou the work of our hands upon us; yea, the work of our hands establish thou it." Moses does not pray that our works might be impressive or famous or profitable in the eyes of men. He prays that they might be established, made permanent, given an enduring significance by the hand of God. For the works of men, in themselves, are as fleeting as the men who perform them. Empires rise and fall. Monuments crumble. Achievements are forgotten. But the work that God establishes, the work that is done in His name and for His glory and by His power, that work shall endure when the heavens and the earth themselves have passed away. Amen.
