PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 17
"Whither Is Thy Beloved Gone?"
Song of Solomon 6:1-3 — Reunion and Mutual Belonging


Welcome, beloved couple. After the dark night of separation, after the bride's passionate description of her beloved, the daughters of Jerusalem are moved. They want to help her find him. And what follows is a beautiful moment of clarity and reunion.

Song of Solomon 6:1-3:

"Whither is thy beloved gone, O thou fairest among women? whither is thy beloved turned aside? that we may seek him with thee. My beloved is gone down into his garden, to the beds of spices, to feed in the gardens, and to gather lilies. I am my beloved's, and my beloved is mine: he feedeth among the lilies."


"Whither is thy beloved gone?" The daughters of Jerusalem, moved by the bride's eloquent description of her beloved, now want to help her find him. The Puritans noted something beautiful here: when you speak well of someone, others are drawn to help you. The bride's lavish praise of her husband did not merely express her own love — it enlisted the sympathy and support of the community around her. Richard Sibbes wrote that "a wife who speaks well of her husband makes him loved by all who hear her."

But look at the bride's response. It is not what we might expect. She does not say, "He is lost, and I do not know where he is." Instead, she suddenly knows exactly where he is: "My beloved is gone down into his garden, to the beds of spices, to feed in the gardens, and to gather lilies." In the very act of describing him — of calling to mind his beauty, his character, his sweetness — she has found him. Or rather, she has remembered where he always is.

The garden, as we learned, is the image of their intimate relationship. He is in the garden. He was always in the garden. The separation was real, but the beloved never truly left. He was in the place of intimacy, waiting for her to remember, to return, to come home.

Thomas Gataker applied this powerfully to marriage. He wrote that in times of disconnection, the answer is often not to search in distant places but to return to the familiar — to come back to the garden of your covenant, to the bed of spices that represent your shared history, your shared commitment, your shared life before God. Sometimes what feels like a vast distance is actually a short walk home.

"I am my beloved's, and my beloved is mine." This declaration appears three times in the Song, each time with a subtle shift. In chapter 2, the bride said, "My beloved is mine, and I am his" — she placed herself first. Here in chapter 6, she reverses the order: "I am my beloved's, and my beloved is mine." She has grown. The dark night, the painful separation, the desperate search have all produced a deeper maturity. She now leads with belonging to him rather than possessing him. She has learned that love is not primarily about what you get but about who you give yourself to.

William Gouge saw in this progression the natural arc of a maturing marriage. Early love is often self-focused: "What does this person do for me? How do they make me feel?" Mature love reverses the question: "How can I belong to them more fully? How can I serve, delight, and honor them?" The happiest couples are not those who have received the most from each other but those who have given the most to each other.

"He feedeth among the lilies." Lilies, throughout the Song, represent purity, beauty, and delight. The beloved feeds among the lilies — he dwells in a place of beauty and peace. And the bride knows this because she knows him. She knows his character. She knows his habits. She knows where he goes when he needs refreshment. Intimate knowledge of your spouse is a compass that always points home.

The Puritans taught that this kind of knowledge does not come naturally — it must be cultivated. Jeremiah Burroughs wrote that the first year of marriage reveals the surface, the first decade reveals the depths, and a lifetime of attentive love reveals the inexhaustible richness of the person God has given you. Do not grow bored with your spouse. You have not yet begun to discover all that is there.

Tonight, remember: your beloved is in the garden. Even when distance creeps in, even when the night feels long, even when the door was not opened quickly enough — the garden of your covenant is still there. Come home to it tonight.


Turn to each other and consider these questions.

Question 1: The bride's order changes from "my beloved is mine" to "I am my beloved's." How has your love matured from a focus on receiving to a focus on giving? Where is there room for further growth?

Question 2: Do you know your spouse well enough to know where they "go" — emotionally, mentally, spiritually — when they need refreshment? Have you asked them recently?

Question 3: What does "coming home to the garden" look like for your marriage right now? What would help you both return to the place of intimacy and connection?


Tonight's prayer focus: Pray for the grace of mutual belonging. Ask God to deepen your sense of being each other's — not in a possessive way, but in the way of covenant: fully given, fully committed, fully belonging. Pray that you would always find your way back to the garden.


I am my beloved's, and my beloved is mine. This is the truth that outlasts every dark night, every missed connection, every season of distance. You belong to each other. Rest in that belonging tonight. Goodnight.
