PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Pastoral Encouragement, Day 14
"Guarding the Mind"
John Owen — Pastoral Counsel on Thought Life

---

Good evening. Before we begin, take a deep breath. Release the tension of the day. You are in a safe place, a place where truth is spoken in love and where the gospel meets you exactly where you are.

--- Scripture ---

Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Philippians 4:8:

"Finally, brethren, whatsoever things are true, whatsoever things are honest, whatsoever things are just, whatsoever things are pure, whatsoever things are lovely, whatsoever things are of good report; if there be any virtue, and if there be any praise, think on these things."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.

--- Pastoral Word ---

Paul's command in Philippians 4:8 is among the most comprehensive instructions for the thought life anywhere in Scripture. It is not merely a suggestion but an imperative: think on these things. The command implies that the content of our thoughts is not accidental or uncontrollable but a matter of obedience. We are responsible for what we think about, what we meditate upon, what we allow to occupy the chambers of our minds.

John Owen, whose treatment of indwelling sin remains unsurpassed, understood that the mind is the battlefield where spiritual victory or defeat is determined. Owen writes that sin always begins in the mind before it expresses itself in action. Every sinful deed was once a sinful thought. Every ungodly word was once a mental rehearsal. The man who wants to overcome sin in his behavior must first learn to overcome it in his thinking.

Owen identifies what he calls the 'first risings' of sin — those initial thoughts and inclinations that, if unchecked, grow into full-blown temptation and eventually into sinful action. Owen's metaphor is vivid: sin in its first rising is like a spark. At that stage, it can be extinguished with minimal effort. But if it is allowed to smolder, it will catch flame, and if it catches flame, it will become a conflagration that is far more difficult to control. The wisest strategy is to extinguish the spark immediately — to reject the sinful thought at its first appearance, before it has time to engage the affections and bend the will.

Owen offers several practical strategies for guarding the mind. First, fill the mind with Scripture. The mind is not a vacuum; it will be filled with something. If it is not filled with the Word of God, it will be filled with the thoughts of the flesh. Owen counsels systematic meditation on Scripture — not merely reading it but turning it over in the mind, asking questions of it, applying it to specific situations, and memorizing it so that it is available when temptation strikes.

Second, Owen counsels the practice of ejaculatory prayer — brief, spontaneous prayers offered throughout the day in response to the movements of the heart. When a sinful thought arises, do not merely try to suppress it; pray against it. 'Lord, deliver me from this thought. Fix my mind on what is true, honest, just, pure, lovely, and of good report.' Owen writes that these short, frequent prayers are among the most effective weapons in the believer's arsenal.

Third, Owen warns against feeding the mind with material that stimulates ungodly thinking. This counsel, written in the seventeenth century, is even more relevant in the twenty-first. The average person today is bombarded with more information, imagery, and stimulation in a single day than our Puritan forebears encountered in a month. Much of this material is designed to provoke lust, envy, anger, discontent, or anxiety. Owen would counsel us to be ruthlessly selective about what we allow into our minds through screens, conversations, and entertainment.

Fourth, Owen emphasizes the importance of regular self-examination. He counsels believers to review their thought life at the end of each day, asking: What did I think about today? Were my thoughts glorifying to God? Did I entertain thoughts that I would be ashamed for others to know about? This is not morbid introspection but healthy spiritual hygiene — the mental equivalent of washing one's hands before eating.

Patrick, the battle for holiness is first and foremost a battle for the mind. The man who controls his thoughts will, by the Spirit's power, control his actions. The man who neglects his thought life will find that ungodliness has taken root in his heart long before it appears in his behavior. Take Paul's command seriously. Guard your mind as you would guard a fortress, for it is the fortress of your soul.

--- Reflection ---

Now let us turn the lens inward. What is God saying to you through tonight's study?

Question 1: What does my thought life look like in the unguarded moments — when I am driving, lying in bed, or waiting in line? Is it shaped by Philippians 4:8?

Question 2: Am I allowing media, entertainment, or online content to feed patterns of thinking that are contrary to godliness?

Question 3: What practical step can I take this week to fill my mind more deliberately with Scripture and less with the noise of the world?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.

--- Closing Prayer ---

Lord, set a guard over my mind as well as my mouth. I confess that my thought life is often undisciplined, wandering into territory that dishonors You. Help me to take every thought captive to the obedience of Christ. Fill my mind with what is true, honest, just, pure, lovely, and of good report. Let my meditation be acceptable in Your sight, O LORD, my strength and my redeemer. Amen.

Press on, dear saint. The race is worth running, and the prize is Christ Himself.
