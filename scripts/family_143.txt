PURITAN GOLD -- Family Devotional, Day 12
"Contentment and Thankfulness"
Pilgrim Families — Week 2, Day 5


Hello and welcome to our family devotional. Come, young and old alike. God's Word has something for every member of this family tonight. Let us draw near to Him.

Shall we pray? Our Father, we gather tonight hungry for Your truth. We know that man does not live by bread alone, but by every word that comes from Your mouth. Feed us now, Lord. Bless our family time together. In the name of Christ we pray. Amen.

God has preserved His Word for thousands of years so that families like ours could hear it tonight. Let us read from 1 Thessalonians 5:18; Psalm 100:1-5:

"In every thing give thanks: for this is the will of God in Christ Jesus concerning you. Make a joyful noise unto the LORD, all ye lands. Serve the LORD with gladness: come before his presence with singing. Know ye that the LORD he is God: it is he that hath made us, and not we ourselves; we are his people, and the sheep of his pasture. Enter into his gates with thanksgiving, and into his courts with praise: be thankful unto him, and bless his name. For the LORD is good; his mercy is everlasting; and his truth endureth to all generations."

That is 1 Thessalonians 5:18; Psalm 100:1-5. Let those words settle in your heart for a moment before we continue.


If murmuring destroys contentment, then thankfulness builds it. They are opposites, and they cannot coexist in the same heart at the same time. You cannot be truly thankful and discontent simultaneously. This is why Scripture repeatedly commands us to give thanks — not as a nice suggestion, but as God's revealed will for our lives.

Paul writes in 1 Thessalonians 5:18: "In every thing give thanks: for this is the will of God in Christ Jesus concerning you." Have you ever wondered what God's will is for your life? Here it is, plainly stated: give thanks in everything. Not just in the good things. In everything. When the promotion comes and when the job is lost. When health is strong and when sickness strikes. When relationships flourish and when they fracture. In everything, give thanks.

This does not mean we thank God for evil. It means we thank God in the midst of everything, because we trust that His purposes are good even when our circumstances are not. Romans 8:28 assures us that "all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose." We can give thanks in everything because we believe God is working in everything.

Psalm 100 gives us the pattern for a life of thanksgiving. It begins with joyful noise — contentment expresses itself in worship. Then gladness in service — a thankful person serves God not from duty but from delight. Then knowledge of who God is — "Know ye that the LORD he is God: it is he that hath made us, and not we ourselves." Thankfulness is rooted in the recognition that everything we have comes from God. We did not create ourselves. We did not earn our next breath. Everything is a gift.

The Psalmist then instructs us to enter God's gates with thanksgiving and His courts with praise. Thanksgiving is the doorway to God's presence. You cannot truly draw near to God while clutching complaints in your fist. But when you open your hands in gratitude, you open your heart to God's nearness.

The Psalm ends with three glorious truths about God: He is good, His mercy is everlasting, and His truth endures to all generations. These are the foundations of thankfulness. We give thanks because God is good — not sometimes, not usually, but always. We give thanks because His mercy never runs out — it is everlasting, never exhausted, never withdrawn from those who trust in Him. We give thanks because His truth endures — His promises do not expire, His Word does not fail, His faithfulness stretches from generation to generation.

Burroughs taught that thankfulness is the parent of all other virtues. When a heart is truly grateful, it naturally becomes generous, patient, kind, and content. A thankful person does not envy others because they are too busy appreciating what God has given them. A thankful person does not hoard because they trust that God will continue to provide. A thankful person does not worry because they know that the God who provided yesterday will provide tomorrow.

For families, cultivating thankfulness is one of the most transformative practices you can adopt. Research consistently shows that grateful people are happier, healthier, and more resilient. But long before any researcher discovered this, God commanded it in His Word. He knows how we are made, and He knows that thankfulness is the soil in which contentment grows.

Here are three practical ways to cultivate thankfulness in your family. First, begin each meal by having every person name one specific thing they are thankful for from that day. Not vague things like "everything" — specific things. "I am thankful for the sunshine this morning." "I am thankful that my friend was kind to me at school." Specificity sharpens gratitude.

Second, end each day by reviewing God's mercies together. The Puritans called this "evening examen" — looking back over the day and noting where God was at work. This trains the heart to see God's hand even in ordinary moments.

Third, when something goes wrong, practice saying, "God is still good, and we can be thankful because..." Finish the sentence together. This rewires the family's default response from complaint to gratitude and builds a habit that will carry your children through every season of life.


There is much we can learn from those who walked with God before us. Consider these words.

Jeremiah Burroughs, in his work "The Rare Jewel of Christian Contentment," wrote these words:

"A thankful heart is not only the greatest virtue, but the parent of all other virtues."

What Jeremiah Burroughs is reminding us is that the truths we have read tonight in 1 Thessalonians 5:18; Psalm 100:1-5 are not new. They have been treasured by faithful Christians for centuries. And they are just as true and just as needed in our family today as they were hundreds of years ago. Let us take these words to heart and let them shape the way we live together as a family.


Family, let us talk about what we have learned. Consider these questions together.

Question 1: Why does Paul say 'in everything' rather than 'for everything'? What is the difference?

Question 2: Psalm 100 says to 'know that the LORD he is God.' How does knowing who God is help us be thankful?

Question 3: What are three specific things from today that you can thank God for right now?

Question 4: How can our family build a daily habit of gratitude? What would that look like practically?

Take a few minutes to talk about these questions together. Let everyone share, and listen to one another with patience and love.


Now, here is our memory verse for today. Let us say it together:

"In every thing give thanks: for this is the will of God in Christ Jesus concerning you." -- 1 Thessalonians 5:18

Try to say it out loud three times right now. Then, before bed tonight, see if you can say it from memory. Tomorrow morning, try again. The more we hide God's Word in our hearts, the more it will guide us when we need it most.


Learning sticks better when we do something with it. Try this activity as a family:

Create a 'Family Thanksgiving Tree.' Cut out leaf shapes from paper. Each day this week, have every family member write one thing they are thankful for on a leaf and tape it to a branch or a tree drawn on poster board. Watch the tree fill up with gratitude.


Lord, as we close tonight's devotional, we place our family in Your hands. Watch over us as we sleep. Guard our dreams. Prepare our hearts for tomorrow. May the truths from 1 Thessalonians 5:18; Psalm 100:1-5 follow us into the new day. We love You, Lord. Amen.

Goodnight. Remember your memory verse, and carry it with you tomorrow.
