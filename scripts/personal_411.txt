PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Pastoral Encouragement, Day 11
"Ruling Your Spirit"
Puritan Counsel on Anger and Self-Control

---

Welcome. Tonight's devotional is personal. It is a word spoken from heart to heart, from one sinner saved by grace to another. Let us lay aside pretense and come before God as we truly are.

--- Scripture ---

Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Proverbs 16:32:

"He that is slow to anger is better than the mighty; and he that ruleth his spirit than he that taketh a city."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.

--- Pastoral Word ---

Solomon declares that the man who rules his spirit is greater than a military conqueror. This is a rebuke to every notion of masculinity that equates strength with aggression. True strength, in the biblical framework, is self-mastery — and specifically, mastery over anger.

Richard Baxter, in 'A Christian Directory,' devotes substantial attention to the sin of sinful anger. Baxter is careful to distinguish between righteous indignation and sinful anger. Righteous indignation is anger at sin because it offends God. Sinful anger is anger at people because they offend us. The first is directed by principle; the second is driven by pride. The first seeks justice; the second seeks revenge. The first is rare; the second is epidemic.

Baxter identifies several marks of sinful anger. First, it is disproportionate to the offense. A minor slight provokes a major explosion. Second, it is hasty. Sinful anger does not pause to assess, to consider, or to pray; it erupts immediately. Third, it is self-centered. The root of most anger is wounded pride: someone has not treated me as I think I deserve. Fourth, it is destructive. Sinful anger does not aim to correct but to punish, not to restore but to wound.

Baxter then offers practical counsel for the man who struggles with anger. His first counsel is to study your triggers. What situations, people, or circumstances most commonly provoke your anger? Baxter advises the angry man to keep a journal of his outbursts, noting the circumstances and the underlying cause. Often, he writes, the real cause is not the immediate provocation but a deeper issue: exhaustion, fear, feelings of inadequacy, or unresolved grief.

His second counsel is to practice delay. When anger rises, do not speak. Do not act. Wait. Baxter writes that most sinful words spoken in anger would never be spoken if the speaker had waited ten minutes. The tongue set on fire by anger does more damage than any other weapon in the household.

His third counsel is to consider the consequences. Baxter asks the angry man to think about the effect of his anger on his wife, his children, his friends, and his own soul. Anger in the home creates an atmosphere of fear. Children who grow up with an angry father learn either to be fearful or to be angry themselves. A wife who lives with an angry husband learns either to walk on eggshells or to build walls of self-protection. Neither outcome is what God intends for the Christian home.

His fourth counsel is to meditate on the patience of God. Baxter writes that the man who understands how much God has endured from him will be less inclined to explode at others for their minor offenses against him. The parable of the unforgiving servant (Matthew 18:21-35) is Baxter's key text: the man who has been forgiven ten thousand talents has no right to seize his brother by the throat over a hundred pence.

Thomas Watson adds that anger must be mortified at the root, not merely suppressed at the surface. The man who grits his teeth and refrains from yelling but seethes inwardly has not conquered his anger; he has merely contained it. Watson counsels the cultivation of meekness as a positive virtue, not merely the absence of anger but the presence of gentleness, patience, and a readiness to overlook offenses.

James Durham, the Scottish Puritan, writes in his commentary on the Song of Solomon that the truly godly man is marked by a spirit of gentleness that draws others to Christ. This gentleness is not natural temperament; it is the fruit of the Spirit, cultivated through prayer, self-examination, and the daily practice of self-denial.

The challenge before you today is honest self-assessment. If you are a man who struggles with anger — and most men do to some degree — do not excuse it as passion, intensity, or righteous zeal. Call it what it is: a failure to rule your spirit. And then, by the grace of God, begin the hard work of change. You may be mighty in many areas of life, but Solomon says the man who rules his spirit is greater still.

--- Reflection ---

The Puritans kept spiritual journals. Consider writing your answers to these questions.

Question 1: Am I honest about the role anger plays in my life, or do I excuse it as justified, necessary, or insignificant?

Question 2: What are the specific triggers that most commonly provoke sinful anger in me, and what lies beneath those triggers?

Question 3: What would my wife and children say about the atmosphere I create in our home — is it marked by gentleness or by the fear of my anger?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.

--- Closing Prayer ---

Father, I confess that I have too often let anger rule me rather than ruling my spirit. Forgive me for the wounds my anger has inflicted on those I love. Grant me the meekness of Christ, the patience of the Spirit, and the strength to rule my tongue and my temper. Amen.

Go in peace. The God who began a good work in you will bring it to completion.
