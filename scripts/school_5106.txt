PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 6
"Of the Fall and Sin"
The Westminster Standards — Lesson 6

---

Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we turn to one of the most solemn and sobering chapters in the Westminster Standards — the teaching on the fall of man and the nature of sin. If the doctrine of creation tells us how the world began, the doctrine of the fall tells us how the world went wrong. And if we do not understand how the world went wrong, we will never understand why we need a Savior, what Christ came to accomplish, or why the gospel is truly good news. The fall of man is not a popular subject in our culture. Modern people would prefer to think of themselves as basically good — as decent, well-meaning individuals who make occasional mistakes but who are fundamentally on the right path. The Westminster Standards paint a very different picture. They teach that humanity, through the sin of Adam, has plunged into a state of total ruin — that every faculty of the soul has been corrupted, that every person born into the world is born in a state of guilt and depravity, and that apart from the sovereign grace of God, no one would ever be saved. This is hard teaching, but it is faithful teaching — teaching that is rooted in the clear testimony of Holy Scripture. Let us pray and begin.

Holy God, as we study the doctrine of the fall today, give us eyes to see the horror of sin as Thou dost see it. Humble us under the weight of our corruption. Strip away every illusion of self-righteousness and every false comfort that we have built upon our own supposed goodness. And having laid bare our ruin, lead us to the cross of Christ, where alone our ruin finds its remedy. In His precious name, Amen.

--- Lesson ---

Part One: The Covenant of Works and the Probation of Adam

To understand the fall, we must first understand the arrangement under which Adam lived before he fell. The Westminster Standards teach that God entered into a covenant with Adam — a covenant that theologians call the covenant of works. The Shorter Catechism, Question 12, asks: "What special act of providence did God exercise toward man in the estate wherein he was created?" and answers: "When God had created man, he entered into a covenant of life with him, upon condition of perfect obedience; forbidding him to eat of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, upon the pain of death."

The Confession elaborates in Chapter 7, Section 2: "The first covenant made with man was a covenant of works, wherein life was promised to Adam, and in him to his posterity, upon condition of perfect and personal obedience."

Several crucial elements must be noted. First, this was a real covenant — a binding agreement between God and man, with clearly stated terms, conditions, and consequences. God was not making a casual suggestion when He commanded Adam not to eat of the forbidden tree. He was establishing a solemn covenant arrangement that would determine the destiny of the entire human race.

Second, the condition of the covenant was "perfect and personal obedience." Adam was required to obey God completely and without any failure. The test was simple — do not eat of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. This was not an arbitrary or burdensome command. It was a single prohibition amid a world of generous permission. "And the LORD God commanded the man, saying, Of every tree of the garden thou mayest freely eat: But of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, thou shalt not eat of it: for in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die" (Genesis 2:16-17, KJV). God gave Adam an entire paradise to enjoy and forbade only one tree. The prohibition was a test of Adam's willing submission to God's authority — would he trust God's word, obey God's command, and acknowledge God's right to rule?

Third, the promise of the covenant was "life" — not merely the continuation of the life Adam already possessed, but a confirmed, glorified, eternal life that would have been his reward for faithful obedience. The Puritans believed that had Adam passed the period of probation successfully, he would have been confirmed in holiness — raised to a state in which sin was no longer possible, where he would enjoy the fullness of communion with God forever. The Puritan Francis Turretin, whose theology deeply influenced the Westminster tradition, taught that the covenant of works offered Adam the prospect of advancing from a state of mutable holiness to a state of immutable blessedness — from the ability not to sin to the inability to sin.

Fourth, Adam stood in this covenant not merely as a private individual but as a public person — as the federal head and representative of the entire human race. His obedience would have secured life for all his posterity. His disobedience would bring death upon all his posterity. This is the doctrine of federal headship, and it is essential to understanding both the fall and the gospel. Just as Adam represented all humanity in the covenant of works, so Christ — the second Adam — represents His people in the covenant of grace. "For as in Adam all die, even so in Christ shall all be made alive" (1 Corinthians 15:22, KJV).

Part Two: The Fall Itself

The Westminster Confession, Chapter 6, Section 1, describes the fall with characteristic brevity and gravity: "Our first parents, being seduced by the subtilty and temptation of Satan, sinned in eating the forbidden fruit. This their sin God was pleased, according to his wise and holy counsel, to permit, having purposed to order it to his own glory."

The Genesis narrative provides the detailed account. The serpent — Satan working through a creature — approached Eve with a strategy of deception. He began by questioning God's word: "Yea, hath God said?" (Genesis 3:1, KJV). He then contradicted God's word: "Ye shall not surely die" (Genesis 3:4, KJV). And he impugned God's motive: "For God doth know that in the day ye eat thereof, then your eyes shall be opened, and ye shall be as gods, knowing good and evil" (Genesis 3:5, KJV). Satan's strategy was threefold — to make Eve doubt God's word, disbelieve God's warning, and distrust God's goodness. And the strategy succeeded.

"And when the woman saw that the tree was good for food, and that it was pleasant to the eyes, and a tree to be desired to make one wise, she took of the fruit thereof, and did eat, and gave also unto her husband with her; and he did eat" (Genesis 3:6, KJV). Notice the progression of sin: Eve saw, she desired, she took, and she ate. The apostle John later identified these same three categories of temptation: "the lust of the flesh, and the lust of the eyes, and the pride of life" (1 John 2:16, KJV). The tree was "good for food" — the lust of the flesh. It was "pleasant to the eyes" — the lust of the eyes. And it was "desired to make one wise" — the pride of life. Every temptation that has assailed the human race since that day is a variation of these three.

The Puritan Thomas Goodwin analyzed the fall with great depth, observing that the sin of Adam was not merely one act of disobedience but involved the breaking of every commandment of the moral law simultaneously. In eating the forbidden fruit, Adam put another god before the true God (he made his own desire supreme), he coveted what God had forbidden, he stole what was not his to take, he bore false witness against God by acting as if God's warning were untrue, he dishonored his Father in heaven, and he committed spiritual murder by plunging himself and all his posterity into death. The Puritan Thomas Watson echoed this analysis, teaching that Adam's sin was of almost infinite magnitude — not because the act itself was outwardly dramatic, but because it was committed against infinite goodness, with infinite consequences, and involved the wholesale repudiation of God's authority, wisdom, and love.

Part Three: The Nature of Adam's Sin

Before we examine the consequences of the fall, it is important to grasp the full nature of Adam's sin. The Westminster divines understood that the eating of the forbidden fruit was not a trivial act of minor disobedience. It was a sin of staggering proportions — a sin that involved the violation of every principle of God's moral law.

Consider what Adam's sin involved. It involved unbelief — Adam chose to believe the serpent's word over God's word. It involved ingratitude — Adam had been given everything he could desire in the Garden, yet he reached for the one thing God had withheld. It involved pride — Adam aspired to "be as gods," exalting himself above his appointed station. It involved rebellion — Adam deliberately violated a clear, explicit command of his Creator. It involved idolatry — Adam made his own desire supreme, placing the creature's will above the Creator's authority. And it involved a betrayal of trust — Adam had been appointed steward and guardian of the Garden, and he failed in his sacred charge.

The Larger Catechism, Question 25, probes the severity of Adam's sin by asking how serious the fall was. The answer explains: "The fall brought mankind into an estate of sin and misery." The Catechism's brevity here masks the immensity of what occurred. In one act of disobedience, Adam destroyed the moral and spiritual condition of every human being who would ever live. He plunged the entire creation into bondage. He forfeited the blessedness that God had prepared for him and his descendants. And he brought upon himself and all his posterity the wrath and curse of a holy God.

The Puritan Stephen Charnock observed that the gravity of any sin is measured by the dignity of the person against whom it is committed. A crime against a fellow citizen is one thing; a crime against a king is another. But a crime against the infinite, eternal, all-glorious God of heaven and earth — the God who created Adam, blessed Adam, loved Adam, and entrusted Adam with the most generous and gracious covenant imaginable — such a crime is of infinite gravity. Adam sinned against infinite love, infinite wisdom, and infinite authority. And the consequences were proportional to the offense.

Moreover, Adam's sin was committed under the most favorable conditions imaginable. He was not deceived as Eve was — the apostle Paul notes that "Adam was not deceived" (1 Timothy 2:14, KJV). He sinned with open eyes, fully aware of what he was doing and what the consequences would be. He was not afflicted with the weakness of a fallen nature — he had no sinful inclination to tempt him from within. He was not oppressed by difficult circumstances — he lived in a paradise of unimaginable beauty and abundance. He was not ignorant of God's will — the command was clear and unambiguous. In short, Adam had every advantage, every resource, and every motivation to obey, and yet he chose to rebel. This is what makes his sin so inexcusable and its consequences so devastating.

Part Four: The Consequences of the Fall — Guilt

The fall brought devastating consequences upon Adam, upon all his posterity, and upon the entire created order. The Westminster Standards organize these consequences under two headings: guilt and corruption.

The Confession, Chapter 6, Section 3, states: "They being the root of all mankind, the guilt of this sin was imputed, and the same death in sin and corrupted nature conveyed to all their posterity, descending from them by ordinary generation."

The Shorter Catechism, Question 16, asks: "Did all mankind fall in Adam's first transgression?" and answers: "The covenant being made with Adam, not only for himself, but for his posterity, all mankind, descending from him by ordinary generation, sinned in him, and fell with him, in his first transgression."

This is the doctrine of original sin — the teaching that the guilt of Adam's sin is imputed to all his descendants. We are not merely affected by Adam's sin in some vague, indirect way. We are counted guilty of Adam's sin because Adam acted as our representative in the covenant of works. When he sinned, we sinned in him. When he fell, we fell with him. The apostle Paul states this plainly: "Wherefore, as by one man sin entered into the world, and death by sin; and so death passed upon all men, for that all have sinned" (Romans 5:12, KJV). And again: "By one man's disobedience many were made sinners" (Romans 5:19, KJV).

This doctrine offends the modern mind, which insists that individuals should be held responsible only for their own personal actions. But the principle of representation is deeply embedded in human experience. A nation's ambassador commits that nation by his words and actions. A parent's decisions affect the welfare of all their children. And Adam, as the divinely appointed head and representative of the human race, committed us all by his fateful choice in the Garden. The Puritan John Owen pointed out that those who object to being condemned in Adam must also reckon with the fact that believers are saved in Christ — by the same principle of representation. If it is unjust for Adam to represent us in sin, then it would be equally unjust for Christ to represent us in righteousness. But Scripture teaches both, and the believer has far more reason to rejoice in Christ's headship than to complain about Adam's.

Part Four: The Consequences of the Fall — Corruption

Beyond guilt, the fall brought corruption — a deep, pervasive, radical corruption of the entire human nature. The Confession, Chapter 6, Section 2, describes it this way: "By this sin they fell from their original righteousness and communion with God, and so became dead in sin, and wholly defiled in all the faculties and parts of soul and body."

The Shorter Catechism, Question 18, asks: "Wherein consists the sinfulness of that estate whereinto man fell?" and answers: "The sinfulness of that estate whereinto man fell, consists in the guilt of Adam's first sin, the want of original righteousness, and the corruption of his whole nature, which is commonly called original sin; together with all actual transgressions which proceed from it."

Notice the comprehensive scope of this corruption. It is not that some parts of human nature were damaged while others remained intact. Every faculty — the mind, the will, the emotions, the conscience, the body — is corrupted. The mind is darkened: "the natural man receiveth not the things of the Spirit of God: for they are foolishness unto him: neither can he know them, because they are spiritually discerned" (1 Corinthians 2:14, KJV). The will is enslaved: "the carnal mind is enmity against God: for it is not subject to the law of God, neither indeed can be" (Romans 8:7, KJV). The heart is depraved: "The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately wicked: who can know it?" (Jeremiah 17:9, KJV). The affections are disordered: humanity now loves what it should hate and hates what it should love. The conscience is defiled: it can be seared, hardened, and rendered unreliable. Even the body, though not inherently evil, is now subject to disease, decay, and death — and its appetites can become instruments of sin.

This is the Reformed doctrine of total depravity. The word "total" does not mean that every person is as wicked as they could possibly be — common grace restrains the full expression of human depravity. Rather, it means that every part of human nature is affected by sin. There is no island of righteousness within fallen man — no untouched faculty, no uncorrupted capacity, no residual spark of spiritual goodness that can serve as the starting point for self-salvation.

The Puritan Jeremiah Burroughs illustrated total depravity by comparing the human heart to a fountain. If the fountain is poisoned at its source, then every stream that flows from it will be poisoned as well. The fountain of the human heart was poisoned at the fall, and therefore every thought, every desire, every word, and every action that flows from it is tainted with sin. "Who can bring a clean thing out of an unclean? not one" (Job 14:4, KJV).

Part Five: The Effects of Sin on Human Understanding

The corruption brought by the fall extends with particular devastation to the human mind. Before the fall, Adam possessed true knowledge of God — he understood the divine character, perceived the divine will, and could reason clearly about spiritual realities. After the fall, the mind was plunged into darkness. "Having the understanding darkened, being alienated from the life of God through the ignorance that is in them, because of the blindness of their heart" (Ephesians 4:18, KJV).

This intellectual corruption manifests in several ways. First, fallen man has no natural capacity to understand spiritual truth. He may be brilliant in mathematics, philosophy, science, or the arts — common grace allows for remarkable intellectual achievement in natural matters — but in spiritual things, he is blind. He cannot perceive the beauty of Christ, the glory of the gospel, or the sweetness of divine grace. "But the natural man receiveth not the things of the Spirit of God: for they are foolishness unto him" (1 Corinthians 2:14, KJV).

Second, fallen man is prone to self-deception. The heart, being corrupt, deceives the mind — leading it to construct elaborate justifications for sin, to rationalize rebellion, and to suppress the knowledge of God that creation makes plain. "For the wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness and unrighteousness of men, who hold the truth in unrighteousness" (Romans 1:18, KJV). The word "hold" here means "suppress" — fallen man actively holds down the truth that God has made available to him, replacing it with lies and idolatries of his own devising.

The Puritan William Perkins, who was one of the founding fathers of Puritan theology at Cambridge University, wrote extensively about the noetic effects of sin — the effects of sin upon the mind. Perkins argued that the fall did not merely weaken human reason; it redirected it. The fallen mind is still rational, still capable of logical thought, still able to accumulate knowledge — but it now uses these capacities in the service of rebellion rather than worship. The mind that was made to know God now labors to suppress the knowledge of God. The reason that was made to pursue truth now invents excuses for unbelief. This is why mere intellectual argument, by itself, can never convert a sinner. The problem is not a lack of evidence but a corruption of the heart that refuses to receive the evidence.

Part Six: The Transmission of Original Sin

The Confession teaches that this corrupted nature is "conveyed to all their posterity, descending from them by ordinary generation." This means that original sin is not merely a legal fiction or a theological abstraction. It is a real condition that is passed from parent to child through natural generation. Every human being born into the world — with the sole exception of the Lord Jesus Christ, who was conceived by the Holy Spirit — is born with a fallen nature, inheriting both the guilt and corruption of Adam's sin.

David confessed this truth in Psalm 51:5: "Behold, I was shapen in iniquity; and in sin did my mother conceive me" (KJV). David was not saying that the act of conception is sinful. He was saying that from the very first moment of his existence, he was a sinner — that the corruption of original sin was present in him from the womb.

The Puritans took this doctrine with great seriousness in their approach to child-rearing. They did not view children as innocent creatures who become sinful only when they grow old enough to consciously choose to do wrong. They recognized that children are born with a sinful nature — with a disposition toward rebellion, selfishness, and disobedience that manifests itself from the earliest days of life. Any parent who has watched a toddler seize a toy and shout "Mine!" has witnessed the evidence of original sin in action. This understanding did not make the Puritans harsh or unloving toward their children. Rather, it made them urgent about the need to bring their children to Christ — knowing that even the youngest child needs the saving grace of God.

The Puritan Richard Baxter counseled parents to begin the work of spiritual instruction as early as possible, precisely because the corruption of nature is present from the beginning. He urged parents to pray over their children, to catechize them, to teach them the Scriptures, and to set before them the example of godly living — not because these efforts would save their children (only God can do that), but because God ordinarily works through such means to bring His elect to saving faith.

Part Six: The Bondage of the Will

One of the most important — and most contested — aspects of the fall is its effect upon the human will. The Confession, Chapter 9, Section 3, states: "Man, by his fall into a state of sin, hath wholly lost all ability of will to any spiritual good accompanying salvation; so as a natural man, being altogether averse from that good, and dead in sin, is not able, by his own strength, to convert himself, or to prepare himself thereunto."

This is the doctrine of the bondage of the will — the teaching that fallen man is unable to do anything spiritually good, unable to turn to God in faith and repentance, and unable to contribute anything to his own salvation. The will is not neutral — standing equidistant between good and evil, free to choose either way. The will is enslaved to sin — inclined by its corrupt nature always toward evil and never toward genuine spiritual good.

This does not mean that fallen man has no will, or that he is forced to sin against his will. The will is still operative — fallen man makes choices every day. But those choices are always shaped and directed by the corrupt nature from which they spring. A poisoned fountain cannot choose to produce clean water. A dead man cannot choose to come alive. And a will that is enslaved to sin cannot choose to love God, trust Christ, or obey the gospel — apart from the regenerating work of the Holy Spirit.

The apostle Paul described this condition with devastating clarity: "There is none righteous, no, not one: There is none that understandeth, there is none that seeketh after God. They are all gone out of the way, they are together become unprofitable; there is none that doeth good, no, not one" (Romans 3:10-12, KJV). And our Lord Himself declared: "No man can come to me, except the Father which hath sent me draw him" (John 6:44, KJV).

The Puritan John Owen wrote extensively on the bondage of the will in his treatise "A Display of Arminianism." Owen argued that the Arminian view — which holds that fallen man retains the natural ability to believe the gospel — effectively undermines the doctrines of grace. If man can save himself by an act of his own will, then salvation is not truly of grace but of human achievement. Owen insisted that the biblical picture is of a man "dead in trespasses and sins" (Ephesians 2:1, KJV) — not merely sick, not merely weakened, but dead — and therefore utterly dependent upon the sovereign, life-giving power of God to raise him from spiritual death to spiritual life.

Part Seven: The Effects of Sin Upon the Created Order

The fall did not merely corrupt humanity — it brought a curse upon the entire created order. When Adam sinned, the ground itself was cursed for his sake. "Cursed is the ground for thy sake; in sorrow shalt thou eat of it all the days of thy life; Thorns also and thistles shall it bring forth to thee" (Genesis 3:17-18, KJV). The creation that God had declared "very good" was now subjected to frustration, decay, and futility.

The apostle Paul provides the most extensive commentary on this cosmic dimension of the fall: "For the creature was made subject to vanity, not willingly, but by reason of him who hath subjected the same in hope, Because the creature itself also shall be delivered from the bondage of corruption into the glorious liberty of the children of God. For we know that the whole creation groaneth and travaileth in pain together until now" (Romans 8:20-22, KJV). The entire natural world — animals, plants, mountains, oceans, the very fabric of physical reality — has been affected by the fall. The death, decay, suffering, and disorder that we see in nature are not features of the world as God originally made it. They are consequences of human sin.

The Puritans understood this truth and applied it with characteristic thoroughness. They recognized that natural disasters — earthquakes, floods, famines, plagues — are not merely random events but are ultimately traceable to the curse that fell upon creation because of Adam's sin. This did not make them fatalistic about suffering. Rather, it deepened their understanding of the comprehensiveness of sin's effects and intensified their longing for the redemption that Christ will bring — not merely the redemption of human souls but the redemption of the entire created order. The Puritan Richard Sibbes spoke of the coming restoration of all things as the great hope of creation itself — the day when the curse will be lifted, when death will be abolished, and when the new heavens and new earth will shine with a glory that surpasses even the original paradise.

Part Eight: Actual Sin

Original sin is the root; actual sins are the fruit. The Shorter Catechism, Question 14, asks: "What is sin?" and answers with a definition of remarkable precision: "Sin is any want of conformity unto, or transgression of, the law of God."

This definition identifies two dimensions of sin. First, sin is "any want of conformity unto the law of God" — that is, any failure to measure up to God's standard of perfect righteousness. This is the sin of omission — the failure to do what God requires. We sin not only when we do what is wrong but when we fail to do what is right. "Therefore to him that knoweth to do good, and doeth it not, to him it is sin" (James 4:17, KJV).

Second, sin is any "transgression of the law of God" — that is, any violation of God's commandments. This is the sin of commission — the active doing of what God forbids. "Whosoever committeth sin transgresseth also the law: for sin is the law of the transgression" (1 John 3:4, KJV).

Together, these two dimensions cover the entire range of human sinfulness. We sin by doing what God forbids and by failing to do what God commands. We sin in thought, word, and deed. We sin in our motives, our intentions, and our actions. We sin by commission and by omission. And every sin — whether great or small in human estimation — is an offense against the infinite holiness of God and deserves His righteous condemnation.

The Larger Catechism, Question 151, addresses the question of whether all sins are equally serious and answers that "some sins in themselves, and by reason of several aggravations, are more heinous in the sight of God than others." The circumstances, the knowledge, the position, and the deliberation of the sinner can all make a sin more or less aggravated. A sin committed by a minister of the gospel is more heinous than the same sin committed by someone who has never heard the gospel. A sin committed deliberately is more heinous than one committed in sudden passion. A sin committed against clear knowledge is more heinous than one committed in ignorance.

The Puritan Ralph Venning, in his masterful work "The Sinfulness of Sin," argued that sin is the worst evil in the universe — worse than suffering, worse than death, worse than hell itself. For suffering, death, and hell are merely the consequences of sin; sin is the cause. If there were no sin, there would be no suffering, no death, and no hell. Venning urged his readers to view sin not as a minor infraction or a trivial mistake but as cosmic treason — a rebellion against the infinite majesty, authority, and goodness of the Most High God. He wrote that sin is contrary to the nature of God, contrary to the law of God, contrary to the image of God in man, contrary to the well-being of the sinner, and contrary to the good of the whole creation.

Part Eight: The Misery of the Fall

The Shorter Catechism, Question 19, asks: "What is the misery of that estate whereinto man fell?" and answers: "All mankind by their fall lost communion with God, are under his wrath and curse, and so made liable to all miseries in this life, to death itself, and to the pains of hell for ever."

This is a comprehensive catalog of human misery, and it traces every form of suffering back to the fall.

First, we "lost communion with God." Before the fall, Adam and Eve enjoyed unbroken fellowship with their Creator. After the fall, that fellowship was shattered. Sin creates a barrier between God and man — a chasm that no human effort can bridge. "But your iniquities have separated between you and your God, and your sins have hid his face from you, that he will not hear" (Isaiah 59:2, KJV). The loss of communion with God is the root of all other miseries, for God is the source of all goodness, all joy, all peace, and all meaning. To be cut off from God is to be cut off from everything that makes life truly worth living.

Second, we are "under his wrath and curse." God is not indifferent to sin. He is holy, and His holiness demands that sin be punished. "The wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness and unrighteousness of men" (Romans 1:18, KJV). The curse of God rests upon sinful humanity — the curse pronounced in Genesis 3, which brought suffering upon childbirth, toil upon labor, thorns upon the ground, and death upon the human race.

Third, we are "made liable to all miseries in this life" — sickness, pain, grief, conflict, disappointment, injustice, poverty, loneliness, anxiety, and every other form of earthly suffering. These are not natural features of the world God made. They are the bitter fruits of the fall.

Fourth, we are liable "to death itself." Death is the wages of sin (Romans 6:23). It entered the world through Adam's transgression and has reigned over humanity ever since. "By one man sin entered into the world, and death by sin; and so death passed upon all men, for that all have sinned" (Romans 5:12, KJV).

Fifth, we are liable "to the pains of hell for ever." The ultimate consequence of sin is eternal separation from God in a state of conscious torment. This is the most terrible truth in all of Scripture, and it is a truth that the Westminster Standards do not shrink from declaring. "The wicked shall be turned into hell, and all the nations that forget God" (Psalm 9:17, KJV).

Part Nine: The Universality of Sin

The Westminster Standards make clear that the effects of the fall are universal — extending to every human being without exception. The Shorter Catechism, Question 15, asks: "What was the sin whereby our first parents fell from the estate wherein they were created?" and answers: "The sin whereby our first parents fell from the estate wherein they were created, was their eating the forbidden fruit." And as we have seen, the guilt and corruption of that sin have been transmitted to every descendant of Adam.

Scripture confirms this universality with emphatic clarity. "For there is not a just man upon earth, that doeth good, and sinneth not" (Ecclesiastes 7:20, KJV). "If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us" (1 John 1:8, KJV). "For all have sinned, and come short of the glory of God" (Romans 3:23, KJV). There is no race, no nation, no class, no family, and no individual that is exempt from the corruption of the fall. The most civilized and cultured person is as much a sinner by nature as the most degraded criminal. The difference between them is not a difference of nature but of circumstance and restraining grace.

The Puritan Thomas Brooks, in his famous work "Precious Remedies Against Satan's Devices," warned believers against the common delusion that some sins are too small to matter. Brooks argued that there is no small sin, because there is no small God to sin against. Every sin — no matter how trivial it may appear in human estimation — is an act of cosmic rebellion against the sovereign Lord of the universe. And every sin, left unrepented and unforgiven, is sufficient to damn the soul for eternity. This is not a truth designed to produce despair but to produce a proper estimation of sin's gravity — and thereby a proper appreciation of the grace that delivers us from sin's penalty and power.

Part Ten: The Necessity of Grace

The doctrine of the fall, understood rightly, drives us to one inescapable conclusion: we need a Savior. If we are guilty, we need someone to bear our guilt. If we are corrupt, we need someone to cleanse and renew us. If our wills are enslaved, we need someone to set us free. If we are dead in sin, we need someone to give us life. And that someone is Jesus Christ — the second Adam, the federal head of a new humanity, the One who came to undo the ruin of the fall and to restore what Adam lost.

The Puritan Thomas Boston, in his monumental work "Human Nature in Its Fourfold State," traced the condition of humanity through four stages: the state of innocence (before the fall), the state of nature (after the fall), the state of grace (after regeneration), and the state of glory (in heaven). Boston showed that the fall reduced humanity from the highest dignity to the lowest misery — from the image of God to the image of the devil, from life to death, from paradise to exile, from communion with God to enmity against God. But he also showed that the grace of God in Christ is sufficient to reverse every aspect of the fall — to restore the image, to give new life, to reconcile the sinner to God, and ultimately to bring the redeemed to a state of glory that exceeds even the glory of Eden.

The Puritan Thomas Manton put it simply and powerfully: the deeper our knowledge of sin, the higher our appreciation of grace. If we think lightly of sin, we will think lightly of the cross. But if we understand the true horror of our condition — the depth of our guilt, the extent of our corruption, the impossibility of self-salvation — then we will cling to Christ with a desperate, grateful, unshakable faith. And that is precisely where God wants us.

Part Ten: Practical Applications

Several practical applications flow from the doctrine of the fall and sin.

First, the doctrine of the fall should produce humility. We have nothing of which to boast. Our best works are tainted with sin. Our most righteous deeds are, in themselves, insufficient to merit God's favor. "But we are all as an unclean thing, and all our righteousnesses are as filthy rags" (Isaiah 64:6, KJV). True self-knowledge is the foundation of all genuine humility.

Second, the doctrine of total depravity should produce dependence upon God. Since we cannot save ourselves, we must cast ourselves entirely upon the mercy of God in Christ. We come to God not with achievements to present but with empty hands to receive.

Third, the doctrine of sin should produce vigilance. If sin is as pervasive and powerful as the Westminster Standards teach, then believers must be constantly on guard against its influence. "Watch and pray, that ye enter not into temptation" (Matthew 26:41, KJV). The Puritans were masters of what they called "the mortification of sin" — the daily, deliberate, Spirit-empowered killing of sinful desires and habits.

Fourth, the doctrine of the fall should produce compassion. If all people are equally fallen, then we have no ground for self-righteous judgment of others. We should look upon the sins of others with grief rather than contempt, knowing that "there but for the grace of God" go we.

Fifth, the doctrine of the fall should produce evangelistic urgency. If people are dead in sin and heading toward eternal judgment, then the task of proclaiming the gospel is the most urgent work in the world. We must warn sinners of their danger and point them to the only Savior who can deliver them. The Puritan Richard Baxter was consumed with this urgency. He labored tirelessly in his parish of Kidderminster, going from house to house to catechize families and to plead with sinners to come to Christ. Baxter understood that the doctrine of the fall is not merely an academic proposition — it is a reality with eternal consequences. Every person you meet is either saved or lost, either alive in Christ or dead in Adam, either on the road to heaven or on the road to hell. That knowledge, rightly understood, should make us urgent, compassionate, and faithful in our witness.

Sixth, the doctrine of the fall should deepen our worship. When we understand how far we have fallen and how great is the grace that has lifted us up, our worship takes on a depth and sincerity that it could never have otherwise. We worship not because we deserve God's favor but because we have received it despite deserving only His wrath. That is the worship of the forgiven sinner — the worship that heaven itself will echo for all eternity.

--- Review Questions ---

Review Question 1: What was the covenant of works? What were its terms, its condition, and its promise? Why was Adam's role as federal head so significant?

Review Question 2: Describe the strategy of Satan's temptation of Eve in Genesis 3. How did Satan attack God's word, God's warning, and God's character? What parallels can you see with temptations you face today?

Review Question 3: Explain the difference between guilt and corruption as consequences of the fall. How does the Confession describe each? What Scripture passages support these teachings?

Review Question 4: What is total depravity? Does it mean that every person is as evil as possible? Explain what it does and does not mean, using the Westminster Standards and Scripture.

Review Question 5: The Shorter Catechism lists five aspects of human misery resulting from the fall. List and explain each one, and discuss how they point to the need for a Savior.

--- Activity ---

Create a study chart with two columns titled "Before the Fall" and "After the Fall." In each column, describe the condition of the human mind, will, emotions, conscience, and body. Use Scripture references and quotations from the Westminster Confession and Catechisms to support your descriptions. At the bottom of the chart, write a paragraph explaining why the fall makes the gospel necessary. Then memorize the Shorter Catechism's definition of sin (Question 14) and be prepared to recite and explain it in your next session.

--- Closing ---

The doctrine of the fall is not a doctrine of despair. It is a doctrine of truth — a truth that clears away our illusions, strips away our self-righteousness, and prepares our hearts to receive the most glorious message in the universe: that God, in His infinite mercy, has provided a Savior for fallen sinners. We are more sinful than we ever dared to imagine. But God's grace is greater than all our sin. As the apostle Paul declared: "But where sin abounded, grace did much more abound" (Romans 5:20, KJV). The same God who allowed the fall has provided the remedy. And that remedy is the subject of our next lesson. Until next time, students.
