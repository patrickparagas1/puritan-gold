PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"Search Me, O God"
May 23, 2026

---

"O Lord, thou hast searched me, and known me. Thou knowest my downsitting and mine uprising, thou understandest my thought afar off. Thou compassest my path and my lying down, and art acquainted with all my ways. For there is not a word in my tongue, but, lo, O Lord, thou knowest it altogether. Search me, O God, and know my heart: try me, and know my thoughts: And see if there be any wicked way in me, and lead me in the way everlasting." -- Psalm 139:1-4, 23-24

This psalm is one of the most personal, most intimate, and most searching in the entire Psalter. It begins with a declaration that would terrify the hypocrite but comforts the sincere: "O Lord, thou hast searched me, and known me." The all-seeing eye of God has penetrated to the very centre of David's being. Not a thought, not a motive, not a secret intention has escaped His notice. And yet David does not flee from this knowledge. He embraces it. For the man who has nothing to hide finds the omniscience of God not a threat but a comfort, not a terror but a refuge.

"Thou knowest my downsitting and mine uprising." The most ordinary, the most mundane, the most insignificant actions of our daily lives are known to God. When we sit down and when we stand up, when we go out and when we come in, when we eat and when we sleep, when we work and when we rest, all is observed, all is noted, all is understood by the infinite mind of God. There is no moment so trivial that God is not present in it, no action so small that God is not aware of it.

"Thou understandest my thought afar off." Before the thought has fully formed in our minds, before it has taken shape in our consciousness, before we ourselves are aware of what we are thinking, God understands it. He reads our thoughts not after they have been expressed but before they have been conceived. He knows us better than we know ourselves. He discerns motives that we cannot discern, perceives patterns that we cannot perceive, and understands connections that we cannot understand. This is the omniscience of God, and it is both the most humbling and the most liberating truth in all of theology.

It is humbling because it strips away every pretence. We cannot impress God with our piety. We cannot deceive Him with our performances. We cannot hide our sins behind a facade of respectability. He sees through every mask, penetrates every disguise, and exposes every hidden corruption. There is no closet in the house of our souls that is locked against Him. He has the key to every door, and He enters every room, and He sees every skeleton.

But it is also liberating, because it means that we never need to pretend. The exhausting work of maintaining an image, of projecting a false self, of keeping up appearances before God, is rendered entirely unnecessary. He already knows the worst about us, and He has chosen to love us anyway. He has searched us and known us, and He has not turned away in disgust. Instead, He has drawn near in mercy. He has covered our nakedness with the robe of Christ's righteousness. He has cleansed our pollution with the blood of His Son. And He has received us into His family, not because we are worthy, but because Christ is worthy on our behalf.

"Thou hast beset me behind and before, and laid thine hand upon me." God is not merely an observer of our lives. He is an active participant. He goes before us, preparing the way. He follows behind us, guarding our steps. And He lays His hand upon us, with a touch that is at once sovereign and gentle, powerful and tender. The hand of God upon the believer is the hand of a Father who guides, protects, corrects, and comforts. It is the hand that Moses spoke of when he said, "The eternal God is thy refuge, and underneath are the everlasting arms."

"Such knowledge is too wonderful for me; it is high, I cannot attain unto it." David is overwhelmed. The contemplation of God's omniscience has carried him to heights that his finite mind cannot scale. He stands at the edge of infinity and looks out upon a vista that stretches beyond the furthest reach of human comprehension. And his response is not frustration but wonder. He does not resent his inability to comprehend God fully. He marvels at it. He worships because of it. For a God who could be fully comprehended by the human mind would not be God at all.

"Whither shall I go from thy spirit? or whither shall I flee from thy presence? If I ascend up into heaven, thou art there: if I make my bed in hell, behold, thou art there. If I take the wings of the morning, and dwell in the uttermost parts of the sea; even there shall thy hand lead me, and thy right hand shall hold me." There is no place in the entire universe, from the highest heaven to the lowest hell, from the rising of the sun to its setting, where God is not present. And this means that there is no place where the believer is beyond the reach of God's care, and no place where the sinner is beyond the reach of God's judgment.

And so the psalm ends with a prayer that is the natural and necessary conclusion of everything that has gone before. "Search me, O God, and know my heart: try me, and know my thoughts: and see if there be any wicked way in me, and lead me in the way everlasting." Having declared that God already knows him completely, David now invites God to search him further, to probe deeper, to expose whatever remains hidden even from David's own self-knowledge. This is the prayer of a man who would rather be exposed and corrected than comfortable and self-deceived. It is the prayer of a man who values truth above tranquillity, holiness above happiness, and the way everlasting above the way of ease. May it be our prayer today. Amen.
