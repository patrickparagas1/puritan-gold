PURITAN GOLD -- Study Topic: The Book of Sirach, Part 8 of 10
"The Sacrifice That Pleases God"
Sirach Chapters 34–35 — True Prayer, Worship, and Righteous Offering

---

Good evening, and welcome back to Puritan Gold. Tonight we come to one of the most theologically rich passages in the entire Book of Sirach — chapters 34 and 35, where Ben Sira confronts a question that echoes throughout the entire Old Testament: what does God truly desire in worship? The prophets asked this question. The psalmists wrestled with it. And here, Ben Sira gives an answer that anticipates the very heart of the Gospel.

Let us read from Sirach chapter 34, verses 18 through 22, and chapter 35, verses 1 through 10:

"He that bringeth an offering of the goods of the poor doeth as one that killeth the son before his father's eyes. The bread of the needy is their life: he that defraudeth him thereof is a man of blood. He that taketh away his neighbour's living slayeth him; and he that defraudeth the labourer of his hire is a bloodshedder."

"He that keepeth the law bringeth offerings enough: he that taketh heed to the commandment offereth a peace offering. He that requiteth a good turn offereth fine flour; and he that giveth alms sacrificeth praise. To depart from wickedness is a thing pleasing to the Lord; and to forsake unrighteousness is a propitiation. Thou shalt not appear empty before the Lord. For all these things are to be done, because of the commandment. The offering of the righteous maketh the altar fat, and the sweet savour thereof is before the most High. The sacrifice of a just man is acceptable, and the memorial thereof shall never be forgotten. Give the Lord his honour with a good eye, and diminish not the firstfruits of thine hands."

--- Teaching ---

Here is one of the great themes of Scripture, stated with remarkable clarity centuries before Christ: God cares infinitely more about the heart of the worshipper than the size of the offering. External religion without internal righteousness is not merely insufficient — it is offensive to God. This was the thundering message of the prophets, and Ben Sira sounds it here with unmistakable power.

Consider the devastating force of Sirach 34:18: "He that bringeth an offering of the goods of the poor doeth as one that killeth the son before his father's eyes." The imagery is deliberately shocking. The man who exploits the poor all week and then brings a generous offering to the temple on the Sabbath is not worshipping God — he is mocking Him. His offering is blood money. His sacrifice is an abomination. Isaiah heard God say the same thing: "To what purpose is the multitude of your sacrifices unto me? saith the Lord... Bring no more vain oblations; incense is an abomination unto me" (Isaiah 1:11,13). And Amos recorded the Lord's terrible verdict: "I hate, I despise your feast days, and I will not smell in your solemn assemblies" (Amos 5:21).

Why does God reject such worship? Because it is a lie. The man who cheats his neighbor and then sings praise to God is bearing false witness with his whole life. He is saying with his lips that God is holy while saying with his hands that holiness does not matter. The Puritans called this "practical atheism" — the profession of faith contradicted by the conduct of life. William Perkins wrote that "many men are orthodox in their creeds and heretical in their deeds." Ben Sira would agree.

But the sage does not merely condemn. He offers a positive vision of true worship that is breathtaking in its simplicity. "He that keepeth the law bringeth offerings enough." Obedience is worship. "To depart from wickedness is a thing pleasing to the Lord; and to forsake unrighteousness is a propitiation." Repentance is sacrifice. "He that giveth alms sacrificeth praise." Generosity is an offering to God.

This anticipates the teaching of the entire New Testament. Paul writes in Romans 12:1: "I beseech you therefore, brethren, by the mercies of God, that ye present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable unto God, which is your reasonable service." The sacrifice that pleases God is not the blood of bulls and goats but the offering of a surrendered life. Hebrews 13:15-16 echoes the same truth: "By him therefore let us offer the sacrifice of praise to God continually, that is, the fruit of our lips giving thanks to his name. But to do good and to communicate forget not: for with such sacrifices God is well pleased."

Ben Sira also teaches that God hears the cry of the oppressed. Chapter 35:14-20 declares that "the Lord is judge, and with him is no respect of persons." The poor widow, the orphan, the stranger — their prayers ascend to God without delay. "The prayer of the humble pierceth the clouds." This truth runs throughout Scripture. God is the defender of the defenseless. He hears the cry of those whom the world ignores.

The Puritans preached this with tremendous conviction. Richard Baxter argued that "the surest evidence of sincere faith is not the eloquence of our prayers but the justice of our dealings." If a man prays beautifully but cheats his servants, his prayer is an abomination. If a woman attends every church service but shows no mercy to the poor, her religion is vain. James writes with the directness of Ben Sira: "Pure religion and undefiled before God and the Father is this, To visit the fatherless and widows in their affliction, and to keep himself unspotted from the world" (James 1:27).

There is also an important word here about the manner of giving. "Give the Lord his honour with a good eye, and diminish not the firstfruits of thine hands." Give to God first, not last. Give generously, not grudgingly. Give with joy, not resentment. Paul teaches the same: "Every man according as he purposeth in his heart, so let him give; not grudgingly, or of necessity: for God loveth a cheerful giver" (2 Corinthians 9:7).

The sacrifice that pleases God, then, is not spectacular but sincere. It is not the size of the gift but the state of the heart. A widow's two mites given in faith are worth more than a Pharisee's public fortune. A cup of cold water given in Christ's name earns an eternal reward. The altar that God desires is the altar of a humble, obedient, generous heart.

--- Application ---

1. Examine the consistency of your life. Does your worship on Sunday match your conduct on Monday? Are there areas where you profess faith but practice otherwise?

2. Give to God first this week — not from your leftovers but from your firstfruits. This applies to time, money, and energy.

3. Look for someone in need and serve them this week. Visit the sick, feed the hungry, comfort the grieving. This is the sacrifice God desires.

4. Pray Psalm 51:17 each morning: "The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit: a broken and a contrite heart, O God, thou wilt not despise." Ask God to give you a heart of genuine worship.

--- Closing ---

Let us pray. Holy God, who lookest not upon the outward appearance but upon the heart, search us and know us. Expose every hypocrisy, every vain offering, every prayer spoken without faith. Give us clean hands and pure hearts, that our worship may be a sweet savour before Thee. Teach us to love justice, show mercy, and walk humbly with Thee. And accept the sacrifice of our praise — not for our merit, but for the sake of Thy Son, Jesus Christ, who offered Himself once for all upon the cross. Amen.

Go in peace, dear friend. In our next session, we come to one of the most celebrated passages in ancient literature — Sirach chapters 44 through 50, the great hymn to the heroes of faith. Until then, let your life be the sacrifice that pleases God.
