PURITAN GOLD — Episode 6
"Guard Your Heart"
Week 1, Day 6 (Saturday) — Proverbs 4

---

There's an old fortress in the Scottish Highlands — I read about it years ago — that stood for centuries, surviving siege after siege, repelling army after army. Its walls were thick. Its position was commanding. Its defenses were legendary. And yet... it finally fell. Not to a battering ram. Not to a superior army. Not to any force from without.

It fell because someone left the gate open.

A small gate. A side entrance. Unguarded for one night. And that was all it took.

I think about that fortress every time I read Proverbs chapter four, verse twenty-three:

"Keep thy heart with all diligence; for out of it are the issues of life."

Your heart is a fortress, friend. And everything — everything — depends on whether you guard the gate.

This is Puritan Gold. And today, as we come to the end of our first week in Proverbs, we're dwelling in chapter four — particularly the closing section, verses 20 through 27. This is some of the most practical, most urgent, most immediately applicable wisdom in the entire book. And I believe it has never been more needed than it is right now.

Let me read the full passage. Proverbs 4, verses 20 through 27:

"My son, attend to my words; incline thine ear unto my sayings. Let them not depart from thine eyes; keep them in the midst of thine heart. For they are life unto those that find them, and health to all their flesh. Keep thy heart with all diligence; for out of it are the issues of life. Put away from thee a froward mouth, and perverse lips put far from thee. Let thine eyes look right on, and let thine eyelids look straight before thee. Ponder the path of thy feet, and let all thy ways be established. Turn not to the right hand nor to the left: remove thy foot from evil."

Now, I want to take this passage apart slowly, because every verse here is loaded with wisdom for how we live our daily lives.

Let's start where Solomon starts — with attention. "Attend to my words. Incline thine ear. Let them not depart from thine eyes. Keep them in the midst of thine heart."

Before Solomon tells us to guard the heart, he tells us to fill the heart. With what? With God's words. His sayings. His truth. You cannot guard an empty fortress. There must be something inside worth guarding. And the most precious thing you can place inside the fortress of your heart... is the Word of God.

The Puritans understood this with a clarity that we, in our age of distraction, desperately need to recover. They memorized Scripture. Not as an academic exercise — not to impress anyone — but because they understood that the Word of God stored in the heart is the first line of defense against temptation, against despair, against the thousand lies the world whispers to us every day.

When the enemy comes with a lie — "You're not good enough. You'll never change. God has forgotten you" — the heart that is full of Scripture has an answer ready. "The Lord is my shepherd; I shall not want." "He who began a good work in you will carry it on to completion." "I have loved thee with an everlasting love."

The Word, hidden in the heart, is a weapon. And a guard. And a light.

And then comes verse 23 — the command that towers over everything else in this chapter:

"Keep thy heart with all diligence; for out of it are the issues of life."

The word "keep" here is the Hebrew word "natsar," and it means to guard, to watch over, to protect — the way a soldier guards a city or a watchman guards a wall. It implies vigilance. Constant attention. An awareness that the enemy is always looking for an unguarded gate.

And the phrase "with all diligence" — literally, "above all guarding" — reinforces the supreme importance of this command. Of everything you guard in your life — your possessions, your reputation, your time, your relationships — guard your heart above all of them. Because the heart is the source. "Out of it are the issues of life." Everything flows from it. Pollute the source... and you pollute the stream.

Now, what does this look like practically? How do we guard our hearts in the real world — in a world that is constantly bombarding us with things that would corrupt the heart if we let them in?

Let me walk through the rest of the passage, because Solomon gives us a practical anatomy of heart-guarding. He addresses four areas — four gates, if you will — that we must watch carefully.

Gate number one: the mouth. Verse 24: "Put away from thee a froward mouth, and perverse lips put far from thee."

Guard what comes out. Your words are not neutral. They are either building up or tearing down. They are either reflecting the wisdom that's in your heart or revealing the corruption that's in your heart. Jesus said it plainly: "Out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh."

This means that guarding your heart will show up in your speech. The gossip you refuse to participate in. The complaint you swallow instead of voicing. The harsh word you replace with a gentle one. The truth you speak when a lie would be easier. Every word is either a guard at the gate... or a crack in the wall.

Spurgeon, in his Lectures to My Students, spoke at length about the power of the tongue — particularly for those in positions of influence. He warned that a careless word from a minister's mouth could do more damage than a thousand words from a layperson, because of the weight people give to the words of those they respect. But the principle applies to all of us. Your words carry weight. Guard them.

Gate number two: the eyes. Verse 25: "Let thine eyes look right on, and let thine eyelids look straight before thee."

Guard what comes in through your eyes. In Solomon's day, this meant avoiding the seductive glance, the envious gaze, the wandering eye that looks where it shouldn't. In our day... it means all of that and so much more.

We live in an age of unprecedented visual stimulation. Images — moving, still, curated, random — assault our eyes from the moment we wake up until the moment we fall asleep. Our phones. Our screens. Our social media feeds. Our streaming services. An endless river of visual content, much of it designed to bypass our rational minds and speak directly to our desires.

And Solomon says: look straight ahead. Don't let your eyes wander. Be intentional about what you gaze upon, because what you gaze upon shapes what you desire, and what you desire shapes who you become.

This is not about living in fear of images. It's about being strategic. It's about recognizing that your eyes are a gate to your heart, and that gate needs a guard.

Practically, this might mean setting boundaries on your screen time. It might mean being honest about which websites, which apps, which shows are feeding your soul and which are starving it. It might mean putting your phone in another room during your devotional time — not because the phone is evil, but because you know yourself well enough to know that the notification ping will pull your attention away from the Word of God.

Spurgeon lived before the age of smartphones, but he understood the principle perfectly. He counseled his students to be masters of their habits, not slaves to them. To choose deliberately what they would read, what they would observe, what they would allow to occupy the landscape of their minds. Because the mind, like the heart, is a garden — and weeds grow fastest when no one is watching.

Gate number three: the feet. Verse 26: "Ponder the path of thy feet, and let all thy ways be established."

Guard where you go. Your physical presence in a place is a decision — and decisions have consequences. The party you attend. The places you frequent. The company you keep. These are not neutral choices. They are directional. They are either taking you closer to wisdom... or further from it.

"Ponder" is a beautiful word here. It means to weigh carefully. To think before you step. Not to stumble forward impulsively, but to ask: "Where is this path leading? If I keep walking this direction, where will I end up in a year? In five years? In twenty?"

The wise person plays the long game. They don't just ask, "Will this feel good right now?" They ask, "Will this honor God in the end?" And they have the discipline to choose the harder path today... because they know it leads to a better destination tomorrow.

Gate number four: the whole direction of life. Verse 27: "Turn not to the right hand nor to the left: remove thy foot from evil."

Stay the course. Don't deviate. When you know the right path — when God has shown you the way — walk it. Don't be pulled aside by every new idea, every attractive distraction, every shiny alternative that promises a shortcut.

The Christian life is not a sprint; it's a long obedience in the same direction. And the great danger is not usually a dramatic fall into spectacular sin. The great danger is drift. The slow, almost imperceptible turning aside — just a degree or two — that, over time, takes you miles off course.

Drift happens when we stop guarding the gates. When we get comfortable. When we assume that because things are going well, we can relax our vigilance. And before we know it... the fortress has been compromised. Not by a battering ram. By an unguarded gate.

So how do we practically guard our hearts in the daily rhythms of life? Let me give you five habits — drawn from the Puritans, from Spurgeon, and from this passage — that serve as guards at the gate.

First... begin each day with the Word of God. Before you check your email. Before you scroll your phone. Before the world gets its hooks into your mind. Let the first voice you hear be God's voice, speaking through His Word. Fill the fortress before the day's assault begins.

Second... practice the evening examination. The Puritans did this faithfully. At the end of each day, they would review their hearts before God. Where did I sin today? Where did I resist temptation? Where was my heart drawn toward God, and where was it pulled away? This isn't morbid introspection. It's maintenance. It's checking the walls for cracks before they become breaches.

Third... control your inputs. Be ruthlessly selective about what you allow into your mind through your eyes and ears. This doesn't mean living in a bubble. It means being intentional. It means curating what you consume rather than passively absorbing whatever the world serves up.

Fourth... speak life. Use your words to build up, to encourage, to speak truth, to praise God. When you guard the gate of your mouth, you're not just protecting others from your careless words — you're protecting your own heart from the corrosive effects of negativity, cynicism, and complaint.

Fifth... walk with the wise. Proverbs 13:20 tells us, "He that walketh with wise men shall be wise: but a companion of fools shall be destroyed." The people you spend time with will shape the condition of your heart. Choose companions who are themselves guarding their hearts — and who will help you guard yours.

Let me bring this to a close with a word of hope.

If you're listening to this and thinking, "I've already let too much in. The gates have been unguarded for too long. My heart is full of things that shouldn't be there" — hear this: it is not too late.

The same God who commands us to guard our hearts is also the God who can cleanse our hearts. "Create in me a clean heart, O God," David prayed in Psalm 51, "and renew a right spirit within me." That prayer is available to you right now. Today. This very moment.

God does not stand outside the fallen fortress shaking His head in disappointment. He enters in. He clears out the debris. He rebuilds the walls. He posts His own guard — the Holy Spirit — at every gate. And He makes all things new.

So start today. Start now. Guard the gate that you can guard... and trust God with the rest.

Let me pray.

Father, we acknowledge that we have been careless with our hearts. We have left gates unguarded. We have allowed things in that should have been kept out. Forgive us, Lord. Cleanse us. And by the power of Your Spirit, help us to guard our hearts with all diligence from this day forward. Guard what we see. Guard what we say. Guard where we go. And above all, guard who we are becoming. We want to be people whose hearts are fortresses of Your truth, overflowing with life and wisdom and love. Make it so, Lord. In Jesus' mighty name. Amen.

One more day together this week, friend. I'll see you tomorrow.
