PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Guided Puritan Reading, Day 4
"Christ's Affections Toward Sinners"
Thomas Goodwin — The Heart of Christ, Chapter 1

---

Good evening. There is a reason the Puritans read so many books alongside their Bibles. They knew that God raises up teachers in every generation to illuminate His Word. Tonight we sit with one of those teachers and listen carefully.

Tonight we draw from the work of Thomas Goodwin, specifically from "The Heart of Christ in Heaven Towards Sinners on Earth." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.

--- Scripture ---

Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Isaiah 53:3-5:

"He is despised and rejected of men; a man of sorrows, and acquainted with grief: and we hid as it were our faces from him; he was despised, and we esteemed him not. Surely he hath borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows: yet we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted. But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.

--- Guided Reading ---

Having established in our introduction that Christ's heart in heaven is full of tender compassion for His people, Goodwin now turns to explore the nature and ground of those affections. What is it that moves Christ's heart? What stirs His compassion? And how can we know that His sympathy is real and not merely a theological assertion?

Goodwin begins by pointing us to Christ's earthly ministry as the window into His heavenly heart. The Christ who wept at Lazarus's tomb, who looked on the rich young ruler and loved him, who had compassion on the multitudes because they were as sheep without a shepherd — that same Christ now sits at the right hand of God. His circumstances have changed, but His heart has not. Or rather, His heart has been perfected: freed from the limitations of mortal flesh, His compassion now operates with an immediacy and fullness that was not possible during His earthly ministry.

Goodwin makes a remarkable argument: Christ's experience of human suffering during His incarnation permanently shaped His glorified human nature. He does not merely remember what it was like to be tempted, weary, sorrowful, and afflicted. Those experiences are woven into the fabric of His perfected humanity. He carries, as it were, the memory of Gethsemane into the throne room of heaven. This is not to say that Christ continues to suffer — He is in perfect blessedness — but that His experience of suffering has given Him an experiential knowledge of human weakness that informs His present intercession.

Consider what this means practically. When you are tempted, Christ does not look down from heaven with the detached concern of one who has never known temptation. He knows what temptation feels like from the inside. He has felt its pull, its pressure, its deceptive allure. And because He conquered it — not by the raw exercise of divine power, but through the same means available to you (dependence on the Father, the Word of God, prayer) — He is able to succor those who are tempted.

When you are sorrowful, Christ does not dismiss your grief as trivial. He has wept. He has groaned in spirit. He has been troubled in soul. The Man of Sorrows carries a permanent sympathy for all who sorrow. Goodwin writes that Christ's compassion is not like that of a wealthy man who gives alms to a beggar without truly understanding poverty. It is the compassion of one who has been in the depths Himself and knows exactly what the depths feel like.

Goodwin then draws out a distinction that is pastorally transformative. He distinguishes between Christ's affections of compassion and His affections of complacency. His affections of compassion are stirred by our miseries — our sins, our sufferings, our weaknesses. His affections of complacency (delight) are stirred by our graces — our faith, however feeble, our love, however imperfect, our obedience, however stumbling. Both kinds of affection flow from His heart simultaneously. When Christ looks at a struggling believer, He sees both the sin that stirs His pity and the grace that stirs His delight. He does not focus on one to the exclusion of the other.

This is a truth that should shatter the lie that Christ is disgusted by your weakness. Yes, He hates sin. But His hatred of sin does not translate into hatred of the sinner who is struggling against it. Quite the opposite: the very fact that you are struggling — that you grieve over your sin, that you long for holiness, that you cry out for help — is evidence of grace in your heart, and that grace delights Christ even as your weakness moves His compassion.

Goodwin then draws out a further practical application of immense value for the believer's daily walk. He argues that Christ's affections of compassion are most powerfully stirred at the very moments when the believer feels most unworthy of them. It is not the strong, confident, triumphant Christian who draws forth the deepest tenderness from Christ's heart; it is the weak, stumbling, shame-filled one. Goodwin compares this to a mother whose compassion is most aroused not by the child who is flourishing but by the child who is sick. The sicker the child, the more the mother hovers, the more she attends, the more she sacrifices. So it is with Christ and His weakest members.

This truth should revolutionize the way we approach Christ in our worst moments. The instinct of the guilty conscience is to withdraw — to wait until we have cleaned ourselves up before approaching the Savior. But Goodwin insists that this instinct is exactly backward. The moment of our greatest weakness is the moment when Christ's heart is most stirred, most tender, most eager to receive us. We do not need to qualify for His compassion; our very misery qualifies us.

Goodwin concludes this section with an exhortation: do not measure Christ's heart by your own. Your heart is fickle, easily offended, quick to withdraw affection. Christ's heart is constant, infinitely patient, and inexhaustibly compassionate. Come to Him with all your misery, and you will find a heart that is already inclined toward you.

--- Reflection ---

The Puritans kept spiritual journals. Consider writing your answers to these questions.

Question 1: Do I tend to imagine that Christ is disgusted by my repeated failures, or do I trust that His compassion is stirred by my very weakness?

Question 2: How does Goodwin's distinction between Christ's compassion (moved by our misery) and His complacency (delighted by our grace) change the way I approach Him?

Question 3: What specific area of suffering or struggle can I bring to Christ today, trusting that He knows from experience what I am going through?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.

--- Closing Prayer ---

Lord Jesus, Man of Sorrows and now glorified King, I thank You that Your experience of human suffering has given You a heart that perfectly understands my weakness. Help me to believe that You are not disgusted by my struggles but moved with compassion toward me. Let me come to You with all my misery and find in You a sympathizing Savior. Amen.

Go in peace. The God who began a good work in you will bring it to completion.
