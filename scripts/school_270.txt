PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 10
"Of Sanctification and Good Works"
The Westminster Standards — Lesson 10

---

Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we take up the doctrine of sanctification and its fruit in good works — the lifelong process by which God transforms believers into the image of His Son and produces in them the obedience that glorifies His name. Justification changes our standing before God. Sanctification changes our character. Justification is a single act, complete and perfect from the moment of faith. Sanctification is a process, gradual and ongoing, extending from the moment of conversion to the moment of death. Both are essential. Both are the work of God's grace. And both flow from our union with Christ. Let us pray and begin.

O Holy Spirit, Thou art the author of our sanctification. Without Thy power, we cannot kill a single sin. Without Thy grace, we cannot produce a single good work. As we study Thy transforming work today, humble us with the knowledge of our own weakness and encourage us with the assurance of Thy strength. Make us not merely students of sanctification but participants in it — pressing on toward the prize of the high calling of God in Christ Jesus. Amen.

--- Lesson ---

Part One: What Is Sanctification?

The Shorter Catechism, Question 35, asks: "What is sanctification?" and answers: "Sanctification is the work of God's free grace, whereby we are renewed in the whole man after the image of God, and are enabled more and more to die unto sin, and live unto righteousness."

The Larger Catechism, Question 75, provides a fuller definition: "Sanctification is a work of God's free grace, whereby they whom God hath, before the foundation of the world, chosen to be holy, are in time, through the powerful operation of his Spirit applying the death and resurrection of Christ unto them, renewed in their whole man after the image of God; having the seeds of repentance unto life, and all other saving graces, put into their hearts, and those graces so stirred up, increased, and strengthened, as that they more and more die unto sin, and rise unto newness of life."

The Westminster Confession, Chapter 13, Section 1, states: "They who are effectually called and regenerated, having a new heart and a new spirit created in them, are further sanctified, really and personally, through the virtue of Christ's death and resurrection, by his Word and Spirit dwelling in them; the dominion of sin is destroyed, and the several lusts thereof are more and more weakened and mortified, and they more and more quickened and strengthened, in all saving graces, to the practice of true holiness, without which no man shall see the Lord."

Let us examine the key features of this doctrine.

First, sanctification is "the work of God's free grace." Just as we cannot justify ourselves, we cannot sanctify ourselves. The transformation of our character is ultimately the work of God — specifically, the work of the Holy Spirit. The Spirit applies the benefits of Christ's death and resurrection to the believer, creating new desires, new affections, and new patterns of behavior. Sanctification is not a self-improvement program. It is a divine work performed in us by the Spirit of God.

This does not mean that we are passive in sanctification. The Puritans were emphatic on this point. While justification requires nothing of us but the empty hand of faith, sanctification requires our active, daily, strenuous cooperation with the Spirit's work. Paul commands: "Work out your own salvation with fear and trembling" (Philippians 2:12). But in the very next verse he adds: "For it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do of his good pleasure" (Philippians 2:13). God works in us; therefore, we work. God provides the power; we exercise the effort. God plants the desire; we act upon it.

Second, sanctification involves renewal "in the whole man after the image of God." The fall corrupted every part of human nature — mind, will, emotions, body. Sanctification addresses every part as well. Our minds are being renewed to think God's thoughts (Romans 12:2). Our wills are being strengthened to choose God's ways. Our emotions are being purified to love what God loves and hate what God hates. Our bodies are being offered as living sacrifices, holy and acceptable to God (Romans 12:1). No area of life is exempt from the Spirit's transforming work.

Third, sanctification involves two simultaneous movements: dying to sin and living to righteousness. The Puritans, following the language of the Reformers, called these "mortification" and "vivification." Mortification is the putting to death of sinful desires, habits, and patterns of behavior. Vivification is the bringing to life of godly desires, habits, and patterns of behavior. The Christian life is not merely about stopping bad behavior — it is about cultivating good character. As the Apostle Paul writes: "Put off concerning the former conversation the old man, which is corrupt according to the deceitful lusts; And be renewed in the spirit of your mind; And that ye put on the new man, which after God is created in righteousness and true holiness" (Ephesians 4:22-24).

Fourth, sanctification is progressive — "more and more." Unlike justification, which is an instantaneous act, sanctification is a gradual process that unfolds over the entire course of the Christian life. Growth in holiness is not uniform. There will be periods of rapid progress and periods of painful slowness. There will be victories and defeats, advances and retreats. But the overall trajectory of the believer's life is upward — toward greater conformity to the image of Christ.

Part Two: The Means of Sanctification

How does God accomplish this transforming work in the lives of His people? The Westminster Standards identify several means through which the Spirit works to sanctify believers.

The Word of God is the primary instrument of sanctification. Jesus prayed for His disciples: "Sanctify them through thy truth: thy word is truth" (John 17:17). The reading, hearing, studying, memorizing, and meditating upon Scripture are the chief means by which the Spirit renews our minds and transforms our character. The Psalmist declared: "Thy word have I hid in mine heart, that I might not sin against thee" (Psalm 119:11). And again: "Wherewithal shall a young man cleanse his way? by taking heed thereto according to thy word" (Psalm 119:9).

The Puritans were people of the Book. They read Scripture daily — often multiple chapters at a time. They memorized entire books of the Bible. They meditated upon individual verses for hours, turning them over in their minds like a jeweler examining a precious stone. William Gurnall, the Puritan pastor of Lavenham in Suffolk from 1644 to 1679, and author of the massive spiritual warfare manual The Christian in Complete Armour, described the Word of God as the believer's primary weapon against sin. "The Word is the sword of the Spirit," Gurnall wrote, "and the Spirit makes the Word sharp and effectual against our enemies."

Prayer is another essential means of sanctification. Through prayer, believers commune with God, confess their sins, seek forgiveness, ask for strength to resist temptation, and plead for the Spirit's power to grow in holiness. The Shorter Catechism, Question 98, defines prayer as "an offering up of our desires unto God, for things agreeable to his will, in the name of Christ, with confession of our sins, and thankful acknowledgment of his mercies."

The sacraments — baptism and the Lord's Supper — are also means of sanctification. The Lord's Supper, in particular, was designed by Christ to nourish and strengthen the spiritual life of believers. As they receive the bread and wine by faith, they are spiritually fed upon the body and blood of Christ — receiving fresh supplies of grace for their ongoing battle against sin. The Confession, Chapter 29, describes the Lord's Supper as a means by which believers are "spiritually nourished and grow in grace."

The fellowship of the church is yet another means of sanctification. Believers are not meant to grow in holiness in isolation. God has placed them in the body of Christ, surrounded by other believers who encourage, admonish, instruct, and support them. As the writer of Hebrews exhorts: "Let us consider one another to provoke unto love and to good works: Not forsaking the assembling of ourselves together, as the manner of some is; but exhorting one another: and so much the more, as ye see the day approaching" (Hebrews 10:24-25).

And the providences of God — the circumstances and experiences of daily life, including trials, afflictions, and suffering — are also instruments of sanctification. James writes: "My brethren, count it all joy when ye fall into divers temptations; Knowing this, that the trying of your faith worketh patience. But let patience have her perfect work, that ye may be perfect and entire, wanting nothing" (James 1:2-4). God uses the difficulties of life to refine His people, burning away the dross of sin and purifying the gold of faith.

Part Three: Mortification — The Killing of Sin

The Puritans devoted extraordinary attention to the subject of mortification — the daily, deliberate, Spirit-empowered putting to death of sinful desires and habits. No Puritan wrote more profoundly on this subject than John Owen, whose treatise On the Mortification of Sin in Believers, published in 1656, remains one of the most important works on the subject ever written.

Owen derived his title and his theme from Romans 8:13: "For if ye live after the flesh, ye shall die: but if ye through the Spirit do mortify the deeds of the body, ye shall live." Owen argued that mortification is a duty incumbent upon every believer — not merely the spiritually advanced, not merely pastors and theologians, but every person who professes faith in Christ. Sin is a deadly enemy that must be fought daily. To neglect the fight is to court spiritual disaster.

Owen set forth several key principles of mortification. First, mortification is the work of the Holy Spirit. We do not kill sin in our own strength. The Spirit provides the power, the illumination, and the motivation for the fight. "If ye through the Spirit do mortify the deeds of the body..." It is through the Spirit — by His power, under His guidance, with His help — that we put sin to death.

Second, mortification requires the constant application of the blood of Christ to the conscience. When we become aware of sin in our lives, we must not merely resolve to do better. We must confess our sin to God, plead the blood of Christ for forgiveness, and rest in the assurance that "the blood of Jesus Christ his Son cleanseth us from all sin" (1 John 1:7). A guilty conscience, unrelieved by the gospel, will either drive us to despair or harden us in sin. Only the blood of Christ can heal the conscience and provide the freedom and motivation to fight sin with renewed vigor.

Third, mortification requires the identification and targeting of specific sins. Owen warned against vague, general resolutions to "be better." The believer must identify the particular sins that most easily beset him — the specific temptations to which he is most vulnerable, the recurring patterns of failure that characterize his life — and direct his attention and effort against those specific enemies. "Set faith at work on Christ for the killing of thy sin," Owen urged. "His blood is the great sovereign remedy for sin-sick souls."

Fourth, mortification is a lifelong battle. There is no point in the Christian life at which the believer can relax his vigilance and declare victory over sin. The Confession, Chapter 13, Section 2, provides a realistic assessment: "This sanctification is throughout in the whole man, yet imperfect in this life: there abideth still some remnants of corruption in every part; whence ariseth a continual and irreconcilable war, the flesh lusting against the Spirit, and the Spirit against the flesh."

This "continual and irreconcilable war" is the daily experience of every genuine Christian. The old nature — the flesh, the remnants of indwelling sin — resists the Spirit's work. It fights back. It does not go quietly. The Apostle Paul described this inner warfare from his own experience in Romans 7:22-24: "For I delight in the law of God after the inward man: But I see another law in my members, warring against the law of my mind, and bringing me into captivity to the law of sin which is in my members. O wretched man that I am! who shall deliver me from the body of this death?"

But the war, though fierce, is not hopeless. The Confession continues: "In which war, although the remaining corruption for a time may much prevail, yet, through the continual supply of strength from the sanctifying Spirit of Christ, the regenerate part doth overcome: and so the saints grow in grace, perfecting holiness in the fear of God." The Spirit's power is greater than sin's power. Grace is stronger than corruption. The believer who fights in the Spirit's strength will ultimately prevail — not perfectly in this life, but progressively, and finally in glory.

Part Four: The Role of the Law in Sanctification

A question that arose frequently among the Puritans — and that remains important for us today — is the role of God's law in the life of the believer. If we are justified by faith alone, apart from the works of the law, does the law have any further purpose in the Christian life?

The Westminster Confession, Chapter 19, Section 6, addresses this question directly: "Although true believers be not under the law as a covenant of works, to be thereby justified or condemned; yet is it of great use to them, as well as to others; in that, as a rule of life, informing them of the will of God and their duty, it directs and binds them to walk accordingly."

The Puritans identified three uses of the law, following the framework established by John Calvin. The first use of the law is its pedagogical function — it reveals our sin and drives us to Christ for mercy. The second use is its civil function — it restrains wickedness in society and promotes outward order. The third use — and the one most relevant to sanctification — is its normative function: the law serves as a rule of life for believers, showing them what pleases God and guiding them in the path of holiness.

This "third use of the law" was central to the Puritan understanding of sanctification. The Puritans did not view the law as an enemy of grace but as a friend of grace. The law tells us what God requires. Grace gives us the desire and power to obey. The law shows us the path. Grace enables us to walk in it. Without the law, we would not know what holiness looks like. Without grace, we could not achieve it.

Samuel Bolton, who served as Master of Christ's College, Cambridge, from 1645 until his death in 1654 and was a member of the Westminster Assembly, wrote a perceptive book titled The True Bounds of Christian Freedom, published in 1645. In this work, Bolton argued that Christian liberty does not mean freedom from the law but freedom in the law — freedom from the law's curse and condemnation, but not from its guidance and direction. The believer who has been freed from the penalty of the law now delights in the law of the Lord and meditates upon it day and night (Psalm 1:2).

Anthony Burgess, the Puritan pastor of Sutton Coldfield in Warwickshire and another member of the Westminster Assembly, wrote an extensive work titled Vindiciae Legis, or A Vindication of the Moral Law and the Covenants, published in 1647. In this work, Burgess defended the abiding authority of the moral law (the Ten Commandments) as the standard of Christian conduct. Burgess argued that the moral law, unlike the ceremonial and judicial laws of the Old Testament, is based upon the unchanging character of God and is therefore perpetually binding upon all people in all ages.

The Ten Commandments, therefore, are not merely ancient rules for the nation of Israel. They are the eternal moral standard of the Creator, written first upon the heart of Adam in the garden, revealed in fuller form at Mount Sinai, and now applied to the hearts of believers by the Holy Spirit in sanctification. When the Spirit sanctifies a believer, He writes the law of God upon the heart — producing a genuine, heartfelt love for God's commandments and a desire to keep them. As the Psalmist exclaimed: "O how love I thy law! it is my meditation all the day" (Psalm 119:97).

Part Five: Vivification — The Cultivation of Godliness

While mortification addresses the negative dimension of sanctification (the killing of sin), vivification addresses the positive dimension (the cultivation of godliness). The Christian life is not merely about avoiding evil. It is about pursuing good. As Paul writes: "The fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, faith, Meekness, temperance: against such there is no law" (Galatians 5:22-23).

The Puritans understood that every sin has a corresponding virtue, and the killing of the one must be accompanied by the cultivation of the other. Where pride is put to death, humility must be cultivated. Where anger is mortified, patience must be nourished. Where lust is subdued, purity must be pursued. Where covetousness is killed, generosity must be practiced. The Christian life is not a vacuum — it is a garden. When weeds are pulled, flowers must be planted in their place.

The Puritan pastor Richard Baxter, in his monumental work A Christian Directory, first published in 1673, provided one of the most comprehensive guides to practical Christian living ever produced. Baxter addressed virtually every aspect of the believer's life — personal devotion, family worship, business ethics, political responsibilities, educational duties, and social relationships — showing how the grace of God ought to transform every dimension of human existence. Baxter's guiding principle was that holiness is not a narrow, otherworldly pietism but a comprehensive, this-worldly obedience that touches everything from how we pray to how we conduct our business to how we treat our neighbors.

Part Five: Good Works — The Fruit of Sanctification

The Westminster Confession, Chapter 16, addresses the doctrine of good works with characteristic precision. Section 1 states: "Good works are only such as God hath commanded in his holy Word, and not such as, without the warrant thereof, are devised by men out of blind zeal, or upon any pretence of good intention."

This opening statement establishes a crucial principle: the standard for good works is God's Word, not human opinion, tradition, or invention. The Puritans were deeply concerned to distinguish between true good works (defined by Scripture) and false good works (invented by human tradition). The medieval church had developed an elaborate system of "works of supererogation" — meritorious deeds that went beyond what God required, such as pilgrimages, the veneration of relics, the purchase of indulgences, and various monastic disciplines. The Reformers rejected all of these as unbiblical, insisting that the only good works are those that God has commanded in His Word.

Section 2 states: "These good works, done in obedience to God's commandments, are the fruits and evidences of a true and lively faith: and by them believers manifest their thankfulness, strengthen their assurance, edify their brethren, adorn the profession of the gospel, stop the mouths of the adversaries, and glorify God, who is their workmanship, created in Christ Jesus thereunto, that, having their fruit unto holiness, they may have the end, eternal life."

Notice the multiple purposes that good works serve. They are fruits and evidences of true faith — they demonstrate that faith is genuine and alive. They manifest thankfulness — they express gratitude to God for His grace. They strengthen assurance — the believer who sees the fruit of the Spirit in his life can take comfort that the Spirit is indeed at work. They edify brethren — good works build up the body of Christ. They adorn the gospel — they make the Christian faith attractive and credible in the eyes of the watching world. They stop the mouths of adversaries — they silence those who accuse Christians of lawlessness or hypocrisy. And above all, they glorify God — for good works are the products of God's workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works (Ephesians 2:10).

Section 3 addresses the source of good works: "Their ability to do good works is not at all of themselves, but wholly from the Spirit of Christ. And that they may be enabled thereunto, besides the graces they have already received, there is required an actual influence of the same Holy Spirit to work in them to will and to do of his good pleasure; yet are they not hereupon to grow negligent, as if they were not bound to perform any duty unless upon a special motion of the Spirit; but they are to be diligent in stirring up the grace of God that is in them."

This paragraph carefully navigates between two errors. On the one hand, it rejects the idea that believers can produce good works in their own strength — "their ability to do good works is not at all of themselves." On the other hand, it rejects the idea that believers should wait passively for the Spirit to move them before they act — they are "to be diligent in stirring up the grace of God that is in them." The Christian life is neither self-reliance nor passivity. It is grace-empowered effort — active obedience sustained and energized by the Spirit's power.

Part Six: The Imperfection of Good Works

The Confession, Chapter 16, Section 5, states a humbling truth: "We cannot, by our best works, merit pardon of sin, or eternal life, at the hand of God, by reason of the great disproportion that is between them and the glory to come, and the infinite distance that is between us and God, whom by them we can neither profit nor satisfy for the debt of our former sins; but when we have done all we can, we are unprofitable servants; and because, as they are good, they proceed from his Spirit; and as they are wrought by us, they are defiled and mixed with so much weakness and imperfection that they cannot endure the severity of God's judgment."

This is a sobering paragraph. Even our best works — our most sincere prayers, our most generous acts of kindness, our most disciplined acts of obedience — are "defiled and mixed with so much weakness and imperfection" that they cannot stand before God's judgment. Our motives are mixed. Our love is imperfect. Our obedience is partial. Even in our best moments, we fall short of the glory of God.

This is why good works can never be the ground of our acceptance before God. If God were to judge us by our works, even our best works, we would be condemned. It is only through the imputed righteousness of Christ — His perfect obedience credited to our account — that we are accepted. And it is only through God's gracious acceptance of our imperfect works in Christ that our obedience has any value at all.

The Confession, Section 6, explains how God does accept our works: "Yet notwithstanding, the persons of believers being accepted through Christ, their good works also are accepted in him; not as though they were in this life wholly unblameable and unreprovable in God's sight; but that he, looking upon them in his Son, is pleased to accept and reward that which is sincere, although accompanied with many weaknesses and imperfections."

This is a wonderful truth. God accepts our imperfect works — not because they meet His standard of perfection, but because He looks upon them through the lens of Christ's righteousness. A father delights in the crayon drawing his child brings home from school — not because it is a masterpiece, but because it is the work of his beloved child, offered with love and sincerity. So God delights in our imperfect obedience, offered in faith and love, because we are His beloved children in Christ.

Part Seven: Good Works and Their Necessity

The relationship between good works and salvation is one of the most important and most frequently misunderstood topics in Christian theology. The Westminster Standards maintain a careful balance: good works are not the cause of salvation, but they are the necessary consequence of salvation.

The Confession, Chapter 16, Section 2, states that good works "are the fruits and evidences of a true and lively faith." A tree is known by its fruit (Matthew 7:16-20). A living faith produces good works, just as a living tree produces fruit. A faith that produces no good works is not a genuine faith at all — it is, as James declares, dead (James 2:17).

This means that good works have a necessary relationship to salvation, even though they are not the ground of salvation. They are necessary not as a cause but as an effect — not as a price to be paid but as a result to be expected. The Reformers summarized this relationship with a formula that has become classic: "Good works are not necessary to earn salvation, but good works are the necessary fruit of salvation."

The Apostle Paul makes this point emphatically in Ephesians 2:8-10: "For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: Not of works, lest any man should boast. For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works, which God hath before ordained that we should walk in them." We are saved by grace, not by works. But we are saved unto good works — we are God's workmanship, created for the purpose of producing works that glorify Him.

The Westminster Larger Catechism, Question 32, teaches that those who are effectually called "do in this life partake of justification, adoption, sanctification, and the several benefits which in this life do either accompany or flow from them." Good works flow from sanctification as inevitably as water flows from a spring. If there are no good works, there is no sanctification. If there is no sanctification, there is no genuine faith. If there is no genuine faith, there is no salvation. This is why the Confession, quoting Hebrews 12:14, insists on "true holiness, without which no man shall see the Lord."

The great Puritan pastor Thomas Brooks, who ministered in London from the 1650s until his death in 1680, addressed the relationship between faith and works with characteristic clarity in his work Heaven on Earth. Brooks warned against two equal and opposite errors. The first error is legalism — the attempt to earn salvation through good works, which robs Christ of His glory and the believer of his peace. The second error is antinomianism — the claim that because salvation is by grace alone, good works are unnecessary, which dishonors God and reveals a heart that has never been truly changed by grace. The true Christian, Brooks insisted, walks between these two errors: trusting wholly in Christ for acceptance, yet laboring diligently in good works as the fruit of gratitude and the evidence of a changed heart.

Part Eight: Good Works and the Glory of God

The ultimate purpose of good works is not our benefit but God's glory. The Shorter Catechism begins with this principle — "Man's chief end is to glorify God, and to enjoy him for ever" — and the doctrine of good works is the practical outworking of that principle. When believers live holy lives, they display the transforming power of God's grace to a watching world. Their good works become, as it were, a sermon without words — a visible demonstration that the gospel is real, that Christ is alive, and that the Holy Spirit is at work in the hearts of human beings.

Jesus Himself taught this principle in the Sermon on the Mount: "Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven" (Matthew 5:16). Good works are not performed to earn applause from men or merit from God. They are performed so that God may be glorified — so that those who observe the lives of believers may be led to praise the God whose grace produces such transformation.

The Apostle Peter echoes this teaching: "Having your conversation honest among the Gentiles: that, whereas they speak against you as evildoers, they may by your good works, which they shall behold, glorify God in the day of visitation" (1 Peter 2:12). The watching world may mock the Christian faith, ridicule its doctrines, and despise its practitioners. But when the world sees genuine holiness — real love, real integrity, real compassion, real self-sacrifice — it is confronted with evidence that cannot easily be dismissed. Good works are the apologetic of the life.

Part Nine: The Puritan Vision of Holy Living

The Puritans pursued holiness with an intensity that is rarely matched in the history of the church. They were not legalists — they understood that salvation is by grace alone through faith alone. But they were deeply convinced that grace produces holiness, and that the genuineness of faith is demonstrated by the quality of one's life.

Richard Sibbes, the great Cambridge Puritan known as "the heavenly Doctor Sibbes," who served as Master of St. Catharine's Hall from 1626 until his death in 1635, preached a famous series of sermons on Isaiah 42:3, published as The Bruised Reed, in which he emphasized the gentleness of Christ toward weak and struggling believers. Sibbes taught that Christ does not break the bruised reed or quench the smoking flax — He tenderly nurtures the faintest spark of grace until it blazes into a bright flame. This pastoral perspective characterized the best of Puritan teaching on sanctification. The Puritans set a high standard for holiness, but they also offered abundant encouragement to those who were struggling to meet it.

John Bunyan, the Bedford tinker who became one of the most beloved Christian writers of all time, dramatized the process of sanctification in his immortal allegory The Pilgrim's Progress, first published in 1678. Bunyan's pilgrim, Christian, journeys from the City of Destruction to the Celestial City, encountering along the way every temptation, trial, and danger that besets the believer in this life. The journey is long, difficult, and full of setbacks. But the pilgrim presses on, sustained by the promises of God and the companionship of faithful friends. Bunyan's message, drawn from his own experience of spiritual struggle, was that the Christian life is a pilgrimage — not a triumphant march but a daily walk of faith, repentance, and perseverance.

The Puritan Jonathan Edwards, who served as pastor in Northampton, Massachusetts, from 1729 to 1750, wrote one of the most searching and profound works on the nature of true sanctification: A Treatise Concerning Religious Affections, published in 1746. In this work, Edwards argued that the essence of true religion is not mere outward conformity to moral rules but the transformation of the heart's affections. True sanctification, Edwards insisted, produces genuine love for God, genuine hatred of sin, genuine delight in holiness, and genuine longing for heaven. These affections are not the product of human effort but the work of the Holy Spirit, who creates in the regenerate heart new spiritual senses that perceive the beauty of God and the ugliness of sin.

Part Eight: Perseverance — The Guarantee of Final Holiness

The Westminster Confession, Chapter 17, Section 1, provides the doctrine of perseverance: "They whom God hath accepted in his Beloved, effectually called and sanctified by his Spirit, can neither totally nor finally fall away from the state of grace; but shall certainly persevere therein to the end, and be eternally saved."

This is one of the most comforting doctrines in the entire Reformed tradition. True believers cannot lose their salvation. The sanctification that God has begun, He will complete. The holiness He has planted, He will bring to full flower. Not because the believer's grip on God is so strong, but because God's grip on the believer is unbreakable.

The Shorter Catechism, Question 36, confirms this by listing perseverance among the benefits that accompany justification, adoption, and sanctification: "The benefits which in this life do accompany or flow from justification, adoption, and sanctification, are, assurance of God's love, peace of conscience, joy in the Holy Ghost, increase of grace, and perseverance therein to the end."

But the doctrine of perseverance does not encourage carelessness or presumption. The Confession, Chapter 17, Section 3, warns: "Nevertheless they may, through the temptations of Satan and of the world, the prevalency of corruption remaining in them, and the neglect of the means of their preservation, fall into grievous sins; and for a time continue therein: whereby they incur God's displeasure, and grieve his Holy Spirit; come to be deprived of some measure of their graces and comforts; have their hearts hardened, and their consciences wounded; hurt and scandalize others, and bring temporal judgments upon themselves."

David's sin with Bathsheba, Peter's denial of Christ, and the many examples of moral failure among believers throughout church history all illustrate this sober warning. Believers can and do fall into serious sin. But they do not remain there permanently. God, in His fatherly discipline, will convict them, humble them, restore them, and bring them back to the path of holiness. The Confession continues in the very next section by affirming that God will never totally desert His people but will preserve them to the end.

Part Ten: Sanctification and the Means of Grace in Family Life

The Puritans placed enormous emphasis on the family as a context for sanctification. They believed that the home was, in effect, a little church — a place where God was to be worshipped, His Word read and taught, prayer offered, and godliness practiced. William Gouge, the pastor of Blackfriars in London for forty-five years and a prominent member of the Westminster Assembly, wrote a comprehensive guide to family life titled Of Domesticall Duties, published in 1622. Gouge addressed the duties of husbands and wives, parents and children, masters and servants, showing how every relationship within the household was to be governed by Scripture and sanctified by grace.

The practice of family worship — gathering the household each day for the reading of Scripture, prayer, and the singing of psalms — was considered by the Puritans to be one of the most important means of sanctification. The Directory for Family Worship, adopted by the General Assembly of the Church of Scotland in 1647, stated that "the head of each family is to take care that all his family attend the worship of God, and that all within his charge be educated in the knowledge of the Lord." The Puritans believed that holiness was not merely an individual pursuit but a family project — that parents had a sacred obligation to nurture their children in the fear of the Lord and to model before them a life of genuine, heartfelt obedience.

For homeschool families today, this Puritan emphasis on family-based sanctification has particular relevance. The homeschool is not merely a school. It is a household of faith, where learning is integrated with worship, where academic instruction is joined to spiritual formation, and where parents have the opportunity to disciple their children in the context of daily life. The doctrine of sanctification reminds us that every moment of the day — every lesson, every meal, every conversation, every discipline — is an opportunity for God's grace to transform both parent and child into the image of Christ.

Part Eleven: The Goal of Sanctification — Glorification

Sanctification reaches its completion in glorification — the final, complete, and irreversible transformation of the believer into the image of Christ. The Shorter Catechism, Question 37, asks: "What benefits do believers receive from Christ at death?" and answers: "The souls of believers are at their death made perfect in holiness, and do immediately pass into glory."

At death, the war is over. The flesh no longer lusts against the Spirit. Sin is utterly and finally destroyed. The soul is made "perfect in holiness" — completely free from every remnant of corruption, every trace of sin, every imperfection. And at the resurrection, the body too will be glorified — raised in power, incorruptible, and perfectly suited for the eternal enjoyment of God.

This is the hope that sustains the believer through the long and often painful process of sanctification. The fight against sin is hard. The journey is weary. The battle is fierce. But the outcome is certain. God, who began the good work, will bring it to completion (Philippians 1:6). The one who endures to the end will be saved (Matthew 24:13). And the promise stands firm: "Beloved, now are we the sons of God, and it doth not yet appear what we shall be: but we know that, when he shall appear, we shall be like him; for we shall see him as he is" (1 John 3:2).

Let this hope purify your hearts and strengthen your hands. The race is not over. The fight is not finished. But the victory is assured. Press on. Endure. Fight the good fight of faith. And know that the God who called you is faithful, and He will do it (1 Thessalonians 5:24).

--- Closing ---

We have traced the great arc of sanctification — from its beginning in the new birth, through its daily outworking in mortification and vivification, to its glorious completion in the eternal state. Sanctification is the lifelong adventure of becoming who you already are in Christ. It is the daily, sometimes painful, always grace-powered process of dying to sin and living to righteousness. It requires your effort, but it depends on God's power. It involves real struggle, but it promises certain victory. And the God who began this good work in you will carry it on to completion until the day of Christ Jesus. Press on, dear students. Fight the good fight. Run with patience the race that is set before you. And look to Jesus, the author and finisher of your faith. Until next time, students.
