SEGMENT 1: MORNING DEVOTION
----------------------------------------

Welcome, young scholars. Today we gather in the tradition of the great Puritan academies, where every subject was studied under the lordship of Christ. No knowledge is secular when it is received from the hand of God.

Our Father, the Puritans prayed before every lesson, and so do we. We ask for Your blessing on today's studies. Let the truths we learn take root in our hearts and produce fruit in our lives. We study not for our own glory, but for Yours. Amen.

Let us still our hearts with the Word of God before we proceed.

Our Scripture for today is John 6:37-44; Acts 16:14; Ezekiel 36:26-27:

"All that the Father giveth me shall come to me; and him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out. For I came down from heaven, not to do mine own will, but the will of him that sent me. And this is the Father's will which hath sent me, that of all which he hath given me I should lose nothing, but should raise it up again at the last day. And this is the will of him that sent me, that every one which seeth the Son, and believeth on him, may have everlasting life: and I will raise him up at the last day. The Jews then murmured at him, because he said, I am the bread which came down from heaven. And they said, Is not this Jesus, the son of Joseph, whose father and mother we know? how is it then that he saith, I came down from heaven? Jesus therefore answered and said unto them, Murmur not among yourselves. No man can come to me, except the Father which hath sent me draw him: and I will raise him up at the last day. And a certain woman named Lydia, a seller of purple, of the city of Thyatira, which worshipped God, heard us: whose heart the Lord opened, that she attended unto the things which were spoken of Paul. A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you: and I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh, and I will give you an heart of flesh. And I will put my spirit within you, and cause you to walk in my statutes, and ye shall keep my judgments, and do them."

If you are a believer today, reflect on this: there was a time when you were dead in your sins, blind to the beauty of Christ, and utterly unable to save yourself. And yet God, in His sovereign mercy, sent His Spirit into your heart, opened your eyes, changed your will, and drew you irresistibly to the Savior. You did not find God — He found you. You did not choose Christ — He chose you and then gave you the desire and the ability to choose Him. Every breath of faith you have ever taken was a gift of His grace. Let this truth fill you with wonder and worship, and let it give you confidence that the God who began this work in you will bring it to completion.

As we prepare for today's lesson, remember that everything we study flows from this one fountain: the Word of God. Our lesson today is part of The Doctrines of Grace — Westminster Standards, and it is titled: Irresistible Grace — The Effectual Call. Let us proceed with minds engaged and hearts open.

SEGMENT 2: MAIN LESSON PART 1 -- BIBLICAL FOUNDATION
----------------------------------------

Having laid the foundation in prayer and Scripture, let us build upon it.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes in John 6:37-44; Acts 16:14; Ezekiel 36:26-27.

The doctrine of irresistible grace — also known as effectual calling — teaches that when God purposes to save a sinner, He sends His Holy Spirit to work inwardly in that person's heart in such a way that they are infallibly brought to saving faith in Christ. This does not mean that God drags sinners kicking and screaming into the kingdom against their will. Rather, it means that God changes the will itself, so that the sinner who once hated Christ now loves Him, the one who once fled from God now runs to Him, the one who was dead in sin is made alive and freely, willingly, joyfully embraces the Savior.

The foundational text for this doctrine is John 6:37-44, one of the most theologically rich passages in all of Scripture. Jesus begins with a statement of absolute certainty: "All that the Father giveth me shall come to me." This is not a wish or a hope or a possibility. It is a divine declaration. Every person whom the Father has given to the Son in the eternal decree of election will, without exception, come to Christ in faith. Not one will fail. Not one will be lost. The word "shall" carries the full weight of divine omnipotence behind it.

But Jesus immediately adds a word of universal invitation: "and him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out." Here is the beautiful balance of the gospel. On the one hand, only those drawn by the Father will come. On the other hand, everyone who does come is welcome — absolutely, unconditionally, and irrevocably welcome. There is no sinner so vile, no rebel so hardened, no wanderer so far gone that Christ will turn them away if they come. The doctrine of irresistible grace does not narrow the invitation of the gospel; it guarantees that the invitation will be effectually received by all whom God intends to save.

In verse 44, Jesus explains why only some come and others do not: "No man can come to me, except the Father which hath sent me draw him." The word "can" indicates ability, not merely permission. It is not that sinners may not come — it is that they cannot come apart from the Father's drawing. Left to themselves, fallen human beings will never choose Christ because they love their sin, they are blind to spiritual truth, and they are dead in trespasses (as we studied in our lesson on total depravity). The drawing of the Father is the necessary and sufficient cause that overcomes this inability.

The Greek word translated "draw" is helkuo, the same word used in John 21:11 where Peter "drew" the net full of fish to shore, and in Acts 16:19 where Paul and Silas were "drawn" into the marketplace by force. It indicates a powerful, effectual action — not a gentle suggestion that can be easily ignored but a mighty work that accomplishes its purpose. God does not merely invite and then wait passively to see who will respond. He actively, powerfully, effectually draws His elect to Christ.

The story of Lydia in Acts 16:14 provides a beautiful narrative illustration of effectual calling. Luke records that Lydia "heard us: whose heart the Lord opened, that she attended unto the things which were spoken of Paul." Notice the precise language. Lydia did not open her own heart. The Lord opened her heart. And the result of this divine heart-opening was that she attended — she gave careful, believing attention — to Paul's preaching. The external call of the gospel came through Paul's words, but the internal, effectual call came from God Himself, who opened her heart to receive what she heard. This is the consistent pattern of conversion throughout Acts: God opens hearts, God grants repentance, God gives faith.

The prophet Ezekiel provides the Old Testament foundation for this doctrine with one of the most glorious promises in all of Scripture. In Ezekiel 36:26-27, God declares through the prophet: "A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you: and I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh, and I will give you an heart of flesh. And I will put my spirit within you, and cause you to walk in my statutes, and ye shall keep my judgments, and do them." Consider the verbs: "I will give," "I will put," "I will take away," "I will cause." Every verb is in the first person — God is the actor. The sinner is the recipient. God does not say, "I will help you change your heart if you cooperate." He says, "I will change your heart." This is a sovereign, unconditional, effectual promise.

The image of the stony heart and the heart of flesh is profoundly instructive. A stone cannot make itself flesh. No amount of willpower, education, religious ritual, or moral effort can transform a heart of stone into a heart of flesh. Only the creative power of God can accomplish this transformation. And this is precisely what God promises to do for His elect — He will perform spiritual surgery, removing the heart that is hard, cold, and dead, and replacing it with a heart that is soft, warm, and alive. This is regeneration, the new birth, and it is entirely the work of God.

The Westminster Confession of Faith, Chapter 10, describes the effectual call with careful precision. It teaches that "all those whom God hath predestinated unto life, and those only, He is pleased, in His appointed and accepted time, effectually to call, by His Word and Spirit, out of that state of sin and death, in which they are by nature, to grace and salvation, by Jesus Christ; enlightening their minds spiritually and savingly to understand the things of God, taking away their heart of stone, and giving unto them an heart of flesh; renewing their wills, and, by His almighty power, determining them to that which is good, and effectually drawing them to Jesus Christ: yet so, as they come most freely, being made willing by His grace."

That final clause is essential: "they come most freely, being made willing by His grace." Irresistible grace does not violate human freedom — it restores it. Before regeneration, the will is in bondage to sin and cannot choose God. After regeneration, the will is freed from that bondage and gladly, willingly, freely chooses Christ. The grace is irresistible not because it overpowers a reluctant will but because it transforms a rebellious will into a willing one. The convert does not say, "I was forced to believe against my will." The convert says, "I was blind, but now I see. I was dead, but now I live. I was unwilling, but God made me willing — and now I cannot imagine wanting anything more than Christ."

This doctrine is confirmed by the experience of every true believer. Ask any genuine Christian why they came to faith, and they will ultimately point not to themselves but to God. They may mention the influence of a friend, a sermon, a crisis — but behind all secondary causes they recognize the hand of God drawing them, opening their heart, giving them faith. The glory of conversion belongs not to the convert but to the God who converts.

This biblical foundation is essential. Without it, everything else we study today would be like a house built on sand. But with it, we stand on solid rock.

SEGMENT 3: MAIN LESSON PART 2 -- PURITAN TEACHING
----------------------------------------

Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. Thomas Goodwin wrote extensively on this subject in "The Work of the Holy Ghost in Our Salvation." The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life.

Thomas Goodwin (1600-1680) was one of the most brilliant and spiritually-minded of all the Puritan theologians. A member of the Westminster Assembly and later president of Magdalen College, Oxford, Goodwin devoted extensive attention to the work of the Holy Spirit in salvation. His writings on effectual calling are among the richest in all of Puritan literature, combining rigorous theological analysis with deep pastoral warmth and experimental knowledge of the soul.

Goodwin taught that the effectual call is a work of the Holy Spirit that corresponds to and flows from the eternal decree of election. Just as the Father chose a people before the foundation of the world, and the Son died to redeem that specific people, so the Holy Spirit applies that redemption to those same people in time by effectually calling them to faith. The three persons of the Trinity cooperate in a single, harmonious work of salvation — the Father elects, the Son redeems, and the Spirit applies. Goodwin saw this Trinitarian pattern as one of the most beautiful and comforting truths in all of theology.

Goodwin distinguished carefully between the outward call and the inward, effectual call. The outward call comes through the preaching of the gospel and is addressed to all who hear it. Every person who hears the gospel is genuinely commanded to repent and believe. But the outward call alone is insufficient to convert a dead sinner — it provides the information but not the power. The inward, effectual call comes through the secret, immediate work of the Holy Spirit upon the heart. The Spirit takes the truth of the gospel and makes it come alive in the soul. What was once foolishness becomes wisdom. What was once offensive becomes beautiful. What was once rejected is now embraced with joy.

Goodwin used the analogy of creation to explain effectual calling. When God said, "Let there be light," the darkness could not resist. Light came into being by the sheer creative power of God's word. Similarly, when God speaks the effectual call into a dead sinner's heart, spiritual light floods in and the darkness of unbelief cannot resist. Goodwin cited 2 Corinthians 4:6: "For God, who commanded the light to shine out of darkness, hath shined in our hearts, to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ." Just as the first creation was by sovereign fiat, so the new creation — regeneration — is by sovereign fiat. God says "Let there be light" in the soul, and there is light.

Goodwin was deeply concerned to show that effectual grace does not violate human freedom but restores it. He argued that fallen man is not free in any meaningful sense — he is enslaved to sin, blinded by Satan, and imprisoned by his own corrupt nature. The man who says "I am free to choose" while his mind is darkened, his will is in bondage, and his affections are disordered is like a prisoner who boasts of freedom while still in chains. True freedom is the ability to choose what is truly good — and only the regenerate heart, liberated by the Spirit, can do this. Goodwin wrote that the Spirit does not compel the will against its nature but transforms its nature so that it freely and gladly chooses Christ.

Goodwin explored the psychology of conversion with remarkable depth. He described how the Spirit works upon each faculty of the soul. Upon the mind, the Spirit works illumination — opening the understanding to see the truth and beauty and necessity of Christ. Upon the will, the Spirit works inclination — bending the will toward Christ so that it moves toward Him as naturally as water flows downhill. Upon the affections, the Spirit works attraction — kindling love for Christ, desire for holiness, and hatred of sin. The result is a total transformation of the inner man, not merely an intellectual assent to doctrinal propositions but a wholehearted, affectionate, joyful embrace of Christ as Lord and Savior.

Goodwin was also attentive to the variety of ways in which the effectual call is experienced. Some, like Paul on the Damascus road, are converted suddenly and dramatically — struck down by grace in a moment. Others, like Lydia, are converted quietly and gently — their hearts are opened without thunder or earthquake. Goodwin cautioned against making any particular conversion experience normative. The essential thing is not the manner of the call but its reality. Whether sudden or gradual, dramatic or quiet, the effectual call always produces the same fruit: repentance from sin and faith in Christ.

The Westminster Confession's treatment of effectual calling in Chapter 10 reflects Goodwin's influence, as he was an active participant in the Assembly that produced it. The Confession teaches that God effectually calls His elect "by His Word and Spirit," indicating that the two always work together. The Spirit does not work apart from the Word, and the Word does not effectually convert apart from the Spirit. This is why preaching is so important — it is the ordinary means by which the Spirit brings sinners to faith. And this is why preaching alone is insufficient — without the Spirit's power, the most eloquent sermon falls on deaf ears.

Goodwin's doctrine of effectual calling has profound implications for prayer and evangelism. If conversion ultimately depends on the sovereign work of the Spirit, then prayer for the unconverted is not merely a polite religious exercise but a desperate necessity. We must pray for God to open hearts because we know that only He can. And if the Spirit uses the preached Word as His instrument, then evangelism is not optional but urgent. We preach because God has ordained preaching as the means through which He calls His elect. We do not know who the elect are — that is God's secret — but we know that the gospel is the power of God unto salvation, and we cast the seed broadly, trusting God to give the increase.

Goodwin's great comfort to believers was this: if God effectually called you, He will never let you go. The same power that raised you from spiritual death will sustain you through every trial, carry you through every temptation, and bring you safely to glory. The effectual call is not a temporary impulse that might wear off — it is the almighty, irrevocable work of the Holy Spirit, and what God begins He always finishes. As Philippians 1:6 declares, "He which hath begun a good work in you will perform it until the day of Jesus Christ." The God who called you will keep you.

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.

SEGMENT 4: DISCUSSION AND APPLICATION
----------------------------------------

True learning requires reflection. Let us discuss today's key ideas.

Discussion Question 1: In John 6:37, Jesus says 'All that the Father giveth me SHALL come to me.' What does the certainty of this promise tell us about the nature of God's grace?

Discussion Question 2: How does the story of Lydia in Acts 16:14 illustrate the difference between the outward call of the gospel and the inward, effectual call of the Spirit?

Discussion Question 3: Thomas Goodwin used the analogy of God's creation command — 'Let there be light' — to explain effectual calling. In what ways is this analogy helpful?

Discussion Question 4: The Westminster Confession says the elect 'come most freely, being made willing by His grace.' How does irresistible grace restore rather than destroy human freedom?

Discussion Question 5: How should the doctrine of irresistible grace affect our prayers for unbelievers and our approach to evangelism?

Take time with each question. The Puritans valued what they called "holy conference," the practice of discussing spiritual truths together. Do not settle for quick, surface answers. Push deeper. Ask follow-up questions. Listen carefully to one another.

Application is where truth becomes life. It is not enough to know what John 6:37-44; Acts 16:14; Ezekiel 36:26-27 says. We must ask: how does this change the way I live today? How does this shape my thoughts, my words, my actions? The Puritans insisted that a truth that does not transform is a truth that has not been truly understood.

SEGMENT 5: AFTERNOON ACTIVITY
----------------------------------------

The afternoon brings us to our activity time. Let us engage our hands as well as our minds.

Students will study and compare three biblical conversion narratives: Paul (Acts 9:1-19), Lydia (Acts 16:13-15), and the Philippian jailer (Acts 16:25-34). For each account, students will create a detailed analysis chart with the following categories: (1) the person's spiritual condition before conversion, (2) the circumstances through which God worked, (3) evidence of the outward call (human instrumentality), (4) evidence of the inward call (divine initiative), (5) the immediate response, and (6) the fruits of conversion. Students will then write a one-page reflection comparing the three accounts and identifying the common elements of effectual calling present in each, noting how the sovereign work of God adapts to different personalities and circumstances while always producing the same essential result: faith and repentance.

Materials needed: Bibles, printed copies of all three passages, large comparison chart template (poster or paper), colored markers for different categories, lined paper for reflection essay, Westminster Confession Chapter 10 for reference

As you complete this activity, think about how it connects to what you studied in today's lesson. The goal is not just to do something, but to reinforce the truths you have been learning. The Puritans believed that education should engage the whole person: mind, heart, and hands.

Take your time with this activity. Quality matters more than speed. When you are finished, reflect on what you learned through the process of doing. Often we understand something far better after we have tried to put it into practice.

SEGMENT 6: CROSS-SUBJECT CONNECTIONS
----------------------------------------

One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

Thomas Goodwin served during one of the most turbulent periods in English history — the English Civil War and the Cromwellian Protectorate. He was a chaplain to Oliver Cromwell and played a significant role in the Westminster Assembly. Study the relationship between Puritan theology and the political upheaval of seventeenth-century England. How did the Puritan conviction that God is sovereign over all things shape their willingness to challenge the established political and religious order?

Literature Connection:

The concept of an irresistible divine call appears throughout literature, from the biblical prophets to modern fiction. Compare the call narratives of Moses (Exodus 3-4), Jeremiah (Jeremiah 1), and Jonah (Jonah 1-2). Notice how each prophet initially resists but is ultimately overcome by God's purpose. Analyze how narrative structure, dialogue, and dramatic tension are used to convey the power of God's call. Consider how these biblical patterns have influenced conversion narratives in English literature, from Augustine's Confessions to John Newton's autobiography.

Science Connection:

The concept of an 'irresistible' force has intriguing parallels in physics. Gravity, for instance, is a force that acts on all objects with mass and cannot be simply willed away — objects fall not because they choose to but because the force of gravity acts upon them. Electromagnetic forces can attract iron filings to a magnet with a power the filings cannot resist. While these physical analogies are imperfect, they help illustrate how a superior force can overcome resistance without destroying the nature of the object being acted upon. The iron filing remains iron; it is simply drawn to where it could not go on its own.

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.

We have reached the final segment of today's lesson. The Puritans placed great value on review and memorization. They knew that what is not reviewed is soon forgotten. Let us take time now to consolidate what we have learned.

Memory Verse:

Read this verse aloud five times. Then close your eyes and try to recite it from memory. Write it out by hand on a card or in your journal. The Puritans memorized vast portions of Scripture, and it was one of the great sources of their spiritual strength. Even one verse, truly hidden in the heart, can sustain you in times of trial.
