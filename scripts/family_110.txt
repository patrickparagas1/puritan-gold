PURITAN GOLD -- Family Devotional, Day 10
"New Mercies Every Morning"
Matthew Henry — Commentary on Lamentations


Welcome, family. Whether you are gathered on the couch, around the dinner table, or riding in the car, God is here with you. Let us turn our attention to Him and see what treasure He has stored up for us tonight.

Let us bow our heads. Dear Father in heaven, what a privilege it is to gather as a family in Your presence. We need Your wisdom tonight. We need Your guidance. Open our ears to hear, our minds to understand, and our hearts to receive. We pray in the precious name of Jesus. Amen.

Let us turn together to Lamentations 3:22-23. Follow along in your Bible if you have one:

"It is of the LORD's mercies that we are not consumed, because his compassions fail not. They are new every morning: great is thy faithfulness."

That is Lamentations 3:22-23. Let those words settle in your heart for a moment before we continue.


Today we come to one of the most beloved passages in all of Scripture — a passage that has comforted millions of believers through the darkest of times. Lamentations 3:22-23 says, 'It is of the LORD's mercies that we are not consumed, because his compassions fail not. They are new every morning: great is thy faithfulness.'

But to truly appreciate these words, we must understand where they come from. Lamentations is one of the saddest books in the Bible. It was written by the prophet Jeremiah after the destruction of Jerusalem by the Babylonians in 586 BC. The temple — God's house — had been burned to the ground. The city walls were rubble. The people were killed or carried away as captives. Everything was lost.

Jeremiah sat in the ashes of the holy city and wept. The first two chapters of Lamentations are filled with grief so deep it is almost unbearable. In chapter 3, Jeremiah describes his personal suffering: 'He hath led me, and brought me into darkness, but not into light... He hath enclosed my ways with hewn stone, he hath made my paths crooked... He hath filled me with bitterness.'

And then, right in the middle of this ocean of sorrow, Jeremiah says something extraordinary. He says, 'This I recall to my mind, therefore have I hope.' And what does he recall? 'It is of the LORD's mercies that we are not consumed, because his compassions fail not.'

In the worst moment of his life, surrounded by destruction and despair, Jeremiah remembers God's mercy. And not just mercy in general — mercy that is fresh and new every single morning. No matter how dark the night, a new morning comes, and with it, new mercies from God.

The word 'mercies' here is the Hebrew word 'chesed,' one of the richest words in the Old Testament. It means loyal love, steadfast kindness, covenant faithfulness. It is the love that will not let go. It is the kindness that keeps coming even when we do not deserve it. And Jeremiah says it is this chesed — this loyal, stubborn, relentless love of God — that keeps us from being utterly destroyed.

The word 'compassions' is equally tender. It comes from the Hebrew word for womb — 'rechem.' God's compassion is like a mother's love for the child of her womb. It is deep, instinctive, fierce, and unfailing. And Jeremiah says these compassions 'fail not' — they never run out, never expire, never dry up.

Then comes the glorious declaration: 'Great is thy faithfulness.' God is faithful. Not sometimes faithful. Not faithful when things are going well. Faithful always. Faithful in the darkness. Faithful in the ashes. Faithful when everything else has failed.

Matthew Henry commented on this passage with deep feeling. He wrote that when we consider our provocations against God — our sins, our ingratitude, our rebellion — it is a wonder of mercy that we exist at all, let alone that we have blessings. Henry saw God's mercy not as something we deserve but as something that should fill us with amazement every single day.

Henry connected this passage to the daily experience of the Christian family. Each morning, he said, is a fresh gift of God's mercy. The fact that we wake up, that we have breath, that we have food and shelter and one another — all of these are mercies. And each morning brings fresh grace for fresh challenges. Yesterday's mercies were sufficient for yesterday. Today, God provides new mercies for today. Tomorrow, He will provide again.

This is why Henry was such a strong advocate for morning thankfulness. He believed that the first thought of every morning should be gratitude. Before we think about our problems, our to-do list, or our worries, we should pause and say, 'Thank You, Lord, for new mercies this morning. Great is Your faithfulness.'

For our youngest ones: every morning when you wake up, God has new gifts of love waiting for you — like a new box of crayons every single day! For older children: think about a time when something was really hard. Did God help you through it? That was His mercy. And He promises to keep giving it, new every morning. For the whole family: let us make it a habit to begin each day by remembering God's faithfulness, especially when life is difficult.


For centuries, faithful Christians have reflected on passages like this one. Let us hear from a trusted voice of the past.

Matthew Henry, in his work "Commentary on the Whole Bible," wrote these words:

"We are not consumed — this is a wonder of mercy indeed. When we consider our provocations, it is of the Lord's mercies that we have a being, much more that we have a well-being."

What Matthew Henry is reminding us is that the truths we have read tonight in Lamentations 3:22-23 are not new. They have been treasured by faithful Christians for centuries. And they are just as true and just as needed in our family today as they were hundreds of years ago. Let us take these words to heart and let them shape the way we live together as a family.


Discussion time. These questions are for every member of the family, young and old alike.

Question 1: What does it mean that God's mercies are 'new every morning'?

Question 2: Jeremiah was very sad when he wrote this. Why is it amazing that he could still praise God?

Question 3: Can you think of a time when God was faithful to your family even during a hard situation?

Question 4: How does knowing God's mercies are new every morning help us face days that feel scary or overwhelming?

Take a few minutes to talk about these questions together. Let everyone share, and listen to one another with patience and love.


Now, here is our memory verse for today. Let us say it together:

"It is of the LORD's mercies that we are not consumed, because his compassions fail not. They are new every morning: great is thy faithfulness." -- Lamentations 3:22-23

Try to say it out loud three times right now. Then, before bed tonight, see if you can say it from memory. Tomorrow morning, try again. The more we hide God's Word in our hearts, the more it will guide us when we need it most.


The Puritans believed that truth must be practiced, not just heard. Here is something you can do together:

Start a 'New Mercies Jar.' Each morning this week, have every family member write one thing they are thankful for on a slip of paper and drop it in a jar. At the end of the week, read all the slips together and marvel at how many new mercies God gave your family in just one week.


Let us bow our heads and pray. Dear Father in heaven, what a privilege it is to gather as a family around Your Word. We ask You to write these truths on our hearts. Bless our family, protect our home, and lead us in the path of righteousness for Your name's sake. In Christ we pray. Amen.

Until tomorrow, family. May the Lord bless you and keep you.
