Welcome back, students, to another lesson in our Puritan Gold series. Today we take up the seventh commandment — a commandment that addresses the most intimate and powerful of all human desires, and calls every person, whether married or unmarried, to purity of heart and body. This is a commandment that the Puritans expounded with a unique combination of gravity and tenderness, recognizing both the beauty of the gift that God has given and the devastation that results when that gift is misused. "Thou shalt not commit adultery" (Exodus 20:14). Let us examine this commandment together with the care and reverence that it deserves.

PART ONE: THE THEOLOGICAL FOUNDATION — MARRIAGE AS A DIVINE INSTITUTION

The seventh commandment cannot be properly understood apart from the doctrine of marriage that undergirds it. To understand why adultery is forbidden, we must first understand what marriage is and why God established it.

The institution of marriage was established by God Himself at the very beginning of human history, before the Fall, before the entrance of sin into the world, and before any human government, church, or social institution existed. In the second chapter of Genesis, we read the account of its institution: "And the LORD God said, It is not good that the man should be alone; I will make him an help meet for him" (Genesis 2:18). God then formed the woman from a rib taken from the man's side, and brought her to Adam, who received her with delight: "And Adam said, This is now bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh: she shall be called Woman, because she was taken out of Man" (Genesis 2:23). Moses then adds the foundational principle of marriage: "Therefore shall a man leave his father and his mother, and shall cleave unto his wife: and they shall be one flesh" (Genesis 2:24).

Three truths emerge from this account that are essential to understanding the seventh commandment. First, marriage is a divine institution — it was not invented by human beings but ordained by God. It is not a social convention that can be redefined at will; it is a creation ordinance that reflects the purposes and character of the Creator. Second, marriage is a covenant of exclusive, lifelong union between one man and one woman. The language of "leaving" and "cleaving" indicates a permanent bond — a bond that supersedes even the closest family ties. The "one flesh" union that marriage creates is not merely physical; it is a profound spiritual, emotional, and personal union of two persons into a single new entity. Third, marriage was established before the Fall and is therefore part of God's original design for human flourishing. It is not a concession to human weakness or a remedy for sin; it is a positive good, a beautiful gift, a reflection of the very nature of God who exists eternally in the loving communion of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

The apostle Paul, in his letter to the Ephesians, elevates marriage to the highest possible plane by declaring it to be a reflection of the relationship between Christ and His church: "Husbands, love your wives, even as Christ also loved the church, and gave himself for it; That he might sanctify and cleanse it with the washing of water by the word, That he might present it to himself a glorious church, not having spot, or wrinkle, or any such thing; but that it should be holy and without blemish. So ought men to love their wives as their own bodies. He that loveth his wife loveth himself. For no man ever yet hated his own flesh; but nourisheth and cherisheth it, even as the Lord the church: For we are members of his body, of his flesh, and of his bones. For this cause shall a man leave his father and mother, and shall be joined unto his wife, and they two shall be one flesh. This is a great mystery: but I speak concerning Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:25-32).

This passage reveals that marriage is not merely a human relationship — it is a sacred image, a living picture of the covenant love between Christ and His people. When this image is defaced by adultery, it is not merely a relationship that is damaged — it is a sacred picture of divine love that is shattered.

PART TWO: THE PURITAN CELEBRATION OF MARRIAGE

One of the most distinctive features of Puritan theology was its celebration of marriage as a relationship of great beauty, intimacy, and spiritual significance. The Puritans stood in sharp contrast to the medieval Catholic tradition, which had elevated celibacy above marriage and regarded the celibate religious life as inherently more spiritual and meritorious than the married state. The medieval church, influenced by certain strands of ascetic theology traceable to Jerome and other early church fathers, had developed a hierarchy of holiness in which monks, nuns, and priests occupied the highest spiritual plane, while married laypeople were consigned to a lower level of spiritual attainment.

The Reformers — Martin Luther, John Calvin, and their heirs — vigorously rejected this hierarchy. Luther himself married the former nun Katharina von Bora in 1525, and his marriage became a model of Protestant domestic life. Calvin, in his Institutes of the Christian Religion published in its definitive form in 1559, argued that marriage was the divinely appointed state for most human beings and that the medieval exaltation of celibacy was both unscriptural and harmful.

The Puritans carried this conviction further than any previous generation of Protestant thinkers. They wrote about marriage with an enthusiasm and tenderness that was revolutionary for their age. Edmund Morgan, the distinguished historian of Puritanism, observed that the Puritans "were anything but ascetic when it came to marital love." They celebrated the physical, emotional, and spiritual dimensions of the marriage relationship with a frankness and joy that is sometimes surprising to modern readers who associate Puritanism with severity and repression.

Thomas Gataker, a member of the Westminster Assembly who lived from 1574 to 1654, published a treatise entitled "A Good Wife God's Gift" in which he described marriage as "the sweetest and dearest of all human relationships" and argued that a godly spouse is one of the greatest blessings God can bestow upon a person. Gataker's vision of marriage was one of deep companionship, mutual support, and shared devotion to God — a vision that elevated the domestic sphere to the status of a sacred calling.

Richard Baxter, in his "Christian Directory" of 1673, wrote extensively about the duties and joys of married life. Baxter counseled husbands and wives to maintain their love through regular expressions of affection, honest communication, shared prayer and worship, mutual respect, and a willingness to overlook minor faults and irritations. "Let your love be as warm as your anger," Baxter urged, "and do not part with one kind word when you could give twenty."

William Gouge, in his "Of Domestical Duties," published in 1622 after twenty years of pastoral experience at St. Ann Blackfriars in London, provided one of the most comprehensive treatments of marriage in Puritan literature. Gouge discussed everything from the selection of a spouse to the management of household finances, from the duties of husbands to the rights of wives, from the education of children to the treatment of servants. His work reveals the Puritans' conviction that marriage is not merely a private relationship but the foundation of all social order — and that the seventh commandment protects not only the purity of individual marriages but the stability of the entire social fabric.

PART THREE: THE BREADTH OF THE SEVENTH COMMANDMENT — THE WESTMINSTER EXPOSITION

As with every commandment, the Puritans understood the seventh commandment to reach far beyond its surface prohibition. The Westminster Larger Catechism, in Questions 138 and 139, provides a remarkably comprehensive exposition of the duties required and the sins forbidden by the seventh commandment.

The duties required include: "chastity in body, mind, affections, words, and behaviour; and the preservation of it in ourselves and others; watchfulness over the eyes and all the senses; temperance, keeping of chaste company, modesty in apparel; marriage by those that have not the gift of continency, conjugal love, and cohabitation; diligent labour in our callings; shunning all occasions of uncleanness, and resisting temptations thereunto."

The sins forbidden include: "adultery, fornication, rape, incest, sodomy, and all unnatural lusts; all unclean imaginations, thoughts, purposes, and affections; all corrupt or filthy communications, or listening thereunto; wanton looks, impudent or light behaviour, immodest apparel; prohibiting of lawful, and dispensing with unlawful marriages; allowing, tolerating, keeping of stews, and resorting to them; entangling vows of single life, undue delay of marriage; having more wives or husbands than one at the same time; unjust divorce, or desertion; idleness, gluttony, drunkenness, unchaste company; lascivious songs, books, pictures, dancings, stage plays; and all other provocations to, or acts of uncleanness, either in ourselves or others."

The breadth of this list is truly remarkable. The seventh commandment, according to the Westminster divines, addresses not only the physical act of adultery but the entire realm of sexual ethics — thoughts, desires, words, behaviors, associations, clothing, entertainment, and social practices. It encompasses both sins of commission — doing what is forbidden — and sins of omission — failing to do what is required. It addresses both the individual and the community, recognizing that personal purity and social purity are intimately connected.

PART FOUR: JESUS AND THE SEVENTH COMMANDMENT — PURITY OF HEART

Our Lord Jesus Christ, in His Sermon on the Mount, drove the seventh commandment straight into the human heart, just as He did with the sixth: "Ye have heard that it was said by them of old time, Thou shalt not commit adultery: But I say unto you, That whosoever looketh on a woman to lust after her hath committed adultery with her already in his heart" (Matthew 5:27-28).

This is one of the most searching and convicting statements in all of Scripture. Jesus was not introducing a new commandment; He was revealing the true depth and intention of the original commandment. The scribes and Pharisees had reduced the seventh commandment to a prohibition against the physical act of adultery. As long as a man did not actually commit the outward act, he considered himself innocent. But Jesus declared that the commandment reaches beyond the body to the heart. Lust — the deliberate cultivation of impure sexual desire — is adultery of the heart. The sin does not begin with the act; it begins with the thought, the glance, the fantasy, the failure to guard the eyes and the mind against impurity.

John Owen, the great Puritan theologian who served as vice-chancellor of Oxford University and who published his classic treatise "On the Mortification of Sin" in 1656, devoted extensive attention to the nature of lust and the duty of mortifying — putting to death — sinful desires. Owen wrote that "sin always aims at the utmost; every unclean thought or glance would be adultery if it could; every covetous desire would be oppression; every thought of unbelief would be atheism." Owen's point is that sinful desires, if unchecked, will inevitably grow into sinful actions. The man who indulges lustful thoughts is not merely committing a minor offense — he is nurturing a sin that, given the opportunity and the removal of restraint, will produce the full act of adultery. The seventh commandment, therefore, requires us to deal with sin at its root — in the desires and imaginations of the heart — rather than waiting until it blossoms into outward action.

Jesus went even further in His exposition of the seventh commandment, using the most dramatic language imaginable to convey the seriousness of the struggle for purity: "And if thy right eye offend thee, pluck it out, and cast it from thee: for it is profitable for thee that one of thy members should perish, and not that thy whole body should be cast into hell. And if thy right hand offend thee, cut it off, and cast it from thee: for it is profitable for thee that one of thy members should perish, and not that thy whole body should be cast into hell" (Matthew 5:29-30). Jesus was not speaking literally, of course — the problem is not in the eye or the hand but in the heart. But His language conveys the urgency and radical commitment that the pursuit of purity requires. We must be willing to take drastic, costly, even painful measures to guard our hearts against impurity. Nothing that leads us into sexual sin is worth retaining, no matter how pleasant or convenient it may be.

PART FIVE: THE EXAMPLE OF JOSEPH — FLEEING TEMPTATION

The Puritans frequently turned to the story of Joseph in Genesis 39 as the preeminent biblical example of sexual purity in the face of intense temptation. Joseph, the son of the patriarch Jacob, had been sold into slavery by his jealous brothers and transported to Egypt, where he was purchased by Potiphar, an officer of Pharaoh. Despite his circumstances, Joseph served faithfully and was eventually placed in charge of Potiphar's entire household. "And the LORD was with Joseph, and he was a prosperous man; and he was in the house of his master the Egyptian. And his master saw that the LORD was with him, and that the LORD made all that he did to prosper in his hand" (Genesis 39:2-3).

It was at this point that Joseph faced his greatest moral trial. Potiphar's wife began to pursue Joseph with persistent and aggressive sexual advances: "And it came to pass after these things, that his master's wife cast her eyes upon Joseph; and she said, Lie with me" (Genesis 39:7). Joseph's response reveals the three principles that guided his resistance.

First, Joseph understood the duty of faithfulness to his earthly master. He said: "Behold, my master wotteth not what is with me in the house, and he hath committed all that he hath to my hand; There is none greater in this house than I; neither hath he kept back any thing from me but thee, because thou art his wife" (Genesis 39:8-9). Joseph recognized that to commit adultery with Potiphar's wife would be a betrayal of the trust that Potiphar had placed in him. Faithfulness in relationships — the refusal to exploit trust for personal gratification — is a fundamental expression of the seventh commandment.

Second, Joseph understood that sexual sin is ultimately an offense against God. He declared: "How then can I do this great wickedness, and sin against God?" (Genesis 39:9). Notice that Joseph did not say "sin against Potiphar" or "sin against your marriage" — though it was certainly both of those things. He said "sin against God." The seventh commandment is not merely a social convention or a rule of etiquette — it is a divine commandment, given by the holy God who created marriage, who defines sexual morality, and who will judge every violation of His law. The ultimate reason for maintaining purity is not the fear of human consequences but the reverence for God's holiness.

Third, when Potiphar's wife escalated her pursuit, physically grabbing Joseph's garment and demanding that he yield, Joseph did the only wise thing: he ran. "And she caught him by his garment, saying, Lie with me: and he left his garment in her hand, and fled, and got him out" (Genesis 39:12). Joseph did not attempt to reason with her. He did not trust in his own strength to resist. He fled. The Puritans saw in Joseph's flight a crucial principle: when confronted with sexual temptation, the wisest course is not to stand and fight but to run. Paul echoed this principle in his first letter to the Corinthians: "Flee fornication" (1 Corinthians 6:18). And again in his second letter to Timothy: "Flee also youthful lusts: but follow righteousness, faith, charity, peace, with them that call on the Lord out of a pure heart" (2 Timothy 2:22).

PART SIX: PRACTICAL STRATEGIES FOR CULTIVATING PURITY

The Puritans were eminently practical in their approach to the cultivation of sexual purity. They did not merely exhort their readers to be pure; they provided specific, actionable strategies for maintaining purity in a world filled with temptation.

The first strategy was the cultivation of a strong marriage. For those who are married, the best defense against the temptation of adultery is a warm, loving, and attentive marriage. Richard Baxter counseled husbands and wives to invest continually in their relationship — to express affection, to communicate honestly, to worship together, to serve one another, and to keep their love fresh and vital. A marriage that is neglected — where intimacy has faded, communication has broken down, and affection has grown cold — becomes vulnerable to the intrusion of outside temptation. The Puritans urged married couples to guard their marriages with the same vigilance with which a soldier guards a fortress.

Baxter also wrote about the selection of a spouse as a critical decision with lifelong consequences. He counseled young people to seek a spouse who shares their faith, their values, and their commitment to godliness — and to avoid choosing a spouse based primarily on physical attraction, wealth, or social status. "Choose such a spouse," Baxter advised, "as you can love with a pure and holy love, and who will be a helper to you in the way to heaven." A marriage founded on shared devotion to God and mutual respect is a marriage that will be strong enough to withstand the storms of temptation.

The second strategy was the avoidance of temptation. The Puritans counseled believers to guard their eyes, their reading material, their associations, and their leisure activities with great care. They understood that temptation gains power through exposure — the more we expose ourselves to impure images, conversations, and influences, the more difficult it becomes to maintain purity of heart. Job declared: "I made a covenant with mine eyes; why then should I think upon a maid?" (Job 31:1). The Puritans saw in Job's covenant a model for every believer: the deliberate, preemptive decision to guard the senses against impurity before temptation arrives.

This principle has enormous relevance in our own age, when impure images and content are available at the touch of a button. The Puritans would have been deeply alarmed by the pervasiveness of sexual imagery in modern entertainment and media, and they would have counseled believers to exercise extreme caution in their consumption of these materials. The principle of Joseph's flight applies here: when temptation presents itself, the wisest course is to remove ourselves from its presence rather than to test our ability to resist.

The third strategy was accountability. The Puritans lived in close-knit communities where behavior was observed, where church members were accountable to one another, and where sin was addressed with both firmness and compassion. While this system could be taken to oppressive extremes, the underlying principle is sound and important. We are weaker alone than we are together. The writer of Ecclesiastes observed: "Two are better than one; because they have a good reward for their labour. For if they fall, the one will lift up his fellow: but woe to him that is alone when he falleth; for he hath not another to help him up" (Ecclesiastes 4:9-10). Every Christian needs trusted friends who can offer support, counsel, and honest correction — friends to whom we can confess our struggles and from whom we can receive encouragement and accountability.

The fourth strategy was the cultivation of the spiritual life. The Puritans understood that the battle for purity is ultimately a spiritual battle that must be fought with spiritual weapons. Prayer, Scripture reading, meditation on God's Word, regular public worship, participation in the Lord's Supper, and the disciplines of fasting and self-examination were all seen as essential means of grace that strengthen the soul against temptation. Owen wrote that "the vigour, and power, and comfort of our spiritual life depends on our mortification of sin." The believer who neglects the means of grace will find himself increasingly vulnerable to temptation; the believer who cultivates a rich and vibrant spiritual life will find in God the strength to resist.

Paul provided the definitive statement of this principle in his letter to the Galatians: "This I say then, Walk in the Spirit, and ye shall not fulfil the lust of the flesh. For the flesh lusteth against the Spirit, and the Spirit against the flesh: and these are contrary the one to the other: so that ye cannot do the things that ye would" (Galatians 5:16-17). The key to overcoming the lusts of the flesh is not mere willpower — it is the power of the Holy Spirit, working in and through the believer who walks in faith and obedience. Purity is not achieved by human effort alone; it is a fruit of the Spirit, cultivated by grace and nourished by the means of grace that God has appointed.

PART SEVEN: THE DEVASTATING CONSEQUENCES OF SEXUAL SIN

The Puritans also warned extensively about the devastating consequences of sexual sin — consequences that extend far beyond the immediate act to affect every dimension of a person's life.

King David provides the most sobering biblical example. David, the man after God's own heart, the greatest king in Israel's history, fell into adultery with Bathsheba, the wife of Uriah the Hittite, as recorded in 2 Samuel 11. David's initial sin of lust led to adultery, and adultery led to deception, and deception led to murder — for David arranged for Uriah to be placed in the most dangerous position in battle, where he was killed. The prophet Nathan confronted David with his sin: "Thou art the man" (2 Samuel 12:7). And the consequences that Nathan pronounced were devastating: "Now therefore the sword shall never depart from thine house; because thou hast despised me, and hast taken the wife of Uriah the Hittite to be thy wife" (2 Samuel 12:10). The child conceived in adultery died. David's family was torn apart by rebellion, rape, and murder. The consequences of David's sin rippled through generations, affecting not only David himself but his children, his kingdom, and his legacy.

The book of Proverbs is filled with warnings about the consequences of sexual sin. "But whoso committeth adultery with a woman lacketh understanding: he that doeth it destroyeth his own soul. A wound and dishonour shall he get; and his reproach shall not be wiped away" (Proverbs 6:32-33). "For the lips of a strange woman drop as an honeycomb, and her mouth is smoother than oil: But her end is bitter as wormwood, sharp as a twoedged sword. Her feet go down to death; her steps take hold on hell" (Proverbs 5:3-5). "Can a man take fire in his bosom, and his clothes not be burned? Can one go upon hot coals, and his feet not be burned? So he that goeth in to his neighbour's wife; whosoever toucheth her shall not be innocent" (Proverbs 6:27-29).

Thomas Watson summarized these consequences under several headings. Sexual sin destroys trust — the foundational element of every relationship. It destroys families — tearing apart the bonds between husbands and wives, parents and children. It destroys health — both physical and mental, bringing disease, guilt, and shame. It destroys reputation — the good name that is "rather to be chosen than great riches" (Proverbs 22:1). It destroys peace of conscience — replacing the joy of a clean heart with the torment of guilty secrets. And above all, it destroys one's relationship with God — for sexual sin is an offense against the God who created marriage, who dwells in the believer as in a temple, and who demands holiness in every dimension of life.

PART EIGHT: THE SEVENTH COMMANDMENT AND THE GOSPEL

Yet the Puritans, for all their solemnity in warning against sexual sin, were equally earnest in proclaiming the gospel of grace to those who had fallen. The God who commands purity is also the God who forgives impurity. The God who demands holiness is also the God who provides holiness through the blood of His Son.

David himself, after his terrible sin with Bathsheba, experienced the mercy of God through genuine repentance. His prayer of confession in Psalm 51 is one of the most powerful expressions of penitence in all of Scripture: "Have mercy upon me, O God, according to thy lovingkindness: according unto the multitude of thy tender mercies blot out my transgressions. Wash me throughly from mine iniquity, and cleanse me from my sin. For I acknowledge my transgressions: and my sin is ever before me. Against thee, thee only, have I sinned, and done this evil in thy sight" (Psalm 51:1-4). David did not minimize his sin or make excuses for it. He acknowledged it fully and cast himself upon the mercy of God. And God, in His grace, forgave David — though the temporal consequences of his sin remained.

Jesus Himself demonstrated the grace of the seventh commandment in His encounter with the woman taken in adultery, recorded in John 8. The scribes and Pharisees brought a woman caught in the very act of adultery and demanded that Jesus pronounce judgment upon her. Jesus' response was both devastating and merciful: "He that is without sin among you, let him first cast a stone at her" (John 8:7). One by one, her accusers departed, convicted by their own consciences. When only Jesus and the woman remained, He said to her: "Woman, where are those thine accusers? hath no man condemned thee? She said, No man, Lord. And Jesus said unto her, Neither do I condemn thee: go, and sin no more" (John 8:10-11).

Notice the perfect balance of grace and truth in Jesus' words. He did not condone her sin — "go, and sin no more." But neither did He condemn her — "neither do I condemn thee." This is the gospel applied to the seventh commandment. God takes sexual sin with absolute seriousness — it is never trivial, never harmless, never without consequence. But God also offers free and full forgiveness to every sinner who comes to Him in genuine repentance and faith. The blood of Jesus Christ "cleanseth us from all sin" (1 John 1:7) — including the sins of the seventh commandment.

PART NINE: THE SEVENTH COMMANDMENT AND THE BROADER MORAL LAW

The Puritans recognized that the seventh commandment is deeply connected to the other commandments of the Decalogue. Adultery often involves the violation of multiple commandments simultaneously. It violates the first commandment, because it places the gratification of desire above obedience to God. It violates the fifth commandment, because it dishonors the family structure that God has ordained. It violates the sixth commandment, because it can destroy lives — the life of the betrayed spouse, the lives of children, and sometimes even the physical life of one of the parties involved. It violates the eighth commandment, because it takes what belongs to another — a spouse's exclusive right to marital intimacy. It violates the ninth commandment, because it almost always involves deception, secrecy, and lies. And it violates the tenth commandment, because it begins with the coveting of what belongs to one's neighbor.

This interconnection of the commandments reinforces the Puritan teaching that the moral law is a unified whole — that the violation of one commandment tends to produce the violation of others, and that the faithful keeping of one commandment supports the keeping of all the rest. James wrote: "For whosoever shall keep the whole law, and yet offend in one point, he is guilty of all" (James 2:10). The commandments are not isolated rules; they are expressions of a single, coherent moral vision — the vision of a life wholly devoted to God and wholly committed to the welfare of one's neighbor.

PART TEN: THE SEVENTH COMMANDMENT IN PURITAN SOCIAL LIFE

The Puritans' commitment to the seventh commandment shaped their entire social order in ways that are often misunderstood by modern observers. Far from being the joyless, repressive prudes that popular caricature suggests, the Puritans were deeply committed to the health and happiness of marriage — and their social structures reflected this commitment.

In Puritan New England, the laws of the Massachusetts Bay Colony provided strong protections for the marriage covenant. Adultery was a serious criminal offense, punishable by public disgrace and, in theory, by death — though the death penalty was rarely if ever carried out for this offense. The severity of the legal framework, however, reveals how seriously the Puritans took the seventh commandment. They understood that adultery was not a private matter between two individuals but a public offense against the entire community, because it struck at the institution upon which the family, the church, and the commonwealth depended.

At the same time, the Puritans were remarkably supportive of healthy marital relationships. They encouraged married couples to maintain their affection and intimacy. They provided pastoral counsel to couples experiencing difficulties. They held husbands accountable for treating their wives with kindness and respect, and they held wives accountable for maintaining a spirit of love and cooperation within the household. The Puritan court records contain numerous cases in which husbands were disciplined for neglecting, abusing, or abandoning their wives — evidence that the Puritan community took seriously the obligation of husbands to love their wives as Christ loved the church.

Samuel Willard, who served as vice-president of Harvard College and as pastor of the Old South Church in Boston from 1678 to 1707, preached a series of sermons on the Westminster Shorter Catechism that included an extensive treatment of the seventh commandment. Willard emphasized that the commandment requires not only the avoidance of sexual sin but the positive cultivation of marital love and faithfulness. He urged husbands and wives to view their marriage as a spiritual calling — a vocation appointed by God, requiring the same diligence, devotion, and self-sacrifice that any other calling demands. Willard's teaching reflects the Puritan conviction that marriage is not merely a legal contract or a social convenience but a sacred trust, established by God and sustained by His grace.

The Puritans also addressed the issue of remarriage after the death of a spouse with characteristic pastoral wisdom. They recognized that widows and widowers often faced genuine hardship — emotional, financial, and practical — and they encouraged remarriage as a legitimate and honorable response to the loss of a spouse. Paul had counseled the younger widows to remarry: "I will therefore that the younger women marry, bear children, guide the house, give none occasion to the adversary to speak reproachfully" (1 Timothy 5:14). The Puritans applied this counsel with sensitivity and compassion, helping bereaved individuals find suitable partners and rebuild their lives within the framework of the seventh commandment.

PART ELEVEN: THE SEVENTH COMMANDMENT AND THE BOOK OF PROVERBS

The book of Proverbs contains some of the most extensive and vivid teaching on sexual purity in all of Scripture, and the Puritans drew upon these passages frequently in their exposition of the seventh commandment. The opening chapters of Proverbs, in particular, contain repeated warnings from a father to his son about the dangers of sexual temptation and the blessings of marital faithfulness.

In Proverbs 5, the father warns his son against the seductive speech of the adulterous woman: "For the lips of a strange woman drop as an honeycomb, and her mouth is smoother than oil: But her end is bitter as wormwood, sharp as a twoedged sword. Her feet go down to death; her steps take hold on hell" (Proverbs 5:3-5). The imagery is vivid and powerful: the initial sweetness of temptation gives way to bitterness, pain, and ultimately death. The father then contrasts the destructive path of adultery with the joyful path of marital faithfulness: "Drink waters out of thine own cistern, and running waters out of thine own well. Let thy fountains be dispersed abroad, and rivers of waters in the streets. Let them be only thine own, and not strangers' with thee. Let thy fountain be blessed: and rejoice with the wife of thy youth" (Proverbs 5:15-18).

The Puritans loved this passage because it presented sexual purity not as a grim duty but as the path to genuine joy. The faithful husband who "rejoices with the wife of his youth" experiences a depth of intimacy, trust, and satisfaction that is impossible in any illicit relationship. Marital love, the Puritans taught, grows richer and deeper over time — unlike the fleeting pleasures of sin, which grow stale and bitter. Matthew Henry, in his commentary on this passage, observed that "the pleasures of lawful love far exceed the pleasures of stolen love, because they are enjoyed with a good conscience, without fear, and with the blessing of God upon them."

Proverbs 7 provides one of the most dramatic narrative warnings in all of Scripture — the story of a young man seduced by an adulterous woman. The father describes the scene from his window: "And, behold, there met him a woman with the attire of an harlot, and subtil of heart" (Proverbs 7:10). She entices the young man with flattery, promises of pleasure, and assurances that they will not be caught. "With her much fair speech she caused him to yield, with the flattering of her lips she forced him. He goeth after her straightway, as an ox goeth to the slaughter, or as a fool to the correction of the stocks" (Proverbs 7:21-22). The father's conclusion is devastating: "Her house is the way to hell, going down to the chambers of death" (Proverbs 7:27).

Charles Bridges, the evangelical commentator deeply influenced by the Puritan tradition, wrote of this passage that "the young man's path to destruction began long before the encounter itself — it began with his decision to walk near the place of temptation." This observation captures the Puritan emphasis on avoidance: the time to resist temptation is not when it is at its strongest but long before it arrives, by avoiding the circumstances, the associations, and the environments in which temptation thrives.

PART TWELVE: THE SEVENTH COMMANDMENT AND THE SINGLE LIFE

While the Puritans celebrated marriage, they also recognized that not every person is called to marry, and they addressed the duties of the seventh commandment as they apply to unmarried persons. Paul wrote to the Corinthians: "I say therefore to the unmarried and widows, It is good for them if they abide even as I. But if they cannot contain, let them marry: for it is better to marry than to burn" (1 Corinthians 7:8-9). Paul acknowledged that some individuals possess the gift of celibacy — the ability to live a chaste and contented single life — and he commended this gift as valuable for the service of God. But he also recognized that most people do not possess this gift and that marriage is God's provision for the channeling of sexual desire within holy bounds.

The Puritans counseled unmarried persons to maintain the highest standards of purity in their thoughts, words, and behavior, knowing that the habits of heart they develop before marriage will shape the character they bring into marriage. A young person who cultivates purity in singleness is preparing himself or herself for faithfulness in marriage. A young person who indulges in impurity before marriage is developing patterns that will make faithfulness in marriage far more difficult.

The Puritans also urged parents to facilitate the marriages of their children at an appropriate age, rather than delaying marriage unnecessarily. They recognized that prolonged singleness in the face of strong sexual desire is a situation fraught with moral danger, and they saw the timely arrangement of godly marriages as an act of parental wisdom and love. This is not to say that they encouraged hasty or ill-considered marriages — Baxter and others wrote at length about the importance of choosing a spouse wisely, with careful attention to spiritual compatibility, moral character, and mutual affection. But they also warned against the opposite extreme of delaying marriage indefinitely while young people struggle with temptations they are not equipped to resist.

PART THIRTEEN: APPLICATION FOR OUR TIME

The seventh commandment speaks with particular urgency to our own age. We live in a culture that has largely abandoned the biblical understanding of marriage and sexual morality. The sacred boundaries that God established for the protection of the marriage covenant and the channeling of human desire are widely ignored, ridiculed, and even actively opposed.

In the face of this cultural decline, the Puritan vision of purity is not a relic of the past — it is a beacon for the future. The Puritans understood that sexual desire is a powerful force that can bring tremendous blessing when channeled according to God's design or tremendous destruction when unleashed apart from His boundaries. They celebrated the beauty and joy of marital love while maintaining an uncompromising standard of purity in all other relationships. They provided practical strategies for cultivating purity — strategies that are as relevant today as they were four centuries ago.

As students, you are at a stage of life where these issues are becoming increasingly real and personally relevant. The pressures you face may be different in their specific form from those the Puritans faced, but they are not different in their essential nature. The temptation to impurity is as old as the human race. The strategies for resisting it are timeless: flee temptation, cultivate accountability, guard your heart and mind, invest in your spiritual life, and above all, walk in the power of the Holy Spirit, who alone can produce in you the purity that God demands and that your own heart truly desires.

--- Closing ---

Purity is not merely the absence of sin — it is the presence of a heart wholly devoted to God, who alone can satisfy the deepest longings of the human soul. The seventh commandment is not God's attempt to deny us joy — it is His design to protect the deepest and most sacred joys of human life: the joy of a faithful marriage, the joy of a clean conscience, and the joy of unbroken fellowship with the holy God who made us for Himself. May God grant us pure hearts, steadfast wills, and the indwelling power of His Spirit to walk in holiness all the days of our lives. Until next time, students.
