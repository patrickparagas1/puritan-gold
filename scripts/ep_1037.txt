PURITAN GOLD -- Melchizedek: A Complete Biblical Study, Part 7 of 8
Old Testament Types and Shadows — The Priest-King Theme

---

Welcome back to Puritan Gold. For the past six episodes, we have worked our way through every major passage directly concerning Melchizedek — Genesis 14, Psalm 110, and the extended argument of Hebrews 5 through 7. Now we take a broader view. In this episode, we examine the Old Testament types and shadows that surround the Melchizedek theme — the priest-king motif that runs like a golden thread through the Hebrew Scriptures. We shall read passages from Genesis, Joshua, the Psalms, and Zechariah, and show how they all converge upon the one great truth: that God intended from the beginning to unite the offices of king and priest in a single Person.

Let us begin by returning to the scene of Melchizedek's ministry. He is called king of Salem. Where is Salem? Psalm 76:2 tells us:

"In Salem also is his tabernacle, and his dwelling place in Zion."

Salem is Jerusalem. The ancient city where Melchizedek reigned as priest-king is the same city where David would later establish his throne, where Solomon would build the temple, and where Christ would be crucified and rise again. This is no coincidence. The Puritan writers saw in this a deliberate, providential connection. John Gill writes that God ordained Salem to be the place of Melchizedek's priesthood so that it might become, in the fullness of time, the place of Christ's sacrifice. The city of the priest-king would become the city of the cross.

The very name Jerusalem appears to be a compound — Jeru-Salem, the foundation or possession of peace. It is the city of peace, the city of the King of Peace. When Abraham met Melchizedek at the valley of Shaveh, near Salem, he was standing on ground that would one day be soaked with the blood of the Lamb of God. Matthew Henry observes that every major event in redemptive history circles back to this city — the binding of Isaac on Mount Moriah, which is the temple mount; the threshing floor of Araunah where David offered sacrifice to stay the plague; the temple of Solomon; and the cross of Calvary. Salem, the city of Melchizedek's peace, is the city of Christ's atonement.

Now let us consider the priest-king theme more broadly. Under the Mosaic covenant, the offices of king and priest were strictly separated. The tribe of Judah provided the kings. The tribe of Levi provided the priests. And when the boundary was crossed, judgment followed swiftly.

Consider the account of King Saul in 1 Samuel 13:8-14. Let us read it:

"And he tarried seven days, according to the set time that Samuel had appointed: but Samuel came not to Gilgal; and the people were scattered from him. And Saul said, Bring hither a burnt offering to me, and peace offerings. And he offered the burnt offering. And it came to pass, that as soon as he had made an end of offering the burnt offering, behold, Samuel came; and Saul went out to meet him, that he might salute him. And Samuel said, What hast thou done? And Saul said, Because I saw that the people were scattered from me, and that thou camest not within the days appointed, and that the Philistines gathered themselves together at Michmash; therefore said I, The Philistines will come down now upon me to Gilgal, and I have not made supplication unto the LORD: I forced myself therefore, and offered a burnt offering. And Samuel said to Saul, Thou hast done foolishly: thou hast not kept the commandment of the LORD thy God, which he commanded thee: for now would the LORD have established thy kingdom upon Israel for ever. But now thy kingdom shall not continue: the LORD hath sought him a man after his own heart, and the LORD hath commanded him to be captain over his people, because thou hast not kept that which the LORD commanded thee."

Saul was a king who dared to act as a priest, and his kingdom was torn from him. The separation of offices was inviolable under the old covenant. No mere man could hold both.

Even more dramatically, consider King Uzziah in 2 Chronicles 26:16-21:

"But when he was strong, his heart was lifted up to his destruction: for he transgressed against the LORD his God, and went into the temple of the LORD to burn incense upon the altar of incense. And Azariah the priest went in after him, and with him fourscore priests of the LORD, that were valiant men: and they withstood Uzziah the king, and said unto him, It appertaineth not unto thee, Uzziah, to burn incense unto the LORD, but to the priests the sons of Aaron, that are consecrated to burn incense: go out of the sanctuary; for thou hast trespassed; neither shall it be for thine honour from the LORD God. Then Uzziah was wroth, and had a censer in his hand to burn incense: and while he was wroth with the priests, the leprosy even rose up in his forehead before the priests in the house of the LORD, from beside the incense altar. And Azariah the chief priest, and all the priests, looked upon him, and, behold, he was leprous in his forehead, and they thrust him out from thence; yea, himself hasted also to go out, because the LORD had smitten him. And king Uzziah was a leper unto the day of his death, and dwelt in a several house, being a leper; for he was cut off from the house of the LORD."

Uzziah was struck with leprosy for presuming to exercise priestly functions. The lesson is unmistakable: under the old covenant, no earthly king could serve as priest. The offices were separated by divine decree, and any attempt to unite them in a fallen human being was met with severe judgment.

And yet — and this is the wonder of it — there existed from the very beginning an order of priesthood in which kingship and priesthood were united. Melchizedek was both king and priest. And Psalm 110 promised that the Messiah would be both king (sit at my right hand) and priest (after the order of Melchizedek). The separation of offices under the law was temporary, pointing to the day when they would be perfectly united in Christ.

Thomas Goodwin writes that the separation of king and priest in Israel was itself a type — a type of the inadequacy of any merely human arrangement to bring salvation. No king could atone for sin. No priest could rule with sovereign authority. The two functions that humanity most desperately needed — righteous governance and atoning mediation — could never be combined in a single fallen man. Only the God-man, the sinless Son of God, could hold both offices together.

Now let us turn to one of the most remarkable prophecies concerning this union of offices. Zechariah 6:12-13:

"And speak unto him, saying, Thus speaketh the LORD of hosts, saying, Behold the man whose name is The BRANCH; and he shall grow up out of his place, and he shall build the temple of the LORD: even he shall build the temple of the LORD; and he shall bear the glory, and shall sit and rule upon his throne; and he shall be a priest upon his throne: and the counsel of peace shall be between them both."

This is extraordinary. The Branch — a well-known Messianic title — shall sit and rule upon His throne and shall be a priest upon His throne. The two offices, king and priest, are united in one person upon one throne. And the counsel of peace shall be between them both — between the kingly office and the priestly office, there shall be perfect harmony, perfect counsel, perfect peace.

John Owen sees in this verse the fullest Old Testament expression of the Melchizedekian principle. Melchizedek was King of Righteousness and King of Peace. The Branch shall be king and priest upon His throne, with the counsel of peace between the two offices. This is exactly what Christ accomplishes. As King, He rules with sovereign authority. As Priest, He makes atonement and intercedes. And between these two offices there is no tension, no conflict, but perfect, harmonious counsel — the counsel of peace.

Owen further writes that this is why the gospel is called the gospel of peace. Christ's work as priest secures peace with God through the blood of the cross. Christ's work as king bestows the peace of God through the rule of His Spirit. The two offices work together — righteousness and peace kiss each other, as Psalm 85:10 declares.

Let us also consider the bread and wine that Melchizedek brought forth. In Genesis 14:18, we read simply: "And Melchizedek king of Salem brought forth bread and wine: and he was the priest of the most high God." The Puritans saw in this a rich typological foreshadowing. Bread and wine are the elements of the Lord's Supper, instituted by Christ the night before His crucifixion. Just as Melchizedek refreshed Abraham with bread and wine after the battle, so Christ refreshes His church with His own body and blood, represented in the bread and cup, after the spiritual battles of this life.

Matthew Henry writes that the bread and wine also point to the priestly provision of Christ. A true priest does not merely offer sacrifice; he also sustains and nourishes the people of God. Christ's priesthood is not limited to the cross. It extends to the table. He feeds His people with Himself. He is the bread of life. He is the true vine. In the Lord's Supper, the church receives from her Melchizedekian Priest the sustenance she needs for the journey through the wilderness of this world.

Finally, consider Joshua the high priest in Zechariah 3:1-5:

"And he shewed me Joshua the high priest standing before the angel of the LORD, and Satan standing at his right hand to resist him. And the LORD said unto Satan, The LORD rebuke thee, O Satan; even the LORD that hath chosen Jerusalem rebuke thee: is not this a brand plucked out of the fire? Now Joshua was clothed with filthy garments, and stood before the angel. And he answered and spake unto those that stood before him, saying, Take away the filthy garments from him. And unto him he said, Behold, I have caused thine iniquity to pass from thee, and I will clothe thee with change of raiment. And I said, Let them set a fair mitre upon his head. So they set a fair mitre upon his head, and clothed him with garments, and the angel of the LORD stood by."

This high priest Joshua, standing in filthy garments, is a picture of the sinful people whom the priest represents. Satan accuses. But the LORD rebukes the accuser, removes the filthy garments, and clothes the priest in clean raiment. This is what Christ our Melchizedekian Priest does for us. He removes the filth of our sin and clothes us in His own righteousness. He silences the accuser. He stands before the Father on our behalf, not in filthy garments but in the perfect garments of His own holiness.

Beloved, the types and shadows of the Old Testament all converge upon this single point: God intended from the beginning to unite kingship and priesthood in one glorious Person. Melchizedek was the initial revelation. Psalm 110 was the prophetic declaration. Zechariah's Branch was the detailed prediction. And Jesus Christ is the eternal fulfillment.

In our final episode, we shall examine how the great Puritan theologians — John Owen, Thomas Goodwin, Matthew Henry, and others — drew these threads together in their expositions and applied the Melchizedek doctrine to the life of the church. Amen.
