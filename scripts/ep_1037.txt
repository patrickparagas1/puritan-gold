PURITAN GOLD -- Study Topic: The Book of Melchizedek, Part 7 of 8
"Melchizedek in Puritan Theology"
The Book of Melchizedek — How Owen, Goodwin, and others interpreted the type

---

Dear friends, we have spent the last several episodes working through the biblical texts on Melchizedek — Genesis 14, Psalm 110, and Hebrews 7. Today we step back from verse-by-verse exposition and ask a different but equally important question: How did the great Puritan theologians understand and apply the Melchizedek typology? What did the princes of Reformed theology see in this mysterious figure, and what can we learn from their reflections?

Let us read from Hebrews 7:3 once more, as our anchor text:

"Without father, without mother, without descent, having neither beginning of days, nor end of life; but made like unto the Son of God; abideth a priest continually."

--- Teaching ---

The Puritans were, above all, exegetes and theologians of the Word. They brought to the Scriptures a combination of scholarly rigor and pastoral warmth that has rarely been equalled. They read the Bible not merely as a historical document or a theological textbook, but as a living word addressed to the soul — a word that illuminates the mind, warms the heart, and transforms the life. Their treatment of the Melchizedek typology beautifully illustrates this approach.

John Owen, the greatest of the Puritan theologians, devoted extensive attention to Melchizedek in his massive commentary on the Epistle to the Hebrews — a work that remains, nearly four centuries later, the most thorough treatment of that epistle ever produced. Owen approached the Melchizedek passages with two primary concerns: first, to establish the precise nature of the typological relationship between Melchizedek and Christ; and second, to draw out the pastoral implications of Christ's Melchizedekian priesthood for the comfort and assurance of believers.

On the first point, Owen was clear and careful. Melchizedek was a real historical person — not an angel, not a theophany, not a mythological figure. He was a man who lived in the land of Canaan in the days of Abraham, who held the offices of king and priest, and who worshipped the one true God in an age of rampant idolatry. But he was more than a man — he was a divinely appointed type of Christ, and every detail of his recorded history was shaped by the Holy Spirit to foreshadow the person and work of the Messiah.

Owen distinguished between what he called the "personal" and the "typical" in Melchizedek. Personally, Melchizedek was a man with a father and mother, a man who was born and who died, a man who lived in a particular time and place. Typically — that is, in his role as a type — he was presented in Scripture without these details, so that his literary portrait might mirror the eternal reality of Christ. The silence of Scripture about his genealogy, birth, and death was not a statement about his actual nature but about his typological function.

This distinction is crucial and it safeguards the integrity of the biblical text. The Holy Spirit did not falsify the record by claiming that Melchizedek was literally without father or mother. Rather, He shaped the narrative presentation of Melchizedek so that the type would faithfully represent the antitype. Owen compared it to a portrait painter who omits certain features in order to emphasize the most important aspects of his subject. The omission does not make the portrait dishonest; it makes it purposeful.

Thomas Goodwin brought a different but complementary emphasis to the Melchizedek typology. Where Owen excelled in exegetical precision, Goodwin excelled in devotional application. In his treatise on the knowledge of God the Father and His Son Jesus Christ, Goodwin reflected at length on what it means for believers to have a High Priest "after the order of Melchizedek."

Goodwin emphasized the perpetuity of Christ's priesthood. The Levitical priests served and died, served and died, generation after generation. But Christ, like Melchizedek in the typological presentation, "abideth a priest continually." For Goodwin, this meant that the intercession of Christ is as constant as His existence. There is never a moment when Christ is not interceding for His people. There is never a gap in the coverage, never a lapse in the advocacy, never a break in the priestly ministry. The believer is perpetually represented before the Father by a priest who never sleeps, never fails, and never ceases.

Goodwin also drew comfort from the dual office of king and priest. A priest who is also a king has the authority to enforce what he secures through intercession. Christ does not merely ask the Father to bless His people; He has the royal authority to bestow those blessings Himself. He does not merely plead for their protection; He has the kingly power to protect them. The combination of priestly compassion and royal power makes Christ the most effective mediator conceivable. He feels our infirmities as a priest and conquers our enemies as a king.

Stephen Charnock, in his great work on the attributes of God, touched upon Melchizedek in the context of divine immutability. Charnock argued that the unchangeableness of Christ's priesthood is a reflection of the unchangeableness of God's nature. God does not change His mind, revise His purposes, or revoke His oaths. The oath of Psalm 110:4 — "Thou art a priest for ever" — stands as firm today as when it was first uttered. It will stand as firm ten thousand years hence. And because the oath does not change, the priesthood does not change, and the salvation secured by that priesthood does not change.

Charnock used this truth to address the fears of believers who worried that their persistent struggles with sin might cause God to abandon them. He argued that if salvation depended upon the steadfastness of human faith, every believer would perish. But salvation depends upon the steadfastness of Christ's priesthood — and that priesthood is as unchangeable as the God who established it. The believer's security is grounded not in the believer's grip upon Christ but in Christ's grip upon the believer.

William Gouge, the Puritan pastor of Blackfriars in London, preached a series of sermons on Hebrews that devoted considerable attention to the Melchizedek typology. Gouge emphasized the practical implications of Christ's priesthood for daily Christian living. If Christ is our priest, then we have perpetual access to God through Him. We need not wait for special seasons, special places, or special mediators. At any moment, in any place, under any circumstance, we may approach the throne of grace through our great High Priest and find mercy and grace to help in time of need (Hebrews 4:16).

The Puritans also found in Melchizedek a model for the relationship between the civil and ecclesiastical orders. Melchizedek was both king and priest — ruling his city with justice and serving his God with devotion. While the Puritans did not advocate for the literal union of church and state, they did believe that the civil magistrate had a duty to uphold and protect the worship of God. The figure of Melchizedek reminded them that righteousness and peace — the two qualities embedded in his very name — are the twin foundations of a well-ordered society.

What united all the Puritan reflections on Melchizedek was a passionate christocentrism. They did not study Melchizedek for antiquarian interest. They studied him because he pointed to Christ. Every detail of the type was a lens through which they beheld the glory of the antitype. The Puritans read the Old Testament the way the apostles read it — as a book about Jesus.

--- Application ---

1. Follow the Puritans in reading the Old Testament christologically. Every type, every shadow, every prophecy finds its fulfillment in Christ. Train your eyes to see Him on every page.

2. Let the unchangeableness of Christ's priesthood strengthen your assurance of salvation. Your security rests not upon your faithfulness but upon His. He is the same yesterday, today, and forever.

3. Combine scholarly rigor with devotional warmth, as the Puritans did. Study the Scriptures carefully, but never merely academically. Let every truth you learn lead you to worship, to prayer, and to practical holiness.

4. Value the combination of kingly authority and priestly compassion in Christ. He is not a distant king who does not care, nor a compassionate friend who lacks the power to help. He is both — and He exercises both offices on your behalf every moment of every day.

--- Closing ---

O Lord, we give Thee thanks for the great cloud of witnesses who have gone before us — for Owen, Goodwin, Charnock, and all the faithful ministers who labored to understand and proclaim the riches of Thy Word. Give us hearts like theirs — hearts that burn with love for Christ, minds that seek to understand His glory, and lives that reflect His righteousness and peace. Through Jesus Christ, our King and Priest forever. Amen.

May the wisdom of the ages and the freshness of the Spirit illuminate your study of God's Word, beloved. Grace and peace until next time.
