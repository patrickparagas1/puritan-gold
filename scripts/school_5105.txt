PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 5
"Creation, the Fall, and the Covenant of Grace"
Westminster Standards — Lesson 5

---

Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we trace the great narrative arc of Scripture as presented in the Westminster Standards — from the glory of creation through the tragedy of the fall to the hope of the covenant of grace. Let us pray and begin.

Creator God, You made all things good, and when we ruined them through sin, You provided a way of rescue. Help us to understand this story — our story — with clarity and gratitude. In Christ's name, Amen.

--- Lesson ---

The Westminster Standards tell a story — the greatest story ever told. It is the story of how God created a perfect world, how humanity ruined it through sin, and how God set in motion a plan of redemption that would ultimately restore all things. Chapters 4 through 7 of the Confession lay out this story with precision and power.

Chapter 4 addresses creation. The Confession affirms that "it pleased God the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost, for the manifestation of the glory of his eternal power, wisdom, and goodness, in the beginning, to create, or make of nothing, the world, and all things therein, whether visible or invisible, in the space of six days, and all very good." Several truths stand out. Creation was a Trinitarian act — Father, Son, and Spirit all participated. Creation was purposeful — it was done for the manifestation of God's glory. Creation was ex nihilo — out of nothing. And creation was good — "all very good."

The crown of creation was the making of man. The Shorter Catechism, Question 10, asks, "How did God create man?" and answers: "God created man male and female, after his own image, in knowledge, righteousness, and holiness, with dominion over the creatures." The image of God in humanity consists not merely in intelligence or creativity but in moral qualities: knowledge, righteousness, and holiness. Humanity was made to reflect God's character and to rule over creation as His representatives.

Chapter 6 addresses the fall. God placed Adam and Eve in the Garden of Eden under a covenant of works: obey, and you will live; disobey, and you will die. This arrangement is sometimes called the "covenant of works" or the "covenant of life." The Shorter Catechism, Question 12, explains: "When God had created man, he entered into a covenant of life with him, upon condition of perfect obedience; forbidding him to eat of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, upon the pain of death."

Adam and Eve, tempted by Satan, chose to disobey. They ate the forbidden fruit. And the consequences were catastrophic. The Confession states that by this sin, "they fell from their original righteousness, and communion with God, and so became dead in sin, and wholly defiled in all the faculties and parts of soul and body." The fall affected every part of human nature — mind, will, emotions, body. This is what theologians call "total depravity." It does not mean that every person is as bad as they could possibly be, but that every part of every person is affected by sin. There is no faculty of the human soul that has been left untouched.

Furthermore, because Adam was the representative head of the entire human race, his sin was imputed to all his descendants. The Shorter Catechism, Question 16, states: "The covenant being made with Adam, not only for himself, but for his posterity; all mankind, descending from him by ordinary generation, sinned in him, and fell with him, in his first transgression." This is the doctrine of original sin. We are born with a sinful nature — not because of our own personal choices, but because we are united to Adam as our federal head. Every human being (except Christ) enters the world already guilty and already corrupted.

But the story does not end in despair. Chapter 7 introduces the covenant of grace — God's plan of redemption. After the fall, "the Lord was pleased to make a second [covenant], commonly called the covenant of grace: wherein he freely offereth unto sinners life and salvation by Jesus Christ, requiring of them faith in him, that they may be saved." This covenant is the golden thread that runs through the entire Bible, from Genesis 3:15 (the first promise of a Savior) to Revelation 22 (the consummation of all things).

The covenant of grace reveals the heart of God. Humanity did not deserve a second chance. We had been given a perfect world, a perfect relationship with God, and a simple command — and we broke it all. God would have been perfectly just to leave us in our sin forever. But He chose, out of His free love and mercy, to provide a way of salvation. And that way is Christ.

The Shorter Catechism, Question 20, asks, "Did God leave all mankind to perish in the estate of sin and misery?" and answers, "God, having out of his mere good pleasure, from all eternity, elected some to everlasting life, did enter into a covenant of grace to deliver them out of the estate of sin and misery, and to bring them into an estate of salvation by a Redeemer." The initiative is entirely God's. The motive is entirely grace. And the means is entirely Christ.

This is the great story: creation, fall, redemption. It is the story the Westminster Standards tell, and it is the story that gives meaning to your life.

--- Review Questions ---

Review Question 1: What does the Westminster Confession teach about the creation of humanity? What is the image of God?

Review Question 2: What was the covenant of works, and what happened when Adam broke it? What is the doctrine of original sin?

Review Question 3: What is the covenant of grace? How does it differ from the covenant of works?

--- Activity ---

Create a three-panel visual presentation of the grand narrative: Creation, Fall, and Redemption. For each panel, include key Scripture references, quotations from the Westminster Standards, and your own illustrations or descriptions. At the bottom of each panel, write one sentence explaining how this part of the story affects your life personally.

--- Closing ---

The story of creation, fall, and grace is not merely ancient history. It is your story. You were created in God's image, fallen in Adam, and offered salvation in Christ. Let this truth shape your understanding of who you are and why you are here. Until next time, students.
