PURITAN GOLD -- Melchizedek: A Complete Biblical Study, Part 2 of 8
Psalm 110 — The Messianic Prophecy

---

Welcome back, beloved friends, to our complete biblical study of Melchizedek on Puritan Gold. In our first episode, we read the full account of Genesis 14, where Melchizedek appeared suddenly to Abraham after the battle of the kings. Now we leap forward roughly a thousand years in redemptive history, from the patriarchal age to the royal age, from the plains of Canaan to the throne room of David.

Psalm 110 is the most quoted psalm in the entire New Testament. Jesus Himself appealed to it to confound the Pharisees. Peter preached it on the day of Pentecost. The writer of Hebrews built his entire argument about Christ's priesthood upon it. And at the heart of this psalm stands one extraordinary verse — the only mention of Melchizedek between Genesis 14 and the book of Hebrews. A thousand years of silence, and then this single, thundering declaration.

Let us read the entire psalm — all seven verses. Psalm 110:

"The LORD said unto my Lord, Sit thou at my right hand, until I make thine enemies thy footstool. The LORD shall send the rod of thy strength out of Zion: rule thou in the midst of thine enemies. Thy people shall be willing in the day of thy power, in the beauties of holiness from the womb of the morning: thou hast the dew of thy youth. The LORD hath sworn, and will not repent, Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek. The LORD shall strike through kings in the day of his wrath. He shall judge among the heathen, he shall fill the places with the dead bodies; he shall wound the heads over many countries. He shall drink of the brook in the way: therefore shall he lift up the head."

--- Teaching ---

Now let us give this psalm the thorough exposition it deserves, verse by verse, as the Puritan fathers would have us do.

Verse 1: "The LORD said unto my Lord, Sit thou at my right hand, until I make thine enemies thy footstool."

This is the verse Jesus used in Matthew 22 to silence the Pharisees. He asked them: if the Messiah is merely David's son, how does David, speaking by the Spirit, call Him Lord? The answer is that the Messiah is both David's son according to the flesh and David's Lord according to His divine nature. Two persons are in view — Jehovah the Father, designated by the sacred name LORD in capital letters, and the Messiah, designated as my Lord. The Father speaks to the Son: Sit thou at my right hand. This is the session of Christ — His enthronement at the place of supreme honor and authority. John Owen writes that this sitting down signifies the completion of Christ's atoning work. The Levitical priests never sat down in the tabernacle, for their work was never finished. But Christ, having offered one sacrifice for sins forever, sat down at the right hand of God. The Father further promises: I will make thine enemies thy footstool. This is the language of total conquest. In the ancient world, a victorious king would place his foot upon the neck of the conquered. Every enemy of Christ — sin, death, Satan, and all opposition — shall be brought into absolute subjection.

Verse 2: "The LORD shall send the rod of thy strength out of Zion: rule thou in the midst of thine enemies."

The rod of strength is the scepter of kingly authority. It goes out from Zion — from the church, from the place of God's dwelling. Matthew Henry observes that this rod is the gospel itself. Christ rules not by military conquest but by the power of His Word and Spirit. And notice — He rules in the midst of His enemies. Christ's kingdom advances not after all opposition has ceased but while opposition rages. The church triumphs not in the absence of conflict but in the very teeth of it. Thomas Goodwin found in this verse a great comfort for the church in times of persecution: Christ's rule is never more evident than when His enemies surround His people, and yet His purposes advance without hindrance.

Verse 3: "Thy people shall be willing in the day of thy power, in the beauties of holiness from the womb of the morning: thou hast the dew of thy youth."

This is one of the most precious verses in the Psalter for Reformed theology. Thy people shall be willing in the day of thy power. The Puritans saw here the doctrine of effectual calling. When Christ exercises His power in the hearts of His elect, they come willingly. It is not forced compliance but joyful surrender. John Owen writes that the Spirit of God so transforms the will that what was once resistant becomes eagerly willing. The stony heart becomes a heart of flesh. The rebel becomes a volunteer. They come in the beauties of holiness — adorned not in worldly finery but in the righteousness of Christ. And they come as the dew — innumerable, fresh, glistening, silent, appearing as if from nowhere. As the dew of the morning covers the fields without anyone hearing it fall, so Christ's people emerge in every generation — quietly, mysteriously, abundantly.

Verse 4: "The LORD hath sworn, and will not repent, Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek."

Here it is. The verse that changes everything. After a thousand years of silence, the name of Melchizedek thunders forth again — and this time, it is accompanied by the oath of God.

Consider the weight of what is said here. God swears. The Almighty, who cannot lie, who needs no oath because His bare word is sufficient, nevertheless condescends to swear. And He will not repent — He will not change His mind, He will not revoke, He will not alter. This oath is irreversible and eternal. What has He sworn? That the Messiah — the Lord whom David worships — is a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek.

The implications are staggering, and the writer of Hebrews will spend three full chapters unpacking them. But let us note them here as the Puritans did.

First, the Messiah is a priest. This was remarkable in the Old Testament context. The kings of Israel came from the tribe of Judah. The priests came from the tribe of Levi. These offices were kept strictly separate. When King Uzziah tried to burn incense in the temple — a priestly act — God struck him with leprosy. Yet here, David's Lord is declared to be both king, as told to sit at God's right hand and rule, and priest. How can this be? Only if His priesthood is of a different order altogether — not of Levi, but of Melchizedek.

Second, this priesthood is forever. The Levitical priests died and were replaced. Generation after generation, the sons of Aaron took up the censer and offered the blood. But this priest serves forever. His priesthood has no end because His life has no end. John Owen expounds at great length that the word forever here means not merely a long time but without termination. Christ's priestly intercession never ceases. He ever lives to make intercession for His people.

Third, this priesthood is established by an oath. The Levitical priesthood was established by the law of a carnal commandment — by genealogical descent and Mosaic regulation. But this priesthood is established by the direct, irrevocable oath of God Himself. The writer of Hebrews will argue that this makes Christ the surety of a better covenant. What is secured by God's oath cannot fail.

Thomas Goodwin writes that Psalm 110:4 is one of the hinges upon which the entire gospel turns. Without a priest, there can be no atonement. Without a permanent priest, there can be no permanent assurance. But God has sworn — with an oath — that His Son is a priest forever. Therefore the atonement is sufficient, the intercession is perpetual, and the salvation of every believer is eternally secure.

Verses 5 through 7: "The Lord at thy right hand shall strike through kings in the day of his wrath. He shall judge among the heathen, he shall fill the places with the dead bodies; he shall wound the heads over many countries. He shall drink of the brook in the way: therefore shall he lift up the head."

The psalm concludes with a vision of the Messiah's final triumph. He who sits as priest will also arise as warrior-judge. He shall strike through kings. He shall judge among the nations. He shall wound the head — a clear echo of Genesis 3:15, where the seed of the woman shall crush the serpent's head. The imagery is of total, decisive victory over every earthly and spiritual power that opposes the kingdom of God.

And then that final, mysterious verse: He shall drink of the brook in the way; therefore shall he lift up the head. Matthew Henry interprets this as Christ's humiliation preceding His exaltation. He drank of the brook of suffering in His earthly sojourn — the brook of Gethsemane, the brook of Calvary — and therefore God has highly exalted Him and given Him a name above every name. The Puritan William Gouge notes that this is the perpetual pattern of the gospel: humiliation precedes glory, the cross before the crown, suffering before reigning.

Beloved, Psalm 110 is the bridge between Genesis 14 and the book of Hebrews. It takes the historical figure of Melchizedek and transforms him into a prophetic category — an order of priesthood. And it does so with the weight of God's own oath behind it. When we turn next to Hebrews chapter 5, we shall see how the apostolic writer takes this psalm and builds from it the most extensive theological argument in the New Testament about the priesthood of Jesus Christ.

Until then, rest in this: the LORD has sworn, and will not repent. Your High Priest serves forever. Amen.
