PURITAN GOLD -- Together: Couples Devotional, Day 27
"Receiving One Another"
Romans for Marriage — Day 27


Good evening, dear couple. The Puritans believed that marriage was one of the greatest means of grace God provides. Tonight we enter into that grace together. Let us open our hearts and our ears to what the Lord has for us.

As we settle in together, let us hear from Romans 14:1-4:

"Him that is weak in the faith receive ye, but not to doubtful disputations. For one believeth that he may eat all things: another, who is weak, eateth herbs. Let not him that eateth despise him that eateth not; and let not him which eateth not judge him that eateth: for God hath received him. Who art thou that judgest another man's servant? to his own master he standeth or falleth. Yea, he shall be holden up: for God is able to make him stand."

That is Romans 14:1-4. Take a breath. Let those words settle between the two of you. God is speaking to your marriage through this passage.


One of the gifts of the Christian tradition is that we do not study alone. We have the wisdom of the saints who came before us. Tonight we hear from Richard Baxter.

In Romans 14, Paul addresses a practical problem in the Roman church: disagreements about secondary matters. Some believed they could eat anything; others ate only vegetables. Some observed certain days as sacred; others treated every day alike. These were genuine differences of conscience — and they were causing division.

Richard Baxter, who spent much of his ministry promoting Christian unity amid disagreement, saw direct application to marriage. In every marriage, there are areas of genuine difference — matters of preference, conviction, personality, and temperament. One spouse may be stricter about certain practices; the other may be more relaxed. One may be more cautious; the other more adventurous. One may need more structure; the other more spontaneity.

Paul's command is clear: receive one another. Do not despise your spouse for their differences. Do not judge them for their convictions. They are God's servant, not yours. They answer to their Master, not to you. And God is able to make them stand.

Baxter counseled couples to distinguish between matters of biblical principle and matters of personal preference. On biblical principles — faithfulness, honesty, love, purity — there is no room for compromise. But on matters of preference — how to spend a Saturday, how to organize the house, what traditions to observe — there must be generous room for difference.

The great Puritan peacemaker observed that more marriages are damaged by petty disputes over preferences than by serious disagreements over principles. Couples who could survive a crisis sometimes cannot survive a disagreement about how to load the dishwasher. The remedy is Romans 14: receive one another. Accept your spouse's differences as part of God's design. He made you different on purpose — to complement, not to compete.

'God is able to make him stand.' This promise applies to your spouse. Where you see weakness, God sees a servant He is holding up. Where you see immaturity, God sees a work in progress He is perfecting. Trust God with your spouse. He is a better judge and a more patient teacher than you are.


Identify the areas of difference in your marriage that cause the most friction. Are they truly matters of biblical principle, or are they matters of personal preference?

If they are matters of preference, practice receiving one another tonight. Choose to accept your spouse's way without criticism. Choose to celebrate the difference rather than resent it. Choose to trust that God is at work in them, even when their approach is different from yours.

Say to each other: 'I receive you — your convictions, your preferences, your personality — as God's gift to me. I will not despise what is different. I will not judge what I do not understand. I will trust God to make you stand.'

The Puritans understood that marriage is not a destination but a journey. Every day presents new opportunities to love, to serve, to forgive, and to grow together. The truths we have studied tonight are not just for tonight. They are for tomorrow morning when the alarm goes off, for the difficult conversation that has been waiting, for the quiet moments when only God sees how you treat each other.


Discussion time. Husband, listen to your wife. Wife, listen to your husband. Let this be a safe and sacred space.

Question 1: What differences between you cause the most conflict? Are they truly matters of principle or matters of preference?

Question 2: How do you respond when your spouse's convictions or preferences differ from yours — with acceptance or with judgment?

Question 3: What would change in your marriage if you truly 'received' each other without trying to change each other?

Take a few minutes with each question. Do not rush. The best conversations in marriage happen when both people feel truly heard.


Let us close our eyes and open our hearts to the Lord in prayer. Husband, pray for your wife. Wife, pray for your husband. There is no greater gift you can give each other.

Tonight's prayer focus: Pray for the grace to receive each other fully — differences and all. Ask God to help you distinguish between biblical principles and personal preferences, and to hold your preferences loosely while holding your spouse tightly.

If one of you finds it difficult to pray out loud, that is perfectly fine. Simply holding hands in silence before the Lord is itself a powerful act of worship. Let the Spirit intercede where words fail.


May you receive each other with the same grace that God has received you. May your differences become strengths, and may your unity be built not on uniformity but on love.

Go in peace, husband and wife. The God who ordained your marriage is faithful to sustain it. Trust Him. Love each other. And come back tomorrow for more. Goodnight.
