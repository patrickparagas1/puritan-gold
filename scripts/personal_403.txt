PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Doctrine Deep-Dive, Day 3
"The Doctrine of Total Depravity"
Sin Explored Through the Puritans


Good evening. The Puritans were theologians of the highest order, but they were also the most practical of people. They knew that theology untethered from life is useless, and that life untethered from theology is dangerous. Tonight, we hold the two together.

Tonight we draw from the work of Multiple (Owen, Edwards, Watson), specifically from "Various works on the doctrine of sin." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Romans 3:10-12:

"As it is written, There is none righteous, no, not one: There is none that understandeth, there is none that seeketh after God. They are all gone out of the way, they are together become unprofitable; there is none that doeth good, no, not one."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


The doctrine of total depravity is one of the most misunderstood and yet most important doctrines in the Christian faith. It does not mean that every person is as evil as they could possibly be. It means that every part of every person has been affected by the fall: the mind, the will, the affections, the body, the conscience. There is no faculty of the human soul that has escaped the corruption of sin. This is what the Reformed tradition means by 'total' depravity: sin's reach is comprehensive, touching the totality of human nature.

John Owen addresses this doctrine with characteristic depth in his work 'The Nature, Power, Deceit, and Prevalency of Indwelling Sin in Believers.' Owen argues that even after regeneration, the believer retains a principle of indwelling sin that affects every dimension of the soul. This remaining corruption, while no longer dominant (the believer is a 'new creature'), is still present and active. Owen writes that indwelling sin is like a deposed king who still has loyalists in the land: it has lost its throne but not its influence.

Owen identifies several characteristics of indwelling sin. First, it is 'always abiding.' Unlike particular temptations that come and go, indwelling sin is a permanent resident until glorification. The believer never reaches a point in this life where the battle is over. Second, it is 'always acting.' Sin does not sleep. Even when we are not conscious of its movements, it is at work in the heart, inclining us toward self, away from God, toward the creature, away from the Creator. Third, it is 'deceitful above all things.' Sin disguises itself as wisdom, freedom, necessity, or even righteousness. The man who thinks he has conquered a particular sin is often the man most in danger of being conquered by it.

Jonathan Edwards, in his treatise 'The Freedom of the Will' and various sermons, brings a different emphasis to the same doctrine. Edwards focuses on the bondage of the will. The natural man is not merely weakened in his ability to choose God; he is unable to choose God. Edwards argues that the will always follows the strongest inclination of the heart, and the natural heart's strongest inclination is always away from God and toward self. This is not an external compulsion but an internal disposition: the sinner sins freely, willingly, and even enthusiastically, because sin is what his nature desires.

Edwards illustrates this with the analogy of a set of scales. The natural man's scales are not balanced, with God on one side and sin on the other, waiting for the will to tip the balance. Rather, the scales are permanently weighted toward sin. Only the sovereign work of the Holy Spirit can change the weight — can give the sinner a new heart with new inclinations. This is what Edwards calls 'a divine and supernatural light': a new principle of spiritual sight and taste that enables the soul to see the beauty of God and choose Him.

Thomas Watson, in 'The Doctrine of Repentance,' takes a more pastoral approach to total depravity. Watson is concerned not merely with the doctrine but with its application to the believer's daily life. He writes that a right understanding of the depth of our sin is essential to right repentance. Watson identifies what he calls 'the ingredients of true repentance,' and the first is 'sight of sin.' The penitent sinner must see not merely individual acts of transgression but the corrupt spring from which they flow. He must understand that he sins not merely because he is tempted but because he is depraved: his nature is bent toward evil.

Watson further argues that this sight of sin must be accompanied by 'sorrow for sin,' which he distinguishes from mere regret for consequences. The truly repentant man grieves not that he was caught or that he suffered but that he offended a holy and gracious God. This sorrow, Watson writes, is the work of the Spirit upon the heart, and it is impossible without a true understanding of depravity.

Why does this doctrine matter for the man who wants to grow in godliness? Because without understanding the depth of your disease, you will never appreciate the greatness of your cure. The man who thinks sin is a surface problem will seek surface solutions: moral effort, behavioral modification, accountability partnerships. These are not bad things, but they are insufficient if they do not address the root. Total depravity teaches us that we need not merely reformation but regeneration, not merely help but a Savior, not merely good advice but the sovereign grace of God. And having received that grace, we must continue to depend upon it daily, for the corruption that remains in us is deeper than our best self-knowledge and stronger than our best self-effort. Only the Spirit of God is sufficient for these things.


Here are some questions to carry with you into the coming days.

Question 1: Do I truly grasp the depth of my own depravity, or do I tend to minimize the seriousness of indwelling sin?

Question 2: How does the doctrine of total depravity increase my dependence on the Holy Spirit rather than on my own moral effort?

Question 3: In what area of my life am I most tempted to rely on behavioral modification rather than on the transforming work of God's Spirit?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


O Lord, open my eyes to see the depth of my sin that I might better see the height of Your grace. Keep me from the pride that minimizes my depravity and from the despair that forgets Your mercy. Teach me to depend wholly upon Your Spirit for every good thing. In Christ's name, Amen.

Rest in His grace tonight. Tomorrow is a new day of growth.
