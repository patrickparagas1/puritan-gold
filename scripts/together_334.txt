PURITAN GOLD -- Together: Couples Devotional, Day 3
"Kindness That Leads to Change"
Romans for Marriage — Day 3

---

Welcome, dear ones. Marriage is a gift from God, and so is this time together in His presence. Whether this has been an easy day or a hard one, you are here, together, and that matters more than you know.

Our Scripture reading this evening is from Romans 2:1-4. Let us receive it together:

"Therefore thou art inexcusable, O man, whosoever thou art that judgest: for wherein thou judgest another, thou condemnest thyself; for thou that judgest doest the same things. But we are sure that the judgment of God is according to truth against them which commit such things. And thinkest thou this, O man, that judgest them which do such things, and doest the same, that thou shalt escape the judgment of God? Or despisest thou the riches of his goodness and forbearance and longsuffering; not knowing that the goodness of God leadeth thee to repentance?"

That is Romans 2:1-4. Take a breath. Let those words settle between the two of you. God is speaking to your marriage through this passage.

--- Puritan Commentary ---

The Puritans understood marriage in a way that the modern world has largely forgotten. Let us hear from Richard Baxter, who wrote with great wisdom on this subject.

Paul's words in Romans 2 are a sharp rebuke against self-righteous judgment — the very sin that most easily infects a marriage. Nothing poisons a relationship faster than one spouse constantly sitting in judgment over the other while ignoring their own faults. Paul says bluntly: 'Thou art inexcusable, O man, whosoever thou art that judgest: for wherein thou judgest another, thou condemnest thyself; for thou that judgest doest the same things.'

Richard Baxter was keenly aware of how destructive a judgmental spirit is within marriage. He counseled couples to look first at their own faults before addressing the faults of their spouse. This is not weakness — it is the application of Christ's teaching: 'First cast out the beam out of thine own eye; and then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother's eye' (Matthew 7:5).

But the crowning verse of this passage is verse 4: 'The goodness of God leadeth thee to repentance.' Not the anger of God. Not the threats of God. Not the nagging of God. The goodness. The riches of His goodness, forbearance, and longsuffering. God changes people through kindness, not through condemnation. He is patient when we are slow to change. He is gentle when we stumble repeatedly. He extends forbearance when we deserve judgment.

Baxter applied this principle directly to marriage: if you want your spouse to change, show them the kindness of God. Do not nag, criticize, belittle, or condemn. Instead, show goodness. Show forbearance. Show longsuffering. Not because sin does not matter, but because you are imitating the God who changed your heart through kindness, not through condemnation.

This does not mean we never address sin in our spouse's life. It means we address it the way God addresses it in ours — with gentleness, patience, and an overwhelming display of grace. The husband or wife who consistently shows kindness in the face of their spouse's failures creates an environment where genuine repentance becomes possible. People do not repent because they are shamed into it — they repent because they are loved into it.

--- Marriage Application ---

Tonight, honestly evaluate: Am I more likely to judge my spouse or to show them kindness? When my spouse fails, is my first instinct to criticize or to extend grace?

Consider the areas where you have been sitting in judgment — perhaps over household responsibilities, parenting decisions, spiritual maturity, or personal habits. Now ask: Do I struggle with similar faults in other areas? Paul says we condemn ourselves when we judge, because we do the same things in different forms.

Make a commitment this week to lead with kindness rather than judgment. When your spouse falls short, ask yourself: How would God respond to me in this situation? He would respond with goodness, forbearance, and longsuffering. Go and do likewise.

The Puritans understood that marriage is not a destination but a journey. Every day presents new opportunities to love, to serve, to forgive, and to grow together. The truths we have studied tonight are not just for tonight. They are for tomorrow morning when the alarm goes off, for the difficult conversation that has been waiting, for the quiet moments when only God sees how you treat each other.

--- Reflection ---

The Puritans practiced what they called holy conference, speaking honestly about spiritual things with those they loved most. Do that now with these questions.

Question 1: In what areas are you most tempted to judge your spouse? How might you show kindness instead?

Question 2: Have you experienced God's kindness leading you to repentance in your own life? How can you extend that same kindness?

Question 3: What would change in your marriage if both of you committed to leading with grace rather than criticism?

Take a few minutes with each question. Do not rush. The best conversations in marriage happen when both people feel truly heard.

--- Prayer ---

It is time to bring what we have heard and discussed before the Lord in prayer. Pray out loud if you can. Let your spouse hear your heart.

Tonight's prayer focus: Confess any patterns of judgment in your marriage. Ask God to replace a critical spirit with His kindness, forbearance, and longsuffering. Pray that your home would be a place where grace — not condemnation — leads to genuine change.

If one of you finds it difficult to pray out loud, that is perfectly fine. Simply holding hands in silence before the Lord is itself a powerful act of worship. Let the Spirit intercede where words fail.

--- Blessing ---

May the riches of God's goodness flow through your marriage. May you lead each other to repentance not through judgment but through the patient, unrelenting kindness of Christ.

May the God who brought you together continue to bind you together in love, in faith, and in purpose. Sleep well tonight. He watches over your marriage.
