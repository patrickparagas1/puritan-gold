PURITAN GOLD — Episode 10
"Warnings Against Folly"
Week 2, Day 3 (Wednesday) — Proverbs 6

---

I watched an ant once. I mean, I really watched one — got down low, followed its path across a sidewalk for a good five minutes. It was carrying something three times its size. No one told it to. No boss was standing over it with a clipboard. No alarm clock went off that morning. It just... worked. Tirelessly. Methodically. Without complaint.

And I remember thinking, as I stood back up and brushed off my knees... that little creature puts me to shame.

Solomon thought so too.

This is Puritan Gold, and today we're in Proverbs chapter six — a chapter packed with some of the most practical, down-to-earth wisdom in all of Scripture. There's no deep allegory here, no hidden mystery. Just a father sitting his son down and saying, "Listen. Here's how to not ruin your life."

And I love this chapter for that reason. Because sometimes we don't need a theological lecture. Sometimes we need someone to look us in the eye and say, "Stop doing that. Start doing this. Here's why."

So let's walk through this chapter together, and I want to focus on three major sections: the warning to the sluggard, the description of the troublemaker, and the seven things God hates.

Let's start with the ant.

Proverbs 6, verses 6 through 11:

"Go to the ant, thou sluggard; consider her ways, and be wise: Which having no guide, overseer, or ruler, provideth her meat in the summer, and gathereth her food in the harvest. How long wilt thou sleep, O sluggard? When wilt thou arise out of thy sleep? Yet a little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands to sleep: So shall thy poverty come as one that travelleth, and thy want as an armed man."

Now, I want to be careful here, because this passage can be misused. It can be twisted into a kind of prosperity gospel hustle-culture that says if you're poor, it's your own fault, and if you just worked harder, everything would be fine. That is not what Solomon is saying. The Bible has deep compassion for the genuinely poor. The prophets thundered against those who exploited the vulnerable. Jesus Himself had nowhere to lay His head.

What Solomon is addressing here is not poverty — it's laziness. It's the person who has the ability to work but won't. The person who always has an excuse. The person who keeps hitting snooze on life's responsibilities, telling himself, "Just a little more sleep. Just a little more time. I'll get to it tomorrow."

And notice how Solomon describes the sluggard's downfall. It doesn't come all at once. It comes gradually. "A little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands." It's the little things. The small compromises. The daily neglect. No one wakes up one morning and discovers their life has collapsed overnight. It happens slowly. Imperceptibly. One lazy morning at a time. One postponed task at a time. One "I'll do it later" at a time.

And then — "so shall thy poverty come as one that travelleth." Like a traveler walking toward you on a road. You can see it coming, but you can't stop it, because you've been asleep at the wheel.

A.W. Pink, in Studies in the Scriptures, connected the sin of slothfulness to the spiritual life in a way that I find deeply convicting. He argued that physical laziness is almost always accompanied by spiritual laziness. The person who won't discipline their body is unlikely to discipline their soul. The person who neglects earthly responsibilities will almost certainly neglect heavenly ones. If you can't be bothered to read your Bible, to pray, to gather with God's people, to serve in your church — if you keep telling yourself "I'll get to it later" — then you are a spiritual sluggard. And the poverty that's coming isn't just financial. It's a poverty of the soul.

Now contrast the sluggard with the ant. What makes the ant remarkable? Three things. First, the ant is self-motivated. She has "no guide, overseer, or ruler." Nobody is making her work. She works because work needs to be done. Second, the ant is forward-thinking. She gathers in the summer, preparing for the winter that's coming. She doesn't live only for today. Third, the ant is consistent. She doesn't take weeks off. She doesn't have seasons of intense activity followed by months of idleness. She's steady. Faithful. Day after day after day.

That's the model. Not frantic, anxious overwork — but steady, faithful diligence. The kind of life that plans for the future while being faithful in the present. The kind of life that doesn't need a boss standing over it because it's governed by an internal compass of responsibility and purpose.

Ask yourself: Am I more like the ant... or the sluggard? And be honest. Not just about your work life, but about your spiritual life. Are you gathering in the summer? Or are you folding your hands?

Now let's move to the second section. Starting in verse 12, Solomon describes a particular kind of person — the troublemaker, the worthless man, the sower of discord. Verses 12 through 15:

"A naughty person, a wicked man, walketh with a froward mouth. He winketh with his eyes, he speaketh with his feet, he teacheth with his fingers; Frowardness is in his heart, he deviseth mischief continually; he soweth discord. Therefore shall his calamity come suddenly; suddenly shall he be broken without remedy."

This is a portrait of a manipulator. Notice how he uses his entire body in deception. His mouth speaks perversely. His eyes wink — communicating one thing to one person and something entirely different to another. His feet and fingers signal hidden meanings. He's a walking conspiracy. Everything about him is crafted to deceive, to manipulate, to divide.

And what is the fruit of his life? He "soweth discord." He breaks relationships. He turns friend against friend, brother against brother, church member against church member. He's the person who walks into a room and, by the time he leaves, two people who loved each other are suspicious of each other.

I've seen people like this. So have you. And the temptation is to admire their cleverness. They're often charming. They're often successful — at least for a time. But Solomon says their calamity will come suddenly. Without warning. Without remedy. The manipulator may win the short game, but God always wins the long game.

And this brings us to the crown jewel of this chapter — one of the most famous passages in all of Proverbs. The seven things God hates. Verses 16 through 19:

"These six things doth the Lord hate: yea, seven are an abomination unto Him: A proud look, a lying tongue, and hands that shed innocent blood, an heart that deviseth wicked imaginations, feet that be swift in running to mischief, a false witness that speaketh lies, and he that soweth discord among brethren."

Now, this list deserves our very careful attention. Because notice what's on it — and what isn't. There's no mention of the sins that we tend to rank as "the big ones." No mention of adultery here, or theft, or drunkenness. Instead, God lists sins that we might be tempted to minimize. Sins that are respectable. Sins that can hide in a church pew without anyone noticing.

Let's walk through them.

A proud look. This is first on the list. Not murder. Not blasphemy. Pride. The look that says, "I am above you. I am better than you. I don't need you — and I certainly don't need God." Pride is the root of every other sin on this list. It's the sin that turned Lucifer into Satan, that turned a garden into a graveyard, that turns every human heart into a throne room where self sits where God should be.

A lying tongue. God is truth. Every lie, no matter how small, is a departure from His nature. And notice — Solomon doesn't qualify this. He doesn't say "big lies" or "harmful lies." A lying tongue. Period. The white lies. The half-truths. The strategic omissions. All of it is hateful to God.

Hands that shed innocent blood. This one seems obvious — murder is wrong. But the broader principle is the harm of the defenseless. The exploitation of the vulnerable. The abuse of power against those who cannot defend themselves. In our day, this extends to the unborn, to the abused, to the oppressed, to anyone whose innocence is trampled by the powerful.

A heart that devises wicked imaginations. Notice — this is a sin of the heart, not of the hands. God cares about what happens in the invisible workshop of your mind. The fantasies you entertain. The revenge you plot. The schemes you devise. You may never act on them, but God sees the heart that manufactures them, and He calls it an abomination.

Feet that are swift in running to mischief. Not reluctant feet. Not feet that stumble accidentally into sin. Swift feet. Eager feet. The person who doesn't just fall into trouble but runs toward it. Who sees an opportunity for mischief and can't get there fast enough.

A false witness that speaks lies. This is related to the lying tongue, but it's specifically about bearing false testimony against another person. Destroying someone's reputation with words. And in our age of social media, where a single accusation can ruin a life, this sin is more prevalent — and more devastating — than ever.

And finally — he that soweth discord among brethren. This is the climax of the list. The divider. The one who takes a community that should be united and tears it apart. And notice — it says "among brethren." This is particularly aimed at the church. The person who gossips, who slanders, who whispers, who turns brother against brother in the body of Christ. God hates this. Not dislikes it. Not frowns upon it. Hates it. It is an abomination.

Friends, I want to ask you to do something uncomfortable right now. Go back through that list. All seven. And ask the Holy Spirit — honestly, bravely — "Lord, is any of this in me?"

Because here's the thing... we tend to read lists like this and immediately think of other people. "Oh, I know someone who does that." But Solomon didn't write this so you could diagnose your neighbor. He wrote it so you could examine your own heart. Is there pride in you? Dishonesty? A heart that devises schemes? A tongue that sows discord?

A.W. Pink argued that the mark of genuine conversion is not perfection — it's sensitivity. The true believer is the one who reads a list like this and trembles. Who says, "Lord, search me. Try me. See if there be any wicked way in me." The hypocrite reads it and thinks of others. The saint reads it and falls on his face.

So here's what I want you to do today. Three things.

First, be the ant. Whatever work is in front of you today — whether it's your job, your household, your studies, your ministry — do it with diligence. Do it without needing someone to stand over you. Do it as unto the Lord.

Second, examine your heart against those seven sins. Not quickly. Not superficially. Slowly. Prayerfully. Ask God to show you what you can't see on your own. And where He reveals sin, confess it. Repent of it. Turn from it.

Third, be a peacemaker, not a peace-breaker. In your family, in your church, in your friendships — be the person who builds bridges, not the one who burns them. Be the one who speaks well of others in their absence. Be the one who refuses to pass along the whisper, the rumor, the juicy piece of gossip. In a world that sows discord, be someone who sows peace.

Let's pray.

Lord God, Your Word is a mirror, and what we see when we look into it is not always flattering. We confess that we have been sluggards in our duties and in our devotion. We confess that we have harbored sins from Your very list — pride, dishonesty, hearts that devise what they shouldn't. Forgive us. Cleanse us. And by Your Spirit, make us diligent like the ant, humble under Your Word, and agents of peace in a world torn by discord. For the glory of Your name. Amen.
