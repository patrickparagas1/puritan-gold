PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Pastoral Encouragement, Day 26
"Opening Your Home for the Gospel"
Puritan Counsel on Hospitality


Good evening. The Christian life is a marathon, not a sprint. And every marathon runner needs a voice along the way saying: keep going, you are not alone, the finish line is real. That is tonight's word for you.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is 1 Peter 4:9:

"Use hospitality one to another without grudging."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


The New Testament command to practice hospitality is not a suggestion for the socially inclined; it is a duty for every Christian household. Peter commands it 'without grudging' — that is, cheerfully, generously, and without resentment. Richard Baxter treats hospitality as one of the essential marks of a well-ordered Christian home.

Baxter defines hospitality broadly. It is not merely having dinner parties or hosting social gatherings. It is the practice of opening your home, your table, and your time to others for the glory of God and the good of souls. Baxter writes that hospitality serves multiple kingdom purposes: it strengthens the bonds of fellowship within the church, it provides a context for evangelism with unbelievers, it meets the material needs of those who are struggling, and it models the generous love of God to a watching world.

Baxter identifies several obstacles to hospitality that the Christian man must overcome. The first is the idol of privacy. Many men view their home as a fortress to be defended rather than a resource to be shared. They work hard to create a comfortable space and then guard it jealously from intrusion. Baxter does not condemn the desire for rest and privacy, but he warns against making an idol of it. Your home is not ultimately yours; it is God's, entrusted to you for His purposes.

The second obstacle is perfectionism. Some families avoid hospitality because their home is not clean enough, their cooking is not good enough, or their circumstances are not impressive enough. Baxter responds with characteristic bluntness: hospitality is about people, not presentation. The family that shares a simple meal with warm fellowship has been more hospitable than the family that serves a feast in an atmosphere of stress and anxiety.

The third obstacle is selfishness with time. Hospitality costs time and energy. It interrupts schedules, disrupts routines, and demands attention that might otherwise be given to personal pursuits. Baxter writes that this cost is part of the gift. The hospitality that costs nothing is not truly hospitality; it is merely socializing.

Baxter then provides practical counsel. First, make hospitality a regular practice, not an occasional event. Baxter commends the habit of inviting others into your home on a weekly or biweekly basis. It need not be elaborate; a simple meal, a time of conversation, perhaps some Scripture reading or prayer. The regularity establishes a rhythm that makes hospitality natural rather than burdensome.

Second, be intentional about whom you invite. Baxter counsels that hospitality should extend beyond your existing circle of friends. Invite the newcomer at church. Invite the neighbor you do not know well. Invite the family that is struggling. Jesus' teaching on this point is direct: 'When thou makest a feast, call the poor, the maimed, the lame, the blind' (Luke 14:13). Hospitality that only reinforces existing social connections has missed the point.

Third, involve your whole family. Baxter encourages fathers to make hospitality a family project. Let your children help prepare the meal, set the table, and welcome guests. They will learn generosity, service, and the joy of sharing what God has given. Baxter writes that 'the family that practices hospitality together grows together in grace.'

Matthew Henry adds that hospitality is a means of grace not only for the guest but for the host. The man who opens his home opens his heart. The practice of generosity enlarges the soul. Henry writes that many believers have been surprised to find that in blessing others through hospitality, they themselves were blessed beyond measure.

The challenge is practical: when was the last time you opened your home to someone outside your immediate circle? The table is one of the most powerful tools of ministry you possess. Use it.


Let these questions search your heart. Answer honestly before God.

Question 1: Am I practicing hospitality as a regular discipline, or is my home primarily a fortress of privacy?

Question 2: What obstacles — perfectionism, selfishness with time, or the idol of comfort — most prevent me from opening my home?

Question 3: Who is someone outside my usual circle that I could invite into my home this week?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Lord, my home belongs to You. Help me to steward it for Your purposes. Overcome my selfishness, my perfectionism, and my love of comfort. Give me the joy of hospitality and the grace to open my table and my heart to those You bring across my path. Amen.

Goodnight. Remember: the same God who spoke to the Puritans speaks to you.
