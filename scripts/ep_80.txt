PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"The Heavens Declare the Glory of God"
May 19, 2026

---

"The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork. Day unto day uttereth speech, and night unto night sheweth knowledge. There is no speech nor language, where their voice is not heard. Their line is gone out through all the earth, and their words to the end of the world." -- Psalm 19:1-4

There are two great books by which God has made Himself known to mankind: the book of nature and the book of Scripture. This psalm celebrates both, beginning with the silent testimony of the heavens and moving to the perfect law of the Lord. Together, these two revelations present a portrait of the Creator so compelling that no honest soul can contemplate it without being driven to worship.

"The heavens declare the glory of God." The verb "declare" is in the continuous tense. The heavens are declaring, ceaselessly, without interruption, without pause, the glory of their Maker. This is not a message that was delivered once in the distant past and has since fallen silent. It is a perpetual proclamation, as fresh today as it was on the morning of creation. Every sunrise is a new announcement. Every starlit night is a fresh sermon. Every changing season is another chapter in the great book of God's self-revelation through His works.

And what do the heavens declare? Not merely their own beauty, though they are beautiful beyond description. Not merely their own vastness, though they stretch beyond the reach of human comprehension. They declare the glory of God. They point beyond themselves to the One who made them. A man may stand beneath the canopy of stars and admire the spectacle, and if he goes no further than admiration, he has missed the entire point. The stars are not an end in themselves. They are messengers. They are preachers. They are heralds of a glory that infinitely surpasses their own.

"Day unto day uttereth speech, and night unto night sheweth knowledge." Here is a relay of testimony that never breaks down. The day shift hands off to the night shift, and the night shift hands off to the day, and neither has ever failed to deliver its message. The sun rises and pours forth its eloquent testimony to the power of God. The sun sets, and the moon and stars take up the theme, declaring the wisdom of God in the silent watches of the darkness. There is no gap in this chain of witnesses. There is no moment when the creation falls silent.

"There is no speech nor language, where their voice is not heard." This is the universality of natural revelation. The heavens speak a language that requires no translator. The Chinese peasant and the English nobleman, the African tribesman and the Arctic fisherman, all hear the same message when they look up at the sky. The testimony of creation transcends every barrier of culture, language, and education. It speaks to the heart of every human being, regardless of his circumstances, and it leaves him without excuse.

The apostle Paul draws upon this very truth in his letter to the Romans when he declares that the invisible things of God from the creation of the world are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made, even His eternal power and Godhead. Natural revelation does not give men saving knowledge of Christ. It does not reveal the way of salvation. It does not communicate the gospel of grace. But it gives men enough knowledge of God to render them accountable. It tells them that there is a Creator, that He is powerful, that He is wise, and that they owe Him worship and gratitude. And their failure to worship and to give thanks is, as Paul solemnly declares, the root of all human wickedness.

But the psalmist does not stop with the heavens. He recognizes that natural revelation, while glorious and universal, is not sufficient for the needs of the soul. And so he turns, in the second half of the psalm, to a far more perfect source of knowledge. The transition is marked by a change in the divine name. In the first six verses, God is called El, the God of creation and power. But from the seventh verse onward, He is called Jehovah, the Lord, the covenant God who reveals Himself personally and savingly to His people.

The heavens may declare the glory of God in general terms. But the law of the Lord, the Word of God, the Scriptures of truth, declare His glory in specific, personal, and saving terms. The heavens tell us that God exists. The Word tells us who He is. The heavens tell us that God is powerful. The Word tells us that He is gracious. The heavens tell us that God is wise. The Word tells us that He is merciful. And it is through the Word, and through the Word alone, that sinful men and women come to know God not merely as their Creator but as their Redeemer.

John Calvin, that towering theologian of the Reformation, compared the Scriptures to spectacles that bring into focus what natural revelation presents only in a blurred and indistinct form. The man who looks at creation without the Scriptures is like a man with poor eyesight trying to read a book. He can see that there is something written on the page, but he cannot make out the words. But when he puts on the spectacles of Scripture, everything becomes clear, and the God who was dimly perceived through nature is now known with certainty and with saving power.

"The law of the Lord is perfect, converting the soul: the testimony of the Lord is sure, making wise the simple." Let us then give ourselves to the study of both books. Let us read the book of nature with wonder and with praise. And let us read the book of Scripture with diligence and with faith. For in both, the glory of God is declared, and in both, the soul that seeks shall find. Amen.
