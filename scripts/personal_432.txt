PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Guided Puritan Reading, Day 1
"The Tender Heart of Christ in Glory"
Thomas Goodwin — The Heart of Christ in Heaven, Introduction


Welcome, friend. Tonight we take up a spiritual discipline that was at the very heart of the Puritan life: the slow, careful, prayerful reading of a great Christian text. We are not skimming. We are not rushing. We are walking through these pages the way the Puritans did, with a Bible in one hand and a prayer in our hearts.

Tonight we draw from the work of Thomas Goodwin, specifically from "The Heart of Christ in Heaven Towards Sinners on Earth." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Hebrews 4:15-16:

"For we have not an high priest which cannot be touched with the feeling of our infirmities; but was in all points tempted like as we are, yet without sin. Let us therefore come boldly unto the throne of grace, that we may obtain mercy, and find grace to help in time of need."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


We begin a remarkable journey through one of the most tender and pastorally rich works in all of Puritan literature: Thomas Goodwin's 'The Heart of Christ in Heaven Towards Sinners on Earth.' Goodwin, who served as President of Magdalen College, Oxford, and was one of the most profound theologians of the seventeenth century, wrote this treatise to answer a question that haunts every struggling believer: What does Christ feel toward me right now, in His glory, when I am weak, sinful, and stumbling?

The answer Goodwin gives is stunning in its warmth. Christ in heaven, seated at the right hand of the Father in all His exaltation and glory, is not cold, distant, or indifferent toward His people. His heart beats with the same compassion, the same tenderness, the same yearning love that moved Him during His earthly ministry. The glorification of Christ did not harden His heart; it perfected it. He is more tender toward sinners now than He was when He walked the roads of Galilee.

Goodwin's foundation text is Hebrews 4:15: 'We have not an high priest which cannot be touched with the feeling of our infirmities.' Goodwin draws out the double negative with care. The author of Hebrews does not merely say that Christ can sympathize with us; he says that Christ cannot not sympathize. It is impossible for the glorified Christ to be unmoved by the sufferings and struggles of His people. Sympathy is woven into the very fabric of His glorified nature. He carried His human heart to heaven, and that heart is forever inclined toward those for whom He died.

This is a truth that transforms the entire Christian life. Goodwin observes that many believers live as though Christ were a reluctant Savior — as though they must beg and plead and somehow convince Him to be gracious. But the opposite is true. Christ's heart runs ahead of our prayers. Before we ask, He is inclined to give. Before we confess, He is inclined to forgive. Before we stumble, He has already prepared the grace that will restore us. Goodwin writes that Christ's affections toward His people are like a river that has been dammed up — when the dam is opened (by our coming to Him in prayer and faith), the waters rush out with a force that reveals how much pressure had been building behind them.

Goodwin also establishes an important principle at the outset: the heart of Christ in heaven is to be understood not merely as a theological abstraction but as a present, personal reality. Christ does not love 'the church' in some vague, general sense while remaining indifferent to individual believers. He knows each of His sheep by name. He knows your particular struggles, your specific sins, your private griefs. And His heart is moved toward you — not toward a category of people that happens to include you, but toward you personally, specifically, and individually.

Goodwin further argues that the glorification of Christ's human nature has enlarged, not diminished, His capacity for compassion. In His earthly ministry, Christ was limited by the constraints of a mortal body. He could be in only one place at a time. He grew weary. He slept. But now, in His glorified state, these limitations have been removed. His heart, freed from the constraints of earthly existence, is able to attend to every one of His people simultaneously, with a fullness of affection that was not possible even during His incarnate ministry.

Why does this matter for you today? Because the single greatest obstacle to your spiritual growth is not the power of your sin but your misapprehension of Christ's heart. If you believe that Christ is reluctant, you will be reluctant to come to Him. If you believe He is angry, you will hide from Him. If you believe He is indifferent, you will drift into indifference yourself. But if you see His heart as it truly is — burning with love, overflowing with compassion, eager to receive you — then you will run to Him in every circumstance, and that running will be the engine of all your growth in grace.

Goodwin also warns against a subtle error: the tendency to think of Christ's sympathy as mere sentimentality. Christ's compassion is not soft; it is strong. It does not merely feel for you; it acts for you. His sympathy expresses itself in His intercession at the Father's right hand, in His providential ordering of your circumstances, in His sending of the Spirit to comfort and strengthen you. Every grace you receive, every temptation you overcome, every comfort you enjoy is an expression of the active, effectual compassion of the glorified Christ.

Furthermore, Goodwin invites the reader to consider the practical difference this doctrine makes in the daily struggle of faith. The believer who does not know the heart of Christ will approach the throne of grace timidly, reluctantly, perhaps not at all. But the believer who has grasped the truth of Christ's tenderness will come boldly — not with the boldness of presumption but with the boldness of one who knows he is welcome. This is the boldness that Hebrews 4:16 commands: a confidence rooted not in our worthiness but in the disposition of the One to whom we come.

As we begin this study, let Goodwin's opening invitation settle into your heart: Christ in glory is not your judge waiting to condemn you; He is your High Priest who cannot help but feel for you. Come boldly to the throne of grace.


Let us pause and reflect. Consider these questions in the quiet of your heart.

Question 1: Do I tend to think of the glorified Christ as distant and stern, or as tender and compassionate toward me in my weakness?

Question 2: How would my prayer life change if I truly believed that Christ's heart runs ahead of my requests with eagerness to bless?

Question 3: What specific struggle am I facing right now that I have been reluctant to bring to Christ, perhaps because I doubt His sympathy?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Lord Jesus, as I begin this journey through Goodwin's meditation on Your heart, open my eyes to see the tenderness You bear toward me even now in glory. Forgive me for the times I have imagined You to be cold or reluctant. Teach me to come boldly to the throne of grace, knowing that I will find mercy and help. Amen.

Goodnight, friend. Walk with God tomorrow.
