Growing in Christ — Sanctification


Welcome to a new day of learning at the Puritan Academy. The Puritans believed that all truth is God's truth, and that the pursuit of knowledge is a form of worship. Let us worship Him through our studies today.

Heavenly Father, we dedicate this day of learning to You. We confess that apart from You we can understand nothing rightly. Illuminate our minds with the light of Your truth. Make us faithful students who study to show ourselves approved. In Christ's name. Amen.

Before we open our lesson books, let us sit quietly with a passage from God's Word.

Our Scripture for today is 1 Thessalonians 5:23-24; 2 Corinthians 3:18; Philippians 1:6; 1 Peter 1:15-16; Romans 6:1-14:

"And the very God of peace sanctify you wholly; and I pray God your whole spirit and soul and body be preserved blameless unto the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ. Faithful is he that calleth you, who also will do it. But we all, with open face beholding as in a glass the glory of the Lord, are changed into the same image from glory to glory, even as by the Spirit of the Lord. Being confident of this very thing, that he which hath begun a good work in you will perform it until the day of Jesus Christ. But as he which hath called you is holy, so be ye holy in all manner of conversation; Because it is written, Be ye holy; for I am holy. What shall we say then? Shall we continue in sin, that grace may abound? God forbid. How shall we, that are dead to sin, live any longer therein? Know ye not, that so many of us as were baptized into Jesus Christ were baptized into his death? Therefore we are buried with him by baptism into death: that like as Christ was raised up from the dead by the glory of the Father, even so we also should walk in newness of life. For if we have been planted together in the likeness of his death, we shall be also in the likeness of his resurrection: Knowing this, that our old man is crucified with him, that the body of sin might be destroyed, that henceforth we should not serve sin. For he that is dead is freed from sin. Now if we be dead with Christ, we believe that we shall also live with him: Knowing that Christ being raised from the dead dieth no more; death hath no more dominion over him. For in that he died, he died unto sin once: but in that he liveth, he liveth unto God. Likewise reckon ye also yourselves to be dead indeed unto sin, but alive unto God through Jesus Christ our Lord. Let not sin therefore reign in your mortal body, that ye should obey it in the lusts thereof. Neither yield ye your members as instruments of unrighteousness unto sin: but yield yourselves unto God, as those that are alive from the dead, and your members as instruments of righteousness unto God. For sin shall not have dominion over you: for ye are not under the law, but under grace."

You are not what you once were, and you are not yet what you shall be. Between these two realities lies the great work of sanctification — the patient, persistent, often painful process by which the Holy Spirit is conforming you to the image of Jesus Christ. Do not be discouraged by how far you have yet to go. Look back and see how far you have come. The sins that once dominated you have lost their grip. The truths that once seemed abstract have become living realities. The Christ who once seemed distant has become your closest companion. This is the Spirit's work, and He who began it will finish it. Do not despise the slowness of your progress — God is never in a hurry, but He is always at work. Trust the process. Trust the One who is conducting it. And press on, knowing that the day is coming when you will see Him face to face and be made perfectly, gloriously, eternally like Him.

As we prepare for today's lesson, remember that everything we study flows from this one fountain: the Word of God. Our lesson today is part of Growing in Christ — Sanctification, and it is titled: What Is Sanctification?. Let us proceed with minds engaged and hearts open.


With our hearts prepared, let us move into the substance of today's lesson.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes in 1 Thessalonians 5:23-24; 2 Corinthians 3:18; Philippians 1:6; 1 Peter 1:15-16; Romans 6:1-14.

Sanctification is the great work of God by which those whom He has justified are progressively made holy in heart and life, conformed more and more to the image of Jesus Christ, and prepared for the eternal glory that awaits them. If justification is the act by which God declares us righteous on the basis of Christ's imputed righteousness, sanctification is the process by which God makes us actually righteous through the indwelling work of the Holy Spirit. Justification changes our standing before God; sanctification changes our character. Justification is instantaneous and complete the moment we believe; sanctification is progressive and continues throughout our entire earthly life.

The apostle Paul prays for the Thessalonian believers with words that encompass the full scope of sanctification: "And the very God of peace sanctify you wholly; and I pray God your whole spirit and soul and body be preserved blameless unto the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ" (1 Thessalonians 5:23). Several truths emerge from this prayer. First, sanctification is the work of God — it is "the very God of peace" who sanctifies. We do not sanctify ourselves by our own efforts any more than we justify ourselves by our own works. Sanctification, like every other aspect of salvation, is a gift of divine grace. Second, sanctification is comprehensive — it encompasses "spirit and soul and body," the whole person. God does not merely make our behavior better while leaving our hearts unchanged. He transforms us from the inside out, renewing our minds, purifying our affections, and even consecrating our bodies as instruments of righteousness. Third, sanctification has a goal — to be "preserved blameless unto the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ." The process is headed somewhere; it will reach its consummation when Christ returns.

Paul immediately adds the guarantee: "Faithful is he that calleth you, who also will do it" (1 Thessalonians 5:24). The God who began the work of sanctification in us is faithful to complete it. This is the bedrock assurance that sustains the believer through every struggle with remaining sin. We are not left to our own resources in the battle against temptation. The God who called us is the God who sanctifies us, and His faithfulness ensures that the work He has begun will not fail. This same confidence is expressed in Philippians 1:6: "Being confident of this very thing, that he which hath begun a good work in you will perform it until the day of Jesus Christ."

In 2 Corinthians 3:18, Paul describes the mechanism of sanctification with beautiful imagery: "But we all, with open face beholding as in a glass the glory of the Lord, are changed into the same image from glory to glory, even as by the Spirit of the Lord." We are changed by beholding. As we gaze upon the glory of Christ — through Scripture, through worship, through communion with Him in prayer — the Holy Spirit transforms us into His likeness. The transformation is progressive: "from glory to glory" — from one degree of glory to the next, steadily, incrementally, persistently. We may not notice the change day by day, just as we do not notice a child growing hour by hour, but over time the transformation is unmistakable.

The call to holiness is grounded in the character of God Himself. Peter writes, "But as he which hath called you is holy, so be ye holy in all manner of conversation; Because it is written, Be ye holy; for I am holy" (1 Peter 1:15-16). The standard of holiness is not a human invention or a cultural convention — it is the very nature of God. We are called to be holy because He is holy. This is not a burden imposed on unwilling subjects but the family likeness expected of the children of God. Just as a child naturally bears resemblance to his parents, so the children of God are expected to grow in resemblance to their heavenly Father.

In Romans 6, Paul addresses the practical implications of our union with Christ for the pursuit of holiness. He begins with a rhetorical question that some were apparently asking: "Shall we continue in sin, that grace may abound?" (Romans 6:1). If God's grace is magnified by our sin, should we sin more so that grace may abound even further? Paul's response is emphatic: "God forbid. How shall we, that are dead to sin, live any longer therein?" (Romans 6:2). The argument is not merely moral but ontological — it has to do with who we are. In our union with Christ, we have died to sin. We were "baptized into his death" and "buried with him by baptism into death" (Romans 6:3-4). The old person that we were — enslaved to sin, dominated by its desires, subject to its tyranny — was crucified with Christ on the cross.

This does not mean that sin is eradicated from our experience. We still struggle with temptation; we still fall into sin; we still groan under the weight of remaining corruption. But our relationship to sin has fundamentally changed. We are no longer slaves; we are free people who sometimes stumble. Sin is no longer our master; it is a defeated enemy whose power has been broken, even though its influence lingers. Paul's exhortation flows from this reality: "Likewise reckon ye also yourselves to be dead indeed unto sin, but alive unto God through Jesus Christ our Lord" (Romans 6:11). The word "reckon" means to count as true, to consider as fact. We are to regard ourselves as dead to sin and alive to God — not because this is wishful thinking but because it is objective reality established by Christ's death and resurrection.

The practical commands follow: "Let not sin therefore reign in your mortal body, that ye should obey it in the lusts thereof. Neither yield ye your members as instruments of unrighteousness unto sin: but yield yourselves unto God, as those that are alive from the dead, and your members as instruments of righteousness unto God" (Romans 6:12-13). Sanctification involves a deliberate, daily, moment-by-moment choice to present our bodies and faculties to God for His purposes rather than yielding them to sin. It is the active cooperation of the believer with the sanctifying work of the Holy Spirit. God does not sanctify us against our will or apart from our effort; He sanctifies us through our effort, empowering our effort by His Spirit.

The crowning promise is in verse 14: "For sin shall not have dominion over you: for ye are not under the law, but under grace." Sin's dominion is broken — not by the law, which can only condemn, but by grace, which both forgives and transforms. The very grace that justified us is the grace that sanctifies us. The same Christ who died for our sins is the Christ who lives to make us holy. And the same Spirit who regenerated our dead hearts is the Spirit who day by day renews our minds, purifies our affections, and empowers our obedience. Sanctification is all of grace, from beginning to end.

This biblical foundation is essential. Without it, everything else we study today would be like a house built on sand. But with it, we stand on solid rock.


Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. John Owen wrote extensively on this subject in "A Discourse Concerning the Holy Spirit." The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life.

John Owen (1616-1683) devoted some of his most important theological writing to the doctrine of sanctification, particularly in his massive work "A Discourse Concerning the Holy Spirit," where he treats the Spirit's role in the believer's progressive growth in holiness. Owen brings to this subject his characteristic combination of rigorous biblical exegesis, systematic theological precision, and profound pastoral sensitivity. His treatment of sanctification remains one of the most comprehensive and balanced in the entire Reformed tradition.

Owen begins by carefully defining sanctification and distinguishing it from other aspects of salvation. He writes that sanctification is "the universal renovation of our natures by the Holy Spirit into the image of God, through Jesus Christ." Several elements of this definition are crucial. It is "universal" — affecting every part of the person, not merely the outward behavior but the inward dispositions, thoughts, desires, and affections. It is a "renovation" — not a creation from nothing (as in regeneration) but a renewal and restoration of what sin has corrupted. It is accomplished "by the Holy Spirit" — the Third Person of the Trinity is the agent of sanctification, working in and through the believer. It is "into the image of God" — the goal is conformity to the divine likeness, the recovery of what was lost in the fall. And it is "through Jesus Christ" — the merits, mediation, and example of Christ are the foundation and pattern of all holiness.

Owen makes a crucial distinction between what he calls "definitive sanctification" and "progressive sanctification." Definitive sanctification occurs at the moment of regeneration, when the believer is set apart for God, delivered from the dominion of sin, and given a new nature that is disposed toward holiness. This is what Paul refers to in Romans 6 when he says that we have "died to sin" and been "raised to newness of life." Progressive sanctification is the lifelong process by which the new nature grows stronger and the remnants of the old nature grow weaker, as the Holy Spirit works through the means of grace to conform the believer more and more to the image of Christ. Owen insists that both aspects are essential to a complete understanding of sanctification.

Owen identifies the Holy Spirit as the primary agent of sanctification, but he is careful to explain how the Spirit works. The Spirit does not sanctify us by immediate, miraculous intervention that bypasses our mental and moral faculties. Rather, He works through means — through the reading and hearing of Scripture, through prayer, through the sacraments, through fellowship with other believers, through the disciplines of the Christian life. Owen writes that the Spirit illuminates the mind to understand God's truth, inclines the will to choose God's ways, and inflames the affections to love God's holiness. He works in us "both to will and to do of his good pleasure" (Philippians 2:13), so that our obedience is genuine — we truly choose, truly act, truly grow — yet the power behind every choice and every act is the Spirit of God.

Owen addresses the common question of why sanctification is so slow and painful. Why does God not simply eradicate sin from the believer at conversion and make us perfectly holy in an instant? Owen gives several reasons. First, the gradual nature of sanctification teaches us humility. If we were made perfect overnight, we would be tempted to pride and self-sufficiency. The ongoing struggle with sin keeps us dependent on grace and drives us continually to Christ. Second, progressive sanctification develops spiritual virtues that could not be developed any other way — patience, endurance, faith, dependence on God. A soldier who has never faced battle has never developed the courage and skill that combat produces. Third, the gradual process gives God more glory. A sudden transformation would be impressive, but a lifelong work of patient, persistent grace — overcoming resistance, healing wounds, transforming character — displays the power, wisdom, and love of God in ways that instantaneous perfection could not.

Owen also addresses the believer's responsibility in sanctification. While insisting that sanctification is a work of grace, Owen equally insists that it requires the active cooperation of the believer. He writes that God's grace does not make human effort unnecessary but makes it effective. The believer must read the Word, pray, attend worship, exercise self-discipline, resist temptation, and actively pursue holiness — but all of this must be done in dependence on the Spirit, not in confidence in the flesh. Owen compares this to a farmer who must plow, sow, water, and tend his fields — but who depends entirely on God for the sun, rain, and soil that make the harvest possible. The farmer truly works, and the work is genuinely his — but the harvest is entirely a gift of God.

Owen devotes particular attention to the role of Scripture in sanctification. He writes that the Word of God is the primary instrument the Spirit uses to transform the believer's mind and heart. As we read, study, meditate upon, and memorize Scripture, the Spirit uses those truths to reshape our thinking, correct our errors, expose our sins, and direct our affections toward God. Owen cites Christ's prayer in John 17:17: "Sanctify them through thy truth: thy word is truth." The truth of God's Word is the cleansing agent that the Spirit applies to the soul. Without regular, serious engagement with Scripture, sanctification will stagnate and the believer's spiritual life will wither.

Owen writes with great insight about the relationship between sanctification and assurance. He observes that many believers doubt their salvation because they still struggle with sin. They reason that if they were truly saved, they would be holier than they are. Owen responds that the very fact of their struggle is evidence of grace. The unregenerate person does not struggle against sin — he embraces it. The fact that you grieve over your sin, that you hate your failures, that you long for greater holiness — this is itself the work of the Holy Spirit and evidence that He is at work in your soul. Owen writes that the believer should find assurance not in the perfection of his holiness but in the genuineness of his desire for holiness. As long as you hate your sin and love righteousness, the Spirit is at work, and the work He has begun He will carry on to completion.

Owen also warns against two dangerous errors regarding sanctification. The first is perfectionism — the belief that complete holiness can be achieved in this life. Owen argues from Scripture and experience that sin remains in the believer until death, and that anyone who claims to have achieved sinless perfection is either deceived or lying. The second error is antinomianism — the belief that since we are saved by grace, the pursuit of holiness is unnecessary. Owen argues with equal force that grace does not abolish the obligation of obedience but provides the power to fulfill it. The justified person is under obligation to pursue holiness, not as a condition of salvation but as the natural fruit of it. A tree that produces no fruit is a dead tree; a faith that produces no holiness is a dead faith.

Owen also draws attention to the prayer of Christ in John 17:17: "Sanctify them through thy truth: thy word is truth." This verse establishes a direct connection between God's Word and the process of sanctification. The truth of Scripture is the instrument the Spirit uses to renew the mind, correct sinful thinking, establish godly patterns, and conform the believer to the image of Christ. Owen writes that a believer who neglects the daily study of Scripture is like a patient who refuses the medicine that could cure him. The Word is the means of grace par excellence — the primary channel through which sanctifying power flows from God to His people. Without regular, serious, meditative engagement with Scripture, sanctification will inevitably stagnate.

The Puritan William Ames (1576-1633), whose work "The Marrow of Theology" was a standard textbook at Harvard and other colonial colleges, provides a helpful distinction between what he calls "habitual sanctification" and "actual sanctification." Habitual sanctification refers to the new dispositions and inclinations that the Spirit implants in the soul at regeneration — the new nature that is inclined toward God and righteousness. Actual sanctification refers to the outworking of these new dispositions in the concrete acts of daily obedience — the specific choices, words, and deeds that flow from the renewed heart. Ames teaches that both are essential and that neither can exist without the other. A new nature that never expresses itself in righteous action is no nature at all; and righteous action that does not spring from a renewed heart is mere moralism, not true sanctification. The goal of the Spirit's work is to produce in us both the inner reality and the outward expression of genuine Christlikeness.

Owen concludes his treatment of sanctification with a vision of its final completion in glorification. The struggle will not last forever. The day is coming when every remnant of sin will be purged away, when we shall see Christ face to face and be made perfectly like Him. "Beloved, now are we the sons of God, and it doth not yet appear what we shall be: but we know that, when he shall appear, we shall be like him; for we shall see him as he is" (1 John 3:2). This hope, Owen writes, is the greatest incentive to present holiness. Those who truly hope in Christ purify themselves, even as He is pure. The certainty of future glory does not produce complacency but zeal — for the one who knows where the journey ends is most eager to make progress along the way.

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.

Application is where truth becomes life. It is not enough to know what 1 Thessalonians 5:23-24; 2 Corinthians 3:18; Philippians 1:6; 1 Peter 1:15-16; Romans 6:1-14 says. The Puritans insisted that a truth that does not transform is a truth that has not been truly understood.


Let us press deeper into the doctrine of sanctification by examining additional Scripture and Puritan teaching.

The prophet Ezekiel records one of the most powerful promises of sanctification in the Old Testament: "Then will I sprinkle clean water upon you, and ye shall be clean: from all your filthiness, and from all your idols, will I cleanse you. A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you: and I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh, and I will give you an heart of flesh. And I will put my spirit within you, and cause you to walk in my statutes, and ye shall keep my judgments, and do them" (Ezekiel 36:25-27). This passage reveals that sanctification is fundamentally a work of God. He cleanses, He gives a new heart, He puts His Spirit within us, He causes us to walk in His ways. The emphasis on divine initiative does not eliminate human responsibility — God calls us to respond to His grace with active obedience — but it makes clear that the source and power of sanctification lie entirely in God.

The apostle Paul develops the theme of union with Christ as the foundation of sanctification with extraordinary depth in his epistles. In Galatians 2:20, he declares: "I am crucified with Christ: nevertheless I live; yet not I, but Christ liveth in me: and the life which I now live in the flesh I live by the faith of the Son of God, who loved me, and gave himself for me." This verse captures the paradox of sanctification: the old self has died with Christ on the cross, yet the believer lives — but it is no longer the old self living but Christ living within. Sanctification is nothing less than the progressive realization in experience of what is already true in our position: we are dead to sin and alive to God in Christ Jesus.

The Puritan theologian Thomas Goodwin (1600-1680), who served as president of Magdalen College, Oxford, and was one of the leading Independent divines of his era, wrote extensively on the believer's union with Christ as the foundation of all spiritual growth. Goodwin teaches that sanctification flows from union with Christ just as naturally as sap flows from the vine into the branches. The believer who is united to Christ by faith participates in Christ's life, draws upon Christ's strength, and is progressively conformed to Christ's image. Goodwin emphasizes that this union is not merely legal or forensic — it is vital, living, organic. Christ is not merely our example or our judge; He is our life. "When Christ, who is our life, shall appear, then shall ye also appear with him in glory" (Colossians 3:4).

Goodwin was educated at Christ's College, Cambridge, and later became a Fellow of Catharine Hall. His early years were spent in spiritual struggle — he came under conviction of sin through the preaching of Thomas Bainbridge but did not find assurance of salvation until years later. This prolonged experience of spiritual conflict gave Goodwin a deep understanding of the inner workings of sanctification and a tender pastoral sensitivity toward struggling believers. His writings on the work of the Holy Spirit and the nature of spiritual experience remain among the most profound in all of Puritan literature.

Consider also the teaching of Hebrews 12:14: "Follow peace with all men, and holiness, without which no man shall see the Lord." This verse makes a startling assertion: without holiness, no one will see the Lord. Holiness is not an optional upgrade for the spiritually ambitious — it is the evidence of genuine salvation. The person who has been justified by faith will inevitably be sanctified by the Spirit. Where there is no growth in holiness, there is reason to question whether genuine conversion has taken place. This does not mean that we are saved by our holiness — we are saved entirely by grace through faith in Christ — but it means that saving faith always produces the fruit of holiness, just as a living tree always produces fruit.

The apostle John reinforces this truth with characteristic directness: "Every one that doeth righteousness is born of him" (1 John 2:29). And again: "Whosoever is born of God doth not commit sin; for his seed remaineth in him: and he cannot sin, because he is born of God" (1 John 3:9). John is not teaching sinless perfection — he has already acknowledged that "if we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves" (1 John 1:8). He is teaching that the pattern of a believer's life is holiness, not sin. The regenerate person may stumble into sin, but sin is no longer the defining pattern of their existence. The seed of God — the new nature implanted in regeneration — remains in them and draws them back to righteousness.


Before we move to our afternoon activity, let us consider specific ways to pursue sanctification in daily life.

First, understand that sanctification is both a gift and a task. God has already set you apart as holy in Christ (definitive sanctification), and He is progressively making you holy through His Spirit (progressive sanctification). Your task is to cooperate with what God is doing — not to achieve holiness by your own effort, but to yield to the Spirit's work in your life.

Second, use the means of grace consistently. The Spirit sanctifies us through the Word, through prayer, through the sacraments, and through the fellowship of the church. Neglecting these means is like refusing to eat while expecting to grow physically. The Puritans had a saying: "Expect nothing from God that He has not promised to give through the means He has appointed."

Third, cultivate a daily awareness of your union with Christ. You are not fighting sin alone. Christ lives in you. His power is available to you. His victory over sin and death is your victory. Begin each day by reminding yourself: "I am dead to sin and alive to God in Christ Jesus." This is not positive thinking — it is biblical truth, and reckoning it to be true is the foundation of daily obedience.

Fourth, do not be discouraged by the slowness of your progress. Sanctification is a lifelong process, and it will not be complete until you see Christ face to face. The Puritans compared spiritual growth to the rising of the tide — it advances and retreats, advances and retreats, but the overall direction is always forward. Trust the process. Trust the God who is conducting it. And press on with patience, knowing that He who began a good work in you will complete it.


Learning by doing was central to Puritan education. Here is today's hands-on activity.

Students will create a 'Sanctification Timeline' that illustrates the three stages of the Christian life: (1) Regeneration/Definitive Sanctification — the moment of new birth when the believer is transferred from the domain of darkness to the kingdom of light, (2) Progressive Sanctification — the lifelong journey of growing in holiness, illustrated as a winding path with hills, valleys, setbacks, and advances, and (3) Glorification — the final perfection when Christ returns. Along the progressive sanctification path, students will mark key 'means of grace' (Scripture, prayer, sacraments, fellowship, suffering) as waypoints, with Scripture verses explaining how each means contributes to growth. Students will also create a two-column chart comparing Owen's view of sanctification with the two errors he warns against (perfectionism and antinomianism), showing how the biblical view avoids both extremes.

Materials needed: Large poster paper or roll paper for timeline, colored markers, printed Scripture cards for waypoints, ruler or straightedge, two-column chart template, reference sheet with Owen's key quotes on sanctification

As you complete this activity, think about how it connects to what you studied in today's lesson. The goal is not just to do something, but to reinforce the truths you have been learning. The Puritans believed that education should engage the whole person: mind, heart, and hands.

Take your time with this activity. Quality matters more than speed. When you are finished, reflect on what you learned through the process of doing. Often we understand something far better after we have tried to put it into practice.


One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

John Owen served as Vice-Chancellor of Oxford University during the Cromwellian period (1652-1658), overseeing one of the most intellectually vibrant periods in Oxford's history. Study how Owen balanced his roles as theologian, university administrator, and political adviser to Oliver Cromwell. How did the Puritan emphasis on sanctification and practical godliness influence their approach to education, governance, and public life during the Interregnum?

Literature Connection:

John Bunyan's 'The Pilgrim's Progress' is essentially a literary portrayal of progressive sanctification — Christian's journey from the City of Destruction to the Celestial City, with all its trials, temptations, companions, and victories. Compare Owen's theological description of sanctification with Bunyan's literary depiction. How does allegory communicate theological truth differently than systematic prose? Identify specific episodes in Pilgrim's Progress that illustrate Owen's theological points about the nature of spiritual growth.

Science Connection:

The biological concept of growth provides a rich analogy for spiritual sanctification. Living organisms grow through the intake of nutrients, the influence of their environment, and the unfolding of their genetic code. Study the biological processes of cell division, differentiation, and maturation. How do these natural growth processes parallel the spiritual growth described in Scripture? Consider also the concept of homeostasis — the body's ability to maintain internal stability while growing and adapting — as an analogy for the believer's need to maintain doctrinal stability while growing in grace.

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.

Summary of Key Truths:

Let us consolidate the essential truths from today's lesson on sanctification. First, sanctification is the work of God by which justified sinners are progressively made holy — it is distinct from justification, which changes our standing, while sanctification changes our character. Second, sanctification is comprehensive — it encompasses the whole person: spirit, soul, and body. Third, sanctification is guaranteed by the faithfulness of God — "Faithful is he that calleth you, who also will do it" (1 Thessalonians 5:24). Fourth, sanctification occurs through beholding Christ — as we gaze upon His glory through the Word, the Spirit transforms us into His image "from glory to glory." Fifth, sanctification requires active human cooperation — we are to reckon ourselves dead to sin, refuse to yield our bodies to unrighteousness, and present ourselves to God as instruments of righteousness. Sixth, the foundation of sanctification is our union with Christ — we died with Him, we were buried with Him, and we were raised with Him to newness of life. Seventh, sanctification will not be complete in this life — both perfectionism and antinomianism are errors to be avoided. Eighth, the certainty of future glorification is the greatest incentive for present holiness — as the apostle John declares, those who truly hope in Christ purify themselves even as He is pure, pressing forward toward the day of final, complete, and glorious eternal transformation.

Memory Verse:

"And the very God of peace sanctify you wholly; and I pray God your whole spirit and soul and body be preserved blameless unto the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ." -- 1 Thessalonians 5:23

Read this verse aloud five times. Then close your eyes and try to recite it from memory. Write it out by hand on a card or in your journal. The Puritans memorized vast portions of Scripture, and it was one of the great sources of their spiritual strength. Even one verse, truly hidden in the heart, can sustain you in times of trial.

Additional Memory Passage:

"Being confident of this very thing, that he which hath begun a good work in you will perform it until the day of Jesus Christ." -- Philippians 1:6

This verse is the believer's anchor when sanctification feels slow and the struggle against sin seems unending. God has begun the work. God will finish the work. Your sanctification is not in your hands but in His — and His hands have never failed. On the days when you stumble, when you fall back into old patterns of sin, when the progress seems impossibly slow — return to this verse and let it anchor your soul. The God who raised Christ from the dead is the God who is at work in you. The power that conquered the grave is the power that is conquering sin in your heart. Trust Him. Lean on Him. And press forward in the confidence that the work He has begun, He will most certainly complete.

Well done, students. Today you have engaged with deep truths from Scripture and from the great Puritan tradition. Carry these truths with you. Let them shape the way you think, the way you act, and the way you worship. Remember that sanctification is not merely a doctrine to be studied but a reality to be lived — a daily, moment-by-moment yielding of yourself to the Spirit of God, who is at work within you to produce the holiness that glorifies the Father, conforms you to the Son, and prepares you for the day when you will see Him face to face and be made perfectly, eternally like Him. Until tomorrow.
