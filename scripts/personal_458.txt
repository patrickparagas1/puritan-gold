PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Doctrine Deep-Dive, Day 27
"Public Worship — Approaching the Living God"
The Doctrine of Worship Explored Through the Puritans


Welcome, friend. The Puritans called doctrine the bones of the body of divinity. Without strong bones, the body collapses. Tonight we strengthen those bones as we study a vital truth together.

Tonight we draw from the work of David Clarkson, specifically from "Public Worship to Be Preferred Before Private." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is John 4:23-24:

"But the hour cometh, and now is, when the true worshippers shall worship the Father in spirit and in truth: for the Father seeketh such to worship him. God is a Spirit: and they that worship him must worship him in spirit and in truth."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


Worship stands at the center of the Christian life. It is not merely one activity among many; it is the activity for which we were created, redeemed, and sustained. The Westminster Shorter Catechism declares that the chief end of man is 'to glorify God and to enjoy him forever.' Worship is the means by which we do both: in worship, we glorify God by ascribing to Him the honor He deserves, and we enjoy Him by entering into His presence and tasting His goodness.

David Clarkson, a colleague and successor of John Owen at his London congregation, preached a remarkable sermon entitled 'Public Worship to Be Preferred Before Private.' Clarkson's argument is not that private worship is unimportant — he insists upon it — but that public, corporate, gathered worship has a priority and a glory that private devotion, however fervent, cannot match. This is a message that the contemporary church desperately needs to hear.

Clarkson gives several reasons for the superiority of public worship. First, he argues that God is more glorified by public worship. When the assembly gathers, the testimony to God's worth is multiplied. The praise of one voice is beautiful; the praise of a hundred voices is magnificent. The prayer of one soul is heard; the united prayer of a congregation carries a weight and a power that individual prayer cannot equal. Clarkson does not disparage private worship, but he insists that there is something unique about the gathered assembly that amplifies the glory given to God.

Second, Clarkson argues that God is more present in public worship. This is a remarkable claim, but Clarkson defends it carefully. He does not mean that God is spatially absent from the private devotion; God is omnipresent. But he means that God has promised a special manifestation of His presence when His people gather in His name. 'Where two or three are gathered together in my name, there am I in the midst of them' (Matthew 18:20). This special presence — what the Puritans called the 'gracious presence' as distinct from the 'essential presence' — is the unique privilege of the gathered church.

Third, Clarkson argues that God communicates more abundantly through public worship. It is in the public assembly that the Word is preached, the sacraments are administered, and the gifts of the body are exercised. These are means of grace that cannot be replicated in private. The sermon is not merely one man's reflections on a text; it is the means by which Christ, through His appointed minister, speaks to His people. The Lord's Supper is not available apart from the gathered body. The gifts of exhortation, encouragement, service, and prayer are exercised person to person in the assembly.

The Puritans also emphasized the manner of worship. Drawing on Christ's words in John 4, they insisted that true worship must be 'in spirit and in truth.' Worship 'in spirit' means that it must engage the heart, not merely the lips. The man who sings the hymns, hears the sermon, and partakes of the sacrament without any inward engagement of the affections has not truly worshipped. He has gone through the motions without making contact with God. Worship 'in truth' means that it must conform to the Word of God. The Puritans held to what is called the regulative principle of worship: only those elements which God has commanded in Scripture may be included in the worship of the church. This principle protects worship from human invention, cultural accommodation, and the idolatry that inevitably creeps in when men begin to worship God according to their own imagination.

John Owen supplements Clarkson's treatment by emphasizing the Trinitarian structure of worship. In true Christian worship, the believer comes to the Father, through the Son, by the Spirit. The Father is the object of worship; the Son is the Mediator who makes access possible; the Spirit is the one who enables genuine worship by opening the heart and stirring the affections. Worship that neglects any Person of the Trinity is deficient.

The Puritan Thomas Watson adds a practical note on preparation for worship. Watson counsels that the worshipper should prepare his heart before he enters the assembly. This means setting aside distracting thoughts, confessing known sin, stirring up desire for God's presence, and approaching the Lord's Day with expectation. Watson writes that the farmer who prepares his soil before planting reaps a far greater harvest than the one who scatters seed on unprepared ground. So the worshipper who prepares his heart will receive far more from the service than the one who arrives distracted, unprepared, and indifferent.

Patrick, how do you approach the worship of God? Do you come to the gathered assembly expecting to meet with the living God, or do you come out of habit, duty, or social expectation? Do you worship in spirit — with genuine engagement of your mind, heart, and will — or merely with your body present? Do you worship in truth — according to the Word of God rather than your own preferences? The Puritans would remind you that worship is not primarily about what you get out of it but about what you give to God. Come to worship ready to give Him the honor, adoration, and obedience He deserves.


Reflection is where truth becomes personal. Consider these questions carefully.

Question 1: Do I approach public worship with a sense of expectation — genuinely anticipating an encounter with the living God — or has it become routine?

Question 2: Am I worshipping in spirit (with genuine engagement of my heart) and in truth (according to the Word of God), or have I settled for outward conformity?

Question 3: Do I prioritize gathered, public worship with the body of Christ, or do I treat it as optional when other activities compete for my time?

Question 4: How can I prepare myself more intentionally for worship — in the hours before the service and in the posture of my heart?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Triune God — Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — I come before You as a worshipper. Teach me to worship in spirit and in truth. Forgive me for the times I have gone through the motions without engaging my heart, and for the times I have approached Your presence carelessly. Give me a high view of public worship, a reverent expectation of Your gracious presence, and a heart that is prepared to give You the glory that is Your due. Through Jesus Christ, my only access to Your throne, Amen.

May the Lord guard your mind and your heart tonight. Until we meet again.
