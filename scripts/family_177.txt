PURITAN GOLD -- Family Devotional, Day 16
"A Church in the House"
Matthew Henry — Family Worship and Bible Study


Good evening, dear family. Welcome back to Puritan Gold. Tonight we explore Matthew Henry's beloved treatise "A Church in the House," where he makes the case that every Christian home should function as a worshiping congregation. Let us open our hearts to God.

Let us pray. Father, we thank You that we need not go to a grand cathedral to worship You. You dwell with the humble and contrite. You inhabit the praises of Your people, even when those people are a small family gathered around a kitchen table. Meet us here tonight. In Christ's name, Amen.

Our Scripture reading this evening comes from Philemon 1:1-2 and Romans 16:3-5. Let us hear the Word of the Lord:

"Paul, a prisoner of Jesus Christ, and Timothy our brother, unto Philemon our dearly beloved, and fellowlabourer, And to our beloved Apphia, and Archippus our fellowsoldier, and to the church in thy house. Greet Priscilla and Aquila my helpers in Christ Jesus: Who have for my life laid down their own necks: unto whom not only I give thanks, but also all the churches of the Gentiles. Likewise greet the church that is in their house."

That is Philemon 1:1-2 and Romans 16:3-5. Let those words settle in your heart for a moment before we continue.


In the early church, there were no grand church buildings. Believers met in homes. Paul writes to Philemon and greets "the church in thy house." He sends greetings to Priscilla and Aquila and "the church that is in their house." The home was the primary gathering place of the people of God, and the household was the foundational unit of the body of Christ.

Matthew Henry seized upon this pattern and made it the foundation of his treatise "A Church in the House." His central argument is that every Christian family should be a church in miniature — a community of worship, instruction, prayer, and mutual accountability. The family that worships together at home is not merely preparing for public worship on the Lord's Day. It is engaged in an act of worship that has its own independent validity and importance.

Henry begins by establishing the authority of the head of household to lead family worship. Just as God appointed priests in Israel to lead worship in the tabernacle and temple, so He has appointed fathers to lead worship in the home. This is not a matter of personal preference or cultural tradition — it is a divine ordinance. Henry writes that when a father gathers his family for worship, he is fulfilling a calling as sacred as that of any minister who stands behind a pulpit.

But Henry is also practical. He knows that many fathers feel inadequate for this task. They may not be eloquent speakers. They may not have formal theological training. They may feel self-conscious praying aloud in front of their families. To these men, Henry offers encouragement: God does not require eloquence. He requires sincerity. A simple prayer from the heart of a father who loves God and loves his family is more pleasing to God than the most polished oration delivered without faith.

Henry outlines a pattern for family worship that is both comprehensive and manageable. He suggests beginning with a brief prayer for God's blessing. Then reading a portion of Scripture — a chapter or a suitable passage — with a brief explanation suited to the understanding of the youngest listener. Then a time of discussion, where family members can ask questions and share observations. Then a closing prayer that gathers up the themes of the reading and applies them to the family's specific needs and circumstances. And if possible, the singing of a psalm or hymn.

Henry is especially insistent on the inclusion of children. Family worship is not meant to be an adult activity that children merely endure. It should be designed with children in mind. The passage should be explained in terms they can understand. The prayers should include matters they care about. The questions should draw them into the conversation. Henry observes that children who are included in family worship from their earliest years will grow up regarding it as a natural and beloved part of their daily routine, rather than as a burdensome interruption.

Henry also addresses the problem of consistency. He acknowledges that family worship is easy to begin but difficult to maintain. Schedules conflict. Emergencies arise. Fatigue sets in. Enthusiasm wanes. His counsel is straightforward: set a time and keep it. Morning worship prepares the family for the day ahead. Evening worship reviews the day and commits the family to God's care for the night. If both are not possible, choose one and be faithful to it. A short time of worship, faithfully maintained, is infinitely better than an ambitious program that collapses within a week.

Henry writes with evident personal conviction. He had seen the fruit of family worship in his own childhood home, in his own household, and in the homes of his congregation. Families that worshiped together were stronger, more united, more resilient in times of trial, and more fruitful in the work of God's kingdom. The family altar, Henry believed, was the fountain from which spiritual life flowed into the church and into the world.

Tonight, let us recommit ourselves to the practice that Matthew Henry commended with such passion. Let our home be a church. Let our table be an altar. Let our family be a congregation. And let the God who inhabits the praises of His people fill this household with His presence.


Let us pause and talk as a family. Here are tonight's discussion questions. Everyone gets a turn to share.

Question 1: Paul mentions "the church in thy house" several times. What does it mean for a home to function as a church? What are the similarities and differences?

Question 2: Henry says fathers should not feel inadequate to lead family worship because God values sincerity over eloquence. How does this encouragement apply to our family?

Question 3: What time of day works best for our family to worship together? What adjustments could we make to protect that time?

Take a few minutes to talk about these questions together. Let everyone share, and listen to one another with patience and love.


Now, here is our memory verse for today. Let us say it together:

"For where two or three are gathered together in my name, there am I in the midst of them." -- Matthew 18:20

Try to say it out loud three times right now. Then, before bed tonight, see if you can say it from memory.


Let us pray together. Lord God, we claim Your promise tonight: where two or three are gathered in Your name, You are in their midst. We are gathered in Your name. Be in our midst. Make this home a church where You are worshiped, where Your Word is honored, and where Your presence is known. In Jesus' name, Amen.

Sleep well tonight, knowing that God watches over your family.
