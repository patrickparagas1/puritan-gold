PURITAN GOLD — Episode 18
"The Art of Divine Contentment"
Week 3, Day 4 (Thursday) — Thomas Watson, Proverbs 15:16-17 & Philippians 4:11-13

---

Let me ask you an honest question. And I want you to sit with it before you answer.

Are you content?

Not "are you comfortable" — that's a different thing. Not "do you have everything you want" — that's a different thing entirely. Are you content? Is there a settled peace in your soul, a quiet satisfaction, a rest that isn't shaken by what you have or what you lack?

If you're anything like most people — like most Christians, even — the honest answer is... not really. Not consistently. Maybe in flashes. Maybe on good days. But beneath the surface, there's a restlessness. A sense that something is missing. A nagging feeling that if only your circumstances were different — if only you had a little more money, a better job, a healthier body, a different relationship, a different city, a different life — then you'd be at peace.

This is Puritan Gold, and today we're going to sit at the feet of Thomas Watson — one of the most gifted and practical of all the Puritan preachers. Watson was a London minister who served faithfully during one of the most turbulent periods in English history. He was ejected from his pulpit in 1662 for refusing to conform to the requirements of the Act of Uniformity. He lost his position, his income, his security. And it was from that place of loss... that he wrote one of the most beautiful and searching works in all of Christian literature: "The Art of Divine Contentment."

And notice that word — art. Not "the duty of contentment." Not "the obligation of contentment." The art. Because Watson understood that contentment is not something that comes naturally. It's not a personality trait that some people have and others don't. It is, as he put it, "a sweet, inward, quiet, gracious frame of spirit, which freely submits to and delights in God's wise and fatherly disposal in every condition."

Read that again slowly. A sweet, inward, quiet, gracious frame of spirit. That submits to God. And delights in God. In every condition.

Every condition. Not just the good ones. Not just the prosperous ones. In every condition — in poverty and in plenty, in sickness and in health, in honor and in shame.

This is what the Apostle Paul was describing when he wrote those extraordinary words in Philippians 4:11-13: "Not that I speak in respect of want: for I have learned, in whatsoever state I am, therewith to be content. I know both how to be abased, and I know how to abound: every where and in all things I am instructed both to be full and to be hungry, both to abound and to suffer need. I can do all things through Christ which strengtheneth me."

"I have learned." Paul didn't say, "I've always known." He said, "I have learned." Contentment was something he had to acquire. It was a lesson. A discipline. An art that had to be practiced and cultivated over time, through trial after trial, until it became the settled posture of his soul.

And notice where the power comes from — "I can do all things through Christ which strengtheneth me." Not through positive thinking. Not through lowering your expectations. Not through stoic resignation. Through Christ. Christ is the source. Christ is the secret. Without Him, contentment is just pretending. With Him, contentment is possible in the darkest valley.

Now, let's hear what Proverbs says about this. Because Solomon, the wisest man who ever lived apart from Christ, had something pointed to say about contentment and its counterfeits.

Proverbs 15:16: "Better is little with the fear of the LORD than great treasure and trouble therewith."

And verse 17: "Better is a dinner of herbs where love is, than a stalled ox and hatred therewith."

Two "better than" proverbs. And both of them cut against the grain of everything our culture tells us.

Better is a little with the fear of the Lord than great treasure with trouble. Better is a humble meal with love than a feast with hatred. In other words — the quality of your inner life matters infinitely more than the quantity of your possessions. A small house filled with the presence of God is better than a mansion haunted by anxiety. A simple dinner with a family that loves one another is better than a banquet in a home full of bitterness.

Watson would say: the world promises contentment through accumulation. Get more. Earn more. Achieve more. Acquire more. And then you'll be satisfied. But it never works, does it? Because the appetite for more is bottomless. It's a pit with no floor. You get what you thought you wanted, and within days — sometimes within hours — you're already wanting the next thing.

Watson called this the "dropsy of covetousness." Dropsy was a disease where the body retained fluid and was constantly thirsty no matter how much the patient drank. That's what discontent does to the soul. It makes you perpetually thirsty, no matter how much you drink from the wells of the world.

And here's Watson's deeper point — one that cuts to the bone. He said that discontentment is not merely an emotional problem. It is a sin. A serious sin. Because at its root, discontentment is a complaint against God.

Think about that. When you are discontent with your circumstances, what are you really saying? You're saying, "God, You've gotten it wrong. You've given me too little, or too much of the wrong thing. My life should look different than it does. You are not managing my affairs well enough."

That's a charge against the wisdom, the goodness, and the sovereignty of God. And Watson says we must see it for what it is. Not a harmless frustration. Not a minor mood. A rebellion of the heart against the providence of God.

Now, Watson is pastoral about this. He doesn't beat you over the head. He understands human weakness. He knows that we struggle. But he also knows that the only way to heal a disease is to diagnose it honestly. And the disease of discontent must be named before it can be cured.

So how is it cured? How do we learn this art of divine contentment?

Watson gives us several remedies, and they're remarkably practical.

First, he says: consider what you deserve. Not in the world's terms — but in God's terms. If God gave you what you actually deserve, as a sinner before a holy God... you would receive nothing but judgment. Everything you have — every breath, every meal, every sunrise, every friendship — is grace. Unmerited, undeserved grace. When you truly grasp that, contentment grows naturally. Because you stop comparing what you have to what others have, and you start comparing what you have to what you deserve. And the gap between those two things... is filled entirely with mercy.

Watson put it memorably: "A man who truly knows himself will be content, for he knows he deserves nothing but wrath, and therefore whatever God gives above that is a bonus of grace."

Second, Watson says: consider that your real treasure is not in this world. If you are in Christ, you have an inheritance that is incorruptible, undefiled, and that does not fade away. Your true riches are stored in heaven, where moth and rust do not destroy and thieves do not break in and steal. The reason we are discontent with earthly things is often because we're expecting them to satisfy like heavenly things. But they can't. They weren't designed to. They're good gifts from a good God, but they are not your portion. God Himself is your portion.

Psalm 73:25-26 — "Whom have I in heaven but thee? and there is none upon earth that I desire beside thee. My flesh and my heart faileth: but God is the strength of my heart, and my portion for ever."

When God is your portion, your contentment doesn't depend on your bank account or your health or your reputation. It depends on God. And God doesn't change. God doesn't fluctuate. God doesn't rise and fall with the market.

Third, Watson says: consider that God often uses affliction for your good. The things that make you discontent — the hardships, the losses, the disappointments — are frequently the very tools God uses to sanctify you. The furnace of affliction purifies the gold of your faith. The pruning shears of suffering cut away the dead wood so that you bear more fruit.

Watson writes that a Christian should look at every affliction and say, "What is my Father teaching me through this? What is He pruning away? What is He cultivating?" When you see trials as the curriculum of a loving Father rather than the punishments of a distant tyrant, contentment becomes possible even in suffering.

Fourth — and this is perhaps the most important — Watson says: get more of Christ. Contentment is not the result of getting more things. It's the result of getting more of Christ. The more you know Him, the more you love Him, the more you commune with Him... the less power the world has over your heart.

Watson says that a person who has Christ has everything — even if he has nothing else. And a person who lacks Christ has nothing — even if he has everything else. Because Christ is the sum of all good. He is the fountain from which every blessing flows. And when you drink from that fountain, your soul is satisfied in a way that no earthly spring can match.

John Bunyan, writing from his prison cell in Bedford — where he spent twelve years for the crime of preaching the gospel — penned these remarkable words: "I have found that Christ is most sweet when the world is most bitter." Bunyan had lost his freedom, his livelihood, his daily contact with his family — including his blind daughter, whom he loved dearly. And yet... he found contentment. Not because his circumstances were good, but because Christ was present. Even in a dungeon. Especially in a dungeon.

Friend, I don't know what your circumstances are today. Maybe you're in a season of abundance, and the temptation is to find your contentment in your blessings rather than in the One who blesses. Maybe you're in a season of scarcity, and the temptation is to believe that God has forgotten you. Maybe you're in a season of ordinary, unremarkable days, and the temptation is boredom — a restless desire for something, anything, to be different.

Wherever you are, the counsel of Watson stands: contentment is an art that must be learned. And the classroom is not comfort — the classroom is Christ. The more of Him you have, the less you need of everything else.

Better is little with the fear of the Lord than great treasure and trouble.

Better is a dinner of herbs where love is than a stalled ox and hatred.

Better is Christ in a prison cell than all the riches of Egypt.

Learn this art, dear friend. It won't come overnight. It didn't come overnight for Paul. It didn't come overnight for Watson. It didn't come overnight for Bunyan. But it came. By grace, through faith, in the school of the Holy Spirit... it came.

And it will come for you too.

Let me pray.

Lord God, forgive our restless hearts. Forgive us for grumbling against Your providence, for wanting what You haven't given, and for despising what You have. Teach us the art of contentment — not the false contentment of the world, which is only numbness, but the true contentment of the gospel, which is satisfaction in You. You are our portion, Lord. You are enough. More than enough. Help us to believe that — not just with our minds, but with our lives. Through Jesus Christ, who for our sake became poor, that we through His poverty might become rich. Amen.
