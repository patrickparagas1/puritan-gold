PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 5
"Stay Me with Flagons"
Song of Solomon 2:5-7 — Love's Intensity and Love's Patience

---

Welcome, dear couple. Tonight we encounter a passage that speaks to the overwhelming intensity of love — and the wise restraint that protects it. These verses are short but packed with wisdom for every marriage.

Song of Solomon 2:5-7:

"Stay me with flagons, comfort me with apples: for I am sick of love. His left hand is under my head, and his right hand doth embrace me. I charge you, O ye daughters of Jerusalem, by the roes, and by the hinds of the field, that ye stir not up, nor awake my love, till he please."

--- Teaching ---

"I am sick of love." What a remarkable phrase. The bride is not sick of love in the modern sense — tired of it, bored with it. She is lovesick. She is overwhelmed by the intensity of her affection. Love has so filled her that she needs sustenance — flagons of wine and apples — just to sustain her under its weight. This is passionate love, the kind that leaves you breathless.

The Puritans did not flinch from passion in marriage. Contrary to the caricature of dour, pleasure-hating Christians, the Puritans celebrated marital passion with remarkable freedom. Richard Baxter wrote in his Christian Directory that married love should have in it "a sweet, prevailing power to cause contentment in each other." William Gouge stated plainly that "a mutual liking" between husband and wife is not optional but essential to a God-honoring marriage. And Thomas Gataker wrote that the fire of marital love should burn hot and constant, never allowed to smolder into mere routine.

"His left hand is under my head, and his right hand doth embrace me." This is a picture of tender, physical intimacy. One arm cradles her head; the other draws her close. There is protection here, and there is passion. The Puritans saw this embrace as a model for the physical dimension of marriage — gentle, mutual, and deeply satisfying. They taught that the marriage bed is a gift from God, not merely for procreation but for the deep comfort and bonding that physical closeness provides.

But then comes the unexpected turn. "I charge you, O ye daughters of Jerusalem, by the roes, and by the hinds of the field, that ye stir not up, nor awake my love, till he please." This charge — this solemn warning — appears three times in the Song of Solomon. It is clearly important. The bride turns to the watching world and says: Do not rush love. Do not force it. Do not awaken it before its proper time.

What does this mean for married couples? It means that even within marriage, love has rhythms. There are seasons of intense passion and seasons of quiet rest. There are moments of overwhelming desire and moments of gentle companionship. The wisdom of the Song is that both are good, both are needed, and neither should be forced.

The Puritans understood that the physical dimension of marriage requires patience, tenderness, and attentiveness to each other's needs. Gouge wrote at length about the duty of each spouse to consider the other's feelings, the other's readiness, the other's state of mind and body. Love is not selfish demand; it is attentive gift. The charge "stir not up love till he please" is a call to honor the natural rhythms of desire and rest in your marriage.

This principle extends beyond the physical. Emotional intimacy also has rhythms. There are seasons when one spouse needs deep conversation and another when silence is the kindest gift. There are times when one partner needs to process grief or anxiety, and the other must wait patiently rather than rushing to fix. The roes and hinds of the field — gentle, easily startled creatures — are the image. Love is like a deer: approach it too aggressively and it flees. Approach it with gentleness and patience, and it draws near.

Richard Sibbes applied this principle to the spiritual life of couples as well. He wrote that spiritual growth in marriage cannot be forced. One spouse may be further along in certain areas of sanctification than the other. The temptation is to push, to lecture, to demand conformity to your timeline. But the Song says: do not stir up love until it pleases. Be patient with each other's growth. Trust God's timing. Create a safe space where the deer of love can come and go freely.

Tonight, consider the rhythms of your marriage. Are you honoring each other's pace — physically, emotionally, spiritually? Or are you trying to force something that needs time and tenderness? The healthiest marriages are those where both spouses feel free — free to desire, free to rest, free to grow at the pace that God has set.

--- Reflection Prompts ---

Turn to each other and consider these questions.

Question 1: Do you feel safe expressing your needs and desires to your spouse — including the need for rest, space, or time? Is there anything you have been afraid to ask for?

Question 2: Are there areas of your marriage where you have been trying to force growth rather than allowing it to unfold naturally? How can you create more patience and freedom in those areas?

Question 3: The bride is overwhelmed by love and asks for sustenance. What sustains your love for each other? What are the "flagons and apples" — the practices and habits — that keep your love strong?

--- Prayer Focus ---

Tonight's prayer focus: Pray for wisdom to honor the rhythms of your marriage. Ask God to give you patience with each other's pace and sensitivity to each other's needs. Pray that your love would be like the gentle deer of the field — free, unforced, and beautiful in its natural movement.

--- Closing ---

Love that is patient is love that lasts. Do not hurry past each other tonight. Rest in the embrace that God has given you, and trust that the best is yet to come. Goodnight, dear couple.
