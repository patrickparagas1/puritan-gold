PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Reading, Day 18
"The Pilgrim's Progress: Vanity Fair"
Bunyan's Allegory of Worldly Temptation


Good evening, friend. Tonight we return to John Bunyan's immortal allegory, "The Pilgrim's Progress," and to one of its most famous and searching episodes: the journey of Christian and Faithful through Vanity Fair. This passage has lost none of its power across the centuries. The Vanity Fair that Bunyan described is the world we walk through every single day.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is 1 John 2:15-17:

"Love not the world, neither the things that are in the world. If any man love the world, the love of the Father is not in him. For all that is in the world, the lust of the flesh, and the lust of the eyes, and the pride of life, is not of the Father, but is of the world. And the world passeth away, and the lust thereof: but he that doeth the will of God abideth for ever."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


"The Pilgrim's Progress" was written by John Bunyan during his imprisonment in Bedford jail for unlicensed preaching. Published in 1678, it tells the story of a man named Christian who flees from the City of Destruction and travels toward the Celestial City. Along the way he encounters every kind of trial, temptation, and adversary that a believer might face in his earthly pilgrimage. The episode of Vanity Fair is among the most powerful scenes in the entire work.

Bunyan tells us that in the town of Vanity, there is a fair — a great marketplace — that has been running since ancient times. It was established by Beelzebub, Apollyon, and Legion as a trap for pilgrims who must pass through on their way to the Celestial City. At Vanity Fair, everything is for sale: honors, titles, pleasures, lands, kingdoms, lusts, bodies, souls, gold, silver, and every fleeting delight the world can offer. The fair operates all year round, and its merchants are eager and relentless.

When Christian and his companion Faithful arrive at the fair, they immediately attract attention — and hostility. Three things set them apart. First, they are dressed differently. Their pilgrim garments mark them as foreigners, strangers passing through rather than residents settling in. Second, they speak differently. Their language — the language of Scripture and holiness — sounds strange to ears accustomed to the dialect of the world. Third, and most provocatively, they have no interest in the merchandise. When the merchants press them to buy, they stop their ears and look upward, signifying that their true commerce is in heaven.

This refusal to buy creates a tumult. The men of the fair are enraged. They mock the pilgrims, smear them with dirt, and throw them into a cage for public ridicule. Eventually, Faithful is brought to trial before Lord Hate-good. The witnesses against him bear telling names: Envy, Superstition, and Pickthank. Faithful is convicted, beaten, and put to death. He dies a martyr — and a chariot carries his soul straight to the Celestial City.

What does Bunyan want us to learn? Several truths stand out with clarity.

First, the world is a marketplace. Bunyan captures a profound truth about the nature of worldliness. The world is not primarily a place of overt wickedness — though it contains that. It is a place where everything has a price and everything is for sale. The lust of the flesh, the lust of the eyes, and the pride of life — these are the goods on display. They are presented with such skill and beauty that even pilgrims are tempted to stop and browse. Thomas Manton, reflecting on this same reality, wrote that the Christian must pass through the world's marketplace without setting up a stall.

Second, the pilgrim will always be a stranger. Christian and Faithful stand out because they do not belong. Their clothes are different, their speech is different, their values are different. Bunyan illustrates the biblical truth that believers are "strangers and pilgrims on the earth" (Hebrews 11:13). If you are perfectly comfortable in the world, if you blend in seamlessly with its values and priorities, you may need to ask whether you are a pilgrim or a permanent resident.

Third, faithfulness may cost everything. Faithful's martyrdom is a reminder that following Christ can lead to suffering, rejection, and even death. Bunyan himself spent twelve years in prison for his convictions. He knew the cost of discipleship firsthand. And yet he shows us that Faithful's death is not defeat but triumph. The world kills his body, but it cannot touch his soul.

Fourth, the world cannot tolerate indifference to its wares. What enrages the men of Vanity Fair is not aggressive preaching but quiet disinterest. The world can tolerate religion that does not interfere with its commerce. What it cannot tolerate is a faith that says, "I have no interest in what you are selling. My treasure is elsewhere."


First, take an honest inventory. What merchandise of Vanity Fair has found its way into your cart? What worldly goods — comfort, status, entertainment, approval — have you been purchasing with your time, your energy, and your affections?

Second, cultivate the pilgrim mindset. Remind yourself daily that this world is not your home. You are passing through. Hold its goods lightly. Fix your eyes on the Celestial City.

Third, prepare for the cost. If you live as a true pilgrim, you will be misunderstood, mocked, and perhaps worse. Count the cost now, so that you are not surprised when it comes.


Lord, we confess that Vanity Fair is attractive to us. We are tempted daily by its wares. Give us pilgrim hearts that long for the Celestial City more than for the comforts of this passing world. Give us the courage of Faithful, who counted the cost and did not shrink back. Fix our eyes on the prize that awaits us. Through Christ our Lord, Amen.

Goodnight, friend. Keep walking. The Celestial City draws nearer with every step.
