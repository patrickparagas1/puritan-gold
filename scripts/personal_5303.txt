PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Pastoral Encouragement, Day 3
"Cultivating a Prayer Life"
Deeper Prayer — Learning to Abide in God's Presence


Welcome, friend. Tonight we set aside the study of books and doctrines and turn to something even more fundamental: the life of prayer. The Puritans were, above all else, men and women of prayer. Their theology was forged on their knees. Their courage was sustained in the closet of devotion. If we would follow in their footsteps, we must learn to pray.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Matthew 6:6:

"But thou, when thou prayest, enter into thy closet, and when thou hast shut thy door, pray to thy Father which is in secret; and thy Father which seeth in secret shall reward thee openly."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


There is perhaps no greater struggle in the Christian life than the struggle to pray. Not to say prayers, mind you, but to truly pray — to commune with the living God, to pour out the heart before Him, to listen for His voice, and to rest in His presence. The Puritans understood this struggle intimately, and their counsel on prayer remains among the most helpful ever written.

John Bunyan, in his little treatise "I Will Pray with the Spirit," makes a striking observation. He writes that prayer is not the recitation of fine words but the pouring out of the heart before God through Christ, in the strength of the Spirit. Bunyan was a tinker, not a scholar, and he knew what it was like to feel inadequate before God. His counsel is wonderfully liberating: God does not require eloquence. He requires sincerity. The stumbling prayer of a broken heart is more precious to God than the polished oration of a proud mind.

The first obstacle to a deeper prayer life is simply neglect. We do not pray because we do not make time to pray. The Puritans were ruthlessly disciplined about setting apart time for God. They rose early. They retired to private places. They guarded their prayer times against interruption. This was not legalism; it was love. A man who loves his wife makes time for her. A man who loves his God makes time for Him. If your prayer life is weak, the first question to ask is not whether you need a new technique but whether you need a new schedule.

The second obstacle is distraction. We sit down to pray and our minds wander to tomorrow's obligations, yesterday's failures, the noise of the world around us. The Puritans were honest about this struggle. Thomas Manton writes that wandering thoughts in prayer are one of the surest evidences of indwelling sin. But he also counsels patience: do not abandon prayer because your mind wanders. Gently bring it back. Confess the distraction. Resume your communion with God. The act of returning to prayer after distraction is itself a kind of victory over the flesh.

The third obstacle is a sense of unworthiness. Many believers avoid prayer because they feel too sinful to approach God. This is a subtle deception of the enemy. The Puritans would remind us that we never approach God on the basis of our own worthiness. We come through Christ. We come by the blood of the covenant. We come because we have been invited — indeed, commanded — to draw near. Richard Sibbes writes that God is more willing to hear than we are to pray. He delights in the prayers of His children, not because they are perfect but because they come through His perfect Son.

How then shall we cultivate a deeper prayer life? The Puritans offer several pieces of practical counsel.

First, begin with Scripture. Let the Word of God fuel your prayers. Read a passage and then pray it back to God. If you read of His faithfulness, praise Him for it. If you read of His commands, ask for grace to obey. If you read of His promises, plead them before His throne. This practice, sometimes called "praying the Scriptures," prevents prayer from becoming mere repetition and keeps it anchored in the truth of God.

Second, be specific. The Puritans prayed for particular mercies, confessed particular sins, and gave thanks for particular blessings. Vague prayers produce vague faith. When you bring specific requests to God, you put yourself in a position to recognize specific answers, and nothing strengthens faith like answered prayer.

Third, cultivate a spirit of thanksgiving. The apostle Paul writes in Philippians 4:6, "Be careful for nothing; but in every thing by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known unto God." The Puritans took this seriously. They kept records of God's mercies. They rehearsed His faithfulness in prayer. Gratitude opens the heart to God in a way that petition alone cannot.

Fourth, persevere. The greatest prayer warriors are not those who feel the most when they pray but those who continue to pray when they feel nothing. George Muller, though not a Puritan himself, embodied their spirit when he said that the key to his prayer life was that he never gave up. There will be seasons of dryness, seasons when heaven seems silent, seasons when prayer feels like speaking into a void. Keep praying. God is listening even when you cannot feel His presence.

Fifth, pray in community. Private prayer is essential, but so is corporate prayer. The Puritans gathered for prayer meetings, family worship, and congregational intercession. There is a power in united prayer that solitary prayer, however fervent, cannot replicate. Jesus promised, "Where two or three are gathered together in my name, there am I in the midst of them" (Matthew 18:20).

Let me leave you with this encouragement: a deeper prayer life is not reserved for spiritual giants. It is the birthright of every child of God. Begin where you are. Begin with five minutes if that is all you have. But begin. And trust that the God who hears prayer will meet you in the place of prayer.


Before we close, sit quietly with these questions.

Question 1: What is the greatest obstacle to my prayer life right now — neglect, distraction, unworthiness, or something else?

Question 2: Am I praying specifically, or have my prayers become vague and routine?

Question 3: What one practical step could I take this week to deepen my communion with God in prayer?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


O Father, teach us to pray. Forgive us for our neglect, our wandering minds, and our unbelief. Draw us into deeper communion with Thee. Let our prayer closets become the most beloved rooms in our lives. Through Christ, who ever liveth to intercede for us, Amen.

Sleep in peace tonight. Your Father is awake and listening.
