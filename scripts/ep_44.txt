PURITAN GOLD -- Study Devotional, Day 13
Series: Romans
"The Law, Sin, and Death"
How the law exposed sin's deadly power and brought conviction

---

Welcome. The practice of sustained Bible study is one of the greatest gifts a Christian can give themselves. Tonight we continue to build our understanding, one passage at a time, guided by the Holy Spirit and the wisdom of the Puritans.

We are studying the book of Romans together. Tonight's lesson is titled "The Law, Sin, and Death." Each session builds on what came before, so if you have been following along, you will see how tonight's passage connects to the larger argument of the book.

--- Scripture Reading ---

Let us read our passage for tonight. It comes from Romans 7:1-13:

"Know ye not, brethren, (for I speak to them that know the law,) how that the law hath dominion over a man as long as he liveth?... Wherefore, my brethren, ye also are become dead to the law by the body of Christ; that ye should be married to another, even to him who is raised from the dead, that we should bring forth fruit unto God. For when we were in the flesh, the motions of sins, which were by the law, did work in our members to bring forth fruit unto death. But now we are delivered from the law, that being dead wherein we were held; that we should serve in newness of spirit, and not in the oldness of the letter... What shall we say then? Is the law sin? God forbid. Nay, I had not known sin, but by the law: for I had not known lust, except the law had said, Thou shalt not covet. But sin, taking occasion by the commandment, wrought in me all manner of concupiscence. For without the law sin was dead... For sin, taking occasion by the commandment, deceived me, and by it slew me... Was then that which is good made death unto me? God forbid. But sin, that it might appear sin, working death in me by that which is good; that sin by the commandment might become exceeding sinful."

Read that passage again to yourself. Mark any words or phrases that stand out. The Puritans read with a pen in hand, always ready to note what God was showing them.

--- Key Verse ---

One verse in particular demands our attention tonight. Let us read it carefully.

"But sin, that it might appear sin, working death in me by that which is good; that sin by the commandment might become exceeding sinful." -- Romans 7:13

This verse is the lens through which we will examine the rest of tonight's passage. Keep it in the front of your mind as we proceed.

--- Exposition ---

Let us now exposit this passage carefully, as the Puritans would have done.

Thomas Goodwin offers valuable commentary on this passage. The Puritans were expositors of the highest order, and their insights remain remarkably fresh.

Thomas Goodwin addressed the relationship between the law, sin, and the believer with characteristic depth and pastoral sensitivity. He understood Romans 7:1-13 as Paul's explanation of why the law — though holy, just, and good — could never be the means of sanctification any more than it could be the means of justification. Goodwin argued that the law is like a lamp in a dark room: it reveals the dirt but cannot clean it. It exposes sin but cannot cure it. It commands obedience but cannot enable it.

Goodwin gave careful attention to Paul's marriage analogy in verses 1-4. Under the old covenant, Israel was bound to the law as a wife to a husband. But through the death of Christ, believers have died to the law (the old husband has, as it were, died) and are now free to be united to Christ (the new husband). The fruit of the first marriage was 'unto death' — the law could only produce guilt and condemnation in fallen humans. But the fruit of the new marriage is 'unto God' — union with the risen Christ produces genuine holiness through the power of the Spirit.

Goodwin was especially penetrating on the paradox of verses 7-11: the commandment that was meant to bring life actually brought death, because sin used the law as a base of operations. He drew from this the sobering lesson that no external rule, however good, can change the heart. The problem is not with the law but with us. Sin is so deeply rooted in fallen human nature that it can even pervert the holy commandment of God into an occasion for greater rebellion. This is why we need not merely instruction but regeneration — not merely a new law but a new heart. Only the Spirit of life in Christ Jesus can accomplish what the law, weakened by our sinful nature, could never do (Romans 8:3).

Romans 7:1-13 addresses one of the most important questions in the Christian life: what is the believer's relationship to the law? Having established in chapter 6 that believers have died to sin, Paul now shows that believers have also died to the law. This is not because the law is bad but because the law, interacting with our sinful nature, produces bondage rather than freedom.

Paul begins with an analogy from marriage law (verses 1-3). The law has dominion over a person as long as they live. A married woman is bound to her husband while he lives, but if the husband dies, she is free to marry another. She is not an adulteress if she remarries after her husband's death. The application in verse 4 is striking: 'Wherefore, my brethren, ye also are become dead to the law by the body of Christ; that ye should be married to another, even to him who is raised from the dead.'

Notice carefully: it is not the law that died (as the analogy might suggest) but we who died. Through union with Christ's death ('by the body of Christ'), we died to the law's authority over us. We are now free to be united to Christ — the risen Lord — and this new union produces fruit that the old relationship never could. 'That we should bring forth fruit unto God.' The law demanded fruit but could not produce it. Union with Christ both demands and enables fruitfulness.

Verse 5 describes the old condition: 'For when we were in the flesh, the motions of sins, which were by the law, did work in our members to bring forth fruit unto death.' The law actually stirred up sinful passions. Not because the law was sinful, but because our fallen nature is so perverse that it responds to prohibition with increased desire. Tell a child not to touch the cookie jar, and the cookies become irresistible. The law said 'thou shalt not covet' and coveting increased. This is the paradox of legalism: the more rules you impose on a sinful heart, the more that heart rebels.

Verse 6 describes the new condition: 'But now we are delivered from the law, that being dead wherein we were held; that we should serve in newness of spirit, and not in the oldness of the letter.' The Christian serves God — the goal is the same — but the means is entirely different. We serve in newness of spirit, not in the oldness of the letter. The Spirit within us produces the obedience that the external law could only command. This is the new covenant reality that Jeremiah and Ezekiel prophesied: God's law written on the heart by the Spirit (Jeremiah 31:33; Ezekiel 36:26-27).

In verses 7-13, Paul addresses the inevitable question: 'Is the law sin?' If the law produces such negative results, is it itself evil? 'God forbid!' Paul defends the law vigorously while explaining its proper function. 'Nay, I had not known sin, but by the law: for I had not known lust, except the law had said, Thou shalt not covet.' The law's function is diagnostic, not therapeutic. It reveals the disease but does not provide the cure. Without the commandment, sin was unrecognized — not absent, but undetected. The law turns on the lights and exposes what was always there.

Verse 8 reveals sin's sinister strategy: 'But sin, taking occasion by the commandment, wrought in me all manner of concupiscence.' Sin used the commandment as a 'base of operations' (the Greek aphorme is a military term for a staging ground). The very commandment against coveting became the launchpad for increased coveting. 'For without the law sin was dead' — not non-existent, but dormant, inactive, unrecognized.

Verses 9-11 describe Paul's personal experience (likely reflecting on his Pharisaic past): 'For I was alive without the law once: but when the commandment came, sin revived, and I died. And the commandment, which was ordained to life, I found to be unto death. For sin, taking occasion by the commandment, deceived me, and by it slew me.' Before the law's full implications struck his conscience, Paul felt spiritually secure. But when the commandment came with its full force — particularly 'thou shalt not covet,' which exposes the heart — sin sprang to life and Paul's self-righteousness was shattered. The commandment that was designed to lead to life instead led to death, because sin hijacked it.

Verse 12 defends the law's character: 'Wherefore the law is holy, and the commandment holy, and just, and good.' The law is God's law. It reflects His holy character. It is perfectly just and entirely good. The problem is not with the law but with us.

Verse 13 explains the divine purpose: 'Was then that which is good made death unto me? God forbid. But sin, that it might appear sin, working death in me by that which is good; that sin by the commandment might become exceeding sinful.' God used the law to unmask sin, to reveal its true nature, to show how utterly evil it really is. Sin is so depraved that it can take something perfectly good (the law of God) and use it as an instrument of death. This revelation makes sin 'exceeding sinful' — showing its true colors so that we will hate it and flee to Christ.

This passage is essential for understanding why moralism always fails. Rules, regulations, and behavioral codes — however good in themselves — cannot transform the human heart. The law can restrain sin externally, but it cannot change sin internally. Only the gospel of grace, applied by the Holy Spirit, can produce the new heart that obeys from within. This is why Paul says we serve 'in newness of spirit, and not in the oldness of the letter.'

--- Application ---

What does this truth look like in the ordinary moments of your life? At work, at home, in your relationships? Let us make it concrete.

Romans 7:1-13 has profound implications for how you approach the Christian life.

First, stop trying to grow through law-keeping alone. If your strategy for spiritual growth is primarily more rules, more discipline, more self-imposed regulations — you are building on the wrong foundation. Rules have their place, but they cannot change the heart. Examine your approach to sanctification: is it fundamentally law-based or Spirit-based? Do you rely primarily on willpower or on the power of the Holy Spirit? Shift your focus from external regulation to internal transformation through prayer, meditation on the gospel, and dependence on the Spirit.

Second, let the law do its proper work. The law is meant to reveal sin, not cure it. When you read God's commandments and feel convicted, do not immediately rush to performance mode — trying harder to measure up. Instead, let the conviction drive you to Christ. The law is a mirror: look at it honestly, see your failure clearly, and then turn to the only One who can make you clean.

Third, understand the married-to-Christ reality. You are no longer bound to the law as your way of relating to God. You are united to the risen Christ. This means your relationship with God is not fundamentally about rules and regulations but about a living Person. Cultivate your relationship with Christ the way you would cultivate any relationship — through time, conversation, trust, and love. He is your husband, not your taskmaster.

Fourth, develop a healthy view of the law. The law is holy, just, and good. Do not despise it or dismiss it. But do not idolize it either. The law reveals God's character, exposes our sin, and drives us to Christ. It is a tutor that leads us to the gospel. Read the law with gratitude — gratitude that God has shown you His standard, and gratitude that Christ has met that standard on your behalf.

Write down one concrete action you will take this week in response to tonight's study. Do not let this truth remain theoretical. The Puritans measured the success of a sermon not by how much the hearer learned but by how much the hearer changed.

--- Closing ---

Holy God, Your law is holy, just, and good, yet we confess that our sinful hearts have used even Your good commandments as occasions for rebellion. We thank You that through the body of Christ we have died to the law and are now united to the risen Savior. Teach us to serve in newness of spirit, not in the oldness of the letter. Let Your law do its proper work of revealing sin, and let Your Spirit do His proper work of transforming our hearts. Deliver us from both legalism and lawlessness, and establish us in the freedom of grace. Through Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.

Goodnight. Remember, the goal of study is not information but transformation.
