PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Guided Puritan Reading, Day 22
"The Omniscience of God"
Stephen Charnock — The Attributes of God: Omniscience


Welcome, friend. Tonight we take up a spiritual discipline that was at the very heart of the Puritan life: the slow, careful, prayerful reading of a great Christian text. We are not skimming. We are not rushing. We are walking through these pages the way the Puritans did, with a Bible in one hand and a prayer in our hearts.

Tonight we draw from the work of Stephen Charnock, specifically from "The Existence and Attributes of God." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Psalm 139:1-6:

"O LORD, thou hast searched me, and known me. Thou knowest my downsitting and mine uprising, thou understandest my thought afar off. Thou compassest my path and my lying down, and art acquainted with all my ways. For there is not a word in my tongue, but, lo, O LORD, thou knowest it altogether. Thou hast beset me behind and before, and laid thine hand upon me. Such knowledge is too wonderful for me; it is high, I cannot attain unto it."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


God's omniscience — His perfect and exhaustive knowledge of all things — is an attribute that simultaneously humbles and comforts the believer. It humbles because it means that nothing is hidden from God: not a thought, not a motive, not a secret sin, not a hidden agenda. It comforts because it means that God knows us perfectly and yet loves us fully. He is not deceived by our pretenses, and He is not disappointed by our failures, because He knew them all before He chose us.

Charnock devotes one of his most expansive discourses to this attribute. He begins by defining the scope of God's knowledge. God knows all things actual — everything that has existed, does exist, or will exist. He knows every atom in the universe, every event in history, every thought of every creature. But God also knows all things possible — everything that could have existed, could have happened, could have been. This is what theologians call God's knowledge of middle knowledge or scientia media. God knows not only what will happen but what would have happened under every conceivable set of circumstances.

Charnock emphasizes that God's knowledge is not derived from observation but is inherent in His nature. He does not learn. He does not discover. He does not reason from premises to conclusions. His knowledge is immediate, intuitive, and eternal. He knew all things before they existed, because He knew His own decree — His eternal plan — from which all things flow. Charnock writes that God's knowledge is like a fountain, not a reservoir: it does not receive from without but flows from within.

Charnock then explores the relationship between God's omniscience and human sincerity. Because God knows everything — including the thoughts and intentions of the heart — all hypocrisy is futile before Him. The man who maintains an outward show of religion while harboring inward rebellion is not deceiving God; he is deceiving only himself. Charnock writes that God's omniscience strips away every mask and exposes the soul in its naked reality. This is terrifying to the hypocrite but liberating to the sincere believer, who no longer needs to pretend.

Charnock draws a remarkable devotional application from this doctrine. If God knows us completely — every weakness, every failure, every shameful thought — and yet has chosen to love us, then His love is absolutely secure. Human love is often based on incomplete knowledge: we love people because we see their best qualities and are ignorant of their worst. But God sees the worst and loves us anyway. His love is not naive; it is fully informed. It is the love of One who has read every page of our story — including the pages we would never show to anyone — and has decided to claim us as His own.

Charnock also addresses the practical implications of omniscience for the devotional life. Because God knows our thoughts, we need not fear that our unspoken prayers go unheard. Before we formulate the words, God knows the groaning of our spirits. Because God knows our circumstances, we need not fear that our situation has escaped His attention. He is fully aware of every detail of our lives and is governing all things for our good.

Charnock makes one more point of penetrating practical significance. God's omniscience means that our secret sins are as fully known to Him as our public ones. There is no darkness dark enough to hide from His gaze. The man who commits a sin in private, unseen by any human eye, has committed it in the full view of God. Charnock writes that this knowledge should be the most powerful deterrent to secret sin: not the fear of being caught by men but the certainty of being known by God.

Charnock draws one final application that connects omniscience to worship. Because God knows all things, our worship must be sincere. The God who sees the heart is not impressed by outward shows of devotion that mask inward indifference. He is not deceived by eloquent prayers spoken by a distracted mind or by generous giving motivated by the desire for human approval. Charnock writes that the omniscient God demands truth in the inward parts — worship that is genuine, heartfelt, and transparent. The worshipper who comes before the all-knowing God must come as he truly is, without pretense or performance, trusting that the God who sees through every mask is also the God who accepts sinners through Christ.

Let Psalm 139 be your response: 'Search me, O God, and know my heart: try me, and know my thoughts: and see if there be any wicked way in me, and lead me in the way everlasting' (Psalm 139:23-24). The prayer of the sincere believer is not that God would stop knowing but that God would keep searching — and keep leading.


Let us pause and reflect. Consider these questions in the quiet of your heart.

Question 1: Does the omniscience of God make me more fearful or more grateful — and what does my answer reveal about the state of my heart?

Question 2: Are there areas of my life where I am living as though God cannot see — secret sins, hidden motives, or unspoken attitudes?

Question 3: How does the knowledge that God sees my worst and loves me anyway deepen my trust in His love?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


All-knowing God, I stand before You fully exposed. You know my thoughts before I think them, my words before I speak them, my sins before I commit them. I cannot hide from You, and I do not want to. Search me, O God, and know my heart. Try me, and know my thoughts. See if there be any wicked way in me, and lead me in the way everlasting. I thank You that Your perfect knowledge of me has not diminished Your perfect love for me. In Christ's name, Amen.

Goodnight, friend. Walk with God tomorrow.
