PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 10
"The Last Things — Death, Judgment, and Eternity"
Westminster Standards — Lesson 10

---

Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we conclude our unit on the Westminster Standards with their teaching on eschatology — the last things: death, the resurrection, the final judgment, and the eternal state. Let us pray and begin.

Eternal God, You hold the future in Your hands. As we study the last things today, give us both sobriety and hope — sobriety about the reality of judgment, and hope in the promise of eternal life. In Christ's name, Amen.

--- Lesson ---

The Westminster Standards conclude their systematic presentation of Christian doctrine with the last things — the events that will bring human history to its appointed end. Chapter 32 of the Confession addresses the state of souls after death and the resurrection of the body. Chapter 33 addresses the last judgment.

The Confession teaches that at death, the body returns to dust, but the soul does not perish or sleep. Rather, "the souls of the righteous, being then made perfect in holiness, are received into the highest heavens, where they behold the face of God in light and glory, waiting for the full redemption of their bodies." The souls of believers enter immediately into the presence of God — what theologians call the "intermediate state." This is conscious, joyful communion with Christ, though it is not yet the final state.

Conversely, "the souls of the wicked are cast into hell, where they remain in torments and utter darkness, reserved to the judgment of the great day." The wicked also enter a conscious intermediate state, but one of suffering rather than joy. This is a sober and sobering truth that the Westminster divines did not shy away from.

But the intermediate state is not the end. The Confession affirms the bodily resurrection: "At the last day, such as are found alive shall not die, but be changed; and all the dead shall be raised up with their self-same bodies, and none other, although with different qualities, which shall be united again to their souls for ever." The resurrection is physical and real. The same bodies that were laid in the grave will be raised — transformed, glorified, and made imperishable, but genuinely the same bodies. This is a truth of enormous significance. Christianity does not teach the escape of the soul from the body. It teaches the redemption of the body — the restoration of the whole person, body and soul together, for eternal life.

The Shorter Catechism, Question 37, asks, "What benefits do believers receive from Christ at death?" and answers: "The souls of believers are at their death made perfect in holiness, and do immediately pass into glory; and their bodies, being still united to Christ, do rest in their graves till the resurrection." Note the phrase "still united to Christ." Even in the grave, the bodies of believers are not abandoned. They are united to Christ and will be raised by His power on the last day.

Question 38 asks, "What benefits do believers receive from Christ at the resurrection?" and answers: "At the resurrection, believers, being raised up in glory, shall be openly acknowledged and acquitted in the day of judgment, and made perfectly blessed in the full enjoying of God to all eternity." This is the final state — perfect blessedness in the full enjoyment of God. Every tear wiped away. Every sorrow healed. Every longing satisfied. Every sin vanquished. The believer stands before God, acknowledged as His child, acquitted of every charge, and welcomed into the eternal joy of the Father's house.

Chapter 33 addresses the last judgment. The Confession teaches that "God hath appointed a day wherein he will judge the world in righteousness by Jesus Christ, to whom all power and judgment is given of the Father." Christ Himself will be the Judge — the same Christ who died for sinners will one day judge all humanity. "In which day, not only the apostate angels shall be judged; but likewise all persons, that have lived upon earth, shall appear before the tribunal of Christ, to give an account of their thoughts, words, and deeds; and to receive according to what they have done in the body, whether good or evil."

The purpose of the final judgment is twofold. For believers, it is a day of vindication and reward. Their works of faith will be publicly honored, and they will enter into the fullness of eternal life. For unbelievers, it is a day of condemnation. Having refused the grace of God in Christ, they will receive the just punishment for their sins.

The Confession closes with a statement about the eternal states: "The wicked, who know not God, and obey not the gospel of Jesus Christ, shall be cast into eternal torments, and punished with everlasting destruction from the presence of the Lord." But "the righteous...shall be received into heaven, where they immediately behold the face of God in light and glory; waiting for the full redemption of their bodies."

The Shorter Catechism's final question provides the most concise summary. Question 107 asks, "What doth the conclusion of the Lord's prayer teach us?" and answers that it teaches us "to take our encouragement in prayer from God alone, and in our prayers to praise him, ascribing kingdom, power, and glory to him."

The Westminster Standards end as they began — with the glory of God. The chief end of man is to glorify God and enjoy Him forever. And the last things reveal how that chief end will be perfectly and eternally fulfilled. For believers, eternity is the endless enjoyment of God — the full, unhindered, face-to-face communion with the One for whom we were made.

--- Review Questions ---

Review Question 1: What happens to believers at death, according to the Westminster Confession? What is the "intermediate state"?

Review Question 2: What is the doctrine of the bodily resurrection, and why does it matter? How are the resurrection bodies different from our present bodies?

Review Question 3: What is the purpose of the final judgment? How does it relate to the eternal states of the righteous and the wicked?

--- Activity ---

Write a one-page essay titled "The Christian Hope" that summarizes what the Westminster Standards teach about death, resurrection, judgment, and eternity. Use at least three direct quotations from the Confession or Catechisms and at least four Scripture references. Conclude your essay with a personal statement about how the doctrine of the last things affects the way you live today. Present your essay to your family or study group and discuss it together.

We have completed our survey of the Westminster Standards. From the doctrine of Scripture to the last things, these documents present a comprehensive, biblical, and life-transforming vision of the Christian faith. Carry these truths with you. Let them shape your thinking, your worship, and your daily life. Until next time, students.
