PURITAN GOLD -- Study Topic: The Book of Enoch, Part 4 of 10
"Enoch's Journeys (1 Enoch 17-36)"
The Book of Enoch — Visions of heaven, hell, and cosmic geography

---

Grace and peace to you, beloved friends. We continue our study of the Book of Enoch, and today we enter some of its most imaginative and awe-inspiring passages — the cosmic journeys of Enoch through the furthest reaches of creation. In chapters 17 through 36, Enoch is taken by angelic guides on a tour of the universe, visiting the places of judgment, the storehouses of the elements, and the very throne of God.

Let us read from 2 Corinthians 12:2-4:

"I knew a man in Christ above fourteen years ago, (whether in the body, I cannot tell; or whether out of the body, I cannot tell: God knoweth;) such an one caught up to the third heaven. And I knew such a man, (whether in the body, or out of the body, I cannot tell: God knoweth;) How that he was caught up into paradise, and heard unspeakable words, which it is not lawful for a man to utter."

--- Teaching ---

The concept of a heavenly journey — of a righteous individual being taken up to see the hidden realities of God's creation — is deeply rooted in the biblical tradition. Elijah was taken up by a whirlwind into heaven (2 Kings 2:11). Isaiah saw the Lord sitting upon a throne, high and lifted up (Isaiah 6:1). Ezekiel was carried in visions of God to behold the glory of the Lord and the architecture of the heavenly temple (Ezekiel 1, 40-48). The apostle John was caught up to see the things which must be hereafter (Revelation 4:1). And Paul himself, as we have just read, was caught up to the third heaven.

The journeys of Enoch in chapters 17 through 36 belong to this tradition of visionary ascent, though as a pseudepigraphal work, they carry a different authority than the canonical visions. Nevertheless, they are richly instructive for understanding how ancient Jewish writers conceived of the cosmos and the spiritual realities that undergird the visible world.

In the first journey (chapters 17-19), Enoch is shown a place of fire and darkness — a chaotic region at the ends of the earth where the elements exist in their raw, untamed form. He sees mountains of fire, rivers of flame, and a great abyss. He beholds the prison of the fallen angels — those Watchers who sinned, now bound in chains of darkness awaiting the day of judgment. The imagery is stark and terrible, designed to impress upon the reader the absolute certainty of divine retribution.

This vision of a place of imprisonment for fallen spiritual beings connects directly to the canonical teaching. We have already noted Peter's reference to angels cast down to Tartarus, "delivered into chains of darkness, to be reserved unto judgment" (2 Peter 2:4). Jude likewise speaks of angels "reserved in everlasting chains under darkness unto the judgment of the great day" (Jude 6). The Enochic visions give elaborate visual form to what the apostles stated in more concise terms.

In the second journey (chapters 20-36), Enoch is guided by the archangels to see the full scope of God's creation. He visits the mountain where the dead await judgment — a concept that would develop in later Jewish thought into the doctrine of Sheol, the abode of the departed. The text describes Sheol as divided into compartments: one for the righteous, who await their reward in peace and light; and others for the wicked, who await their punishment in suffering and darkness.

This multi-chambered view of the afterlife finds a remarkable echo in our Lord's parable of the rich man and Lazarus (Luke 16:19-31). In that parable, the beggar Lazarus is carried to Abraham's bosom — a place of comfort — while the rich man finds himself in torment, with a great gulf fixed between them. While we must be careful not to build doctrine upon parables, the parallel between Christ's teaching and the Enochic tradition is noteworthy. Both present the intermediate state as one of conscious existence, with a clear distinction between the fates of the righteous and the wicked.

Enoch also beholds what the text calls the "tree of life" — a fragrant tree set apart on a holy mountain, which the righteous will be given to eat from after the final judgment. The language deliberately echoes Genesis 2-3, where the tree of life stood in the garden of Eden, and Revelation 2:7, where the risen Christ promises, "To him that overcometh will I give to eat of the tree of life, which is in the midst of the paradise of God." The vision thus connects the original paradise lost with the paradise to be restored — a grand narrative arc that is fundamental to the biblical story of redemption.

Throughout these journeys, Enoch repeatedly responds with worship. When he beholds the majesty of God's creation — the orderly movements of the heavenly bodies, the vast storehouses of wind and rain and snow, the appointed places of reward and punishment — he falls on his face and blesses the Lord. This is precisely the response that the canonical Scriptures call for. The Psalmist declares, "The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork" (Psalm 19:1). The vision of cosmic order leads not to cold scientific curiosity but to warm-hearted worship.

The cosmology presented in these chapters is, of course, the cosmology of the ancient Near East — a flat earth covered by a dome, with storehouses for the elements at the edges of the world. We need not accept the scientific framework to appreciate the theological message. The Puritan commentator Matthew Poole, though writing about the canonical text, frequently noted that Scripture uses the language of appearance and accommodation — describing the world as it appears to the human observer — rather than the language of precise astronomy. The same principle applies here. The theological truths conveyed by these visions are not dependent upon their cosmological framework.

What are those theological truths? First, that God's sovereignty extends to every corner of creation. There is no place — not the highest heaven, not the deepest abyss — that is outside His dominion. The Psalmist knew this: "Whither shall I go from thy spirit? or whither shall I flee from thy presence? If I ascend up into heaven, thou art there: if I make my bed in hell, behold, thou art there" (Psalm 139:7-8).

Second, that the created order is purposeful and meaningful. Nothing in the universe exists by chance. Every star, every mountain, every element serves a purpose appointed by God. The Book of Enoch, like the canonical Scriptures, presents a universe that is saturated with divine intention.

Third, that judgment is certain and inescapable. The places of punishment that Enoch beholds are not hypothetical. They are prepared and waiting. The wicked cannot flee from divine justice any more than they can flee from the presence of God. As the author of Hebrews declares, "It is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God" (Hebrews 10:31).

Fourth, that the righteous have a sure hope. The compartment of Sheol reserved for the faithful, the tree of life promised to the redeemed, the final restoration of all things — these visions, imperfect as they are, point forward to the full revelation of gospel hope. Christ has abolished death and brought life and immortality to light through the gospel (2 Timothy 1:10).

--- Application ---

1. Let the vision of God's cosmic sovereignty expand your view of His greatness. Too often we shrink God down to the size of our problems. The God who governs the courses of the stars and the storehouses of the elements is more than sufficient for every trial you face.

2. Take comfort in the certainty of divine justice. When you see wickedness prospering and righteousness suffering, remember that the places of judgment are prepared. God will set all things right in His appointed time.

3. Let the hope of future glory sustain you in present suffering. The tree of life awaits those who overcome. The paradise of God shall be restored. Your labor in the Lord is not in vain.

4. Respond to creation with worship. The next time you behold the stars, the sunrise, or the changing of the seasons, let your heart ascend in praise to the God who made and sustains all things.

--- Closing ---

O Lord our God, how excellent is Thy name in all the earth! Thou hast set Thy glory above the heavens. When we consider the works of Thy hands — the moon and the stars which Thou hast ordained — what is man that Thou art mindful of him? Yet Thou hast crowned him with glory and honor. We worship Thee, O God of all creation, through Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.

Until our next gathering, beloved, may your eyes be opened to behold the wonders of God's creation, and may your hearts be lifted in unceasing praise.
