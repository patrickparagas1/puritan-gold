PURITAN GOLD — Episode 5
"Trust in the Lord"
Week 1, Day 5 (Friday) — Proverbs 3

---

I want to tell you about a moment that many Christians experience but few talk about openly.

It's that moment when you've done everything right — or at least everything you know how to do — and it still isn't working. The job you prayed about... didn't come through. The relationship you invested in... fell apart. The ministry you poured yourself into... seems to be bearing no fruit. The healing you asked for... didn't come.

And in that moment, there's a voice — quiet, reasonable, almost gentle — that says, "Maybe you should take matters into your own hands. Maybe trusting God isn't enough. Maybe you need a backup plan."

It's in moments like these that Proverbs chapter three becomes more than a nice saying on a greeting card. It becomes a lifeline.

This is Puritan Gold. And today we are going to walk through one of the richest chapters in the entire book of Proverbs — chapter three — with special attention to those famous verses, five through eight. But we won't stop there. Because the whole chapter is a treasury, and I don't want us to miss any of it.

Let's begin at the beginning. Proverbs 3, verses 1 through 4:

"My son, forget not my law; but let thine heart keep my commandments: For length of days, and long life, and peace, shall they add to thee. Let not mercy and truth forsake thee: bind them about thy neck; write them upon the table of thine heart: So shalt thou find favour and good understanding in the sight of God and man."

Notice how Solomon begins — with the heart. "Let thine heart keep my commandments." Not just your behavior. Not just your outward compliance. Your heart. This is internal, not external. God is not interested in mere religious performance. He wants the inside to match the outside. He wants obedience that flows from love, not obligation.

And then those two words: mercy and truth. Bind them around your neck. Write them on the tablet of your heart. These are not just virtues to practice occasionally. They are to be your identity. As close to you as a necklace around your throat. As permanent as words engraved in stone.

Mercy — that's compassion, kindness, steadfast love toward others. Truth — that's integrity, honesty, faithfulness to what is right. And when these two walk together in a person's life... the result is remarkable: "favour and good understanding in the sight of God and man."

But now we come to the heart of the chapter. Verses 5 through 8. And I want to read these slowly, because each phrase deserves our full attention:

"Trust in the Lord with all thine heart; and lean not unto thine own understanding. In all thy ways acknowledge him, and he shall direct thy paths. Be not wise in thine own eyes: fear the Lord, and depart from evil. It shall be health to thy navel, and marrow to thy bones."

Four commands. Four promises. Let's take them one at a time.

Command one: "Trust in the Lord with all thine heart."

The Hebrew word for trust here — "batach" — means to lean your full weight on something. It's not a tentative, half-hearted reliance. It's the kind of trust you exercise when you sit down in a chair without first testing whether it will hold you. You commit your full weight. You hold nothing back.

And notice: it's "with all thine heart." Not with part of your heart while keeping a reserve of self-reliance tucked away in the back pocket, just in case. All of it. The whole thing. Every corner of your heart entrusted to God.

Now... this is terrifying for most of us. Because we've been hurt before. We've trusted people who let us down. We've made plans that fell apart. And somewhere along the way, we learned to hold something back. To always have a plan B. To never fully commit, because full commitment means full vulnerability.

But God is not people. God does not fail. God does not forget His promises. God does not change His mind or lose His power or run out of resources. And when He asks for your full trust... it's not because He needs it. It's because you need it. You need to let go of the illusion that you're in control... so that the One who actually is in control can work freely in your life.

Command two: "Lean not unto thine own understanding."

This is the other side of the coin. Trusting God means not trusting yourself — at least, not ultimately. Not as your final authority. Your understanding is limited. Your perspective is partial. You see a tiny fraction of what God sees. You know almost nothing about what tomorrow holds, let alone next year or next decade.

And yet... how often do we make decisions based entirely on what makes sense to us? How often do we chart our course using only the light we can see... forgetting that God sees the entire landscape from above?

Spurgeon once observed that the Christian who leans on his own understanding is like a man trying to navigate a dark forest with a candle, when the sun is available to him. The candle is not worthless — human reason has its place. But compared to the light of God's wisdom, it is a flickering, inadequate thing. And the wise person holds up the candle while asking God for the sun.

Command three: "In all thy ways acknowledge him."

"All thy ways." Not just the big decisions. Not just the crises. All your ways. The mundane. The ordinary. The daily commute and the grocery run and the conversation with a coworker and the choice of what to do with your evening.

To acknowledge God in all your ways means to bring Him into every corner of your life. To ask for His guidance not just when you're at a crossroads... but when you're walking down what seems like a straight and obvious road. Because sometimes the straight and obvious road is not God's road. And you won't know that unless you ask.

The Puritans practiced this kind of constant communion with God. They had a phrase for it: "living coram Deo" — living before the face of God. Every moment. Every decision. Every interaction. Conducted with an awareness that God is present, God is watching, and God has something to say about how you spend your next five minutes.

Command four: "Be not wise in thine own eyes: fear the Lord, and depart from evil."

This is the warning against self-sufficiency. Against the kind of pride that says, "I've got this figured out." Against the spiritual complacency that comes from thinking you've arrived, you've got enough knowledge, you've got enough experience, you don't need counsel or correction or the daily, desperate dependence on God that marks a truly wise person.

The fear of the Lord again. We come back to it because Solomon keeps coming back to it. It's the thread that runs through the entire book. And here it's paired with a practical command: "depart from evil." Not just avoid evil when it's convenient. Depart from it. Turn around. Walk the other direction. Put distance between yourself and anything that would draw you away from God.

And now... the promise. Verse 8: "It shall be health to thy navel, and marrow to thy bones."

Trusting God is good for you. Not just spiritually, but holistically. The person who trusts God with all their heart, who refuses to lean on their own understanding, who acknowledges God in every part of life, who fears the Lord and departs from evil... that person experiences a kind of wholeness that nothing else can provide. Health. Vitality. Strength in the deepest parts of their being.

This is not a promise of perfect physical health — the Bible never makes that promise. But it is a promise that the life of trust is a life of flourishing. A life that works the way God designed it to work. A life where the soul is at rest even when the circumstances are not.

Let me continue through the chapter. Verses 9 and 10:

"Honour the Lord with thy substance, and with the firstfruits of all thine increase: So shall thy barns be filled with plenty, and thy presses shall burst out with new wine."

Generosity. Trusting God with your money is one of the most concrete, practical expressions of trust there is. Because money represents security. Options. Control. And when you give — when you give the first portion, not the leftovers — you are saying to God, "I trust You more than I trust this."

And then, one of the most tender passages in Proverbs. Verses 11 and 12:

"My son, despise not the chastening of the Lord; neither be weary of his correction: For whom the Lord loveth he correcteth; even as a father the son in whom he delighteth."

This is crucial. Because trusting God doesn't mean life will be painless. It means that even the pain has a purpose. Even the discipline is an expression of love. The father who corrects his child is not being cruel... he is being kind. He sees what the child cannot see. He knows what the child does not know. And his correction — though it hurts in the moment — is aimed at the child's good.

Spurgeon preached on this theme many times, particularly during his seasons of suffering. He knew depression. He knew physical pain. He knew the grief of harsh criticism and public misunderstanding. And he testified, again and again, that God's hand of discipline had been one of the greatest means of grace in his life. Not because he enjoyed it — no one enjoys discipline in the moment. But because it drew him closer to God. It deepened his dependence. It refined his character. It made him more useful in the Master's hand.

The chapter continues with a magnificent celebration of wisdom in verses 13 through 20. Let me read verses 13 through 18:

"Happy is the man that findeth wisdom, and the man that getteth understanding. For the merchandise of it is better than the merchandise of silver, and the gain thereof than fine gold. She is more precious than rubies: and all the things thou canst desire are not to be compared unto her. Length of days is in her right hand; and in her left hand riches and honour. Her ways are ways of pleasantness, and all her paths are peace. She is a tree of life to them that lay hold upon her: and happy is every one that retaineth her."

A tree of life. Do you hear the echo of Eden? Wisdom is a tree of life — connecting us back to the garden, back to the paradise that was lost, back to the intimate fellowship with God that humanity was created for. To lay hold of wisdom is to reach for the life God always intended for us.

And then the chapter closes with practical wisdom for daily relationships. Verses 27 through 30:

"Withhold not good from them to whom it is due, when it is in the power of thine hand to do it. Say not unto thy neighbour, Go, and come again, and to morrow I will give; when thou hast it by thee. Devise not evil against thy neighbour, seeing he dwelleth securely by thee. Strive not with a man without cause, if he have done thee no harm."

Do good when you can. Don't delay kindness. Don't scheme against those who trust you. Don't pick fights needlessly. This is wisdom with work clothes on. This is trust in the Lord expressed through how you treat the person next door.

And the final verse — verse 35:

"The wise shall inherit glory: but shame shall be the promotion of fools."

Two paths. Two outcomes. The same choice we face every day: will I trust in the Lord with all my heart... or will I lean on my own understanding?

Friend, I know that some of you are in the middle of a situation right now where trusting God feels impossibly hard. The circumstances don't make sense. The path forward is unclear. The temptation to take control — to figure it out yourself, to force a solution — is overwhelming.

I want to say to you, as gently and as firmly as I can: trust Him. Lean your full weight on Him. He has not forgotten you. He has not lost track of your situation. He is working — right now, even now — in ways you cannot see. And He will direct your paths.

Not your preferred paths, necessarily. Not the paths you would have chosen. But His paths. And His paths, Solomon tells us, are always — always — paths of pleasantness and peace.

Let me close with a prayer.

Lord, we bring You our fear. We bring You our need to control. We bring You the tight grip we hold on our own understanding, and we ask You to pry our fingers open — gently, Lord, but firmly. Teach us to trust You. Really trust You. Not just with our words, but with our whole hearts. Not just on Sundays, but in all our ways. And as we lean on You today — as we let go of ourselves and fall into Your faithfulness — direct our paths. Lead us in Your way. And let us find, at the end of this day, that Your road was better than anything we could have planned for ourselves. Through Christ our Lord. Amen.

Rest well tonight, friend. He's got you.
