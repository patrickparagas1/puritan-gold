PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Pastoral Encouragement, Day 17
"The Ministry of Presence"
Puritan Counsel on Fatherhood and Presence

---

Good evening. If you are weary tonight, you are in good company. The saints of old knew weariness well. And yet they also knew the God who gives strength to the faint. Let us draw near to Him together.

--- Scripture ---

Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Deuteronomy 6:6-7:

"And these words, which I command thee this day, shall be in thine heart: And thou shalt teach them diligently unto thy children, and shalt talk of them when thou sittest in thine house, and when thou walkest by the way, and when thou liest down, and when thou risest up."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.

--- Pastoral Word ---

The Shema's command in Deuteronomy 6 is not merely about teaching content; it is about the context in which that teaching occurs. Notice the four settings: sitting in the house, walking by the way, lying down, and rising up. These are not formal classroom settings. They are the everyday moments of life lived together. The implication is clear: spiritual formation happens not primarily in scheduled lessons but in the constant presence of a godly father who weaves the truth of God into the fabric of ordinary life.

Richard Baxter writes in 'A Christian Directory' that the father who is absent from his children, even if he provides materially, has failed in his primary duty. Baxter does not deny the importance of provision — he condemns laziness and irresponsibility in the strongest terms. But he insists that provision without presence is a hollow gift. The child who has food on the table but no father at the table has been given the lesser thing and denied the greater.

Baxter identifies several ways that fathers are absent even when they are physically present. The first is mental absence: the father who is in the room but absorbed in his own thoughts, his work, or his devices. His body occupies the space, but his attention is elsewhere. The child who speaks to a distracted father learns that he is not important enough to warrant full attention.

The second is emotional absence: the father who is physically present but emotionally detached. He goes through the motions of family life without genuine engagement. He does not know his children's fears, their joys, their struggles, or their questions. He provides structure but not warmth, rules but not relationship.

The third is spiritual absence: the father who does not bring God into the daily life of the family. He may take his children to church on Sundays, but between Sundays there is no prayer, no Scripture, no spiritual conversation. The children learn that God is relevant for one day a week but irrelevant for the other six.

Baxter's counsel for overcoming these forms of absence is both simple and costly: be there. Be fully present. Put down your work when your child speaks to you. Look them in the eye. Listen to their stories, even when they seem trivial to you. Enter their world. Play their games. Answer their questions. Not because these activities are inherently important, but because your child is inherently important, and your presence communicates that truth more powerfully than any words.

Thomas Watson adds that the presence of a godly father is one of God's primary means of grace in a child's life. Watson observes that children learn by imitation more than instruction. A father who reads his Bible will have children who are curious about the Bible. A father who prays will have children who understand that prayer is normal. A father who repents will have children who know that confession is safe.

The Puritan Cotton Mather, in his diary, records specific ways he sought to be present with his children: taking walks with individual children to engage them in personal conversation, telling them stories from Scripture and history during meals, praying with each child individually at bedtime, and involving them in his work so they could see his faith lived out in practical contexts.

Mather also records his failures and his grief over them. He was a busy man — pastor, author, civic leader — and the demands on his time were immense. He confesses seasons of neglect, times when ministry consumed what should have been given to his children. His honesty is both convicting and comforting: even the Puritans struggled with the tension between calling and family.

The challenge is this: your children are growing up. The years you have with them are finite and fleeting. The ministry of presence cannot be deferred. You cannot be present tomorrow for the moment that is passing today. The question is not whether you are busy — every man is busy. The question is whether you are present where it matters most.

--- Reflection ---

Here are some questions to carry with you into the coming days.

Question 1: Am I truly present with my children — mentally, emotionally, and spiritually — or am I merely occupying the same space?

Question 2: What would my children say is the quality of my attention when I am with them?

Question 3: What is one specific change I can make this week to be more fully present in my children's lives?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.

--- Closing Prayer ---

Father, forgive me for the presence I have withheld from my children. Help me to see that my attention is one of the greatest gifts I can give them. Teach me to be fully present — to listen, to engage, to teach, and to love — in the everyday moments that shape their souls. Amen.

Rest in His grace tonight. Tomorrow is a new day of growth.
