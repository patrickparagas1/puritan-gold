PURITAN GOLD -- Study Devotional, Day 7
Series: Romans
"Abraham Believed God"
The father of faith justified apart from works and circumcision

---

Good evening. Whether this is your first time joining our study series or you have been with us from the beginning, you are welcome. God's Word is inexhaustible, and there is always more to discover.

We are studying the book of Romans together. Tonight's lesson is titled "Abraham Believed God." Each session builds on what came before, so if you have been following along, you will see how tonight's passage connects to the larger argument of the book.

--- Scripture Reading ---

Let us read our passage for tonight. It comes from Romans 4:1-12:

"What shall we say then that Abraham our father, as pertaining to the flesh, hath found? For if Abraham were justified by works, he hath whereof to glory; but not before God. For what saith the scripture? Abraham believed God, and it was counted unto him for righteousness. Now to him that worketh is the reward not reckoned of grace, but of debt. But to him that worketh not, but believeth on him that justifieth the ungodly, his faith is counted for righteousness. Even as David also describeth the blessedness of the man, unto whom God imputeth righteousness without works, Saying, Blessed are they whose iniquities are forgiven, whose sins are covered. Blessed is the man to whom the Lord will not impute sin... And he received the sign of circumcision, a seal of the righteousness of the faith which he had yet being uncircumcised: that he might be the father of all them that believe, though they be not circumcised; that righteousness might be imputed unto them also."

Read that passage again to yourself. Mark any words or phrases that stand out. The Puritans read with a pen in hand, always ready to note what God was showing them.

--- Key Verse ---

The Puritans called the key verse of a passage its 'doctrine text.' Tonight, ours is this:

"For what saith the scripture? Abraham believed God, and it was counted unto him for righteousness." -- Romans 4:3

This verse is the lens through which we will examine the rest of tonight's passage. Keep it in the front of your mind as we proceed.

--- Exposition ---

The text is before us. Let us dig into its riches.

Thomas Goodwin offers valuable commentary on this passage. The Puritans were expositors of the highest order, and their insights remain remarkably fresh.

Thomas Goodwin regarded Romans 4 as the definitive exposition of the doctrine of imputed righteousness, and he returned to this chapter repeatedly in his theological writings. Goodwin stressed that Paul's choice of Abraham as the test case for justification by faith was theologically strategic. If the father of the Jewish nation, the friend of God, the patriarch of patriarchs, was not justified by works, then no one can be. Abraham was the strongest possible case for works-righteousness, and Paul demolishes it.

Goodwin focused with great precision on the mechanics of imputation described in verses 3-5. He argued that 'counted' (Greek: logizomai) is a bookkeeping term meaning to credit to one's account. When Abraham believed God, righteousness was deposited in his ledger — not because his faith was itself righteous, but because faith was the instrument by which he received God's gift. Goodwin was careful to distinguish between the ground of justification (Christ's righteousness) and the instrument of justification (faith). Faith is not a work that earns righteousness; it is an empty hand that receives it.

Goodwin also drew attention to the explosive phrase in verse 5: God 'justifieth the ungodly.' This is, by any human standard, a scandalous statement. Proverbs 17:15 declares, 'He that justifieth the wicked, and he that condemneth the just, even they both are abomination to the LORD.' Yet God justifies the ungodly — and He does so righteously, because Christ's substitutionary death has satisfied every demand of the law. Goodwin saw in this the inexhaustible marvel of the gospel: God does not wait for sinners to become righteous before declaring them righteous. He declares the ungodly righteous, and then, by the power of the Spirit, proceeds to make them what He has declared them to be. Justification precedes sanctification; the verdict precedes the transformation.

Having established the doctrine of justification by faith in Romans 3:21-31, Paul now provides the Old Testament proof. His chosen case study is Abraham, the patriarch of the Jewish nation and the most revered figure in all of Judaism. If Paul can demonstrate that Abraham was justified by faith and not by works, his argument is unassailable.

The question in verse 1 is pointed: 'What shall we say then that Abraham our father, as pertaining to the flesh, hath found?' What was Abraham's experience? How did he come into right standing with God? The Jewish position was clear: Abraham was justified by his obedience. The book of Jubilees, a Jewish writing from the intertestamental period, celebrated Abraham as one who kept the whole law even before it was given. James 2:21 asks, 'Was not Abraham our father justified by works, when he had offered Isaac his son upon the altar?' But Paul and James are addressing different questions. James combats dead faith that produces no works; Paul combats works-righteousness that claims merit before God.

Paul's answer is decisive in verse 2: 'For if Abraham were justified by works, he hath whereof to glory; but not before God.' Even if Abraham had accumulated a record of good works (which Paul is willing to concede for the sake of argument), this would not give him grounds for boasting before God. Human works, however impressive by earthly standards, cannot satisfy the infinite demands of divine justice.

Verse 3 settles the matter by appealing to Scripture: 'For what saith the scripture? Abraham believed God, and it was counted unto him for righteousness.' This is Genesis 15:6, and its context is crucial. God had just promised the childless, elderly Abraham that his descendants would be as numerous as the stars. Abraham believed this impossible promise, and God credited righteousness to his account. Note carefully: Abraham was not circumcised until Genesis 17, years later. He had not yet offered Isaac (Genesis 22). He had not yet kept the Mosaic law (which would not be given for centuries). His righteousness was not earned but given — credited to him on the basis of faith alone.

In verses 4-5, Paul draws out the logic with razor precision. 'Now to him that worketh is the reward not reckoned of grace, but of debt.' If you earn something, you are owed it. It is a wage, not a gift. But salvation is not a wage; it is a gift. 'But to him that worketh not, but believeth on him that justifieth the ungodly, his faith is counted for righteousness.' The justified person is described as one who 'worketh not' — who brings no works as the basis of acceptance — and who 'believeth on him that justifieth the ungodly.' The object of saving faith is not a distant deity but the specific God who justifies the ungodly. This is the God of the gospel: not the God who rewards the righteous (that would be mere justice) but the God who declares the unrighteous righteous (that is grace).

Paul then calls a second witness: King David. In verses 6-8, he quotes Psalm 32:1-2: 'Blessed are they whose iniquities are forgiven, whose sins are covered. Blessed is the man to whom the Lord will not impute sin.' David knew this blessedness from personal experience. After his sin with Bathsheba and the murder of Uriah, David was a broken man. He could not earn his way back to God. All he could do was cry out, 'Have mercy upon me, O God, according to thy lovingkindness' (Psalm 51:1). And God forgave him — not because David deserved it, but because God is gracious. The non-imputation of sin is the negative side of what the imputation of righteousness is positively. God both forgives our sin and credits us with Christ's righteousness.

In verses 9-12, Paul addresses the crucial question of timing. Was Abraham's justification connected to circumcision? The answer is emphatic: no. Abraham was declared righteous in Genesis 15, but he was not circumcised until Genesis 17 — a gap of at least fourteen years. Circumcision was 'a seal of the righteousness of the faith which he had yet being uncircumcised.' The sign came after the reality, confirming what was already true. This has enormous implications. It means that Abraham is the father not only of circumcised Jews who believe but of uncircumcised Gentiles who believe. The door of justification by faith swings wide open to the entire human race.

This is the beauty of God's redemptive design. He justified Abraham before circumcision so that Abraham would be the father of all believers — Jew and Gentile alike. The gospel is not a Jewish message expanded to include Gentiles; it is a universal message that happened to be entrusted first to the Jews. From the beginning, God's plan was to bless all nations through Abraham's seed (Genesis 12:3), and that seed is Christ (Galatians 3:16).

The doctrine of imputation that Paul establishes here is foundational to the entire Christian gospel. Imputation means that God credits to our account a righteousness that is not our own. Just as our sins were imputed to Christ on the cross (He bore what was not His), so His righteousness is imputed to us through faith (we receive what is not ours). This is the great exchange that Luther celebrated: Christ takes our sin, we receive His righteousness. It is not a legal fiction but a legal reality, grounded in the representative work of Christ as the second Adam (which Paul will develop in Romans 5).

--- Application ---

The true test of understanding is application. If we have truly grasped tonight's passage, it will change the way we live tomorrow.

The justification of Abraham by faith has direct and powerful implications for your daily life.

First, believe the impossible promises of God. Abraham was justified when he believed God's promise of descendants as numerous as the stars — a promise that seemed biologically impossible given his age. What promises of God do you find hard to believe? That He works all things together for good (Romans 8:28)? That He will supply all your needs (Philippians 4:19)? That He will never leave you or forsake you (Hebrews 13:5)? Faith is not pretending difficulties do not exist; it is trusting God's word over your circumstances. Choose one promise that is hard for you to believe right now and make it the focus of your prayers this week.

Second, stop trying to earn God's favor. The person who is justified is described as one who 'worketh not' in the sense of offering works as payment for acceptance. This does not mean Christians should be lazy or disobedient; it means we should stop treating obedience as the currency by which we purchase God's approval. You are accepted in the Beloved (Ephesians 1:6). Your standing is secure. Obey from gratitude, not from fear that God will reject you if your performance slips.

Third, rest in the double blessing of imputation. God both forgives your sin (will not impute sin to you) and credits you with Christ's righteousness (imputes righteousness to you). You are not merely forgiven and left in a neutral state; you are clothed in the perfect righteousness of Christ. When you pray, you come before God not in your own tattered garments but in the spotless robe of Christ's obedience. Let that truth transform your prayer life from timid petition to bold, confident access.

Fourth, see your fellow believers through the lens of grace. If God justifies the ungodly, then every Christian you know was once ungodly and is now clothed in Christ's righteousness. This should destroy all spiritual elitism and produce a deep humility and compassion in the fellowship of the church.

Write down one concrete action you will take this week in response to tonight's study. Do not let this truth remain theoretical. The Puritans measured the success of a sermon not by how much the hearer learned but by how much the hearer changed.

--- Closing ---

Father of Abraham, we believe Your promises even when they seem impossible. Thank You for the doctrine of imputation — that You credit us with a righteousness not our own, the very righteousness of Your Son. Forgive us for trying to earn what You freely give. Help us to rest in the finished work of Christ, to stop working for acceptance and start living from acceptance. Make us like Abraham — people who take You at Your word against all odds. To the glory of Your grace alone. Amen.

May the Lord bless your continued study of His Word. Until next time.
