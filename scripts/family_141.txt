PURITAN GOLD -- Family Devotional, Day 10
"Contentment vs. the World"
Pilgrim Families — Week 2, Day 3

---

Welcome, family. Whether you are gathered on the couch, around the dinner table, or riding in the car, God is here with you. Let us turn our attention to Him and see what treasure He has stored up for us tonight.

Let us bow our heads. Dear Father in heaven, what a privilege it is to gather as a family in Your presence. We need Your wisdom tonight. We need Your guidance. Open our ears to hear, our minds to understand, and our hearts to receive. We pray in the precious name of Jesus. Amen.

Let us turn together to 1 John 2:15-17; Matthew 6:19-21. Follow along in your Bible if you have one:

"Love not the world, neither the things that are in the world. If any man love the world, the love of the Father is not in him. For all that is in the world, the lust of the flesh, and the lust of the eyes, and the pride of life, is not of the Father, but is of the world. And the world passeth away, and the lust thereof: but he that doeth the will of God abideth for ever. Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth, where moth and rust doth corrupt, and where thieves break through and steal: But lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven, where neither moth nor rust doth corrupt, and where thieves do not break through nor steal: For where your treasure is, there will your heart be also."

That is 1 John 2:15-17; Matthew 6:19-21. Let those words settle in your heart for a moment before we continue.

--- Teaching ---

The greatest enemy of contentment is worldliness. The world constantly whispers that we need more, that we deserve more, that happiness is just one purchase away. But the Apostle John issues a stark command: "Love not the world, neither the things that are in the world." This is not a suggestion; it is an imperative. And the reason is devastating: "If any man love the world, the love of the Father is not in him."

John identifies three categories that encompass everything the world offers: the lust of the flesh (the craving for physical pleasure), the lust of the eyes (the craving for possessing what we see), and the pride of life (the craving for status and recognition). Every temptation, every advertisement, every worldly ambition falls into one of these three categories. And all three are enemies of contentment because they create endless desire that can never be satisfied.

Jesus cuts to the heart of the matter in Matthew 6:19-21: "Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth, where moth and rust doth corrupt, and where thieves break through and steal." Earthly treasures are temporary. Moths eat clothing. Rust corrodes metal. Thieves steal valuables. Nothing on earth is permanent. But heavenly treasures — acts of faith, deeds of love, growth in holiness — these last forever.

Then comes the key verse: "For where your treasure is, there will your heart be also." Your heart follows your treasure. If your treasure is a new phone, your heart is with that phone. If your treasure is a bigger house, your heart is consumed with that house. If your treasure is Christ and His kingdom, your heart is in heaven. Contentment, therefore, is ultimately a treasure issue. What do you value most?

Burroughs made a brilliant observation: "A little in the world will content a Christian for his passage, but all the world will not content a Christian for his portion." Think about that. A traveler on a journey needs very little — some food, some shelter, some clothing. They are not building a permanent home on the road; they are passing through. Christians are travelers. This world is not our home. We are pilgrims on the way to the Celestial City. A pilgrim needs enough for the journey, but a pilgrim's true treasure is at the destination.

But if a Christian tries to make this world their portion — their permanent possession, their ultimate satisfaction — then nothing will ever be enough. You could own everything on earth and still be empty, because the human soul was made for something bigger than anything this world can offer. Augustine said it well: "Thou hast made us for Thyself, O Lord, and our heart is restless until it finds its rest in Thee."

For families, this teaching is urgently practical. We live in a consumer culture that targets our children from a young age. Advertisements, social media, peer pressure — all conspire to make our children (and us) dissatisfied with what we have. The remedy is not merely saying no to things, but saying yes to something better. We must teach our children that the greatest treasure in the universe is knowing God through Jesus Christ.

John warns that "the world passeth away, and the lust thereof." Everything the world offers is temporary. The latest fashion will be outdated next year. The newest gadget will be obsolete within months. The social media post that got a hundred likes will be forgotten by tomorrow. But "he that doeth the will of God abideth for ever." The person who pursues God's will has something that lasts for all eternity.

As a family exercise tonight, evaluate your treasures. Not to feel guilty, but to refocus. Are there things you are pursuing that will not last? Are there ways you could invest more in eternal treasures — in relationships, in generosity, in knowing God's Word? The family that stores up heavenly treasure is the family that finds true contentment, because their hearts are fixed on something that can never be taken away.

--- Puritan Wisdom ---

For centuries, faithful Christians have reflected on passages like this one. Let us hear from a trusted voice of the past.

Jeremiah Burroughs, in his work "The Rare Jewel of Christian Contentment," wrote these words:

"A little in the world will content a Christian for his passage, but all the world will not content a Christian for his portion."

What Jeremiah Burroughs is reminding us is that the truths we have read tonight in 1 John 2:15-17; Matthew 6:19-21 are not new. They have been treasured by faithful Christians for centuries. And they are just as true and just as needed in our family today as they were hundreds of years ago. Let us take these words to heart and let them shape the way we live together as a family.

--- Family Discussion ---

Discussion time. These questions are for every member of the family, young and old alike.

Question 1: John lists three categories of worldly temptation: lust of the flesh, lust of the eyes, and pride of life. Can you give examples of each from our everyday life?

Question 2: What does Burroughs mean by the difference between a 'passage' and a 'portion'? How does seeing ourselves as pilgrims change our attitude toward things?

Question 3: Jesus says moths, rust, and thieves destroy earthly treasures. What are some 'treasures' your family has seen fade or break?

Question 4: What are some 'heavenly treasures' our family can store up together?

Take a few minutes to talk about these questions together. Let everyone share, and listen to one another with patience and love.

--- Memory Verse ---

Now, here is our memory verse for today. Let us say it together:

"For where your treasure is, there will your heart be also." -- Matthew 6:21

Try to say it out loud three times right now. Then, before bed tonight, see if you can say it from memory. Tomorrow morning, try again. The more we hide God's Word in our hearts, the more it will guide us when we need it most.

--- Family Activity ---

The Puritans believed that truth must be practiced, not just heard. Here is something you can do together:

Go through one room in your house together. Pick out items that are broken, outdated, or no longer used. Talk about how earthly things fade away. Then make a list of five 'heavenly treasures' your family wants to invest in — things like family worship, generosity, time with others, and knowing Scripture.

--- Closing ---

Let us bow our heads and pray. Dear Father in heaven, what a privilege it is to gather as a family around Your Word. We ask You to write these truths on our hearts. Bless our family, protect our home, and lead us in the path of righteousness for Your name's sake. In Christ we pray. Amen.

Until tomorrow, family. May the Lord bless you and keep you.
