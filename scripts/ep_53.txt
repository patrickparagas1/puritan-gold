PURITAN GOLD -- Study Devotional, Day 22
Series: Romans
"O the Depth!"
Israel's future restoration and the great doxology of sovereign wisdom


Good evening, and welcome to our study devotional from Puritan Gold. We are continuing our journey through one of the most important books in all of Scripture. The Puritans treasured careful, verse-by-verse study, and tonight we follow in their footsteps.

We are studying the book of Romans together. Tonight's lesson is titled "O the Depth!." Each session builds on what came before, so if you have been following along, you will see how tonight's passage connects to the larger argument of the book.


Let us read our passage for tonight. It comes from Romans 11:25-36:

"For I would not, brethren, that ye should be ignorant of this mystery, lest ye should be wise in your own conceits; that blindness in part is happened to Israel, until the fulness of the Gentiles be come in. And so all Israel shall be saved: as it is written, There shall come out of Sion the Deliverer, and shall turn away ungodliness from Jacob: For this is my covenant unto them, when I shall take away their sins... For God hath concluded them all in unbelief, that he might have mercy upon all. O the depth of the riches both of the wisdom and knowledge of God! how unsearchable are his judgments, and his ways past finding out! For who hath known the mind of the Lord? or who hath been his counsellor? Or who hath first given to him, and it shall be recompensed unto him again? For of him, and through him, and to him, are all things: to whom be glory for ever. Amen."

Read that passage again to yourself. Mark any words or phrases that stand out. The Puritans read with a pen in hand, always ready to note what God was showing them.


Let us focus on one verse that captures the heart of tonight's passage.

"O the depth of the riches both of the wisdom and knowledge of God! how unsearchable are his judgments, and his ways past finding out!" -- Romans 11:33

This verse is the lens through which we will examine the rest of tonight's passage. Keep it in the front of your mind as we proceed.


Now let us move into the exposition of this passage.

Thomas Goodwin offers valuable commentary on this passage. The Puritans were expositors of the highest order, and their insights remain remarkably fresh.

Thomas Goodwin was one of the Puritan theologians who held a strong hope for the future conversion of Israel, and Romans 11:25-36 was central to his eschatological vision. Goodwin argued that 'all Israel shall be saved' refers to a future, large-scale turning of the Jewish people to Christ, not merely the gradual salvation of individual Jews throughout history. He believed this would happen near the end of history and would be accompanied by extraordinary spiritual blessing for the entire world — which Paul hints at in verse 15: 'life from the dead.'

Goodwin saw in this passage the resolution of the great tension that runs through Romans 9-11. God's sovereignty in election (chapter 9), human responsibility in believing (chapter 10), and God's faithfulness to His covenant promises (chapter 11) all find their harmony in God's mysterious plan to use Israel's temporary rejection to bring in the Gentiles and then to use Gentile faith to provoke Israel to return. The result is mercy for all — Jew and Gentile alike — so that God alone receives the glory.

Goodwin reserved his most exalted language for the doxology of verses 33-36. He called these verses the highest point of worship in all of Scripture, the moment when Paul's theological argument dissolves into pure adoration. Having traced God's plan from the fall of Adam through the justification of believers to the future restoration of Israel, Paul can do nothing but cry out in wonder: 'O the depth!' Goodwin argued that this doxology is the proper response to every deep doctrine — not argument, not complaint, not confusion, but worship. When we reach the limits of our understanding, we have not reached the limits of God's wisdom. We have merely arrived at the point where knowledge must yield to adoration.

Romans 11:25-36 brings the great theological argument of chapters 9-11 to its breathtaking conclusion. Paul reveals a mystery, declares a promise, and then erupts into the most magnificent doxology in all of Scripture.

Verse 25 introduces the mystery: 'For I would not, brethren, that ye should be ignorant of this mystery, lest ye should be wise in your own conceits; that blindness in part is happened to Israel, until the fulness of the Gentiles be come in.' A 'mystery' in Paul's usage is not a puzzle to be solved but a truth previously hidden that is now revealed. The mystery has three components. First, Israel's blindness is partial — 'in part,' not total. A remnant believes. Second, it is temporary — it lasts 'until' a certain event. Third, it will end when 'the fulness of the Gentiles be come in' — when the full number of elect Gentiles has been gathered.

Verse 26 makes the astonishing declaration: 'And so all Israel shall be saved.' The interpretation of this phrase has been debated for centuries. Some read 'all Israel' as the church (spiritual Israel). Others read it as every individual Jew. The most natural reading, in context, is that it refers to the nation of Israel as a whole — not every individual without exception but Israel as a corporate entity. Just as 'all Israel' in the Old Testament could refer to the nation collectively (1 Samuel 7:5; 1 Kings 12:1), so here it refers to a future, large-scale turning of the Jewish people to their Messiah.

Paul supports this from Isaiah 59:20-21 and 27:9: 'There shall come out of Sion the Deliverer, and shall turn away ungodliness from Jacob: For this is my covenant unto them, when I shall take away their sins.' This is a covenant promise — and God does not break His covenants. The Deliverer who came from Zion the first time (in the incarnation) will complete His work by turning ungodliness from Jacob.

Verses 28-29 explain the paradox of Israel's current position: 'As concerning the gospel, they are enemies for your sakes: but as touching the election, they are beloved for the fathers' sakes. For the gifts and calling of God are without repentance.' Israel is currently opposing the gospel — and this opposition has served God's purpose of bringing salvation to the Gentiles. But God has not revoked His election of Israel. His gifts and calling are irrevocable. What He promised to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, He will fulfill.

Verses 30-32 reveal the grand strategy: 'For as ye in times past have not believed God, yet have now obtained mercy through their unbelief: Even so have these also now not believed, that through your mercy they also may obtain mercy. For God hath concluded them all in unbelief, that he might have mercy upon all.' The logic is breathtaking. Gentiles were once unbelieving but obtained mercy through Israel's unbelief. Now Israel is unbelieving, but will obtain mercy through the mercy shown to the Gentiles. God has imprisoned all in unbelief so that He might show mercy to all. No one can claim merit. Everyone receives mercy. God alone is glorified.

This brings Paul to the great doxology of verses 33-36, which is the climax not just of chapters 9-11 but of the entire doctrinal section of Romans (chapters 1-11). 'O the depth of the riches both of the wisdom and knowledge of God!' The 'depth' suggests fathomless profundity — we have plunged deep into God's purposes but have not reached the bottom. His wisdom (His plan) and knowledge (His understanding) are inexhaustibly rich.

'How unsearchable are his judgments, and his ways past finding out!' We cannot trace God's ways to their ultimate source or follow them to their ultimate destination. We see glimpses, fragments, partial reflections — but the whole is beyond us. This is not frustrating; it is comforting. We are in the hands of a God whose wisdom infinitely exceeds our own.

Verse 34 quotes Isaiah 40:13: 'For who hath known the mind of the Lord? or who hath been his counsellor?' God did not consult anyone when He devised His plan of redemption. He did not ask for advice. His plan is entirely His own — original, independent, and perfect.

Verse 35 quotes Job 41:11: 'Or who hath first given to him, and it shall be recompensed unto him again?' No one has put God in their debt. No one has given God something that obligates Him to repay. Grace is free precisely because no one has a prior claim on God's favor.

Verse 36 concludes with the ultimate statement of God's centrality: 'For of him, and through him, and to him, are all things: to whom be glory for ever. Amen.' All things originate in God (of him), all things are sustained by God (through him), and all things exist for God (to him). He is the source, the means, and the goal of everything that exists. The only appropriate response is: 'to whom be glory for ever. Amen.'

This doxology is the destination toward which all theology should lead. Paul has spent eleven chapters arguing, explaining, proving, defending — and now he worships. The greatest theologian becomes the greatest worshiper. When doctrine does not lead to doxology, it has failed its purpose. When the study of God does not produce the worship of God, something has gone terribly wrong.


Now let us consider: how does this truth apply to our lives? The Puritans never separated doctrine from practice. Every truth they studied was immediately applied.

Romans 11:25-36 calls us to worship the God whose ways are past finding out. Here are specific applications.

First, let theology lead to doxology. The proper response to deep doctrine is not merely intellectual satisfaction but heartfelt worship. After studying Romans 1-11, do not merely close your Bible and go about your business. Fall on your knees and cry with Paul, 'O the depth!' Let every theological insight you gain become fuel for worship. If your study of God does not make you worship God, you are studying wrongly.

Second, humble yourself before divine mystery. 'How unsearchable are his judgments, and his ways past finding out!' You will not understand everything God does. You will encounter paradoxes, tensions, and apparent contradictions that your finite mind cannot resolve. That is okay. You are not God. Be content to know in part (1 Corinthians 13:12) and trust the One who knows in full.

Third, pray for Israel's restoration. If 'all Israel shall be saved' is a future promise, then it is a promise worth praying for. Pray that God would hasten the day when the blindness is lifted, when the Deliverer turns ungodliness from Jacob, when the natural branches are grafted back into their own olive tree. This is not merely a political prayer but a spiritual one — for the salvation of souls and the glory of God.

Fourth, live for God's glory. 'For of him, and through him, and to him, are all things.' Your life exists for God's glory. Every decision, every relationship, every use of time and money and talent should be directed toward this one supreme purpose: that God might be glorified. Ask yourself regularly: is this choice bringing glory to God? If not, change course. To Him be glory forever. Amen.

Write down one concrete action you will take this week in response to tonight's study. Do not let this truth remain theoretical. The Puritans measured the success of a sermon not by how much the hearer learned but by how much the hearer changed.


O the depth of the riches both of the wisdom and knowledge of God! How unsearchable are Your judgments, and Your ways past finding out! We bow before mysteries we cannot comprehend and worship a God we cannot fully fathom. Thank You for Your irrevocable promises to Israel. Thank You for Your mercy to the Gentiles. Thank You that You have concluded us all in unbelief so that You might have mercy upon all. Of You, and through You, and to You are all things. To You be glory forever. Amen and amen.

Until next time, keep studying. The Word of God will never run dry.
