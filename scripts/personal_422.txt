PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Guided Puritan Reading, Day 22
"The Danger of Unmortified Sin"
John Owen — Mortification of Sin, Chapters 12-13


Welcome, friend. Tonight we take up a spiritual discipline that was at the very heart of the Puritan life: the slow, careful, prayerful reading of a great Christian text. We are not skimming. We are not rushing. We are walking through these pages the way the Puritans did, with a Bible in one hand and a prayer in our hearts.

Tonight we draw from the work of John Owen, specifically from "The Mortification of Sin." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Hebrews 3:13:

"But exhort one another daily, while it is called To day; lest any of you be hardened through the deceitfulness of sin."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


As Owen approaches the conclusion of his treatise, he sounds the alarm about the consequences of neglecting mortification. These chapters are among the most sobering in the entire work, intended not to discourage but to awaken the complacent soul.

Owen's central argument in these chapters is that unmortified sin has a hardening effect on the conscience. The writer of Hebrews warns against being 'hardened through the deceitfulness of sin,' and Owen traces the mechanics of this hardening process.

The process begins with familiarity. A sin that is not mortified becomes familiar. The first time a man commits a particular sin, his conscience is sharp and painful. The second time, less so. The tenth time, he barely notices. The hundredth time, it feels normal. Owen writes that this process of familiarization is one of the most dangerous dynamics in the spiritual life. The man who has grown comfortable with his sin has not overcome it; he has been overcome by it.

The next stage is rationalization. The familiar sin begins to construct arguments for its own legitimacy. 'This sin is not so bad.' 'Everyone struggles with this.' 'God understands my weakness.' 'I have tried to fight it and failed, so perhaps God does not expect me to conquer it.' Owen exposes each of these rationalizations as the voice of the flesh disguised as reason. The flesh is, as Owen repeatedly insists, 'deceitful above all things.' It is the most skilled advocate for its own preservation.

The next stage is the dulling of spiritual senses. Owen writes that the man who tolerates sin in one area will find his entire spiritual life diminished. Prayer becomes mechanical. The Word becomes dry. Worship becomes dutiful rather than delightful. Fellowship becomes shallow. The soul that once burned with love for Christ grows lukewarm. Owen traces this directly to unmortified sin: the Spirit is grieved, and a grieved Spirit does not withdraw His presence entirely (in the case of a true believer), but He does withdraw the sweetness of communion.

Owen's most alarming warning concerns the possibility of scandalous falls. He has seen it happen: men of great gifts and apparent godliness who fell into grievous public sin. In every case, Owen observes, the fall was preceded by a long season of neglected mortification. The great sin that shocked the community was not the beginning of the problem but its terrible fruit. The root had been growing undetected for months or years.

Owen draws an analogy to physical disease. A disease that is treated early is usually manageable. A disease that is ignored becomes dangerous. A disease that is denied until it reaches a critical stage may be fatal. So it is with sin. The man who deals with his sin at the stage of inclination is doing a small but important work. The man who waits until the sin has become habitual is fighting for his life.

But Owen does not leave his readers in despair. He reminds them of the sufficiency of grace. Even the man who has long neglected mortification can turn back. The promise of 1 John 1:9 stands: 'If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness.' The blood of Christ is not limited in its cleansing power. The Spirit is not exhausted by the believer's failures. There is always a way back, and the way back is always the same: confession, repentance, faith in Christ, and renewed dependence on the Spirit.

Owen's counsel for the man who has been negligent is to begin immediately. Do not wait for a more convenient season. Do not wait until you feel ready. Do not wait until you have gathered enough resolve. Begin today. Name the sin. Confess it. Forsake it. Ask for the Spirit's help. And trust that the God who began a good work in you is able and willing to carry it on to completion.

The urgency of Owen's message is captured in one of his most memorable statements: 'Do you mortify? Do you make it your daily work? Be always at it whilst you live; cease not a day from this work; be killing sin or it will be killing you.'


Let us pause and reflect. Consider these questions in the quiet of your heart.

Question 1: Is there a sin in my life that has progressed through Owen's stages — from familiarity to rationalization to dulled spiritual senses?

Question 2: How has neglected mortification affected my prayer life, my love for God's Word, and my joy in worship?

Question 3: Am I willing to begin the work of mortification today, without waiting for a more convenient time?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Father, I hear Owen's warning and I am sobered. I do not want to be hardened by the deceitfulness of sin. Soften my heart today. Sharpen my conscience. Restore the sweetness of communion that sin has stolen. I begin again today, trusting not in my resolve but in Your grace. Amen.

Goodnight, friend. Walk with God tomorrow.
