SEGMENT 1: MORNING DEVOTION
----------------------------------------

Welcome to today's lesson. The Puritans were some of the most learned men and women of their age, and yet they counted all their learning as nothing compared to knowing Christ. Let us follow their example today.

God of all knowledge, we begin with You. Without Your light, even the brightest minds walk in darkness. Shine Your truth into our studies today. Help us to love learning and to learn in love. For the glory of Your name. Amen.

Let us read a passage slowly, letting each word settle into our minds.

Our Scripture for today is Acts 2:37-41:

"Now when they heard this, they were pricked in their heart, and said unto Peter and to the rest of the apostles, Men and brethren, what shall we do? Then Peter said unto them, Repent, and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ for the remission of sins, and ye shall receive the gift of the Holy Ghost. For the promise is unto you, and to your children, and to all that are afar off, even as many as the Lord our God shall call. And with many other words did he testify and exhort, saying, Save yourselves from this untoward generation. Then they that gladly received his word were baptized: and the same day there were added unto them about three thousand souls."

Consider this morning the miracle of your own conversion. There was a time when you were dead in trespasses and sins, living in the City of Destruction, unaware of your danger. But God, who is rich in mercy, awakened your soul and opened your eyes to see your need. He pricked your heart with conviction and drew you to the cross, where your burden of sin fell away forever. If this has been your experience, give thanks. If you are still carrying that burden, look to the cross today — for there, and only there, is the weight of sin removed. God's invitation still stands: 'Repent, and believe the gospel.' Today is the day of salvation.

As we prepare for today's lesson, remember that everything we study flows from this one fountain: the Word of God. Our lesson today is part of The Pilgrim's Journey, and it is titled: Starting the Journey — Conviction & Conversion. Let us proceed with minds engaged and hearts open.

SEGMENT 2: MAIN LESSON PART 1 -- BIBLICAL FOUNDATION
----------------------------------------

Let us proceed to the main body of today's teaching.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes in Acts 2:37-41.

Every true Christian journey begins with conviction and conversion — the moment when a sinner is awakened to the reality of their lost condition and turns in faith to Christ for salvation. Acts 2:37-41 records one of the most powerful examples of mass conviction and conversion in all of Scripture: the day of Pentecost, when Peter's sermon cut to the heart of his hearers and three thousand souls were saved.

The passage begins with the response of the crowd to Peter's preaching: "Now when they heard this, they were pricked in their heart." The word "pricked" translates the Greek word "katanusso," which means to pierce deeply, to stab, to cause sharp pain. This was not a mild emotional response but a deep, piercing conviction of sin. Peter had just proclaimed that Jesus of Nazareth — whom they had crucified — was both Lord and Christ (Acts 2:36). The full weight of their guilt crashed upon them like a wave. They had rejected their Messiah. They had murdered the Son of God. They had sinned against infinite love and infinite holiness. The Holy Spirit drove this truth deep into their consciences, and they were undone.

Their response was the cry of every genuinely convicted sinner: "Men and brethren, what shall we do?" This question reveals several important things about true conviction. First, it is personal — "what shall we do?" They did not ask an abstract theological question but a desperately personal one. Second, it is urgent — they needed an answer immediately, not next week or next year. Third, it is humble — they addressed Peter and the apostles as those who had the answer they lacked. True conviction strips away pride and self-sufficiency and produces a willingness to hear and obey the word of God.

Peter's answer is the essence of the gospel: "Repent, and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ for the remission of sins, and ye shall receive the gift of the Holy Ghost." Repentance is the first requirement — a complete turning away from sin and turning toward God. The Greek word "metanoia" means a change of mind that results in a change of direction. It is not merely feeling sorry for sin but forsaking sin. Baptism is the outward expression of the inward change — a public identification with Christ in His death, burial, and resurrection. And the promise is twofold: remission (forgiveness) of sins and the gift of the Holy Spirit.

The passage in John 3:3-7 takes us deeper into the nature of conversion. When Nicodemus, a ruler of the Jews, came to Jesus by night, our Lord told him, "Except a man be born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God." Nicodemus was confused: "How can a man be born when he is old? can he enter the second time into his mother's womb, and be born?" Jesus answered, "Except a man be born of water and of the Spirit, he cannot enter into the kingdom of God. That which is born of the flesh is flesh; and that which is born of the Spirit is spirit. Marvel not that I said unto thee, Ye must be born again." The new birth is not a human achievement but a divine miracle. Just as a baby does not cause its own birth, a sinner does not cause his own regeneration. The new birth is the sovereign work of the Holy Spirit, who gives spiritual life to those who are spiritually dead.

Ezekiel 36:26 provides the Old Testament foundation for understanding the new birth: "A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you: and I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh, and I will give you an heart of flesh." This is God's promise of regeneration — He will replace the hard, unresponsive heart of the sinner with a soft, responsive heart that loves God and desires to obey Him. Notice that this is entirely God's work: "I will give... I will put... I will take away... I will give." The sinner contributes nothing to his own new birth except the sin from which he needs to be saved.

The apostle Paul confirms this in 2 Corinthians 5:17: "Therefore if any man be in Christ, he is a new creature: old things are passed away; behold, all things are become new." Conversion is not mere reformation — an improvement of the old nature — but regeneration — the creation of an entirely new nature. The old self, with its love of sin and hostility toward God, passes away. A new self, with a love for God and a hunger for righteousness, comes into being. This is the most radical change that can happen to a human being, and it happens in an instant by the power of the Holy Spirit.

The marks of true conversion, as seen in Acts 2:41-47, include gladly receiving God's Word, being baptized, continuing in the apostles' doctrine and fellowship, breaking bread, prayer, a sense of reverential awe, generosity, and joyful worship. Conversion is not a momentary experience that fades over time but the beginning of a whole new life — a life characterized by growth in grace, fellowship with other believers, and an ever-deepening knowledge of Christ.

The urgency of conversion cannot be overstated. Peter exhorted his hearers to "save yourselves from this untoward generation." This was not a call to self-salvation but a call to respond to the gospel while the opportunity was present. The same urgency echoes throughout Scripture: "Today if ye will hear his voice, harden not your hearts" (Hebrews 3:15). "Behold, now is the accepted time; behold, now is the day of salvation" (2 Corinthians 6:2). Conversion is not something to be postponed or delayed. The soul that hears the gospel and is convicted by the Spirit must respond now, for tomorrow is promised to no one.

This biblical foundation is essential. Without it, everything else we study today would be like a house built on sand. But with it, we stand on solid rock.

SEGMENT 3: MAIN LESSON PART 2 -- PURITAN TEACHING
----------------------------------------

Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. John Bunyan wrote extensively on this subject in "The Pilgrim's Progress." The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life.

John Bunyan (1628-1688) is perhaps the most beloved of all Puritan writers, and his masterpiece "The Pilgrim's Progress" has been translated into more languages than any book except the Bible. Bunyan wrote from deep personal experience — he was a tinker (a mender of pots and pans) from Bedford, England, who experienced a long and agonizing period of spiritual conviction before finding peace in Christ. His autobiography, "Grace Abounding to the Chief of Sinners," records years of spiritual turmoil, doubt, and despair before the light of the gospel finally broke through. This personal experience gave Bunyan an unparalleled ability to describe the inner workings of conviction and conversion.

In "The Pilgrim's Progress," the journey begins with a man named Christian standing in the City of Destruction, reading a book (the Bible) and crying out, "What shall I do to be saved?" This opening scene parallels the cry of the crowd at Pentecost: "Men and brethren, what shall we do?" Bunyan understood that every genuine spiritual journey begins with a sense of danger — an awakening to the reality that one is lost, condemned, and heading for destruction. Christian's burden, which he carries on his back throughout the early stages of his journey, represents the weight of sin and guilt that presses upon the conscience of every awakened sinner.

Bunyan depicted the process of conviction with remarkable psychological accuracy. Christian does not simply decide one day that he would like to be religious. He is driven by terror and desperation. He sees in the book that the city where he lives is destined for destruction, and he cannot rest until he finds a way of escape. His family thinks he is mad. His neighbors mock him. But the conviction of the Spirit is stronger than all human opposition, and Christian flees from the city, putting his fingers in his ears and crying, "Life! Life! Eternal life!" This portrayal captures the biblical truth that conviction of sin is not a pleasant experience but a painful and desperate one — yet it is absolutely necessary, for without it, there can be no genuine conversion.

The character Evangelist directs Christian to the Wicket Gate (which represents Christ, the door of salvation — John 10:9). Along the way, Christian must pass through the Slough of Despond — a bog that represents the doubts, fears, and discouragements that often assail those who are seeking salvation. Bunyan drew this image from his own experience. He had spent years sinking in the slough of spiritual despair, wondering whether his sins were too great to be forgiven, whether he had committed the unpardonable sin, whether God's promises truly applied to him. Many seekers have had the same experience. The path to conversion is not always smooth; it often leads through valleys of doubt and anguish before reaching the solid ground of faith.

When Christian finally reaches the cross, Bunyan describes the moment of conversion with simple, powerful beauty. As Christian gazes upon the cross, the burden on his back — the weight of his sin — comes loose and rolls away, tumbling down the hill and into an open sepulchre, where it is seen no more. Christian is filled with joy and wonder, and he cries out, "He hath given me rest by His sorrow, and life by His death!" This scene perfectly illustrates the biblical truth that conversion is the removal of guilt through the finished work of Christ. The sinner does not earn forgiveness by carrying his burden far enough or suffering long enough. Forgiveness comes solely through looking to Christ and His cross.

Bunyan's theology of conversion was thoroughly biblical and deeply Puritan. He emphasized several key truths. First, conviction of sin is the work of the Holy Spirit, not the result of human effort. The Spirit opens blind eyes to see the truth and softens hard hearts to feel the weight of sin. Without this divine initiative, no one would ever seek God. Second, conversion is by grace alone through faith alone. Christian did not earn his deliverance from the burden — he received it as a free gift at the cross. Third, conversion produces immediate and visible change. After his burden is removed, Christian is clothed in new garments (representing the righteousness of Christ), given a scroll (representing assurance of salvation), and set on the path to the Celestial City with new companions and new purpose.

Bunyan was also deeply aware of the counterfeits of true conversion. In "The Pilgrim's Progress," he introduces characters like Formalist and Hypocrisy, who join the path to the Celestial City without entering through the Wicket Gate. They represent those who adopt the outward forms of Christianity — church attendance, religious vocabulary, moral behavior — without experiencing genuine conviction of sin and faith in Christ. Bunyan warned that the difference between true and false conversion would only become apparent when trials came. The genuinely converted would persevere through difficulty; the merely religious would eventually turn back.

Another important character is Talkative, who has a great deal of religious knowledge and can speak eloquently about spiritual matters but has never been truly changed in heart. Faithful exposes Talkative's hypocrisy by observing that "the soul of religion is the practical part" — that true conversion produces not just right beliefs but a right life. This warning against empty profession runs throughout Puritan literature. The Puritans were deeply concerned about what they called "false assurance" — the danger of people who believed they were saved because they held correct doctrine or had a dramatic emotional experience, but whose lives showed no evidence of genuine transformation.

Bunyan's own conversion, as recorded in "Grace Abounding," reminds us that the path to faith is not always instantaneous. Some, like the three thousand at Pentecost, are converted quickly and decisively. Others, like Bunyan himself, pass through a prolonged season of spiritual struggle before finding peace. What matters is not the speed of the process but its reality — whether genuine conviction of sin leads to genuine faith in Christ, producing a genuine change of heart and life. As Bunyan himself wrote: "Conversion is not the smooth, easy-going process some men seem to think it. It is wounding work, this breaking of the hearts, but without wounding there is no saving.'

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.

SEGMENT 4: DISCUSSION AND APPLICATION
----------------------------------------

Let us consider together what these truths mean for our lives.

Discussion Question 1: What does it mean to be 'pricked in the heart' (Acts 2:37)? Why is this kind of deep conviction necessary before genuine conversion can take place?

Discussion Question 2: Jesus told Nicodemus that he must be 'born again' (John 3:3). What does this tell us about the nature of conversion — is it something we do or something God does?

Discussion Question 3: In Pilgrim's Progress, why did Bunyan have Christian's burden fall off at the cross rather than at any other point in the journey? What theological truth does this illustrate?

Discussion Question 4: Bunyan warned about the danger of false conversion through characters like Formalist and Talkative. How can we distinguish between genuine conversion and mere religious profession?

Discussion Question 5: How does Peter's command to 'repent' (Acts 2:38) relate to Bunyan's depiction of Christian fleeing from the City of Destruction? What do both teach us about the urgency of conversion?

Take time with each question. The Puritans valued what they called "holy conference," the practice of discussing spiritual truths together. Do not settle for quick, surface answers. Push deeper. Ask follow-up questions. Listen carefully to one another.

Application is where truth becomes life. It is not enough to know what Acts 2:37-41 says. We must ask: how does this change the way I live today? How does this shape my thoughts, my words, my actions? The Puritans insisted that a truth that does not transform is a truth that has not been truly understood.

SEGMENT 4B: DEEPER THEOLOGICAL ANALYSIS
----------------------------------------

Let us press deeper into the doctrine of conviction and conversion by examining additional Scripture passages and the insights of other Puritan writers who devoted careful attention to this vital subject.

Consider the conversion of Saul of Tarsus on the road to Damascus, recorded in Acts 9:1-6: "And Saul, yet breathing out threatenings and slaughter against the disciples of the Lord, went unto the high priest, And desired of him letters to Damascus to the synagogues, that if he found any of this way, whether they were men or women, he might bring them bound unto Jerusalem. And as he journeyed, he came near Damascus: and suddenly there shined round about him a light from heaven: And he fell to the earth, and heard a voice saying unto him, Saul, Saul, why persecutest thou me? And he said, Who art thou, Lord? And the Lord said, I am Jesus whom thou persecutest: it is hard for thee to kick against the pricks. And he, trembling and astonished, said, Lord, what wilt thou have me to do?" Saul's conversion demonstrates the irresistible power of divine grace. Here was a man actively engaged in persecuting the church — breathing out threatenings and slaughter — when the sovereign Christ intervened and turned his life completely around. Saul did not seek Christ; Christ sought Saul. This illustrates the Puritan conviction that conversion is initiated by God, not by man.

The conversion of the Ethiopian eunuch in Acts 8:26-39 provides another instructive example. The Holy Spirit directed Philip to the Ethiopian's chariot, where the man was reading from Isaiah 53 but could not understand it. Philip "began at the same scripture, and preached unto him Jesus" (Acts 8:35). The eunuch believed and was baptized immediately. This account shows us several important truths about conversion: it is orchestrated by divine providence, it centers on the preaching of Christ, it requires the illumination of the Holy Spirit, and it produces immediate, visible results.

The prophet Hosea records one of the most tender descriptions of God's initiative in conversion in Hosea 2:14-15: "Therefore, behold, I will allure her, and bring her into the wilderness, and speak comfortably unto her. And I will give her her vineyards from thence, and the valley of Achor for a door of hope: and she shall sing there, as in the days of her youth, and as in the day when she came up out of the land of Egypt." Despite Israel's persistent unfaithfulness — described throughout Hosea as spiritual adultery — God promises to "allure" His wayward bride back to Himself. He will bring her into the wilderness, away from the distractions and idols that have captured her heart, and there He will speak to her heart with words of comfort and love. The "valley of Achor" — which means "valley of trouble" — becomes "a door of hope." This is the pattern of conversion: God uses the troubles and trials of life to strip away our false securities and bring us to the place where we can hear His voice and respond to His love.

The Puritan pastor William Perkins (1558-1602) was one of the most influential theologians in the English Reformation and is often called "the father of Puritanism." Perkins developed a detailed understanding of the process of conversion that became foundational for subsequent Puritan theology. In his work "A Golden Chain," Perkins described what he called the "order of salvation" — the logical sequence of divine acts that brings a sinner from death to life. This sequence includes effectual calling (God's sovereign summons to the sinner), illumination (the opening of the mind to understand spiritual truth), conviction of sin (the painful awareness of one's guilt before God), faith (trusting in Christ alone for salvation), justification (being declared righteous in God's sight), sanctification (being progressively made holy), and glorification (being brought to eternal perfection).

Perkins was careful to insist that this order does not necessarily describe a temporal sequence — some of these events happen simultaneously, and the believer may not be consciously aware of each distinct step. But the order is logically coherent and biblically grounded. What matters most, Perkins taught, is that the entire process is initiated and sustained by the grace of God. From first to last, conversion is God's work. The sinner does not contribute anything to his own salvation except the sin from which he needs to be saved.

The Puritan divine Richard Baxter (1615-1691), author of "The Reformed Pastor" and "The Saints' Everlasting Rest," wrote with great urgency about the necessity of conversion. Baxter was a tireless evangelist who spent his ministry pleading with sinners to flee from the wrath to come. He wrote: "I preached as never sure to preach again, and as a dying man to dying men." For Baxter, the doctrine of conversion was not an academic subject but a matter of eternal life and death. He believed that every sermon should press the claims of Christ upon the conscience of the hearer and call for an immediate response of repentance and faith.

Baxter's approach to conversion was balanced and pastoral. He recognized that while some conversions are sudden and dramatic — like Paul's on the road to Damascus — others are gradual and quiet. Some believers can point to a specific moment when they passed from death to life. Others cannot identify a precise moment but can testify to a gradual awakening that eventually brought them to conscious faith in Christ. Baxter taught that what matters is not the manner of conversion but the reality of it. The question is not "Can you describe the exact moment of your conversion?" but "Are you now trusting in Christ alone for salvation? Is there evidence of a changed life?" The Puritans called these evidences "the marks of grace" or "the fruit of the Spirit" (Galatians 5:22-23), and they regarded them as more reliable indicators of genuine conversion than dramatic emotional experiences.

Consider also the teaching of Titus 3:3-7: "For we ourselves also were sometimes foolish, disobedient, deceived, serving divers lusts and pleasures, living in malice and envy, hateful, and hating one another. But after that the kindness and love of God our Saviour toward man appeared, Not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to his mercy he saved us, by the washing of regeneration, and renewing of the Holy Ghost; Which he shed on us abundantly through Jesus Christ our Saviour; That being justified by his grace, we should be made heirs according to the hope of eternal life." This passage draws a stark contrast between what we were before conversion and what God has done for us in conversion. Before: foolish, disobedient, deceived, enslaved to lusts. After: saved, washed, renewed, justified, and made heirs of eternal life. And this transformation was accomplished "not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to his mercy." Conversion is mercy, pure and simple — undeserved, unearned, and freely given.

The Puritan emphasis on the reality of conversion stands as an important corrective for every generation. In an age of easy believism, where many claim to be Christians because they prayed a prayer, walked an aisle, or grew up in a Christian home, the Puritan insistence on genuine conviction of sin, heartfelt repentance, living faith in Christ, and visible evidence of transformation is desperately needed. As Jesus Himself warned, "Not every one that saith unto me, Lord, Lord, shall enter into the kingdom of heaven; but he that doeth the will of my Father which is in heaven" (Matthew 7:21).

SEGMENT 4C: PRACTICAL APPLICATION
----------------------------------------

Before we proceed to the afternoon activity, let us consider specific ways to apply the doctrine of conviction and conversion to daily life.

First, examine yourself to see whether you are in the faith (2 Corinthians 13:5). The Puritans practiced regular self-examination, and they urged every person — not just unconverted sinners — to test the reality of their faith. Ask yourself: Have I truly been convicted of my sin? Have I repented — not merely felt sorry, but actually turned away from sin and toward God? Am I trusting in Christ alone for my salvation, or am I relying on my own goodness, my church attendance, or my family heritage? Is there evidence of genuine transformation in my life — new desires, new attitudes, new patterns of behavior? These questions are not meant to produce anxiety but to promote honest self-assessment in the light of Scripture.

Second, remember your own conversion with gratitude and wonder. If God has genuinely saved you, never lose the sense of amazement at what He has done. The Puritans cultivated what they called "a lively sense of free grace" — a vivid, daily awareness that they were sinners saved entirely by the mercy of God. Ephesians 2:4-5 expresses this with magnificent clarity: "But God, who is rich in mercy, for his great love wherewith he loved us, Even when we were dead in sins, hath quickened us together with Christ, (by grace ye are saved)." You were dead. God made you alive. This was His doing, not yours. Let this truth produce in you a deep, abiding gratitude that colors every day of your life.

Third, pray for the conversion of those you love who do not yet know Christ. The Puritans were fervent intercessors for the unconverted. They knew that conversion is God's work, and they appealed to Him constantly to save their family members, neighbors, and friends. Paul's prayer for Israel in Romans 10:1 is a model: "Brethren, my heart's desire and prayer to God for Israel is, that they might be saved." Bring the names of the unconverted before God regularly. Plead with Him to open their eyes, convict their hearts, and draw them to Christ. God is sovereign in salvation, and prayer is one of the means through which He accomplishes His sovereign purposes.

Fourth, be ready to share the gospel clearly and urgently. First Peter 3:15 instructs, "But sanctify the Lord God in your hearts: and be ready always to give an answer to every man that asketh you a reason of the hope that is in you with meekness and fear." You may be the Philip whom God sends to an Ethiopian eunuch. You may be the instrument through which the Holy Spirit brings conviction to a heart that has been hardened by sin. Be ready. Know the gospel. Live the gospel. And when God opens a door of opportunity, speak the gospel with clarity, compassion, and urgency.

Fifth, do not despise the process of conviction. If God is convicting you of sin — if you feel the weight of guilt, the sting of conscience, the uneasiness of an awakened soul — do not try to silence that conviction with entertainment, distraction, or self-justification. The Spirit's work of conviction is painful, but it is the necessary precursor to the joy of salvation. As Bunyan wrote, "Conversion is wounding work — but without wounding there is no saving." Submit to the Spirit's work. Let Him break the hard ground of your heart. And then look to the cross, where the burden falls away forever.

SEGMENT 5: AFTERNOON ACTIVITY
----------------------------------------

Let us transition from study to practice. Here is your afternoon activity.

Students will create a detailed map of the first portion of Christian's journey in 'The Pilgrim's Progress,' from the City of Destruction to the cross. Using a large sheet of poster paper, students should illustrate and label each location: the City of Destruction, the Slough of Despond, the Wicket Gate, the Interpreter's House, the Hill of Difficulty, and the Cross. The map should include paths, terrain features, and key characters encountered along the way. This visual exercise helps students internalize Bunyan's allegorical method and connect his imagery to biblical doctrine.

Materials needed: Large poster paper, colored markers, pencils for sketching, copies of or access to 'The Pilgrim's Progress' (at least a summary of the early chapters), Bibles for cross-referencing

As you complete this activity, think about how it connects to what you studied in today's lesson. The goal is not just to do something, but to reinforce the truths you have been learning. The Puritans believed that education should engage the whole person: mind, heart, and hands.

Take your time with this activity. Quality matters more than speed. When you are finished, reflect on what you learned through the process of doing. Often we understand something far better after we have tried to put it into practice.

SEGMENT 6: CROSS-SUBJECT CONNECTIONS
----------------------------------------

One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

John Bunyan was imprisoned for twelve years (1660-1672) for preaching without a license from the Church of England. Study the historical context of Puritan persecution under the Restoration government of Charles II and the various Acts of Uniformity. Discuss how Bunyan used his imprisonment productively to write 'The Pilgrim's Progress' and how persecution throughout history has often produced the church's greatest literature and deepest spiritual insight.

Literature Connection:

The Pilgrim's Progress is the supreme example of Christian allegory in the English language. Allegory is a literary form in which characters, places, and events represent abstract ideas or moral qualities. Study the techniques Bunyan uses — personification, symbolic geography, dialogue that reveals character — and compare his allegorical method with other allegories such as C.S. Lewis's 'The Chronicles of Narnia.' Discuss the strengths and limitations of allegory as a means of communicating theological truth.

Science Connection:

The concept of transformation is central to both the biblical doctrine of conversion and the natural sciences. In biology, metamorphosis describes the dramatic transformation of a caterpillar into a butterfly — a complete change of form and function. In chemistry, certain reactions produce entirely new substances with different properties. These natural transformations provide helpful analogies for the spiritual transformation described in 2 Corinthians 5:17, where a person becomes an entirely 'new creature' in Christ.

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.

SEGMENT 4D: THE MARKS OF TRUE CONVERSION AND THE WORK OF THE SPIRIT
----------------------------------------

Let us now examine more closely the marks that distinguish genuine conversion from its counterfeits, drawing upon additional Scripture and the careful pastoral theology of the Puritans, who gave perhaps more attention to this subject than any other generation of Christian teachers.

The Lord Jesus Christ Himself established the most searching test of genuine conversion in Matthew 7:16-20: "Ye shall know them by their fruits. Do men gather grapes of thorns, or figs of thistles? Even so every good tree bringeth forth good fruit; but a corrupt tree bringeth forth evil fruit. A good tree cannot bring forth evil fruit, neither can a corrupt tree bring forth good fruit. Every tree that bringeth not forth good fruit is hewn down, and cast into the fire. Wherefore by their fruits ye shall know them." The fruit of genuine conversion is not merely a one-time emotional experience or a verbal profession of faith — it is a changed life that consistently produces the fruit of the Spirit. Galatians 5:22-23 identifies this fruit: "But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, faith, Meekness, temperance: against such there is no law." Where these qualities are genuinely present and growing, there is strong evidence of true conversion. Where they are absent or consistently contradicted by the pattern of life, there is grave reason for concern.

The apostle John provides another set of tests for genuine conversion in his first epistle. In 1 John 2:3-4, he writes: "And hereby we do know that we know him, if we keep his commandments. He that saith, I know him, and keepeth not his commandments, is a liar, and the truth is not in him." Obedience to God's commandments is a mark of genuine conversion. This does not mean sinless perfection — John himself acknowledges that believers do sin (1 John 2:1). But it means a settled pattern of obedience, a genuine desire to please God, and sincere grief when that desire is not realized. The truly converted person has a new disposition toward God's law — he loves it, meditates upon it, and strives to walk in it, even though he falls short.

In 1 John 3:14, John offers another diagnostic: "We know that we have passed from death unto life, because we love the brethren. He that loveth not his brother abideth in death." Love for fellow believers is one of the surest marks of genuine conversion. This love is not merely a warm feeling toward those who are pleasant and agreeable but a settled, active, sacrificial commitment to the welfare of other Christians — even those who are difficult, different, or demanding. The truly converted person finds himself drawn to the company of God's people. He takes genuine pleasure in their fellowship, shares in their sorrows, rejoices in their successes, and serves their needs without expecting recognition or reward.

The Puritan pastor Jonathan Edwards (1703-1758), who stands at the culmination of the Puritan tradition in New England, wrote the definitive work on distinguishing true from false conversion in his masterpiece "Religious Affections." Edwards observed that during the Great Awakening of the 1740s, many people exhibited powerful emotional responses to preaching — tears, trembling, outcries, fainting — but that not all of these experiences resulted in lasting conversion. Some who appeared most dramatically affected by the revival later fell away entirely, while others whose initial response was quieter proved to be genuinely and permanently changed.

Edwards therefore undertook a careful biblical analysis of the "distinguishing signs of truly gracious and holy affections." He identified twelve marks that, taken together, provide strong evidence of genuine conversion. Among the most important were: a spiritual sense of the divine excellence and beauty of divine things (not merely a rational understanding but a heartfelt appreciation of God's glory); a love for God and divine things that is based on their own inherent beauty and excellence, not merely on the benefits they provide to the believer; a humble spirit that recognizes its own unworthiness and dependence upon grace; a genuine change in the nature and disposition of the soul, producing new desires, new appetites, and new patterns of behavior; and a life of consistent Christian practice that endures through trials and persists over time.

Edwards was particularly insistent that genuine conversion produces lasting change. He wrote that "the chief evidence of our sincerity in religion is perseverance." A temporary burst of religious enthusiasm followed by a return to the old way of life is not evidence of conversion but evidence against it. The parable of the sower in Matthew 13 illustrates this clearly: some seed fell on rocky ground and sprang up quickly but withered when the sun rose, representing those who receive the word with joy but have no root and fall away when trouble comes. Only the seed that fell on good ground — producing lasting, abundant fruit — represents genuine conversion.

Consider also the role of the Holy Spirit in genuine conversion as taught in John 16:8-11: "And when he is come, he will reprove the world of sin, and of righteousness, and of judgment: Of sin, because they believe not on me; Of righteousness, because I go to my Father, and ye see me no more; Of judgment, because the prince of this world is judged." The Holy Spirit convicts the world of three things: sin (particularly the sin of unbelief), righteousness (the perfect righteousness of Christ, who has ascended to the Father), and judgment (the certainty that Satan and all who follow him will be judged). This threefold conviction is the prerequisite for genuine conversion. Without the Spirit's work of conviction, no one would ever see their need for Christ or turn to Him in repentance and faith.

The Puritan pastor Thomas Shepard (1605-1649), who served as minister of the church in Cambridge, Massachusetts, and helped establish Harvard College, wrote with penetrating clarity about the work of the Holy Spirit in conversion. In his work "The Parable of the Ten Virgins," Shepard examined the difference between the wise virgins (who had oil in their lamps, representing the indwelling Holy Spirit) and the foolish virgins (who had lamps but no oil, representing those who have the outward form of religion but lack the inward reality of the Spirit). Shepard warned that it is possible to attend church, read the Bible, pray, and even preach, while remaining unconverted. The critical question is not "What do you do?" but "What has God done in you?" Has the Spirit of God regenerated your heart? Has He given you new spiritual life? Has He opened your eyes to see the beauty of Christ and the ugliness of sin? These are the marks of genuine conversion that no outward observance can replace.

Romans 8:14-16 describes the ongoing witness of the Spirit in the converted soul: "For as many as are led by the Spirit of God, they are the sons of God. For ye have not received the spirit of bondage again to fear; but ye have received the Spirit of adoption, whereby we cry, Abba, Father. The Spirit itself beareth witness with our spirit, that we are the children of God." The truly converted person is "led by the Spirit" — guided, directed, and empowered by the Holy Spirit in the daily conduct of life. And the Spirit bears witness to their adoption — He creates in their hearts an inner assurance, a filial confidence, that enables them to cry out to God as "Abba, Father" — the most intimate and affectionate term for a father in the Aramaic language.

Practical Application — Testing and Strengthening Your Conversion:

1. Examine the fruit of your life honestly and prayerfully. Do you see evidence of the fruit of the Spirit — love, joy, peace, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, faith, meekness, and temperance — growing in your life? These fruits may be small and imperfect, but they should be present and increasing. If you see no evidence of spiritual fruit, do not rest until you have sought God earnestly for the reality of His converting grace.

2. Ask yourself whether you genuinely love the brethren. Do you find pleasure in the fellowship of other believers? Do you pray for them, serve them, and bear with their weaknesses? Or do you find the company of believers tedious and prefer the company of the world? Your answer to this question is a significant indicator of the reality of your conversion (1 John 3:14).

3. Examine your attitude toward sin. Does sin grieve you? Do you fight against it, confess it when you fall, and earnestly desire to be free from its power? Or have you made peace with sin, tolerating it without remorse? The truly converted soul hates sin — not perfectly, not consistently, but genuinely and increasingly. As Paul cried out in Romans 7:24, "O wretched man that I am! who shall deliver me from the body of this death?"

4. Test whether your faith endures through trials. Fair-weather faith — faith that flourishes in prosperity but withers in adversity — is not saving faith. Genuine conversion produces a faith that perseveres through difficulty, clings to Christ in the darkness, and emerges from trials stronger and more refined. First Peter 1:7 describes the trial of faith as "much more precious than of gold that perisheth."

5. If upon honest examination you are uncertain of your conversion, do not despair — come to Christ. The door of salvation is still open. The invitation still stands: "Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest" (Matthew 11:28). Christ has never turned away a sincere seeker. He will not turn you away. Come to Him today with all your doubt, all your sin, all your unworthiness, and He will receive you with open arms.
