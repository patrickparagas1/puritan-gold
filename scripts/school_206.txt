SEGMENT 1: MORNING DEVOTION
----------------------------------------

Welcome to today's lesson. The Puritans were some of the most learned men and women of their age, and yet they counted all their learning as nothing compared to knowing Christ. Let us follow their example today.

God of all knowledge, we begin with You. Without Your light, even the brightest minds walk in darkness. Shine Your truth into our studies today. Help us to love learning and to learn in love. For the glory of Your name. Amen.

Let us read a passage slowly, letting each word settle into our minds.

Our Scripture for today is Acts 2:37-41:

"Now when they heard this, they were pricked in their heart, and said unto Peter and to the rest of the apostles, Men and brethren, what shall we do? Then Peter said unto them, Repent, and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ for the remission of sins, and ye shall receive the gift of the Holy Ghost. For the promise is unto you, and to your children, and to all that are afar off, even as many as the Lord our God shall call. And with many other words did he testify and exhort, saying, Save yourselves from this untoward generation. Then they that gladly received his word were baptized: and the same day there were added unto them about three thousand souls."

Consider this morning the miracle of your own conversion. There was a time when you were dead in trespasses and sins, living in the City of Destruction, unaware of your danger. But God, who is rich in mercy, awakened your soul and opened your eyes to see your need. He pricked your heart with conviction and drew you to the cross, where your burden of sin fell away forever. If this has been your experience, give thanks. If you are still carrying that burden, look to the cross today — for there, and only there, is the weight of sin removed. God's invitation still stands: 'Repent, and believe the gospel.' Today is the day of salvation.

As we prepare for today's lesson, remember that everything we study flows from this one fountain: the Word of God. Our lesson today is part of The Pilgrim's Journey, and it is titled: Starting the Journey — Conviction & Conversion. Let us proceed with minds engaged and hearts open.

SEGMENT 2: MAIN LESSON PART 1 -- BIBLICAL FOUNDATION
----------------------------------------

Let us proceed to the main body of today's teaching.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes in Acts 2:37-41.

Every true Christian journey begins with conviction and conversion — the moment when a sinner is awakened to the reality of their lost condition and turns in faith to Christ for salvation. Acts 2:37-41 records one of the most powerful examples of mass conviction and conversion in all of Scripture: the day of Pentecost, when Peter's sermon cut to the heart of his hearers and three thousand souls were saved.

The passage begins with the response of the crowd to Peter's preaching: "Now when they heard this, they were pricked in their heart." The word "pricked" translates the Greek word "katanusso," which means to pierce deeply, to stab, to cause sharp pain. This was not a mild emotional response but a deep, piercing conviction of sin. Peter had just proclaimed that Jesus of Nazareth — whom they had crucified — was both Lord and Christ (Acts 2:36). The full weight of their guilt crashed upon them like a wave. They had rejected their Messiah. They had murdered the Son of God. They had sinned against infinite love and infinite holiness. The Holy Spirit drove this truth deep into their consciences, and they were undone.

Their response was the cry of every genuinely convicted sinner: "Men and brethren, what shall we do?" This question reveals several important things about true conviction. First, it is personal — "what shall we do?" They did not ask an abstract theological question but a desperately personal one. Second, it is urgent — they needed an answer immediately, not next week or next year. Third, it is humble — they addressed Peter and the apostles as those who had the answer they lacked. True conviction strips away pride and self-sufficiency and produces a willingness to hear and obey the word of God.

Peter's answer is the essence of the gospel: "Repent, and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ for the remission of sins, and ye shall receive the gift of the Holy Ghost." Repentance is the first requirement — a complete turning away from sin and turning toward God. The Greek word "metanoia" means a change of mind that results in a change of direction. It is not merely feeling sorry for sin but forsaking sin. Baptism is the outward expression of the inward change — a public identification with Christ in His death, burial, and resurrection. And the promise is twofold: remission (forgiveness) of sins and the gift of the Holy Spirit.

The passage in John 3:3-7 takes us deeper into the nature of conversion. When Nicodemus, a ruler of the Jews, came to Jesus by night, our Lord told him, "Except a man be born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God." Nicodemus was confused: "How can a man be born when he is old? can he enter the second time into his mother's womb, and be born?" Jesus answered, "Except a man be born of water and of the Spirit, he cannot enter into the kingdom of God. That which is born of the flesh is flesh; and that which is born of the Spirit is spirit. Marvel not that I said unto thee, Ye must be born again." The new birth is not a human achievement but a divine miracle. Just as a baby does not cause its own birth, a sinner does not cause his own regeneration. The new birth is the sovereign work of the Holy Spirit, who gives spiritual life to those who are spiritually dead.

Ezekiel 36:26 provides the Old Testament foundation for understanding the new birth: "A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you: and I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh, and I will give you an heart of flesh." This is God's promise of regeneration — He will replace the hard, unresponsive heart of the sinner with a soft, responsive heart that loves God and desires to obey Him. Notice that this is entirely God's work: "I will give... I will put... I will take away... I will give." The sinner contributes nothing to his own new birth except the sin from which he needs to be saved.

The apostle Paul confirms this in 2 Corinthians 5:17: "Therefore if any man be in Christ, he is a new creature: old things are passed away; behold, all things are become new." Conversion is not mere reformation — an improvement of the old nature — but regeneration — the creation of an entirely new nature. The old self, with its love of sin and hostility toward God, passes away. A new self, with a love for God and a hunger for righteousness, comes into being. This is the most radical change that can happen to a human being, and it happens in an instant by the power of the Holy Spirit.

The marks of true conversion, as seen in Acts 2:41-47, include gladly receiving God's Word, being baptized, continuing in the apostles' doctrine and fellowship, breaking bread, prayer, a sense of reverential awe, generosity, and joyful worship. Conversion is not a momentary experience that fades over time but the beginning of a whole new life — a life characterized by growth in grace, fellowship with other believers, and an ever-deepening knowledge of Christ.

The urgency of conversion cannot be overstated. Peter exhorted his hearers to "save yourselves from this untoward generation." This was not a call to self-salvation but a call to respond to the gospel while the opportunity was present. The same urgency echoes throughout Scripture: "Today if ye will hear his voice, harden not your hearts" (Hebrews 3:15). "Behold, now is the accepted time; behold, now is the day of salvation" (2 Corinthians 6:2). Conversion is not something to be postponed or delayed. The soul that hears the gospel and is convicted by the Spirit must respond now, for tomorrow is promised to no one.

This biblical foundation is essential. Without it, everything else we study today would be like a house built on sand. But with it, we stand on solid rock.

SEGMENT 3: MAIN LESSON PART 2 -- PURITAN TEACHING
----------------------------------------

Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. John Bunyan wrote extensively on this subject in "The Pilgrim's Progress." The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life.

John Bunyan (1628-1688) is perhaps the most beloved of all Puritan writers, and his masterpiece "The Pilgrim's Progress" has been translated into more languages than any book except the Bible. Bunyan wrote from deep personal experience — he was a tinker (a mender of pots and pans) from Bedford, England, who experienced a long and agonizing period of spiritual conviction before finding peace in Christ. His autobiography, "Grace Abounding to the Chief of Sinners," records years of spiritual turmoil, doubt, and despair before the light of the gospel finally broke through. This personal experience gave Bunyan an unparalleled ability to describe the inner workings of conviction and conversion.

In "The Pilgrim's Progress," the journey begins with a man named Christian standing in the City of Destruction, reading a book (the Bible) and crying out, "What shall I do to be saved?" This opening scene parallels the cry of the crowd at Pentecost: "Men and brethren, what shall we do?" Bunyan understood that every genuine spiritual journey begins with a sense of danger — an awakening to the reality that one is lost, condemned, and heading for destruction. Christian's burden, which he carries on his back throughout the early stages of his journey, represents the weight of sin and guilt that presses upon the conscience of every awakened sinner.

Bunyan depicted the process of conviction with remarkable psychological accuracy. Christian does not simply decide one day that he would like to be religious. He is driven by terror and desperation. He sees in the book that the city where he lives is destined for destruction, and he cannot rest until he finds a way of escape. His family thinks he is mad. His neighbors mock him. But the conviction of the Spirit is stronger than all human opposition, and Christian flees from the city, putting his fingers in his ears and crying, "Life! Life! Eternal life!" This portrayal captures the biblical truth that conviction of sin is not a pleasant experience but a painful and desperate one — yet it is absolutely necessary, for without it, there can be no genuine conversion.

The character Evangelist directs Christian to the Wicket Gate (which represents Christ, the door of salvation — John 10:9). Along the way, Christian must pass through the Slough of Despond — a bog that represents the doubts, fears, and discouragements that often assail those who are seeking salvation. Bunyan drew this image from his own experience. He had spent years sinking in the slough of spiritual despair, wondering whether his sins were too great to be forgiven, whether he had committed the unpardonable sin, whether God's promises truly applied to him. Many seekers have had the same experience. The path to conversion is not always smooth; it often leads through valleys of doubt and anguish before reaching the solid ground of faith.

When Christian finally reaches the cross, Bunyan describes the moment of conversion with simple, powerful beauty. As Christian gazes upon the cross, the burden on his back — the weight of his sin — comes loose and rolls away, tumbling down the hill and into an open sepulchre, where it is seen no more. Christian is filled with joy and wonder, and he cries out, "He hath given me rest by His sorrow, and life by His death!" This scene perfectly illustrates the biblical truth that conversion is the removal of guilt through the finished work of Christ. The sinner does not earn forgiveness by carrying his burden far enough or suffering long enough. Forgiveness comes solely through looking to Christ and His cross.

Bunyan's theology of conversion was thoroughly biblical and deeply Puritan. He emphasized several key truths. First, conviction of sin is the work of the Holy Spirit, not the result of human effort. The Spirit opens blind eyes to see the truth and softens hard hearts to feel the weight of sin. Without this divine initiative, no one would ever seek God. Second, conversion is by grace alone through faith alone. Christian did not earn his deliverance from the burden — he received it as a free gift at the cross. Third, conversion produces immediate and visible change. After his burden is removed, Christian is clothed in new garments (representing the righteousness of Christ), given a scroll (representing assurance of salvation), and set on the path to the Celestial City with new companions and new purpose.

Bunyan was also deeply aware of the counterfeits of true conversion. In "The Pilgrim's Progress," he introduces characters like Formalist and Hypocrisy, who join the path to the Celestial City without entering through the Wicket Gate. They represent those who adopt the outward forms of Christianity — church attendance, religious vocabulary, moral behavior — without experiencing genuine conviction of sin and faith in Christ. Bunyan warned that the difference between true and false conversion would only become apparent when trials came. The genuinely converted would persevere through difficulty; the merely religious would eventually turn back.

Another important character is Talkative, who has a great deal of religious knowledge and can speak eloquently about spiritual matters but has never been truly changed in heart. Faithful exposes Talkative's hypocrisy by observing that "the soul of religion is the practical part" — that true conversion produces not just right beliefs but a right life. This warning against empty profession runs throughout Puritan literature. The Puritans were deeply concerned about what they called "false assurance" — the danger of people who believed they were saved because they held correct doctrine or had a dramatic emotional experience, but whose lives showed no evidence of genuine transformation.

Bunyan's own conversion, as recorded in "Grace Abounding," reminds us that the path to faith is not always instantaneous. Some, like the three thousand at Pentecost, are converted quickly and decisively. Others, like Bunyan himself, pass through a prolonged season of spiritual struggle before finding peace. What matters is not the speed of the process but its reality — whether genuine conviction of sin leads to genuine faith in Christ, producing a genuine change of heart and life. As Bunyan himself wrote: "Conversion is not the smooth, easy-going process some men seem to think it. It is wounding work, this breaking of the hearts, but without wounding there is no saving.'

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.

SEGMENT 4: DISCUSSION AND APPLICATION
----------------------------------------

Let us consider together what these truths mean for our lives.

Discussion Question 1: What does it mean to be 'pricked in the heart' (Acts 2:37)? Why is this kind of deep conviction necessary before genuine conversion can take place?

Discussion Question 2: Jesus told Nicodemus that he must be 'born again' (John 3:3). What does this tell us about the nature of conversion — is it something we do or something God does?

Discussion Question 3: In Pilgrim's Progress, why did Bunyan have Christian's burden fall off at the cross rather than at any other point in the journey? What theological truth does this illustrate?

Discussion Question 4: Bunyan warned about the danger of false conversion through characters like Formalist and Talkative. How can we distinguish between genuine conversion and mere religious profession?

Discussion Question 5: How does Peter's command to 'repent' (Acts 2:38) relate to Bunyan's depiction of Christian fleeing from the City of Destruction? What do both teach us about the urgency of conversion?

Take time with each question. The Puritans valued what they called "holy conference," the practice of discussing spiritual truths together. Do not settle for quick, surface answers. Push deeper. Ask follow-up questions. Listen carefully to one another.

Application is where truth becomes life. It is not enough to know what Acts 2:37-41 says. We must ask: how does this change the way I live today? How does this shape my thoughts, my words, my actions? The Puritans insisted that a truth that does not transform is a truth that has not been truly understood.

SEGMENT 5: AFTERNOON ACTIVITY
----------------------------------------

Let us transition from study to practice. Here is your afternoon activity.

Students will create a detailed map of the first portion of Christian's journey in 'The Pilgrim's Progress,' from the City of Destruction to the cross. Using a large sheet of poster paper, students should illustrate and label each location: the City of Destruction, the Slough of Despond, the Wicket Gate, the Interpreter's House, the Hill of Difficulty, and the Cross. At each location, students should write a brief description of what happens there and the biblical truth it represents. For example, at the Slough of Despond, students would write about the doubts and fears that assail seeking sinners and reference relevant Scriptures. The map should include paths, terrain features, and key characters encountered along the way. This visual exercise helps students internalize Bunyan's allegorical method and connect his imagery to biblical doctrine.

Materials needed: Large poster paper, colored markers, pencils for sketching, copies of or access to 'The Pilgrim's Progress' (at least a summary of the early chapters), Bibles for cross-referencing

As you complete this activity, think about how it connects to what you studied in today's lesson. The goal is not just to do something, but to reinforce the truths you have been learning. The Puritans believed that education should engage the whole person: mind, heart, and hands.

Take your time with this activity. Quality matters more than speed. When you are finished, reflect on what you learned through the process of doing. Often we understand something far better after we have tried to put it into practice.

SEGMENT 6: CROSS-SUBJECT CONNECTIONS
----------------------------------------

One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

John Bunyan was imprisoned for twelve years (1660-1672) for preaching without a license from the Church of England. Study the historical context of Puritan persecution under the Restoration government of Charles II and the various Acts of Uniformity. Discuss how Bunyan used his imprisonment productively to write 'The Pilgrim's Progress' and how persecution throughout history has often produced the church's greatest literature and deepest spiritual insight.

Literature Connection:

The Pilgrim's Progress is the supreme example of Christian allegory in the English language. Allegory is a literary form in which characters, places, and events represent abstract ideas or moral qualities. Study the techniques Bunyan uses — personification, symbolic geography, dialogue that reveals character — and compare his allegorical method with other allegories such as C.S. Lewis's 'The Chronicles of Narnia.' Discuss the strengths and limitations of allegory as a means of communicating theological truth.

Science Connection:

The concept of transformation is central to both the biblical doctrine of conversion and the natural sciences. In biology, metamorphosis describes the dramatic transformation of a caterpillar into a butterfly — a complete change of form and function. In chemistry, certain reactions produce entirely new substances with different properties. These natural transformations provide helpful analogies for the spiritual transformation described in 2 Corinthians 5:17, where a person becomes an entirely 'new creature' in Christ.

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.

SEGMENT 7: EVENING REVIEW AND MEMORY WORK
----------------------------------------

We have reached the final segment of today's lesson. The Puritans placed great value on review and memorization. They knew that what is not reviewed is soon forgotten. Let us take time now to consolidate what we have learned.

Review Questions:

Review Question 1: What does the word 'pricked' mean in Acts 2:37, and what caused this reaction in Peter's audience?

Review Question 2: What two things did Peter command his hearers to do in Acts 2:38?

Review Question 3: What does Ezekiel 36:26 promise that God will do in the hearts of His people?

Review Question 4: In Pilgrim's Progress, what does Christian's burden represent, and where does it finally fall off?

Review Question 5: Why did Bunyan include characters like Formalist and Talkative in his allegory?

Write out your answers to these questions. Use complete sentences and support your answers with what you learned in today's lesson. If you are unsure of an answer, go back and review that section of the lesson.

Memory Verse:

"Therefore if any man be in Christ, he is a new creature: old things are passed away; behold, all things are become new." -- 2 Corinthians 5:17

Read this verse aloud five times. Then close your eyes and try to recite it from memory. Write it out by hand on a card or in your journal. The Puritans memorized vast portions of Scripture, and it was one of the great sources of their spiritual strength. Even one verse, truly hidden in the heart, can sustain you in times of trial.

We have covered much ground today. Take time this evening to reflect on what stood out to you most. Talk about it with your family. Write it in your journal. Let these truths take root. Until next time, students.
