PURITAN GOLD — Episode 7
"The Puritan Practice of Daily Prayer"
Week 1, Day 7 (Sunday) — Devotional Episode & Proverbs 2:1-5

---

It's Sunday. The Lord's Day.

And I thought... on this day of rest, this day of worship, this day that the Puritans considered the crown jewel of the week... we should talk about the practice that held their entire lives together.

Prayer.

Not prayer as a topic. Not prayer as a theological concept to be studied and debated. Prayer as a daily, hourly, moment-by-moment reality. The air the Puritans breathed. The foundation on which they built everything else.

Because if this first week together has taught us anything, it's this: wisdom is not self-generated. It comes from God. It is sought from God. It is received from God. And the primary means by which we seek and receive and commune with God... is prayer.

Solomon knew this. Listen again to Proverbs 2, verses 1 through 5:

"My son, if thou wilt receive my words, and hide my commandments with thee; So that thou incline thine ear unto wisdom, and apply thine heart to understanding; Yea, if thou criest after knowledge, and liftest up thy voice for understanding; If thou seekest her as silver, and searchest for her as for hid treasures; Then shalt thou understand the fear of the Lord, and find the knowledge of God."

"If thou criest after knowledge, and liftest up thy voice for understanding." That's prayer. Right there in the middle of the pursuit of wisdom... is the cry of the human heart to God. "Lord, I cannot understand this on my own. I need You to open my eyes. I need You to teach me. I need You."

That cry — that honest, humble, desperate cry — is the heartbeat of the Christian life. And the Puritans organized their entire days around it.

So let me take you into a Puritan home. Let me show you what a day looked like for a family that took prayer as seriously as breathing.

The day began early. Before dawn, in many households. The father — or mother, in homes where the husband was absent — would rise while the house was still dark and still. And the first act of the day was not work. It was not breakfast. It was not checking on the farm or the shop or the business.

It was prayer.

This was not a rushed affair. The Puritans believed that the morning hours — those quiet, undisturbed hours before the world woke up — were sacred. They were the hours when the soul was most receptive, when the mind was clearest, when the distractions of the day had not yet begun their assault. And they guarded those hours with fierce intentionality.

The morning devotion typically included three elements: Scripture reading, meditation, and prayer. And these three were woven together, not separated into rigid compartments.

The head of the household would read a passage of Scripture — often from the Old Testament in the morning and the New Testament in the evening. And this was not a hasty skimming. The passage would be read aloud, slowly, with attention to every word. In many families, the text would be read twice — once straight through, and once with pauses for reflection.

Then came meditation. The Puritans distinguished carefully between reading and meditation. Reading, they said, is like a bee flying over a garden. Meditation is like a bee settling on a flower and drawing out the nectar. You don't just pass over the words — you dwell on them. You turn them over. You ask, "What does this mean? What does this tell me about God? About myself? About how I should live today?"

Richard Baxter, one of the most influential Puritans, taught that meditation without prayer is like a bow without an arrow. It is incomplete. And so the meditation naturally led into prayer — prayer that was shaped by the Scripture just read, prayer that responded to what God had said with the heart's honest reply.

These morning prayers were often structured — not rigidly, but with a pattern that helped the one praying to be thorough rather than haphazard. The Puritans typically included several elements in their prayers:

Adoration — beginning not with their own needs, but with the greatness and goodness of God. Praising Him for who He is. Marveling at His attributes. Setting the tone of the prayer not as a shopping list of requests, but as an audience with the King of kings.

Confession — honest, specific acknowledgment of sin. Not vague generalities like "forgive me for being bad," but particular sins named and repented of. The Puritans believed that vague confession was often no confession at all. They examined their hearts — using the Ten Commandments as a mirror, or reflecting on the previous day's failures — and brought specific sins into the light.

Thanksgiving — gratitude for specific mercies. The Puritans kept what they called "mercy journals" — records of God's faithfulness in their lives. And they would review these journals regularly, reminding themselves of answered prayers, of providential deliverances, of daily bread provided. Gratitude was not an afterthought. It was a discipline.

Supplication — bringing their needs and the needs of others before God. This included prayers for their family, their church, their nation, their pastors, the spread of the gospel, the conversion of the lost, and their own growth in holiness. Many Puritans kept prayer lists — written lists of people and concerns — so that nothing would be forgotten.

And finally, surrender — committing the day to God. Placing every plan, every task, every relationship into His hands. Acknowledging that the day belongs to Him, and asking for His guidance and strength in every moment.

This morning devotion might last thirty minutes. It might last an hour. For some of the most devoted Puritans, it lasted two hours or more. They were not in a hurry. They had learned what the Psalmist meant when he wrote, "My soul waiteth for the Lord more than they that watch for the morning."

But the morning was not the end of it.

Throughout the day, the Puritans practiced what they called "ejaculatory prayer" — brief, spontaneous prayers sent up to God in the midst of daily activities. A quick prayer for wisdom before a difficult conversation. A whispered word of thanks after a small kindness. A silent cry for help in a moment of temptation. These were not formal prayers. They were the ongoing conversation of a soul that lived in constant awareness of God's presence.

Brother Lawrence, though not technically a Puritan, captured this spirit perfectly in his Practice of the Presence of God. He described the possibility of maintaining a continuous inner dialogue with God — while washing dishes, while walking to work, while sitting in traffic. The Puritans shared this vision of prayer not as an isolated activity... but as a way of life.

And then, at the close of the day, came the evening devotion.

If the morning devotion was about preparation — equipping the soul for the day ahead — the evening devotion was about reflection. It was about looking back over the day with honest eyes and bringing what you found to God.

The Puritans took the evening examination of conscience very seriously. They would ask themselves questions like: Where did I see God at work today? Where did I resist temptation, and where did I give in? Where was my heart drawn toward God, and where did it wander? What do I need to confess? What do I need to give thanks for?

This practice was not morbid or anxious. It was cleansing. It was the spiritual equivalent of washing the day's dust from your feet before entering the rest of sleep. And it ensured that nothing — no sin, no anxiety, no unresolved conflict — was carried into the night and allowed to fester.

The evening devotion also included prayers for protection during the night. The Puritans were deeply aware of their vulnerability — physical and spiritual — and they committed themselves and their families to God's keeping before they closed their eyes.

And then... on the Lord's Day, everything was intensified. Sunday was the day when the rhythm of prayer reached its highest expression.

The Puritans prepared for Sunday the way athletes prepare for a competition. Saturday evening was often spent in prayer and self-examination, making the soul ready for the worship of the next day. They did not stumble into the Lord's Day unprepared. They entered it deliberately, expectantly, with hearts already warmed by private devotion.

Sunday morning began earlier than usual, with extended personal prayer before the family gathered for worship. The Puritans understood something that we have largely forgotten: what you bring to public worship matters. If you arrive at church with a cold, distracted, unprepared heart... the sermon will fall on hard ground. But if you come with a heart that has been softened by private prayer and meditation... the same sermon becomes living water.

After the morning service, many Puritan families would spend the afternoon discussing the sermon together. Parents would question children about what they had heard. Husbands and wives would talk through the application of the text to their own lives. The sermon was not entertainment to be passively consumed. It was food to be chewed, digested, and turned into spiritual energy for the week ahead.

And then the evening service. And then more prayer. And then the day would close with the same practice that began it: a family gathered around the Word of God, lifting their voices to heaven, committing themselves once more to the care of their faithful Father.

Now... I know what some of you are thinking. "That sounds beautiful, but it also sounds impossible. I can barely find ten minutes for devotions in the morning. How am I supposed to pray like a Puritan?"

And here's what I want to say to you. The point is not to replicate the Puritans' exact schedule. The point is to catch their spirit. The spirit that says: prayer is not optional. It is not a luxury for the spiritually elite. It is as necessary as breathing. And the shape of your prayer life will determine the shape of your spiritual life.

Spurgeon understood this. For all his busy schedule — and he was extraordinarily busy, pastoring the largest church in the world, running an orphanage, overseeing a college, writing sermons and books and articles at a breathtaking pace — Spurgeon never let his busyness crowd out his prayer life. He said that he had so much to do that he could not afford not to pray. The busier the day, the more prayer it required.

And he was right. Because prayer is not time taken away from work. Prayer is the power that makes work effective. It is not a delay on the way to the real task. It is the real task. Everything else flows from it.

So here is my encouragement to you on this Lord's Day.

Start where you are. If you've been praying five minutes a day... try ten. If you haven't been praying at all... start with five. Don't let the perfect be the enemy of the good. God doesn't demand a two-hour Puritan devotion from you before He'll listen. He simply asks you to come. To open your mouth. To lift up your voice. To cry after knowledge. To seek understanding.

And He promises — He promises — that if you seek Him, you will find Him.

Try adding one element of the Puritan practice this week. Maybe it's the evening examination — taking five minutes before bed to review the day with God. Maybe it's the practice of ejaculatory prayer — training yourself to send up brief prayers throughout the day. Maybe it's keeping a mercy journal — writing down one thing each day that you're grateful for.

Whatever it is... start. Begin. Take the first step. And trust that the God who invited you to pray... will meet you there.

Solomon said it plainly: "If thou criest after knowledge, and liftest up thy voice for understanding... then shalt thou understand the fear of the Lord, and find the knowledge of God."

The promise is real. The invitation is open. And the God who waits to hear from you... is the same God who is listening right now.

Let me close this week with a benediction — a blessing drawn from the heart of Proverbs and spoken over you as you enter a new week.

May the Lord give you wisdom — not the wisdom of this world, but the wisdom that comes down from above. May He guard your heart and direct your paths. May the fear of the Lord be your beginning and your foundation, your compass and your anchor. May you seek His truth like silver and find it more precious than gold. May you trust Him with all your heart, in all your ways, with all your life. And may the peace of God — that peace which passes all understanding — keep your heart and mind in Christ Jesus, now and always.

Amen.

Thank you for walking through this first week with me, friend. It has been a gift. I'll see you in Week 2... Lord willing.

Grace and peace to you.
