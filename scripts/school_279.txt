PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 19
"Sharing Your Faith"
Apologetics for Young Christians — Lesson 19

---

Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we move from the realm of knowledge into the realm of practice. We have spent many lessons building a foundation for understanding and defending the Christian faith — examining arguments for God's existence, evidence for the reliability of Scripture, the case for the resurrection, the uniqueness of Christ among the world's religions, and answers to common objections. Now it is time to put it all together and learn how to share and defend your faith in real conversations with real people, combining intellectual confidence with genuine love, biblical truth with practical wisdom, and courage with compassion. Let us pray and begin.

Lord God, Thou who didst send Thy Son into the world not to condemn the world but to save it, send us also as Thy ambassadors to a lost and dying generation. Equip us with truth and clothe us with humility. Give us the courage of the apostles, the wisdom of the Puritans, and the compassion of our Saviour. Let our words be faithful, our manner be gracious, and our aim be always Thy glory and the salvation of precious souls. In the name of Jesus Christ, who loved us and gave Himself for us, Amen.

--- Lesson ---

Students, the Apostle Peter gave us the great commission of apologetics in his first epistle: "But sanctify the Lord God in your hearts: and be ready always to give an answer to every man that asketh you a reason of the hope that is in you with meekness and fear" (1 Peter 3:15). This single verse contains an entire curriculum for sharing your faith. It tells us three things: what to prepare (the Lord sanctified in our hearts), what to do (give an answer — a reasoned defense — to everyone who asks), and how to do it (with meekness and fear — that is, with gentleness, humility, and reverence). Today we will unpack these principles in detail, drawing from Scripture, from the wisdom of the Puritans, and from practical experience.

Before we discuss method, however, we must establish motivation. Why should we share our faith at all? The answer is threefold: because God commands it, because love compels it, and because the need demands it.

God commands it. The Great Commission of our Lord Jesus Christ is not a suggestion or a recommendation. It is a command: "Go ye therefore, and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost: Teaching them to observe all things whatsoever I have commanded you: and, lo, I am with you alway, even unto the end of the world" (Matthew 28:19-20). Every Christian is called to be a witness. You do not need to be ordained. You do not need a seminary degree. You do not need to be an expert in philosophy or theology. You need only to know Christ and to be willing to tell others about Him. The Apostle Paul, writing from a Roman prison, described himself as "an ambassador in bonds" (Ephesians 6:20). If Paul could be an ambassador for Christ in chains, you can be an ambassador for Christ in your classroom, your neighborhood, and your community.

Love compels it. The Apostle Paul wrote: "For the love of Christ constraineth us" (2 Corinthians 5:14). When you truly understand what Christ has done for you — when you grasp, even dimly, the infinite cost of your salvation and the immeasurable love that motivated it — you cannot keep silent. The love of Christ overflows from the heart of every genuine believer into a passionate desire for others to experience the same grace. Richard Baxter (1615-1691) wrote with characteristic urgency in his great work "The Reformed Pastor": "Oh, sirs, how many do you think are panting and groaning for help while you sit still and let them perish? Can you hear a poor sinner cry, 'What must I do to be saved?' and not run to him to help him? If you have tasted that the Lord is gracious, you will not be content to feast alone." Baxter's words burn with the fire of holy love — a love that cannot bear to see others perishing when the remedy is at hand.

The need demands it. We live in a world where millions of people are lost — separated from God, enslaved to sin, and heading toward eternal judgment. They may not know they are lost. They may feel happy, successful, and self-sufficient. But as Jesus said, "What shall it profit a man, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul?" (Mark 8:36). The need for the gospel is more urgent today than it has ever been. In Western civilization, biblical literacy has plummeted, church attendance has declined, and a rising generation is growing up with little or no knowledge of the God who made them and the Saviour who died for them. The fields are white unto harvest, and the laborers are few (John 4:35; Matthew 9:37). You — yes, you, even as a young student — are called to be one of those laborers.

The history of the church is filled with examples of ordinary believers who shared their faith with extraordinary effectiveness. Consider the Apostle Andrew, the brother of Simon Peter. In the Gospel of John, Andrew is never recorded as preaching a sermon. He is never shown performing a miracle. But three times in the Gospel of John, Andrew is shown doing something profoundly simple and profoundly important: he brings someone to Jesus. In John 1:40-42, Andrew finds his brother Peter and says, "We have found the Messias," and he "brought him to Jesus." In John 6:8-9, Andrew brings the boy with five loaves and two fishes to Jesus. In John 12:20-22, when certain Greeks want to see Jesus, Andrew brings them to Him. Andrew's ministry was the ministry of introduction — connecting people with Christ. And through one of those introductions — bringing Peter to Jesus — Andrew indirectly influenced the conversion of thousands at Pentecost and the entire course of church history. You never know what God will do through a simple act of faithful witness.

Consider also the example of the woman at the well in John chapter 4. This unnamed Samaritan woman — a social outcast with a checkered past — encountered Jesus at Jacob's well outside the village of Sychar. After a brief but life-changing conversation, she left her water pot, went into the city, and said to the men there, "Come, see a man, which told me all things that ever I did: is not this the Christ?" (John 4:29). Her testimony was simple, unpolished, and incomplete — she did not understand all the theology of the incarnation or the mechanics of atonement. But she knew what had happened to her, and she told others. And the result? "Many of the Samaritans of that city believed on him for the saying of the woman" (John 4:39). This is a powerful reminder that you do not need to be a theologian to be a witness. You need only to have met Christ and to be willing to tell others where to find Him.

The Puritan era produced some of the most zealous evangelists and missionaries the church has ever known. John Eliot (1604-1690), the "Apostle to the Indians," spent decades learning the Algonquin language and translating the entire Bible into the Algonquin tongue — the first complete Bible printed in the New World. Eliot's methods were simple but effective: he lived among the people he wanted to reach, he learned their language and culture, he addressed their real needs (both physical and spiritual), and he presented the gospel with patience and persistence. His work was slow — for years he seemed to make little progress — but he persevered, and eventually entire communities embraced the Christian faith.

David Brainerd (1718-1747) was a young missionary to the Native Americans who died of tuberculosis at the age of twenty-nine. His short life, recorded in his famous journal, became one of the most influential missionary biographies in history. Brainerd's journal reveals a man of intense prayer, deep spiritual devotion, and tireless evangelistic labor. Despite his frail health and the enormous hardships of frontier life, Brainerd preached, taught, and visited among the Delaware Indians with a passion that was fueled entirely by his love for Christ and his compassion for the lost. His journal entries frequently record long hours of agonized prayer for the conversion of the Indians. Jonathan Edwards (1703-1758), who edited and published Brainerd's journal, believed it to be one of the most powerful testimonies of Christian devotion ever written. Brainerd's example inspired generations of missionaries, including William Carey, Henry Martyn, and Adoniram Judson.

George Whitefield (1714-1770), the great evangelist of the First Great Awakening, was perhaps the most effective preacher in the English-speaking world. Whitefield preached an estimated eighteen thousand sermons during his ministry, often to crowds of ten thousand, twenty thousand, or even thirty thousand people in the open air. Benjamin Franklin, who was not a Christian but was deeply impressed by Whitefield, calculated that Whitefield's voice could carry to an audience of thirty thousand without amplification. Whitefield's preaching was marked by an extraordinary combination of theological depth, emotional power, dramatic delivery, and genuine love for his hearers. He once said: "I am willing to go to prison and to death for you, but I am not willing to go to heaven without you." Whitefield understood that effective evangelism flows from a heart that genuinely loves the people it addresses.

These examples — from Andrew to the woman at the well, from John Eliot to David Brainerd to George Whitefield — share several common characteristics. None of them relied solely on intellectual arguments. All of them were characterized by genuine personal devotion to Christ. All of them were motivated by love — love for God and love for the people they sought to reach. And all of them were willing to endure hardship, rejection, and even death for the sake of the gospel. These are the marks of faithful witness.

Now let us turn to the principles that should guide us as we share our faith. These are not techniques or tricks. They are postures of the heart and habits of the mind that, when cultivated, will make you an effective and faithful witness for Christ.

The first principle is: Sanctify the Lord God in your hearts. Peter puts this first because it is foundational. Before you can speak for God to others, you must be right with God yourself. The word "sanctify" means to set apart as holy — to give Christ the supreme place of honor, authority, and lordship in your heart. This is not merely a theological statement. It is a daily, practical reality. It means beginning each day in prayer. It means feeding your soul on the Word of God. It means confessing your sins promptly and turning from them decisively. It means cultivating an intimate, living, dynamic relationship with Jesus Christ through the power of the Holy Spirit.

Why is this important for apologetics? Because you cannot give what you do not have. If Christ is not real to you, you will not be able to make Him real to others. If your faith is merely intellectual — a collection of arguments and propositions stored in your head but not burning in your heart — your witness will be cold, mechanical, and unconvincing. But if Christ is the treasure of your life, the joy of your heart, and the anchor of your soul, that reality will shine through every conversation, every argument, and every encounter. The Puritan pastor John Owen (1616-1683) wrote: "The best preparation for the work of the ministry — or, indeed, for any Christian service — is the daily exercise of communion with God. The man who walks with God will speak of God naturally, as a man speaks of his closest friend." Owen understood that effective witness flows from intimate relationship.

The second principle is: Listen before you speak. One of the most common mistakes Christians make in conversations about faith is jumping to answers before they understand the questions. Every person you talk to has a unique story — unique experiences, unique wounds, unique intellectual struggles, and unique reasons for their doubts or objections. If you take the time to listen — truly listen, with patience, attention, and genuine interest — you will be far more effective than if you launch into a pre-packaged argument.

Ask questions. "What do you mean by that?" "Can you tell me more about why you think that?" "What experiences have shaped your views on this?" "When you say you don't believe in God, what kind of God don't you believe in?" These questions accomplish several important things. They show respect for the other person, demonstrating that you care about them as a human being, not merely as a target for your arguments. They help you understand their actual concerns, which are often different from what you initially assume. And they often reveal that the stated objection is not the real objection. Many people who say, "I don't believe in God because of the problem of evil" are really saying, "I'm angry at God because something terrible happened to me or someone I love." The intellectual argument is a shield protecting an emotional wound. If you respond with a philosophical argument when what the person needs is compassion, understanding, and the tender love of Christ, you will miss the mark entirely.

The great Puritan pastor Richard Sibbes (1577-1635) understood this principle deeply. Known as "the heavenly Doctor Sibbes" and "the sweet dropper" for the gentleness of his preaching, Sibbes taught that the first task of ministry is to understand the condition of the soul you are addressing. In his masterwork "The Bruised Reed and Smoking Flax," Sibbes wrote: "A bruised reed shall he not break, and smoking flax shall he not quench." This is a quotation from Isaiah 42:3, which Matthew applied to Jesus (Matthew 12:20). Sibbes argued that Christ does not come to bruised, wounded souls with harsh arguments and stern rebukes. He comes with tenderness, patience, and compassion. And so should we. "We must not be too rigorous with tender souls," Sibbes counseled. "Christ did not come to put out the smoking flax but to fan it into flame." When we listen before we speak, we follow the example of our Master, who always knew what was in a person's heart and addressed the real need, not merely the surface question.

The third principle is: Be honest about what you know and what you do not know. Intellectual honesty is one of the most powerful tools in the apologist's arsenal. You do not need to have an answer to every question. No one does — not the most learned theologian, not the most brilliant philosopher, not the most experienced pastor. It is perfectly acceptable — and indeed it is far more credible — to say, "That's a great question. I don't have a complete answer, but here's what I do know..." or "I haven't studied that particular objection in depth. Let me look into it and get back to you." Pretending to know everything undermines your credibility. Honesty builds trust.

The Puritan scholar William Ames (1576-1633), one of the most influential theologians of the early Puritan movement and the author of "The Marrow of Theology," insisted that intellectual humility was an essential virtue for the Christian scholar. Ames wrote: "The truly wise man is he who knows the limits of his own knowledge and does not pretend to know what he does not know. Such humility, far from being a weakness, is a great strength — for it opens the door to further learning and earns the respect of honest inquirers." When you are honest about the boundaries of your knowledge, you demonstrate integrity. And when you follow up — when you actually research the question and come back with a thoughtful answer — you demonstrate that you take the other person's concerns seriously.

The fourth principle is: Keep the main thing the main thing. The main thing in Christian apologetics is not winning a debate. It is not proving that you are smarter than the person you are talking to. It is not demolishing every objection and leaving your opponent intellectually defeated. The main thing is introducing people to Jesus Christ. Apologetics is a servant of evangelism, not a substitute for it. Apologetics clears the intellectual and emotional obstacles that stand between a person and the gospel. But it is the gospel itself — the good news that God has sent His Son to save sinners — that has the power to save. "For I am not ashamed of the gospel of Christ: for it is the power of God unto salvation to every one that believeth" (Romans 1:16).

This means that every apologetic conversation should eventually point to the cross. It should point to the God who loved the world so much that He gave His only begotten Son, so that whoever believes in Him should not perish but have everlasting life (John 3:16). Arguments are useful — they can remove barriers, answer questions, and create openness — but the gospel is powerful. It is "the power of God unto salvation." Never let your arguments replace your gospel witness. Never get so caught up in the intellectual sparring that you forget why you are having the conversation in the first place.

The Puritan preacher Thomas Goodwin (1600-1680), who served as chaplain to Oliver Cromwell and as president of Magdalen College, Oxford, wrote about this danger with great insight. Goodwin observed: "A man may win every argument and lose every soul. The aim of our discourse with unbelievers is not to demonstrate our own learning but to display the beauty of Christ. If, at the end of a conversation, the other person is impressed with your intellect but unmoved by the gospel, you have failed in the one thing that matters." Goodwin's words are a sobering reminder that apologetics is not a performance art. It is a labor of love.

The fifth principle is: Embody what you profess. Your life is your most powerful argument. People are watching how you handle difficulty, how you treat others, how you respond to injustice, how you deal with failure, and how you face fear. If your life reflects the love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control of the Holy Spirit (Galatians 5:22-23), your words will carry enormous weight. If your life contradicts your message, no amount of clever argumentation will compensate.

Jesus said, "Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven" (Matthew 5:16). Notice that Jesus says your good works will cause people to glorify God. Your life — your character, your integrity, your love — is a living argument for the reality of the gospel. When people see a Christian who endures suffering with grace, who forgives those who have wronged them, who speaks the truth with love, and who serves others without seeking recognition, they see something that cannot be explained by mere human effort. They see the power of the Holy Spirit at work in a human life. And that is more convincing than any philosophical argument.

The Puritan divine Richard Baxter (1615-1691) made this point unforgettably in "The Reformed Pastor": "It is a palpable error of some ministers who make such a disproportion between their preaching and their living — who study hard to preach exactly and study little to live exactly. All the week long is little enough to study how to speak two hours, and yet one hour seems too much to study how to live all the week. They are loath to misplace a word in their sermons, or to be guilty of any notable infirmity — and yet they make nothing of misplacing affections, words, and actions in the course of their lives." Baxter's rebuke was aimed at pastors, but it applies to every Christian. The consistency between our message and our manner, between our doctrine and our daily life, is one of the most powerful witnesses we can offer to a watching world.

The sixth principle is: Trust the Holy Spirit. Apologetics is not a purely human endeavor. The Holy Spirit is the great Evangelist — He is at work in every genuine conversation about faith, convicting the world of sin, and of righteousness, and of judgment (John 16:8). He is the one who opens blind eyes, softens hard hearts, and draws sinners to the Saviour. Jesus told His disciples: "When he, the Spirit of truth, is come, he will guide you into all truth" (John 16:13). And Paul reminded the Corinthians that "no man can say that Jesus is the Lord, but by the Holy Ghost" (1 Corinthians 12:3). The work of conversion is ultimately God's work, not ours. This takes enormous pressure off you. You do not have to be brilliant. You do not have to be eloquent. You do not have to have all the answers. You have to be faithful, clear, and loving. The Spirit will do the rest.

The Puritan theologian John Owen (1616-1683), in his magnificent work "The Holy Spirit: His Gifts and Power," wrote extensively about the Spirit's role in conversion. Owen argued that without the sovereign work of the Spirit, no amount of human persuasion can bring a sinner to saving faith. "The Spirit of God is the great agent in the new creation," Owen wrote. "As He moved upon the face of the waters in the first creation, bringing order out of chaos and light out of darkness, so He moves upon the hearts of sinners, bringing life out of death and faith out of unbelief. Our arguments may prepare the soil, but only the Spirit can plant the seed and give the increase." Trusting the Holy Spirit does not mean we should be passive or lazy in our witness. It means we should be diligent in our preparation, faithful in our proclamation, and humble in our expectation — knowing that the results belong to God.

The seventh principle is: Be prepared with Scripture. The Word of God is "quick, and powerful, and sharper than any twoedged sword, piercing even to the dividing asunder of soul and spirit, and of the joints and marrow, and is a discerner of the thoughts and intents of the heart" (Hebrews 4:12). Human arguments may be impressive, but the Word of God carries divine authority and divine power. Memorize key passages of Scripture that you can use in conversations about faith. Here are some essential verses every young apologist should know by heart:

On the existence and nature of God: "The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork" (Psalm 19:1). "For the invisible things of him from the creation of the world are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made, even his eternal power and Godhead; so that they are without excuse" (Romans 1:20).

On the sinfulness of man: "For all have sinned, and come short of the glory of God" (Romans 3:23). "The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately wicked: who can know it?" (Jeremiah 17:9).

On the penalty for sin: "For the wages of sin is death; but the gift of God is eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord" (Romans 6:23). "And as it is appointed unto men once to die, but after this the judgment" (Hebrews 9:27).

On the love of God and the work of Christ: "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life" (John 3:16). "But God commendeth his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8). "He that spared not his own Son, but delivered him up for us all, how shall he not with him also freely give us all things?" (Romans 8:32).

On salvation by grace through faith: "For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: Not of works, lest any man should boast" (Ephesians 2:8-9). "Not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to his mercy he saved us" (Titus 3:5).

On the invitation of Christ: "Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest" (Matthew 11:28). "Him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out" (John 6:37). "Behold, I stand at the door, and knock: if any man hear my voice, and open the door, I will come in to him, and will sup with him, and he with me" (Revelation 3:20).

The Puritan pastor Thomas Watson urged believers to hide the Word of God in their hearts so that it would be ready when needed: "The Word hidden in the heart is a sword in the hand — ready for use when the enemy attacks or when a hungry soul asks for bread. The Christian who knows his Bible is armed for every encounter."

Now let us consider some practical counsel for sharing your faith in everyday situations.

First, look for natural opportunities. You do not need to ambush people with the gospel. The best conversations about faith arise naturally — from shared experiences, from questions people ask, from current events, from personal struggles, from moments of honesty and vulnerability. When a classmate mentions that a grandparent has died, you have an opportunity to talk about hope beyond the grave. When a friend expresses anxiety about the future, you can share the peace that comes from trusting in a sovereign God. When a conversation turns to questions of meaning, purpose, or morality, you can gently introduce the Christian perspective. Be alert to these opportunities, and ask God to create them.

Second, build genuine friendships. People are far more open to hearing about your faith from someone they know, trust, and respect than from a stranger. Invest in real relationships — be a loyal friend, a patient listener, a generous helper, and a person of integrity. The Puritan pastor Cotton Mather (1663-1728) advocated what he called "doing good" as a way of opening doors for the gospel. In his influential treatise "Bonifacius: An Essay upon the Good," Mather urged Christians to look for practical ways to serve their neighbors — lending books, visiting the sick, helping the poor, tutoring those who struggle — and to use these acts of kindness as bridges for spiritual conversation. Mather wrote: "The best way to win a man's ear is first to win his heart. And the way to win his heart is to do him good."

Third, share your personal testimony. Your story of how Christ has changed your life is one of the most powerful tools you possess. It is impossible to argue with someone's personal experience. When you say, "I used to struggle with fear and anxiety, but God has given me a peace I cannot explain," or "I used to have no sense of purpose, but since I came to know Christ, I know why I exist" — these are statements that no one can refute. They are your story, your experience, and your testimony to the reality of God's work in your life. The Apostle Paul used his personal testimony repeatedly in his evangelistic ministry (Acts 22:1-21; 26:1-23). If it was good enough for Paul, it is good enough for you.

Fourth, know the basic gospel message and be able to communicate it clearly. The gospel is not complicated: God created us for a relationship with Him. We sinned and broke that relationship. The penalty for sin is death. God sent His Son to die in our place. Through faith in Christ, we can be forgiven and restored to God. This message can be communicated in two minutes or in two hours, depending on the situation. Practice articulating it clearly and simply, so that when the opportunity arises, you are ready.

Fifth, learn to handle disagreement gracefully. Not every conversation about faith will end in agreement. Some people will listen politely and walk away unconvinced. Others will argue vigorously and even become hostile. In every case, maintain your composure, your courtesy, and your love. Never resort to personal attacks, mockery, or condescension. Remember Paul's instruction to Timothy: "And the servant of the Lord must not strive; but be gentle unto all men, apt to teach, patient, In meekness instructing those that oppose themselves; if God peradventure will give them repentance to the acknowledging of the truth" (2 Timothy 2:24-25). Notice that Paul says "if God peradventure will give them repentance." The outcome is in God's hands. Your responsibility is to be faithful, gentle, and patient.

Sixth, know when to stop. Not every conversation needs to be pushed to a conclusion. Sometimes the wisest thing you can do is to plant a seed and trust God to give the growth. "I have planted, Apollos watered; but God gave the increase" (1 Corinthians 3:6). You may be one link in a long chain of witnesses that God is using to draw someone to Himself. Your conversation may not be the one that leads to conversion, but it may be the one that removes a critical barrier, answers a nagging question, or plants a thought that will germinate for years before bearing fruit. Be faithful in your moment, and trust God with the timeline.

The Puritan theologian Thomas Manton (1620-1677) offered beautiful counsel on this point: "The husbandman doth not reap the same day he soweth. He waiteth for the harvest with patience, knowing that the seed must lie in the ground through rain and cold before it springs up. So must we wait upon God for the fruit of our labors, casting our bread upon the waters and trusting that after many days it shall be found."

Seventh, be willing to follow up. A single conversation rarely leads to conversion. Most people come to faith through a process — a series of conversations, experiences, and encounters that gradually move them closer to Christ. Be willing to continue the conversation over weeks, months, or even years. Lend books. Share articles. Invite people to church or to a Bible study. Keep praying for them. Show them, through your consistent presence and your faithful love, that you care about them as people, not just as projects.

The Puritan minister John Bunyan (1628-1688), author of "The Pilgrim's Progress," understood that the journey to faith is often long and winding. In his own autobiography, "Grace Abounding to the Chief of Sinners," Bunyan described his conversion as a process that took years — years of struggle, doubt, despair, and gradually dawning understanding. He was influenced by numerous people along the way: the godly women of Bedford whose conversation about the new birth arrested his attention, his pastor John Gifford who patiently instructed him, and the authors of Puritan books whose writings illuminated his darkness. Bunyan's story reminds us that evangelism is rarely a single event. It is usually a process, and God uses many different people and means to bring a soul to Himself. Be willing to be one link in that chain, and trust God to complete the work He has begun.

Eighth, pray without ceasing for the people you are trying to reach. The Apostle Paul modeled this practice throughout his ministry. In nearly every one of his epistles, he mentions that he prays constantly for the churches and individuals he is addressing. "For this cause I bow my knees unto the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ," he wrote to the Ephesians, "That he would grant you, according to the riches of his glory, to be strengthened with might by his Spirit in the inner man; That Christ may dwell in your hearts by faith" (Ephesians 3:14, 16-17). Keep a prayer list of people you are praying for — friends, family members, classmates, neighbors — and bring their names before God daily. Ask God to open their eyes, soften their hearts, and draw them to Himself. Prayer is the most powerful evangelistic tool in the universe, for it enlists the power of the Almighty on behalf of those we love.

The Puritan pastor John Flavel (1627-1691) wrote with great conviction about the power of prayer in evangelism: "Many souls have been brought to Christ not by the eloquence of the preacher but by the prayers of the saints. The closet is the furnace where the weapons of spiritual warfare are forged. Pray, and pray again, and never cease to pray, for the God who hears prayer is the God who saves souls." Flavel's words remind us that behind every effective witness stands a praying Christian — someone who has wrestled with God in secret before speaking to men in public.

Let us also consider some common mistakes to avoid when sharing your faith.

Avoid the mistake of treating apologetics as a competition. The goal is not to defeat your opponent but to win your neighbor. If you "win" the argument but lose the relationship, you have accomplished nothing of eternal value.

Avoid the mistake of answering questions that no one is asking. Listen to what people are actually saying, not what you assume they are thinking. Meet people where they are, not where you think they should be.

Avoid the mistake of being argumentative about secondary issues. Not every theological debate is worth having in an evangelistic conversation. The doctrine of predestination, the age of the earth, the details of eschatology — these may be important topics for study within the church, but they are rarely helpful in a conversation with an unbeliever who does not yet believe that God exists or that the Bible is true. Focus on the essentials: the existence of God, the sinfulness of man, the person and work of Christ, and the offer of salvation by grace through faith.

Avoid the mistake of relying solely on arguments and neglecting prayer. Every evangelistic conversation should be undergirded by prayer — prayer before, during, and after. Pray for the person you are talking to. Pray for wisdom and the right words. Pray for the Holy Spirit to work in their heart. And pray for yourself, that you would be a faithful and loving witness. The Puritan pastor Samuel Rutherford (1600-1661) wrote from his exile: "Pray for Christ's ambassadors, for they carry the King's message through hostile territory. They need the prayers of the saints as the soldier needs his armor."

Finally, remember that you are not alone in this work. You stand in a tradition that stretches back two thousand years — from the apostles Peter and Paul, through the church fathers Athanasius and Augustine, through the medieval witnesses like Wycliffe and Hus, through the Reformers Luther and Calvin, through the Puritans Owen, Baxter, Bunyan, and Flavel, through the missionaries Carey, Hudson Taylor, and Adoniram Judson, through the apologists C.S. Lewis, Francis Schaeffer, and countless others — to the present day. Millions of brilliant, faithful Christians have walked this path before you. Their arguments, their writings, their examples, and their prayers are your inheritance. Draw from them freely.

As Peter's first epistle reminds us, the call to defend the faith is given to all believers, not just to pastors and scholars. Peter was writing to ordinary Christians — some of them slaves, many of them poor, all of them facing persecution. And yet he told them to "be ready always to give an answer." Always. To every man. This is a universal call. You, students, are not too young to be faithful witnesses. You are not too inexperienced to share the gospel. You are not too ordinary to be used by God. In fact, God delights in using the weak things of the world to confound the mighty, and the foolish things of the world to confound the wise (1 Corinthians 1:27). Your youth is not an obstacle — it is an advantage, for Timothy was told: "Let no man despise thy youth; but be thou an example of the believers, in word, in conversation, in charity, in spirit, in faith, in purity" (1 Timothy 4:12).

The Puritan pastor William Perkins (1558-1602), one of the earliest and most influential Puritans, wrote a famous treatise on the calling of every Christian to bear witness to the truth. Perkins argued that every believer, regardless of age, education, or social standing, has both the privilege and the duty of commending Christ to others. "The meanest Christian," Perkins wrote, "who knows Christ and loves Christ, is a better witness to His power than the greatest philosopher who knows Him not. For the testimony of experience outweighs the testimony of speculation." Perkins believed that the simplest testimony of a transformed life carries more persuasive power than the most elaborate philosophical argument. And history has proved him right. The spread of Christianity across the Roman Empire in its first three centuries was accomplished not primarily by professional clergy or trained apologists but by ordinary believers — merchants, soldiers, slaves, women, artisans — who simply told others what Christ had done for them.

And remember, above all, the promise of Christ Himself: "Lo, I am with you alway, even unto the end of the world" (Matthew 28:20). You go not in your own strength but in His. You speak not in your own wisdom but in the power of the Spirit. And you serve not your own purposes but the eternal purposes of the God who loved you and gave His Son for you. Go, therefore, with confidence and with joy. The fields are white unto harvest, and the Lord of the harvest is with you.

--- Activity ---

With a family member, friend, or study partner, practice a role-play conversation about faith. Follow these steps:

Step 1: Have the other person play the role of a thoughtful, respectful non-Christian who raises one of the objections studied in this apologetics unit (the problem of evil, the reliability of the Bible, the exclusivity of Christ, the relationship between science and faith, a moral objection to Christianity, or a question about other religions).

Step 2: Practice responding using the six principles discussed today. Listen carefully before responding. Ask clarifying questions. Be honest about what you know and what you do not know. Keep the conversation pointed toward the gospel. Be gentle and respectful throughout.

Step 3: After the role-play, spend ten minutes discussing what went well and what could be improved. Ask your partner: Did I listen well? Did I understand your actual concern? Was I respectful? Did I eventually point to Christ?

Step 4: Repeat with a different objection, switching roles if desired.

Step 5: Write a one-page reflection on what you learned from the exercise, including at least one specific area where you want to grow in your ability to share your faith. Include a Scripture verse that encourages you in this area.

Materials needed: Bible (KJV), notebook or writing paper, and a willing conversation partner.

--- Closing ---

You have been given the greatest message the world has ever heard — the good news that God saves sinners through the death and resurrection of His Son. Share this message with confidence, for it is the power of God unto salvation. Share it with humility, for you yourself are a sinner saved by grace. Share it with love, for the people you speak to are precious souls for whom Christ died. Share it with patience, for the fruit of faithful witness often takes time to ripen. And share it with joy, for there is no greater privilege on earth than to be an ambassador for the King of Kings. As the Puritan John Flavel wrote: "To bring a soul to Christ is the highest achievement of any man or woman on earth." Go, therefore, and be that man or woman. Until next time, students.
