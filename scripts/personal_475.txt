PURITAN GOLD -- My Growth, Day 14
"The Ministry of Encouragement"
Pastoral Day — Building Up the Body of Christ


Welcome. Today is a Pastoral day. Our subject is one the Puritans treasured: the practical ministry of encouragement — building up fellow believers through words, actions, and faithful presence.

Let us read Hebrews 10:24-25 (KJV):

"And let us consider one another to provoke unto love and to good works: Not forsaking the assembling of ourselves together, as the manner of some is; but exhorting one another: and so much the more, as ye see the day approaching."


The Puritans understood that the Christian life is not a solo endeavor. We are members of a body, branches of a vine, stones in a building. And one of the most vital functions within the body is the ministry of encouragement — what the New Testament calls paraklesis, which means coming alongside to strengthen, comfort, and exhort.

Richard Baxter devoted significant attention to this subject in his Reformed Pastor and his Christian Directory. He identified several forms that encouragement takes in the life of the church and in daily relationships.

First, the encouragement of presence. Sometimes the most powerful thing you can do for a struggling brother or sister is simply to be there. Job's friends, for all their later failures, got one thing right: they sat with him for seven days in silence. Richard Sibbes wrote that the ministry of presence — showing up, sitting down, staying close — is often more valuable than the ministry of words. Many people do not need advice. They need company.

Second, the encouragement of Scripture. The Puritans were people of the Book, and they believed that the most lasting encouragement comes from the Word of God. When a friend is suffering, share a promise. When a brother is doubting, point to a truth. When a sister is afraid, read a psalm. Human words are comforting, but divine words are transformative. Thomas Goodwin urged believers to build a mental treasury of comforting Scriptures — verses stored in the heart for the moment when someone nearby is sinking.

Third, the encouragement of testimony. Sharing your own experience of God's faithfulness is a powerful form of encouragement. When you tell a fellow believer, "I went through something similar, and God was faithful," you are not offering a platitude. You are offering evidence. The Puritans valued personal testimony as a means of strengthening faith, provided it was offered with humility and pointed toward God's glory rather than the speaker's heroism.

Fourth, the encouragement of practical help. Encouragement is not only verbal. James writes that faith without works is dead, and the same is true of encouragement without action. A meal brought to a struggling family, a note written to a discouraged pastor, an offer to watch someone's children so they can rest — these tangible acts of love speak louder than any sermon.

Fifth, the encouragement of honest love. Sometimes encouragement requires speaking hard truths. The Puritans distinguished between flattery and faithful encouragement. Flattery tells people what they want to hear. Faithful encouragement tells people what they need to hear — but does so with such love and gentleness that the truth is received as a gift rather than a wound. Proverbs 27:6 says, "Faithful are the wounds of a friend."

Baxter warned against two errors in the ministry of encouragement. The first is harshness — approaching struggling people with judgment, impatience, or condescension. The person who needs encouragement already feels weak. Do not add to their burden by making them feel stupid or faithless. The second error is superficiality — offering quick, glib answers to deep problems. "Just trust God" or "everything happens for a reason" may be technically true, but delivered without thought or empathy, they can be devastating. Engage deeply. Listen carefully. And then speak — with wisdom, with compassion, and with the authority of Scripture.

The writer of Hebrews says we should do this so much the more as we see the day approaching. As the days grow darker, the need for encouragement grows greater. In a world of isolation, anxiety, and spiritual exhaustion, the Christian who masters the ministry of encouragement is a gift beyond price.


Who needs your encouragement today? A spouse, a child, a friend, a pastor, a stranger? Go to them. Be present. Speak truth. Offer help. Lord, make me an encourager. Give me eyes to see the weary, ears to hear the hurting, and a tongue seasoned with grace. Let me build up what the world tears down. Amen.
