PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Guided Puritan Reading, Day 4
"The Necessity of Mortification"
John Owen — Mortification of Sin, Chapter 2

---

Good evening. There is a reason the Puritans read so many books alongside their Bibles. They knew that God raises up teachers in every generation to illuminate His Word. Tonight we sit with one of those teachers and listen carefully.

Tonight we draw from the work of John Owen, specifically from "The Mortification of Sin." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.

--- Scripture ---

Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Colossians 3:5:

"Mortify therefore your members which are upon the earth; fornication, uncleanness, inordinate affection, evil concupiscence, and covetousness, which is idolatry."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.

--- Guided Reading ---

Having established the foundation in his opening chapter, Owen now presses the question: why is mortification necessary? His answer is comprehensive and urgent. He gives several reasons why the constant, diligent mortification of sin is an absolute necessity for every believer.

Owen's first argument is that indwelling sin, if not mortified, will produce the 'deeds of the flesh.' Though the believer has been freed from sin's dominion, sin's presence remains and is always active. Owen uses the language of warfare: sin is an enemy that never calls a truce. If you cease fighting, sin does not cease advancing. Owen writes that 'sin is always acting, always conceiving, always seducing and tempting.' The believer who thinks he can take a holiday from mortification is like a soldier who falls asleep on guard duty: the enemy will not wait for him to wake.

Owen's second argument concerns the progressive nature of unmortified sin. A sin that is not mortified does not remain static; it grows. Owen traces the progression: first, there is an inclination or desire; then, that desire is entertained in the mind; then, it takes hold of the affections; then, it issues in action; then, the action becomes habitual; and finally, the habit hardens the conscience. At each stage, the sin becomes more deeply rooted and more difficult to uproot. Owen warns that the man who does not fight his sin at the stage of inclination will find himself fighting it at the stage of habit, by which time the battle is far more desperate.

Owen illustrates this with a vivid metaphor. Indwelling sin is like a river. If you dam it at its source, it is a manageable task. But if you let it flow unchecked, it gathers force from every tributary, and soon it is a torrent that sweeps away everything in its path. The man who says 'this sin is small and does not trouble me' is the man who will one day say 'this sin has mastered me and I cannot escape.'

Owen's third argument addresses the spiritual consequences of unmortified sin. He identifies several effects: first, the loss of peace. The believer who harbors sin in his heart will not enjoy the peace of God. The Spirit is grieved, the conscience is troubled, and the soul is restless. Second, the loss of strength. Just as a disease weakens the body, unmortified sin weakens the soul. The believer finds that he cannot pray with fervency, cannot read the Word with profit, cannot serve with joy. Third, the loss of usefulness. The man whose life is compromised by unmortified sin is of little use to the church or to his family. His testimony is weakened, his counsel is unreliable, and his example is confused.

Owen also addresses those who claim that since they are justified by grace, mortification is unnecessary. This, Owen says, is a profound misunderstanding of grace. Grace does not remove the duty of mortification; it provides the power for it. The justified man is not free to sin; he is free to fight sin. And the very grace that justified him also sanctifies him — not automatically, but through the diligent use of the means God has appointed.

Owen's fourth argument is perhaps the most sobering: unmortified sin, if left unchecked, may lead to scandalous falls. Owen has seen this in his own pastoral experience. Good men, earnest men, gifted men, who neglected the duty of mortification and eventually fell into grievous sin that brought reproach upon the name of Christ. Owen warns that no amount of theological knowledge, ministerial giftedness, or public reputation can protect a man who is not daily killing his sin.

The conclusion of Owen's argument is a call to arms. 'Be killing sin or it will be killing you.' This is one of the most famous sentences in all of Puritan literature, and it captures the essence of Owen's entire treatise. There is no middle ground. There is no peaceful coexistence with sin. You are either actively putting it to death, or it is actively putting you to death. The question is not whether you will fight, but whether you will fight or surrender.

Let this truth settle into your soul today. The battle against sin is not optional. It is not for the spiritually elite. It is the daily, urgent, lifelong calling of every believer. And the resources for the fight are not your own; they are the resources of the Spirit of God, given freely to all who ask.

--- Reflection ---

The Puritans kept spiritual journals. Consider writing your answers to these questions.

Question 1: Is there a sin in my life that I have been tolerating rather than mortifying, assuming it is too small to matter?

Question 2: Can I trace Owen's progression in any area of my own experience — from inclination to entertainment to action to habit?

Question 3: How does Owen's warning that 'be killing sin or it will be killing you' land in my heart today?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.

--- Closing Prayer ---

Lord, I confess that I have been negligent in the fight against sin. I have tolerated what I should have killed. Awaken me to the urgency of mortification and equip me by Your Spirit to engage in this battle with fresh resolve. For the glory of Christ, Amen.

Go in peace. The God who began a good work in you will bring it to completion.
