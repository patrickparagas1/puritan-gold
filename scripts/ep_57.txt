PURITAN GOLD -- Study Devotional, Day 26
Series: Romans
"Put On the Lord Jesus Christ"
Love as the fulfillment of the law and the urgency of the hour


Good evening, student of the Word. Every time we open the Scriptures, we stand on holy ground. The God of the universe has spoken, and He invites us to listen, to understand, and to respond. Let us listen well tonight.

We are studying the book of Romans together. Tonight's lesson is titled "Put On the Lord Jesus Christ." Each session builds on what came before, so if you have been following along, you will see how tonight's passage connects to the larger argument of the book.


Let us read our passage for tonight. It comes from Romans 13:8-14:

"Owe no man any thing, but to love one another: for he that loveth another hath fulfilled the law. For this, Thou shalt not commit adultery, Thou shalt not kill, Thou shalt not steal, Thou shalt not bear false witness, Thou shalt not covet; and if there be any other commandment, it is briefly comprehended in this saying, namely, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself. Love worketh no ill to his neighbour: therefore love is the fulfilling of the law. And that, knowing the time, that now it is high time to awake out of sleep: for now is our salvation nearer than when we believed. The night is far spent, the day is at hand: let us therefore cast off the works of darkness, and let us put on the armour of light. Let us walk honestly, as in the day; not in rioting and drunkenness, not in chambering and wantonness, not in strife and envying. But put ye on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make not provision for the flesh, to fulfil the lusts thereof."

Read that passage again to yourself. Mark any words or phrases that stand out. The Puritans read with a pen in hand, always ready to note what God was showing them.


Here is the key verse for tonight's study. Let it anchor everything else we discuss.

"But put ye on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make not provision for the flesh, to fulfil the lusts thereof." -- Romans 13:14

This verse is the lens through which we will examine the rest of tonight's passage. Keep it in the front of your mind as we proceed.


Having read the text, let us explore its meaning in depth.

Thomas Goodwin offers valuable commentary on this passage. The Puritans were expositors of the highest order, and their insights remain remarkably fresh.

Thomas Goodwin regarded Romans 13:8-14 as one of the most powerful passages for motivating holy living in all of Paul's epistles. He was struck by the connection between love and law in verses 8-10, arguing that Paul does not abolish the moral law but fulfills it through a higher principle — love. Goodwin observed that the law says 'thou shalt not,' imposing external prohibitions. Love says 'thou canst not,' creating internal impossibilities. The person who genuinely loves their neighbor cannot steal from them, lie to them, or harm them — not because a rule forbids it but because love makes it unthinkable.

Goodwin gave extended treatment to the eschatological urgency of verses 11-12. He argued that Paul lived with a constant awareness of the nearness of Christ's return, and that this awareness should shape the moral intensity of every believer's life. 'The night is far spent, the day is at hand.' Goodwin saw in this a powerful motivation for holiness: we are living in the last hours of the night. Dawn is about to break. The coming of the Lord is closer today than it has ever been. This should produce not anxiety but alertness — a wakeful readiness to meet the King.

Goodwin was especially moved by the final verse: 'But put ye on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make not provision for the flesh, to fulfil the lusts thereof.' He saw in this command the positive and negative aspects of sanctification. Positively, we clothe ourselves in Christ — His character, His priorities, His love become our daily wardrobe. Negatively, we refuse to make arrangements for sin — we do not set up the conditions under which temptation will thrive. Goodwin famously noted that this was the very verse that God used to convert Augustine. The young man, torn between Christ and his passions, heard a child's voice saying 'Take up and read.' He opened to Romans 13:13-14 and was instantly converted. Goodwin took this as evidence that these verses carry a peculiar power to break the chains of sin and draw souls to Christ.

Romans 13:8-14 brings together three powerful themes: love as the fulfillment of the law, the urgency of the eschatological hour, and the call to clothe ourselves in Christ. Together they create an irresistible case for radical, joyful, urgent holy living.

Verses 8-10 establish love as the debt that can never be fully paid: 'Owe no man any thing, but to love one another: for he that loveth another hath fulfilled the law.' Paul is not forbidding credit or financial obligations (he has just commanded paying taxes). Rather, he is saying that of all debts, the debt of love is the one that can never be discharged in full. You can pay off a financial debt, but you can never finish loving your neighbor. The obligation of love is perpetual.

Paul then demonstrates how love fulfills the moral law by listing commandments from the second table: 'Thou shalt not commit adultery, Thou shalt not kill, Thou shalt not steal, Thou shalt not bear false witness, Thou shalt not covet.' All of these, and any other commandment, are 'briefly comprehended in this saying, namely, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself' (Leviticus 19:18). Jesus taught the same: the whole law hangs on two commandments — love God and love your neighbor (Matthew 22:37-40).

Verse 10 explains why: 'Love worketh no ill to his neighbour: therefore love is the fulfilling of the law.' Love does not merely avoid harm; it actively seeks the neighbor's good. When you truly love someone, you do not need a rule telling you not to steal from them, lie about them, or covet what belongs to them. Love makes the prohibition unnecessary by making the sin unthinkable. This does not mean the law is abolished; it means the law is internalized. The Spirit writes God's requirements on the heart through love.

Verses 11-12 shift to eschatological urgency: 'And that, knowing the time, that now it is high time to awake out of sleep: for now is our salvation nearer than when we believed.' Paul calls believers to wake up. Spiritual drowsiness — the lethargy that allows sin to go unchallenged and growth to stagnate — is inappropriate given the hour. 'Our salvation is nearer than when we believed.' Every day brings us closer to Christ's return. Every sunrise shortens the distance between the present age and the age to come.

'The night is far spent, the day is at hand.' We live in the night — the present age of sin, suffering, and spiritual warfare. But dawn is breaking. The day — the return of Christ, the resurrection, the new creation — is approaching. This awareness should produce moral urgency: 'Let us therefore cast off the works of darkness, and let us put on the armour of light.' The imagery is of a soldier rising from sleep, throwing off his nightclothes, and putting on his armor. The 'works of darkness' are listed in verse 13: 'rioting and drunkenness' (dissipation and excess), 'chambering and wantonness' (sexual immorality), 'strife and envying' (relational conflict). These belong to the night. They have no place in the lives of people who are children of the day.

Verse 14 delivers the climactic command, one of the most powerful in all of Scripture: 'But put ye on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make not provision for the flesh, to fulfil the lusts thereof.' There are two commands here, one positive and one negative.

Positively: put on the Lord Jesus Christ. This means to clothe yourself in Christ — to adopt His character, His mindset, His priorities as your daily attire. In Galatians 3:27, Paul says, 'As many of you as have been baptized into Christ have put on Christ.' Positionally, we already wear Christ. But practically, we must put Him on daily — choosing to think as Christ thinks, speak as Christ speaks, act as Christ acts. He is not merely our Savior but our pattern, our model, our clothing.

Negatively: make not provision for the flesh. The word 'provision' (pronoia) means forethought, advance planning. Do not think ahead about how to satisfy sinful desires. Do not set up situations where temptation will be maximized. Do not leave doors open to sin. If lust is your temptation, do not browse content that feeds it. If anger is your temptation, do not cultivate relationships that provoke it. If greed is your temptation, do not browse catalogs of things you do not need. Cut off the supply lines of sin.

This verse was instrumental in the conversion of Augustine of Hippo. In his Confessions, Augustine describes how, torn between faith and his enslaving sins, he heard a child's voice chanting 'Tolle lege' — take up and read. He opened the Scriptures and his eyes fell on Romans 13:13-14. 'Not in rioting and drunkenness, not in chambering and wantonness... But put ye on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make not provision for the flesh.' The chains broke. Augustine was converted. Fifteen centuries later, these words still carry the same transforming power.


Knowledge puffs up, Paul warns, but love builds up. Let us ensure that what we have studied tonight leads not to pride but to faithful, loving obedience.

Romans 13:8-14 calls for urgent, love-driven, Christ-clothed living. Here are specific applications.

First, pay the debt of love. You owe love to everyone you encounter today — your spouse, your children, your coworkers, your neighbors, even your enemies. Love is not optional; it is a perpetual debt. Choose one specific act of love you will perform for each person in your immediate circle this week. Not just feelings of love — actions of love. 'Let us not love in word, neither in tongue; but in deed and in truth' (1 John 3:18).

Second, wake up. 'It is high time to awake out of sleep.' Are you spiritually drowsy? Has routine dulled your spiritual sensitivity? Has comfort made you complacent? The day is at hand. Christ is returning. This is no time for lethargy. Set an alarm — literally or figuratively — to remind yourself throughout the day that you are living in the last hours of the night. Let urgency fuel your prayers, your witness, and your obedience.

Third, put on Christ this morning. Before you put on your clothes, put on Christ. Ask: 'Lord, how would You handle today's challenges? What would Your response be to that difficult person? How would You spend this evening?' Clothe yourself in His compassion, His patience, His courage, His love. Make Him your wardrobe.

Fourth, cut off provision for the flesh. Identify one specific way you have been making provision for sinful desires and eliminate it today. Delete the app. End the subscription. Avoid the place. Change the routine. Do not merely resist temptation — remove its supply lines. Make no provision for the flesh to fulfill its lusts.

Write down one concrete action you will take this week in response to tonight's study. Do not let this truth remain theoretical. The Puritans measured the success of a sermon not by how much the hearer learned but by how much the hearer changed.


Lord Jesus Christ, we put You on today. Clothe us in Your righteousness, Your love, Your holiness, Your courage. Wake us from spiritual slumber — for the night is far spent and the day is at hand. Help us to cast off every work of darkness and put on the armor of light. Teach us to love our neighbors with a love that fulfills every law. And give us the wisdom and strength to make no provision for the flesh. You are our clothing, our armor, our identity. May all who see us see Christ in us, the hope of glory. Amen.

Until our next study, walk in the truth you have received.
