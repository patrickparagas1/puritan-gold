PURITAN GOLD -- Together: Couples Devotional, Day 13
"Put Away All Bitterness"
Ephesians 4:31 — Guarding Against Resentment


Welcome, dear couple. Tonight we confront one of the most dangerous enemies of married love — an enemy that often goes undetected until it has done enormous damage.

Our Scripture tonight is Ephesians 4:31:

"Let all bitterness, and wrath, and anger, and clamour, and evil speaking, be put away from you, with all malice."

Paul lists six poisons here, and every one of them is deadly to a marriage.


The Puritans understood bitterness as the root from which all the other poisons grow. Bitterness is resentment that has been nursed rather than released — a wound that has been rehearsed rather than healed. And in the close quarters of marriage, bitterness finds fertile soil.

William Gouge described the progression with clinical precision. First comes the offense — real or perceived. Then comes the hurt, which is natural and understandable. Then comes the choice: will you bring this hurt to God and to your spouse for healing, or will you bury it? If you bury it, it does not die. It festers. It becomes bitterness — a deep-rooted resentment that colors every interaction, poisons every conversation, and eventually erupts in one of the other forms Paul names: wrath (sudden explosive anger), anger (slow-burning hostility), clamour (raised voices and harsh arguments), evil speaking (cutting words designed to wound), and malice (a settled desire to see the other person suffer).

Richard Baxter warned that bitterness in marriage is especially dangerous because it is so easily justified. "Of course I am bitter," a spouse might say. "Look at what they did to me." And the offense may be real. The pain may be genuine. But bitterness is never justified, because bitterness always does more harm to the one who carries it than to the one who caused it. Bitterness is a fire that burns the one holding it.

The Puritans offered a clear remedy: put it away. Paul does not say "manage your bitterness" or "learn to live with it." He says put it away — actively, decisively, completely. And the power to do this comes from the next verse, which we studied two nights ago: "Be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, even as God for Christ's sake hath forgiven you."

Thomas Gataker counseled married couples to conduct regular "bitterness checks" — to periodically ask each other, with genuine openness: "Is there anything I have done that is causing you pain? Is there any resentment building in your heart that we need to address?" This practice of preemptive honesty prevents small hurts from becoming deep roots of bitterness.

The Puritans also recognized that some offenses in marriage are too serious to be resolved by the couple alone. In cases of deep betrayal, persistent sin, or patterns of abuse, they counseled seeking the help of a wise pastor or trusted elder. Forgiveness is always required, but reconciliation is a process that sometimes needs outside help.

Tonight, take an honest inventory of your hearts. Is there any bitterness hiding in the corners of your marriage? Any old hurt that has never been fully addressed? Any resentment that surfaces during arguments? If so, tonight is the night to begin the work of putting it away — not by pretending it does not exist, but by bringing it into the light and releasing it to God.


Discuss together with gentleness:

Question 1: Is there any bitterness or resentment in your heart toward your spouse — even a small amount? Can you name it honestly and gently?

Question 2: When conflict arises, which of Paul's six poisons are you most likely to reach for — wrath, anger, clamour, evil speaking, or malice? What would it look like to "put it away"?

Question 3: Would you be willing to establish a regular practice of checking in with each other about unresolved hurts? What would that look like?


Pray together for deliverance from bitterness. If there is a specific hurt that needs healing, name it before the Lord together. Ask God for the grace to forgive completely and the wisdom to prevent future bitterness from taking root. Pray for tenderness to replace every trace of hardness.


May the Lord who bore all our bitterness on the cross set you free from every root of resentment. May your hearts be soft toward each other, and may your home be a bitterness-free zone. The peace of Christ rule in your hearts. Goodnight.
