PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Pastoral Encouragement, Day 14
"Redeeming the Time"
Puritan Counsel on the Stewardship of Time


Good evening. Before we begin, take a deep breath. Release the tension of the day. You are in a safe place, a place where truth is spoken in love and where the gospel meets you exactly where you are.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Ephesians 5:15-16:

"See then that ye walk circumspectly, not as fools, but as wise, Redeeming the time, because the days are evil."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


Paul's command to 'redeem the time' carries the sense of buying back something that has been lost or squandered. The image is of a merchant seizing a fleeting opportunity: the deal will not last; he must act now or lose forever. So it is with time. Every hour that passes is an hour that cannot be recovered. The Puritans understood this with a gravity that our age has largely lost.

Richard Baxter opens his section on time in 'A Christian Directory' with a statement that should stop every reader in his tracks: 'Time is so precious that it is a great sin to waste it.' Baxter does not treat time management as a productivity hack; he treats it as a matter of stewardship before God. You will give an account for every hour you have spent. Not merely for the sins you committed in those hours, but for the good you failed to do because you squandered them.

Baxter identifies several common time-wasters that rob the Christian man of his effectiveness. The first is excessive sleep. Baxter is not an enemy of rest; he commends sufficient sleep as necessary for health. But he observes that many men sleep far more than they need, not from physical necessity but from sloth. The man who lingers in bed long after his body has been refreshed is stealing time from his duties.

The second is unnecessary recreation. Again, Baxter is not opposed to leisure. He writes that moderate recreation refreshes the body and mind for further service. But recreation that dominates the schedule, that crowds out prayer, that leaves no time for family worship or service to the church, has become an idol. In our day, this manifests most obviously in screen time: the hours consumed by social media, streaming entertainment, and endless scrolling.

The third is idle conversation. Baxter notes that many men spend hours in conversation that produces no good: gossip, complaint, trivial debate, and the rehashing of matters already settled. He commends speech that is purposeful, edifying, and directed toward the glory of God.

Baxter then offers practical counsel for redeeming the time. His first direction is to begin each day with a deliberate plan. Know what you intend to do before you do it. Baxter commends the practice of rising early and spending the first portion of the day in prayer and Scripture, setting the spiritual course for everything that follows.

His second direction is to prioritize the things of greatest importance. Baxter establishes a clear hierarchy: God first, then family, then church, then vocation, then all else. The man who inverts this order — who gives his best hours to work and his leftover minutes to God and family — has fundamentally misallocated his most precious resource.

His third direction is to fight against procrastination. Baxter writes that the man who says 'I will do it tomorrow' is a man who has already begun to waste today. The duty that lies before you now is the duty that God has appointed for now. Do not defer what can and should be done today.

Jonathan Edwards, in his famous 'Resolutions,' provides a powerful example of Puritan time stewardship. Resolution number 5 reads: 'Resolved, never to lose one moment of time; but improve it the most profitable way I possibly can.' Edwards was not a mechanical legalist about time; he was a man who understood that life is short, eternity is long, and every moment is a gift to be used for God's glory.

The Puritan Thomas Brooks writes: 'Time is a talent that God will call us to account for. He that wastes his time wastes his soul.' This is not meant to produce anxiety but urgency. You have been given a finite number of hours on this earth. Use them well. Not merely productively in the worldly sense, but purposefully in the eternal sense: knowing God, loving your family, serving the church, and glorifying Christ in all things.


Now let us turn the lens inward. What is God saying to you through tonight's study?

Question 1: If I were to audit how I spend my time over a typical week, what would the data reveal about my true priorities?

Question 2: Which of Baxter's time-wasters — excessive sleep, unnecessary recreation, or idle conversation — is most applicable to my life?

Question 3: What would it look like to begin each day this week with a deliberate plan that puts God and family before all other obligations?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Lord, forgive me for the time I have wasted and cannot recover. Teach me to number my days, that I may apply my heart unto wisdom. Help me to redeem the time You have given me, investing it in things of eternal value. For Christ's sake, Amen.

Press on, dear saint. The race is worth running, and the prize is Christ Himself.
