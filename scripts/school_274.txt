PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 14
"The Problem of Evil"
Apologetics for Young Christians — Lesson 14


Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we confront what is widely regarded as the most formidable objection to the Christian faith — the problem of evil and suffering. This is not merely an academic question. It is a deeply personal one. Every human being who has ever lived has experienced pain, loss, disappointment, or injustice, and has wondered — if only for a moment — why a good God would allow such things. The question has haunted philosophers for millennia. It has shaken the faith of believers and hardened the hearts of skeptics. And it deserves the most careful, the most honest, and the most compassionate answer we can give. Let us pray and begin.

Almighty God, our heavenly Father, we approach this difficult subject with humility and reverence. Thou art good, and Thou doest good; yet we live in a world filled with sorrow, suffering, and sin. Help us to think clearly about the mystery of evil without dishonoring Thy name or diminishing Thy glory. Give us minds that are sharp enough to follow the argument wherever it leads, and hearts that are tender enough to weep with those who weep. Above all, direct our eyes to the cross of Thy dear Son, where Thou didst demonstrate once and for all that Thou art neither indifferent to our suffering nor powerless to overcome it. In the precious name of Jesus Christ our Lord, Amen.


PART ONE: STATING THE PROBLEM

Students, of all the objections raised against the existence of God and the truth of the Christian faith, none is more emotionally powerful, more philosophically persistent, or more personally challenging than the problem of evil. The argument, in its classical form, runs as follows: If God is omnipotent — that is, all-powerful — then He has the ability to prevent evil. If God is omnibenevolent — that is, perfectly good — then He has the desire to prevent evil. But evil exists. Therefore, either God is not all-powerful, or God is not perfectly good, or God does not exist at all.

This argument has ancient roots. It strikes at the very heart of the Christian claim that God is both sovereign and loving. The Greek philosopher Epicurus, who lived approximately three centuries before Christ, is often credited with formulating an early version of it. In the eighteenth century, the Scottish philosopher David Hume restated the problem with characteristic force in his Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, philosophers such as J.L. Mackie and William Rowe refined the argument into what are now called the logical problem of evil and the evidential problem of evil, respectively.

Before we respond to this objection, we must acknowledge something important. Many people who raise the problem of evil are not engaging in a detached intellectual exercise. They are hurting. They have lost a child, or watched a parent die of cancer, or survived abuse, or witnessed unspeakable cruelty. When a person in the midst of suffering asks, "Where is God?" — they deserve more than a clever syllogism. They deserve compassion, patience, and a genuine willingness to sit with them in their pain. The Apostle Paul instructed the church at Rome: "Rejoice with them that do rejoice, and weep with them that weep" (Romans 12:15, KJV). Before we offer arguments, we must offer love. The Puritan pastor Richard Baxter wisely counseled: "He that would convince another must first show that he careth for him. Arguments without sympathy are as seeds cast upon rocks — they may be true, but they will not take root."

That said, the problem of evil does have intellectually rigorous answers, and we must learn them — not to dismiss the suffering of others, but to strengthen our own faith and to offer genuine help to those who are seeking truth in the midst of their pain.

PART TWO: THE LOGICAL PROBLEM OF EVIL

Let us begin with the logical version of the problem. The philosopher J.L. Mackie, in his influential 1955 essay "Evil and Omnipotence," argued that the simultaneous existence of God and evil is logically contradictory — that is, it involves a genuine logical impossibility, like a square circle or a married bachelor. If God is all-powerful and all-good, Mackie contended, then evil simply cannot exist. Since evil does exist, God — at least the God described by traditional theism — cannot.

This is a powerful argument, but it contains a hidden assumption that, once exposed, reveals a fatal flaw. The argument assumes that an all-powerful, all-good God could have no morally sufficient reason for permitting evil. But is this assumption justified? Is it logically impossible that God might have good reasons — reasons consistent with His perfect goodness — for allowing evil to exist, at least temporarily?

The Christian philosopher Alvin Plantinga addressed this question in what has become known as the Free Will Defense. Plantinga argued that it is logically possible that God could not have created a world containing free creatures who always choose good. If God desired to create beings with genuine freedom — beings capable of real love, real obedience, and real moral choices — then He had to allow for the possibility that those beings would use their freedom to choose evil. A world of free creatures who sometimes choose wrongly may be more valuable, in God's estimation, than a world of automata who always behave correctly but who are incapable of love, virtue, or genuine moral action.

To understand this point, consider what love requires. Love, by its very nature, must be freely given. A love that is forced, programmed, or mechanically determined is not love at all — it is mere behavior. When God created Adam and Eve, He created them with the capacity for genuine love — love that flows from a free choice of the will, love that is given voluntarily and not under compulsion. But the same freedom that makes love possible also makes rebellion possible. You cannot have one without the other. The freedom to love God is also the freedom to reject God. The freedom to choose good is also the freedom to choose evil. God, in His wisdom, determined that a creation containing free beings who could genuinely love Him — even at the risk of rebellion — was more worthy than a creation of puppets who obeyed without understanding, loved without choosing, and served without devotion.

The Westminster Larger Catechism, Question 17, affirms this reality when it teaches that God, "having made man, entered into a covenant of life with him, upon condition of perfect obedience; forbidding him to eat of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, upon the pain of death." The very existence of a command implies the freedom to obey or to disobey. God did not create robotic servants. He created image-bearers with genuine moral agency, and He tested that agency with a real command carrying real consequences.

Plantinga's argument does not claim to explain why God permits every specific instance of evil. It makes a more modest but devastating point against the logical problem: there is no logical contradiction between the existence of God and the existence of evil. Even most atheistic philosophers now concede this point. The philosopher William Rowe, himself an atheist, acknowledged that the logical argument from evil is now widely regarded as discredited. J.L. Mackie himself, shortly before his death, admitted that the logical problem of evil does not succeed as a proof against God's existence.

The Scripture itself teaches that God created human beings with genuine freedom. The Westminster Confession, in Chapter 4, Section 2, affirms that God created man and woman "under a possibility of transgressing, being left to the liberty of their own will, which was subject unto change." Adam and Eve were not programmed robots. They were free moral agents who chose to rebel against their Creator. The evil that entered the world was not God's creation but humanity's choice. "God made man upright; but they have sought out many inventions" (Ecclesiastes 7:29, KJV).

The Puritan theologian Thomas Manton (1620-1677) wrote with precision on this point: "God is the author of a free creature, but not of a sinful action. He that made the hand is not the cause of the theft. He that gave the tongue is not the cause of the blasphemy. God gave man the power to act, but man himself determined the act toward evil. The fountain of sin is in the creature's will, not in the Creator's decree."

PART THREE: THE EVIDENTIAL PROBLEM OF EVIL

While the logical problem of evil has been largely abandoned by philosophers, a more subtle version of the argument remains widely discussed: the evidential problem of evil. This version, associated primarily with the philosopher William Rowe, does not claim that God and evil are logically incompatible. Instead, it argues that the amount, intensity, and distribution of evil in the world make the existence of God improbable. Even if it is logically possible that God has reasons for permitting evil, the sheer weight of suffering in the world — the Holocaust, childhood cancer, natural disasters that kill thousands of innocents — makes it unlikely that such reasons actually exist.

This is a more nuanced argument, and it requires a more nuanced response. Let us consider several lines of reasoning.

First, the argument from cognitive limitation. The evidential argument assumes that if we cannot see a good reason for God's permitting a particular evil, then no such reason exists. But this is an enormous assumption. It assumes that the human mind is capable of comprehending all of God's purposes — that if a reason existed, we would be able to discern it. But is this plausible? Consider the vast disparity between our knowledge and God's. We are finite; He is infinite. We see a tiny fragment of reality; He sees the whole. We know the present moment; He knows the entire sweep of history from beginning to end.

The prophet Isaiah recorded the Lord's own declaration on this matter: "For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways, saith the LORD. For as the heavens are higher than the earth, so are my ways higher than your ways, and my thoughts than your thoughts" (Isaiah 55:8-9, KJV). The Apostle Paul, after surveying the depths of God's sovereign purposes in the salvation of both Jews and Gentiles, exclaimed: "O the depth of the riches both of the wisdom and knowledge of God! how unsearchable are his judgements, and his ways past finding out! For who hath known the mind of the Lord? or who hath been his counsellor?" (Romans 11:33-34, KJV).

The Puritan theologian Stephen Charnock (1628-1680), in his magisterial work "The Existence and Attributes of God," applied this truth directly to the problem of evil: "We must not measure the infinite wisdom of God by the short line of our own understanding. A child cannot comprehend the reasons of a wise father, and shall a creature of yesterday presume to comprehend the counsels of the eternal God? Our ignorance of His reasons is no proof that He has none. It is only proof that we are finite and He is infinite."

Second, the argument from known goods. While we may not be able to identify God's specific reason for permitting every particular evil, we can identify several great goods that God brings out of evil — goods that could not exist without it. These include moral virtues such as courage, compassion, patience, and self-sacrifice, which can only be developed in the presence of adversity and suffering. They include the deepening of faith, which often grows strongest in the crucible of trial. And they include the demonstration of God's own character — His justice in punishing evil, His mercy in forgiving sinners, His power in overcoming death, and His love in bearing the cross.

The Apostle Paul wrote from his own profound experience of suffering: "And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose" (Romans 8:28, KJV). And again: "For our light affliction, which is but for a moment, worketh for us a far more exceeding and eternal weight of glory; while we look not at the things which are seen, but at the things which are not seen: for the things which are seen are temporal; but the things which are not seen are eternal" (2 Corinthians 4:17-18, KJV). Paul did not call his sufferings trivial. He called them "light" only in comparison with the "eternal weight of glory" that God was producing through them.

Third, the argument from the cross. The Christian faith does not merely offer a philosophical answer to the problem of evil. It offers a historical answer — and it is this historical answer that sets Christianity apart from every other worldview's attempt to address the problem of suffering. In the incarnation, crucifixion, and resurrection of Jesus Christ, God Himself entered into the suffering of His creation, bore the full weight of evil upon Himself, and conquered it from the inside. The cross is God's definitive answer to the problem of evil — not an explanation from a distance but a demonstration up close.

Fourth, the argument from eschatological hope. The Bible teaches that the present world of suffering is not the final state of things. God has promised a future in which evil will be utterly and permanently eradicated. "And I saw a new heaven and a new earth: for the first heaven and the first earth were passed away; and there was no more sea" (Revelation 21:1, KJV). "And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away. And he that sat upon the throne said, Behold, I make all things new" (Revelation 21:4-5, KJV). The problem of evil, from the Christian perspective, is a temporary problem. God has not yet finished His work. He is working all things toward a glorious consummation in which every wrong will be righted, every tear will be dried, and every injustice will be addressed — either through the mercy of forgiveness in Christ or through the justice of final judgment. The story is not over, and it is premature to judge the Author before the final chapter has been written.

PART FOUR: NATURAL EVIL AND THE DOCTRINE OF THE FALL

Before we turn to the biblical narrative, we must address a category of evil that the Free Will Defense does not directly cover: natural evil. Natural evil refers to suffering caused not by human choices but by natural forces — earthquakes, hurricanes, diseases, floods, famines, birth defects, and the predatory violence of the animal kingdom. How does the Christian worldview account for these forms of suffering, which seem to have nothing to do with human freedom?

The answer lies in the doctrine of the fall. The Christian faith teaches that the entire created order was affected by Adam's sin. When humanity rebelled against God, the consequences did not remain confined to human hearts and human relationships. They rippled outward through the entire fabric of creation, introducing corruption, disorder, suffering, and death into a world that had originally been "very good" (Genesis 1:31, KJV). The Apostle Paul described this cosmic corruption in vivid terms: "For the creature was made subject to vanity, not willingly, but by reason of him who hath subjected the same in hope, Because the creature itself also shall be delivered from the bondage of corruption into the glorious liberty of the children of God. For we know that the whole creation groaneth and travaileth in pain together until now" (Romans 8:20-22, KJV).

The creation "groans." It is in "bondage." It "travails in pain." These are not the characteristics of a world functioning as God originally designed it. They are the characteristics of a world that has been broken by sin — a world under a curse. When God pronounced judgment upon Adam after the fall, He cursed the very ground: "Cursed is the ground for thy sake; in sorrow shalt thou eat of it all the days of thy life; Thorns also and thistles shall it bring forth to thee" (Genesis 3:17-18, KJV). The natural world, in its present state, bears the marks of the fall. Earthquakes, diseases, and natural disasters are not part of God's original design. They are consequences of the cosmic disruption caused by sin.

The Puritan theologian Thomas Goodwin (1600-1680) wrote with great insight about the relationship between the fall and the corruption of nature: "When Adam sinned, he did not sin for himself alone. He sinned as the head and representative of the entire creation that had been entrusted to his care. As a king's treason brings ruin upon his kingdom, so Adam's rebellion brought ruin upon the world he was appointed to govern. The thorns in the field, the diseases in the body, the storms in the sky — all are echoes of that first great rebellion in Eden."

This understanding of natural evil is important because it preserves the goodness of God. God did not create a world of suffering. He created a world of perfection. Suffering entered through the door of human rebellion, and it will be removed when God restores all things through the work of Christ. The present state of the natural world is not God's final word. It is a temporary condition — tragic, painful, and real — but destined to be overcome when "the creation itself also shall be delivered from the bondage of corruption" (Romans 8:21, KJV).

PART FIVE: THE BIBLICAL RESPONSE — THE BOOK OF JOB

The most sustained treatment of the problem of evil in the entire Bible is the book of Job. Job was a righteous man — "perfect and upright, and one that feared God, and eschewed evil" (Job 1:1, KJV) — who lost everything: his wealth, his children, and his health. His friends came to him and argued that his suffering must be a punishment for some hidden sin. Job protested his innocence and demanded an explanation from God. For thirty-seven chapters, the debate rages — Job crying out for answers, his friends insisting on simple retribution theology, and the reader waiting to hear what God will say.

When God finally speaks — in chapters 38 through 41 — He does not answer Job's question directly. He does not explain why Job suffered. Instead, He asks Job a series of questions designed to remind him of the infinite gap between divine wisdom and human understanding: "Where wast thou when I laid the foundations of the earth? declare, if thou hast understanding. Who hath laid the measures thereof, if thou knowest? or who hath stretched the line upon it?" (Job 38:4-5, KJV). "Canst thou bind the sweet influences of Pleiades, or loose the bands of Orion?" (Job 38:31, KJV). "Hast thou an arm like God? or canst thou thunder with a voice like him?" (Job 40:9, KJV).

God's answer to Job is not a logical syllogism. It is a revelation of Himself — His power, His wisdom, His sovereignty, His intimate knowledge of every detail of His creation. And Job's response is not, "Now I understand why I suffered." It is something far deeper: "I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear: but now mine eye seeth thee. Wherefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes" (Job 42:5-6, KJV). Job did not receive an explanation. He received something better — a deeper knowledge of God Himself. And in that knowledge, he found peace.

The Puritan pastor Joseph Caryl (1602-1673), who spent twenty-four years preaching through the book of Job and produced a twelve-volume commentary on it, observed: "God did not answer Job's questions, but He answered Job. He did not remove the mystery, but He removed the sting. For when a soul truly sees God — His majesty, His holiness, His wisdom, His love — it no longer demands explanations. It rests in the character of the One who holds all explanations in His hands."

PART FIVE: THE PURITAN UNDERSTANDING OF SUFFERING

The Puritans had an extraordinarily rich theology of suffering. They did not minimize it. They did not ignore it. And they certainly did not pretend that it was easy. But they understood it within a framework of divine sovereignty, divine goodness, and divine purpose that gave them strength to endure what would otherwise have been unbearable.

The Westminster Confession, Chapter 5, Section 5, teaches: "The most wise, righteous, and gracious God doth oftentimes leave for a season his own children to manifold temptations, and the corruption of their own hearts, to chastise them for their former sins, or to discover unto them the hidden strength of corruption and deceitfulness of their hearts, that they may be humbled; and to raise them to a more close and constant dependence for their support upon himself, and to make them more watchful against all future occasions of sin, and for sundry other just and holy ends."

Notice the purposes the Confession identifies in God's allowance of suffering among His own people: to chastise them, to humble them, to reveal hidden sin, to drive them to greater dependence on God, and to make them more watchful. Suffering, in the Puritan understanding, is not random. It is not meaningless. It is not evidence of God's absence. It is a tool in the hands of a wise and loving Father who is shaping His children into the image of His Son.

The Apostle Paul taught the same truth: "And not only so, but we glory in tribulations also: knowing that tribulation worketh patience; and patience, experience; and experience, hope: and hope maketh not ashamed; because the love of God is shed abroad in our hearts by the Holy Ghost which is given unto us" (Romans 5:3-5, KJV). There is a chain of grace at work in suffering — tribulation produces patience, patience produces experience, experience produces hope, and hope is anchored in the unshakable love of God.

The Puritan pastor Thomas Brooks (1608-1680), in his work "The Mute Christian Under the Smarting Rod," counseled suffering believers with remarkable tenderness: "God's house of correction is a school of instruction. Every affliction is a lesson. The rod has a voice, and the wise man will hear it. God does not afflict willingly nor grieve the children of men, but when He does afflict, it is always for their profit — that they might be partakers of His holiness."

The Puritan John Flavel (1627-1691) wrote in his treatise on providence: "Some of the best things that have ever happened to the saints happened to them in their worst times. Joseph was sold into slavery, and it made him the savior of nations. David was hunted like a partridge on the mountains, and it produced the Psalms. Paul was imprisoned, and it gave us the epistles. The seed must be buried in the dark earth before it can spring up into the light."

The Puritan understanding also emphasized that God proportions His grace to the severity of the trial. He does not give His children more suffering than they can bear but always provides the strength necessary to endure. The Apostle Paul testified: "And he said unto me, My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness. Most gladly therefore will I rather glory in my infirmities, that the power of Christ may rest upon me. Therefore I take pleasure in infirmities, in reproaches, in necessities, in persecutions, in distresses for Christ's sake: for when I am weak, then am I strong" (2 Corinthians 12:9-10, KJV). And again: "There hath no temptation taken you but such as is common to man: but God is faithful, who will not suffer you to be tempted above that ye are able; but will with the temptation also make a way to escape, that ye may be able to bear it" (1 Corinthians 10:13, KJV).

Samuel Rutherford (1600-1661), who suffered exile, imprisonment, and the loss of his wife and children, wrote letters from Aberdeen that remain among the most beautiful expressions of Christian hope ever penned. In one letter he wrote: "When I am in the cellar of affliction, I look for the Lord's choicest wines." And in another: "I would not exchange my crosses for all the world's crowns. Christ and His cross are sweet company, and to this poor prisoner, the chains of Christ are heavier than gold." Rutherford did not minimize his pain. He felt it deeply. But he experienced in the midst of his suffering a sweetness of communion with Christ that he had never known in times of ease.

PART SEVEN: THE CROSS — GOD'S ULTIMATE ANSWER

The deepest and most powerful Christian response to the problem of evil is not a philosophical argument but a historical event: the crucifixion and resurrection of Jesus Christ. On the cross, the problem of evil and the love of God collide with such force that the entire universe is shaken.

Consider what happened at Calvary. The most evil act in the history of the world — the murder of the sinless Son of God by the hands of wicked men — was simultaneously the most loving act in the history of the world — the voluntary self-sacrifice of God Himself for the salvation of sinners. The Apostle Peter declared in his sermon at Pentecost: "Him, being delivered by the determinate counsel and foreknowledge of God, ye have taken, and by wicked hands have crucified and slain: Whom God hath raised up, having loosed the pains of death: because it was not possible that he should be holden of it" (Acts 2:23-24, KJV). The cross was both the worst thing men ever did and the best thing God ever did. It was the nadir of human evil and the zenith of divine love.

This is the pattern of God's dealings with evil throughout the Bible. He does not merely permit evil — He overrules it for good. Joseph's brothers sold him into slavery, but God meant it for good, "to save much people alive" (Genesis 50:20, KJV). Pharaoh oppressed the Israelites, but God delivered them through the Exodus and displayed His glory to the nations. The Babylonians destroyed Jerusalem and carried God's people into exile, but God used the exile to purify His people and to spread the knowledge of Himself throughout the ancient world.

The cross is the supreme instance of this pattern. On the cross, God took the worst evil the world could produce — the betrayal, the injustice, the cruelty, the murder of His own Son — and transformed it into the instrument of the world's salvation. This is not a God who is indifferent to evil. This is a God who has entered into evil, borne its full weight upon His own shoulders, and conquered it from within.

The prophet Isaiah, writing seven centuries before the crucifixion, described the suffering of the Messiah in terms that anticipate the cross with breathtaking precision: "He is despised and rejected of men; a man of sorrows, and acquainted with grief: and we hid as it were our faces from him; he was despised, and we esteemed him not. Surely he hath borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows: yet we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted. But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed" (Isaiah 53:3-5, KJV). Notice the language: He bore our griefs. He carried our sorrows. He was wounded for our transgressions. The sufferings of Christ were not an accident. They were the deliberate, voluntary, substitutionary bearing of the evil that we deserved, so that we might receive the grace that He deserved. This is the mystery at the heart of the gospel — and it is the deepest answer to the problem of evil that has ever been given or ever will be given.

The resurrection of Christ on the third day is the guarantee that God's victory over evil is complete and irreversible. Death, the final consequence of evil, has been defeated. "O death, where is thy sting? O grave, where is thy victory? The sting of death is sin; and the strength of sin is the law. But thanks be to God, which giveth us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ" (1 Corinthians 15:55-57, KJV). Because Christ has risen, evil does not have the last word. Because Christ has risen, suffering is not the end of the story. Because Christ has risen, every believer can face the darkest night with the confidence that morning is coming.

The Puritan theologian John Owen (1616-1683) wrote with penetrating insight about the cross and the problem of evil: "At the cross, we see that God does not stand at a distance from our suffering. He does not observe it from the safety of heaven. He enters into it. He bears it. He exhausts it. He transforms it. The cross is God's answer to every charge that He is indifferent to human pain. He is so far from indifferent that He bore the greatest pain Himself — not for His own sake, but for ours."

The Apostle Paul drew an astonishing conclusion from the cross: "He that spared not his own Son, but delivered him up for us all, how shall he not with him also freely give us all things?" (Romans 8:32, KJV). If God loved us enough to give His Son for us — if He was willing to bear the ultimate cost to save us from evil — then we can trust Him absolutely with every lesser trial. The cross is the guarantee that God is good, that He is for us, and that no evil, however terrible, will have the last word.

PART EIGHT: THE PROBLEM OF EVIL FOR ATHEISM

Before we close, it is worth noting that the problem of evil is actually a far greater difficulty for atheism than it is for Christianity. This may seem counterintuitive, but consider the logic carefully.

The very concept of evil presupposes an objective moral standard — a standard of what ought to be, against which what is can be measured and found wanting. When we say that something is evil — that a child's suffering is wrong, that genocide is an atrocity, that injustice is intolerable — we are appealing to a moral reality that transcends human opinion, cultural convention, and evolutionary utility. But where does this objective moral standard come from?

If atheism is true — if the universe is nothing more than matter in motion, the product of blind, purposeless natural forces — then there is no objective moral standard. There is no "ought." There is only what is. The universe is not good or evil; it simply is. In a purely materialistic universe, suffering is not wrong — it is merely unpleasant. Murder is not evil — it is merely disadvantageous for the victim. Injustice is not an atrocity — it is merely a pattern of behavior that some human beings happen to dislike.

The Christian apologist C.S. Lewis described his own experience with this paradox. Before his conversion, Lewis was an atheist who rejected God partly because of the problem of evil. But he came to realize that his very argument against God depended upon the existence of a moral standard that only made sense if God existed. Lewis wrote that his argument against God was itself evidence for God — because the sense that the world was unjust presupposed a standard of justice that a godless universe could not provide.

The Apostle Paul taught that the moral law is written on every human heart: "For when the Gentiles, which have not the law, do by nature the things contained in the law, these, having not the law, are a law unto themselves: Which shew the work of the law written in their hearts, their conscience also bearing witness, and their thoughts the mean while accusing or else excusing one another" (Romans 2:14-15, KJV). The universal human sense that evil is genuinely wrong — not merely unpleasant but morally wrong — is itself evidence for the existence of a moral Lawgiver.

The Puritan pastor Thomas Watson (c. 1620-1686) made a similar observation: "The very existence of conscience — that inner voice which accuses us when we do wrong and approves us when we do right — is a testimony to the existence of God. For if there were no God, there would be no law. And if there were no law, there would be no conscience. The man who says there is no God must also say there is no evil. And the man who says there is no evil contradicts every instinct of his own heart."

PART NINE: PRACTICAL APPLICATION

How should we, as young Christians, apply what we have learned today?

First, be honest about the difficulty. Do not pretend that the problem of evil is easy. Do not offer glib answers to people who are suffering. Acknowledge the weight of the question. Sit with the tension. The Apostle Paul himself groaned under the burden of suffering: "For we that are in this tabernacle do groan, being burdened" (2 Corinthians 5:4, KJV). Honesty about the difficulty of suffering is not a weakness of faith — it is a mark of genuine humanity.

Second, trust the character of God even when you cannot trace His purposes. You do not need to understand every reason God permits evil in order to trust that He has good reasons. You trust Him not because you see the full picture but because you know the kind of God He is — holy, wise, good, and loving. "Though he slay me, yet will I trust in him," declared Job (Job 13:15, KJV). This is the faith that honors God in the darkness — not a faith that has all the answers but a faith that knows the One who does.

Third, look to the cross. When the weight of suffering threatens to overwhelm you, look to Calvary. There you will see a God who is not distant from your pain but who has entered into it, borne it, and conquered it. "Surely he hath borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows" (Isaiah 53:4, KJV). The cross does not explain every instance of suffering, but it proves beyond any doubt that God is not indifferent to it.

Fourth, hold fast to the promise of future glory. The Bible does not promise that suffering will end in this life. But it does promise that suffering will end. "And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away" (Revelation 21:4, KJV). The problem of evil is real, but it is temporary. The glory that is coming is eternal.

Fifth, use the problem of evil as an opportunity for witness. When unbelievers raise this objection, do not become defensive. Engage the question honestly and compassionately. Point out the self-defeating nature of the atheistic version of the objection. And above all, point to the cross — where God's answer to evil was not a lecture but a sacrifice. Remember that many of the people who raise this question are not hostile — they are hurting. They need a listener before they need a lecturer. They need compassion before they need a syllogism. Let your first response be empathy, and let your arguments be seasoned with grace.

Sixth, remember that you are called to be an agent of God's response to evil in this present world. The Christian does not merely explain evil — he fights it. You are called to feed the hungry, clothe the naked, comfort the grieving, defend the oppressed, and proclaim the gospel to the lost. "Pure religion and undefiled before God and the Father is this, To visit the fatherless and widows in their affliction, and to keep himself unspotted from the world" (James 1:27, KJV). Your life of active compassion and sacrificial love is itself a powerful answer to the problem of evil — a living demonstration that God has not abandoned the world to its suffering but is actively working through His people to bring healing, hope, and redemption. The Puritan pastor Richard Sibbes (1577-1635) wrote: "Christ's people are His body, and His hands, and His voice in this world. When the world asks where God is in the midst of suffering, let them find Him in you — in your prayers, in your tears, in your deeds of mercy, in your words of truth."

Seventh, cultivate the discipline of thanksgiving in the midst of trial. The Apostle Paul commanded: "In every thing give thanks: for this is the will of God in Christ Jesus concerning you" (1 Thessalonians 5:18, KJV). This does not mean that we thank God for evil itself. It means that we thank God in the midst of evil — for His presence, for His promises, for His past faithfulness, and for the certain hope that He will bring all things to a glorious end. Thanksgiving in suffering is an act of faith — a declaration that God is still good, still sovereign, and still worthy of praise, even when our circumstances seem to say otherwise.

The problem of evil is the hardest question any Christian will ever face. But it is not an unanswerable question. The logical version of the argument has been refuted. The evidential version rests on assumptions that finite creatures are in no position to make. And the deepest answer is not philosophical at all — it is historical. On a hill outside Jerusalem, two thousand years ago, God Himself entered into the suffering of His creation, bore the full weight of evil upon His own shoulders, and conquered it through death and resurrection. The cross does not remove the mystery of suffering, but it removes the charge that God is indifferent to it. He is so far from indifferent that He bore the worst of it Himself. As the Puritan John Flavel wrote: "The same hand that wounds the saint is the hand that heals him. The same love that permits the furnace is the love that sits beside it, watching the gold." Trust Him, students. Even in the darkness. Especially in the darkness. Until next time.
