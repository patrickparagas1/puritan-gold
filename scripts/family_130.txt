PURITAN GOLD -- Family Devotional, Day 30
"Contentment in All Circumstances"
Richard Baxter — The Christian Directory


Hello, dear ones. How wonderful it is to sit together as a family and open the Scriptures. Welcome to Puritan Gold's family devotional. Let us begin with thankful hearts.

Let us pray together. Lord God, we come before You as a family, grateful for Your love and Your provision. We ask that You would quiet our minds and soften our hearts as we hear from You tonight. Help us to understand, to remember, and to obey what You teach us. In Christ's name, Amen.

Our Scripture reading this evening comes from Philippians 4:11-13. Let us hear the Word of the Lord:

"Not that I speak in respect of want: for I have learned, in whatsoever state I am, therewith to be content. I know both how to be abased, and I know how to abound: every where and in all things I am instructed both to be full and to be hungry, both to abound and to suffer need. I can do all things through Christ which strengtheneth me."

That is Philippians 4:11-13. Let those words settle in your heart for a moment before we continue.


Today we learn about one of the rarest and most precious virtues in the Christian life — contentment. Paul writes from a Roman prison in Philippians 4:11-13, and his words are among the most remarkable in all his letters.

'Not that I speak in respect of want: for I have learned, in whatsoever state I am, therewith to be content.' Paul is writing a thank-you letter to the Philippian church for a financial gift they sent him. But he is careful to say that he is not writing because he is desperate. He has learned — notice that word, learned — to be content in whatever state he finds himself.

Contentment is not natural. It is learned. Nobody is born content. Our natural tendency is to always want more — more toys, more food, more money, more praise, more comfort. Paul says he had to learn contentment, implying it was a process that took time and experience.

Then he explains what this contentment looks like: 'I know both how to be abased, and I know how to abound: every where and in all things I am instructed both to be full and to be hungry, both to abound and to suffer need.' Paul knows how to handle poverty and prosperity, hunger and fullness, abundance and deprivation. Contentment is not a one-way virtue. It works in both directions. The truly content person does not become arrogant in prosperity or despairing in poverty.

And then comes one of the most quoted verses in the Bible: 'I can do all things through Christ which strengtheneth me.' This verse is often misused to mean 'I can accomplish any goal I set.' But in context, Paul is saying something far more profound. He is saying that through Christ's strength, he can endure any circumstance — poverty or plenty, comfort or suffering — with contentment. Christ gives him the strength to be at peace regardless of outward conditions.

First Timothy 6:6-8 expands on this: 'But godliness with contentment is great gain. For we brought nothing into this world, and it is certain we can carry nothing out. And having food and raiment let us be therewith content.' If we have food and clothing, we have enough. Everything beyond that is bonus — a gift to be enjoyed with gratitude, not an entitlement to be demanded.

Hebrews 13:5 connects contentment to trust in God: 'Let your conversation be without covetousness; and be content with such things as ye have: for he hath said, I will never leave thee, nor forsake thee.' The foundation of contentment is the promise of God's presence. If God is with us, we have everything we truly need.

Jesus taught the futility of discontentment in Luke 12:15: 'Take heed, and beware of covetousness: for a man's life consisteth not in the abundance of the things which he possesseth.' Life is not measured by possessions. A person with very little who knows God is infinitely richer than a billionaire who does not.

Richard Baxter wrote that contentment is not found in having everything you want but in wanting nothing more than what God has given. The contented Christian sees God's wisdom in every circumstance and God's goodness in every provision.

Baxter identified several enemies of contentment. First, comparison — looking at what others have and feeling dissatisfied with our own portion. Second, ingratitude — taking God's blessings for granted and focusing on what we lack. Third, unbelief — doubting God's goodness and wisdom in what He has given or withheld. Fourth, worldliness — believing that material things will satisfy the hunger of the soul.

Baxter also gave remedies for discontentment. First, count your blessings — literally list the things God has given you. The exercise of gratitude is a powerful antidote to grumbling. Second, remember your unworthiness — we deserve nothing from God. Everything is grace. Third, trust God's wisdom — He knows what you need better than you do. What He gives is good, and what He withholds is also good. Fourth, look to eternity — our light affliction, which is but for a moment, works for us a far more exceeding and eternal weight of glory (2 Corinthians 4:17).

For our youngest: God gives us everything we truly need. When we have food, clothes, a home, and people who love us, we have so much to be thankful for! For older children: think about something you really want but do not have. How does it make you feel? Now think about five things you do have that you are grateful for. Which list is longer? For the whole family: discontentment is one of the great thieves of joy. Let us practice contentment by cultivating gratitude and trusting God's wisdom in what He provides.


The Puritans were men and women who treasured God's Word above all else. Let us hear from one of them now.

Richard Baxter, in his work "The Christian Directory," wrote these words:

"Contentment is not found in having everything you want but in wanting nothing more than what God has given. The contented Christian has learned to see God's wisdom in every circumstance and God's goodness in every provision."

What Richard Baxter is reminding us is that the truths we have read tonight in Philippians 4:11-13 are not new. They have been treasured by faithful Christians for centuries. And they are just as true and just as needed in our family today as they were hundreds of years ago. Let us take these words to heart and let them shape the way we live together as a family.


Let us pause and talk as a family. Here are tonight's discussion questions. Everyone gets a turn to share.

Question 1: What does it mean to be content? Is it the same as not wanting anything?

Question 2: Paul says he 'learned' to be content. Why is contentment something we have to learn?

Question 3: What are some things that make us feel discontent? How can gratitude help?

Question 4: How does trusting God make it easier to be content with what we have?

Take a few minutes to talk about these questions together. Let everyone share, and listen to one another with patience and love.


Now, here is our memory verse for today. Let us say it together:

"I have learned, in whatsoever state I am, therewith to be content." -- Philippians 4:11b

Try to say it out loud three times right now. Then, before bed tonight, see if you can say it from memory. Tomorrow morning, try again. The more we hide God's Word in our hearts, the more it will guide us when we need it most.


To make tonight's lesson come alive, try this activity together as a family:

Do a 'Gratitude Scavenger Hunt.' Each family member has five minutes to find five things in the house they are grateful for and bring them to the table (or take a photo). Share why you are thankful for each one. Then thank God together for His generous provision.


Let us pray together. Lord God, we thank You for this time as a family. Thank You for speaking to us through Your Word. Plant these truths deep in our hearts, especially in the hearts of our children. Help us to walk in wisdom tomorrow and every day. In Jesus' name, Amen.

Sleep well tonight, knowing that God watches over your family.
