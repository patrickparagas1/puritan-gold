PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Guided Puritan Reading, Day 16
"Directions for Killing Sin: Part One"
John Owen — Mortification of Sin, Chapter 9


Good evening. One of the great losses of the modern church is the loss of slow reading. The Puritans read deeply, digesting every paragraph, pausing to pray, pausing to think. Tonight, we recover that practice as we walk through a treasured text together.

Tonight we draw from the work of John Owen, specifically from "The Mortification of Sin." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Psalm 51:10:

"Create in me a clean heart, O God; and renew a right spirit within me."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


Having laid the foundation — the necessity of mortification, the role of the Spirit, the nature of true mortification, and the importance of knowing your sin — Owen now turns to specific directions for the actual work of killing sin. These chapters are the most practically useful in the entire treatise.

Owen's first direction: Consider whether the sin you are fighting has a dangerous symptom. Owen identifies several symptoms that indicate a sin has reached a critical stage. Is the sin long-standing and habitual? Has the heart become hardened to it through repeated indulgence? Has the conscience been seared, so that it no longer accuses as sharply as it once did? Does the sin dominate the thoughts, intruding even during prayer and worship? If so, Owen warns, the situation is urgent. A sin that has been allowed to grow unchecked is far more dangerous than one that is caught early.

Owen's second direction: Get a clear and abiding sense of the guilt, danger, and evil of the sin. This is not mere intellectual acknowledgment; it is a deep, felt conviction of the heinousness of sin before a holy God. Owen writes that many believers fight their sin halfheartedly because they do not truly feel how evil it is. They see the inconvenience of sin — the shame, the consequences, the disruption — but they do not see its offense against the majesty of God.

Owen recommends several exercises for deepening this sense of guilt. First, consider what the sin cost Christ. Your pet sin, whatever it is, was part of the burden that pressed the blood from the Savior's brow in Gethsemane. It was part of the offense that nailed Him to the cross. To entertain that sin is to trample on the blood of Christ. Second, consider what the sin does to the Spirit. The Holy Spirit dwells in the believer, and when the believer indulges sin, the Spirit is grieved. Owen asks: would you invite an honored guest into your home and then insult him to his face? That is what you do when you harbor sin in the temple of the Spirit. Third, consider what the sin does to your own soul. It weakens your communion with God, dulls your spiritual senses, robs you of assurance, and makes you useless for service.

Owen's third direction: Do not allow the sin any provision or advantage. Owen draws this from Romans 13:14: 'Make not provision for the flesh, to fulfil the lusts thereof.' This means removing the occasions and opportunities for sin. If a particular companion tempts you to a particular sin, limit your exposure to that companion. If a particular activity weakens your resistance, avoid it. If a particular habit feeds your besetting sin, change the habit.

Owen is realistic about this: he does not claim that removing occasions will remove the desire. The desire comes from within, not from external circumstances. But external circumstances provide fuel for internal desires, and wisdom demands that we cut off the fuel supply wherever possible.

Owen's fourth direction: Rise up against the very first motions of sin. Do not wait until the temptation has gained strength; fight it at its first appearance. Owen uses the metaphor of a fire: a spark is easily extinguished, but a blaze may consume the house. The thought that is dismissed immediately has no power; the thought that is entertained becomes a desire, the desire becomes an intention, and the intention becomes an act.

Owen writes that the first motion of sin in the heart is the critical moment. At that moment, the believer must do several things simultaneously: recognize the temptation for what it is, reject it with the will, cry out to God for help, and redirect the mind toward Christ. This requires discipline and practice, but it becomes more natural over time as the believer develops what Owen calls 'a spiritual taste' that instinctively recoils from sin.

These first four directions are foundational. They address the heart, the mind, and the external circumstances. Together, they form a comprehensive strategy for engaging the enemy. But Owen is careful to remind his readers: the strategy is not the power. The power is always and only the Holy Spirit. Use the directions, but trust the Spirit.


Before we close, take a few moments with these reflection questions.

Question 1: Which of Owen's dangerous symptoms do I recognize in my own besetting sin — is it long-standing, habitual, or does it intrude even during worship?

Question 2: Do I have a deep, felt sense of the evil of my sin, or do I merely acknowledge it intellectually?

Question 3: What 'provision for the flesh' am I currently making that I need to eliminate?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Create in me a clean heart, O God. Give me eyes to see the horror of my sin as You see it. Help me to cut off every provision for the flesh, to resist the first motions of temptation, and to cry out to You in the moment of testing. By Your Spirit, grant me victory. Amen.

Until next time. May the Lord deepen your faith with every passing day.
