PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 11
"What Is Apologetics and Why Does It Matter?"
Apologetics for Young Christians — Lesson 1

---

Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we begin a new and vitally important unit: Apologetics for Young Christians. Over the coming lessons, we will learn how to defend the Christian faith with confidence, clarity, and grace. This is not merely an academic exercise. This is a calling that belongs to every believer, young and old, learned and simple. Let us pray and begin.

Lord, Your Word tells us to be ready always to give an answer to everyone who asks us for a reason of the hope that is in us. We ask Thee now to equip us for this sacred task. Sharpen our minds, strengthen our convictions, and fill our hearts with both truth and love. Grant us the wisdom to speak boldly and the humility to speak gently. May this study draw us closer to Thee and make us more useful instruments in Thy hands. In Christ's name, Amen.

--- Lesson ---

I want you to imagine something with me. You are sitting in a room full of people — perhaps a classroom, perhaps a gathering of friends, perhaps a family dinner with relatives you do not see often. The conversation turns, as conversations sometimes do, to matters of faith. Someone says, casually but with confidence, "I don't see how any thinking person could believe in God in the twenty-first century." Another person adds, "The Bible is just a bunch of myths written by ancient people who didn't know any better." A third person says, "If God is real, why is the world so full of suffering?"

Every eye in the room turns to you. They know you are a Christian. They are waiting for your response.

What do you say? Do you stammer and change the subject? Do you respond with anger? Do you simply say, "I just have faith," and hope the conversation moves on? Or do you have something more substantial to offer — a thoughtful, well-reasoned, compassionate defense of the faith that has shaped your life and sustained your soul?

This, dear students, is why we study apologetics.

Part One: The Meaning and Origin of the Word

The word "apologetics" comes from the Greek word apologia. In the ancient Greco-Roman world, an apologia was a formal speech of defense. When a citizen stood accused in a court of law, he was given the opportunity to present his apologia — a structured, reasoned argument demonstrating his innocence or the justice of his cause. The word appears seventeen times in the Greek New Testament and its related forms. It was a word that carried weight and dignity. It meant more than merely saying, "I'm sorry." It meant, "Here is my case. Here is my evidence. Here is my reasoning. Judge for yourselves whether what I say is true."

One of the most famous uses of this word in the ancient world, outside the Bible, was Plato's account of the trial of Socrates. In 399 BC, the philosopher Socrates stood before an Athenian jury of five hundred and one citizens, accused of impiety and corrupting the youth of Athens. Socrates delivered his apologia — his defense — which Plato later recorded in a work titled, appropriately, The Apology. Socrates did not grovel or beg. He presented a reasoned case for his life and his philosophical mission. Though the jury convicted him and sentenced him to death, his apologia became one of the foundational texts of Western civilization.

The most important use of this word for our purposes, however, is found in the first epistle of Peter, chapter three, verse fifteen. Let me read the passage to you in its fuller context, beginning at verse thirteen: "And who is he that will harm you, if ye be followers of that which is good? But and if ye suffer for righteousness' sake, happy are ye: and be not afraid of their terror, neither be troubled; But sanctify the Lord God in your hearts: and be ready always to give an answer to every man that asketh you a reason of the hope that is in you with meekness and fear: Having a good conscience; that, whereas they speak evil of you, as of evildoers, they may be ashamed that falsely accuse your good conversation in Christ."

That phrase, "be ready always to give an answer," uses the Greek word apologia. Peter is writing to Christians who are suffering persecution in the Roman Empire. They are being accused, misrepresented, slandered, and oppressed. And Peter tells them — he commands them, by apostolic authority — to be prepared to defend their faith. Not with swords. Not with political power. Not with anger or hostility. But with a reasoned, gentle, respectful defense. An apologia.

Part Two: The Historical Context of Peter's Command

Now, let us pause and consider the historical context of this command. Peter wrote this epistle around the year 63 or 64 AD, during the reign of the Roman emperor Nero. This was a time of extraordinary danger for Christians. Nero, who would later blame the Great Fire of Rome in July of AD 64 upon the Christians and unleash a horrific persecution against them, was already creating an atmosphere of suspicion and hostility toward the followers of Christ. The Roman historian Tacitus, writing in his Annals around 116 AD, described how Nero arrested Christians and subjected them to the most horrifying punishments — covering them in animal skins to be torn apart by dogs, crucifying them, and coating them in pitch to be set on fire as living torches to illuminate his garden parties at night. Tacitus recorded that Nero's cruelty was so extreme that it actually generated sympathy for the Christians, even though the Roman populace regarded them with suspicion.

Christians were a minority in a pagan empire. They refused to worship the Roman gods. They refused to offer incense to the emperor, an act of political loyalty that the Roman state required. They gathered in private homes for worship, which made their neighbors suspicious. Rumors spread that they were cannibals because they spoke of eating the body and drinking the blood of their Lord. They were called atheists because they rejected the pantheon of Roman deities. They were accused of political subversion because they proclaimed Jesus, not Caesar, as Lord.

In this context, Peter does not tell them to hide. He does not tell them to keep silent. He does not tell them to blend in. He tells them to be ready to explain their faith — clearly, calmly, and with gentleness. This is the birth of Christian apologetics. It was born not in a university lecture hall but in the furnace of persecution. It was born from the conviction that the Christian faith is true and that the truth can be defended, even in the most hostile circumstances.

Part Three: Apologetics in the New Testament

The apostle Peter was not alone in practicing and commanding apologetics. The apostle Paul was perhaps the greatest apologist of the New Testament era. Paul's ministry, as recorded in the book of Acts, is a masterclass in defending the faith before diverse audiences.

In Acts chapter seventeen, beginning at verse sixteen, we read of Paul's visit to the city of Athens, around the year 51 AD. Athens was the intellectual capital of the ancient world — the city of Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle. Though its political power had long since faded, it remained the premier center of philosophical thought in the Mediterranean world. The city was filled with idols — the Greek geographer Pausanias, writing in the second century AD, estimated that Athens contained more statues of gods than all the rest of Greece combined. The city's philosophers spent their days in the agora, the marketplace of ideas, debating the latest theories about the nature of reality, morality, and the good life.

When Paul arrived, the Scripture tells us his spirit was stirred within him when he saw the city wholly given to idolatry. But Paul did not simply preach a sermon and leave. He engaged the culture. He reasoned with the Jews in the synagogue and with the Greek philosophers in the public square. The Epicurean and Stoic philosophers invited him to speak at the Areopagus — the ancient council of Athens that met on a rocky hill northwest of the Acropolis, where matters of religion and philosophy were debated. And there Paul delivered one of the most remarkable speeches in the history of the world.

He began not by quoting Scripture — his audience would not have recognized its authority — but by pointing to something they already knew. "Ye men of Athens, I perceive that in all things ye are too superstitious," he said, using the Greek word deisidaimon, which could mean either "very religious" or "superstitious." "For as I passed by, and beheld your devotions, I found an altar with this inscription, TO THE UNKNOWN GOD. Whom therefore ye ignorantly worship, him declare I unto you. God that made the world and all things therein, seeing that he is Lord of heaven and earth, dwelleth not in temples made with hands; Neither is worshipped with men's hands, as though he needed any thing, seeing he giveth to all life, and breath, and all things; And hath made of one blood all nations of men for to dwell on all the face of the earth, and hath determined the times before appointed, and the bounds of their habitation; That they should seek the Lord, if haply they might feel after him, and find him, though he be not far from every one of us: For in him we live, and move, and have our being; as certain also of your own poets have said, For we are also his offspring."

Notice what Paul does here. He quotes not the Hebrew Scriptures but the Greek poets Epimenides of Crete, who wrote "For in him we live, and move, and have our being," and Aratus of Soli, who wrote "For we are also his offspring." Paul was meeting his audience where they were, using their own cultural references to point them toward the truth of the one true God. This is apologetics at its finest — contextual, intelligent, respectful, and uncompromising in its commitment to truth.

In Acts chapter twenty-six, Paul stood before King Herod Agrippa II and the Roman governor Porcius Festus in the city of Caesarea Maritima, around the year 59 AD, and gave a formal apologia for his life and ministry. "I think myself happy, king Agrippa," he said, "because I shall answer for myself this day before thee touching all the things whereof I am accused of the Jews: Especially because I know thee to be expert in all customs and questions which are among the Jews: wherefore I beseech thee to hear me patiently." Paul then recounted his upbringing as a Pharisee, his persecution of the early church, and his dramatic conversion on the Damascus road. He explained the content of the gospel — the death and resurrection of Jesus Christ — and appealed to the king's knowledge of the Hebrew prophets. At the end of the speech, Agrippa said, "Almost thou persuadest me to be a Christian." Paul had used reason, evidence, personal testimony, and Scripture to make his case before one of the most powerful men in the region.

The apostle Paul also wrote about apologetics in his letters. In Second Corinthians chapter ten, verses four and five, he wrote, "For the weapons of our warfare are not carnal, but mighty through God to the pulling down of strong holds; Casting down imaginations, and every high thing that exalteth itself against the knowledge of God, and bringing into captivity every thought to the obedience of Christ." The word translated "imaginations" is logismous, which refers to reasonings, arguments, or speculations. Paul is telling us that part of the Christian's spiritual warfare involves demolishing false arguments and ideas that oppose the knowledge of God. This is the work of apologetics.

And in the book of Jude, verse three, we read this exhortation: "Beloved, when I gave all diligence to write unto you of the common salvation, it was needful for me to write unto you, and exhort you that ye should earnestly contend for the faith which was once delivered unto the saints." The word "contend" is epagonizesthai, an intensified form of the word from which we get "agonize." Jude is telling us to fight for the faith — to struggle, to contend, to defend it vigorously. Apologetics is not a luxury. It is a necessity.

Part Four: The History of Christian Apologetics

The practice of apologetics did not end with the apostles. In the second and third centuries, a group of Christian writers known as the Church Fathers took up the task of defending the faith against both Roman persecution and philosophical objection.

Among the earliest and most important of these was Justin Martyr, born around the year 100 AD in Flavia Neapolis, a Roman city in Samaria. Justin was a pagan philosopher before his conversion. He studied Stoicism, Aristotelianism, Pythagoreanism, and Platonism, searching for truth among the philosophical schools of the day. One day, while walking along the seashore, he met an elderly Christian man who pointed him to the Hebrew prophets and to Jesus Christ. Justin was converted, and he spent the rest of his life using his philosophical training to defend the faith. Around the year 155 AD, he addressed his First Apology to the Roman emperor Antoninus Pius, arguing that Christians were being unjustly persecuted and that the Christian faith was not only compatible with reason but was the fulfillment of all that the best pagan philosophers had been seeking. He wrote a Second Apology addressed to the Roman Senate, and his Dialogue with Trypho records a lengthy conversation with a Jewish scholar about the messianic prophecies of the Old Testament. Justin was eventually arrested and beheaded for his faith around the year 165 AD — hence his title, "Martyr." But his writings endure as powerful examples of early Christian apologetics.

Another great early apologist was Tertullian of Carthage, born around 155 AD in the Roman province of Africa, in what is today Tunisia. Tertullian was a brilliant lawyer and rhetorician who converted to Christianity and became one of its most vigorous defenders. His Apologeticum, written around 197 AD, is a masterful legal defense of Christianity addressed to the Roman governors of the African provinces. Tertullian argued that the persecution of Christians was illegal under Roman law, irrational in its assumptions, and unjust in its application. He demonstrated that Christians were loyal, productive citizens whose moral lives put their pagan neighbors to shame. Tertullian is credited with coining the famous phrase, "The blood of the martyrs is the seed of the church."

Origen of Alexandria, born around 185 AD in Egypt and who died around 254 AD, wrote his great work Contra Celsum — Against Celsus — in response to the pagan philosopher Celsus, who had written a comprehensive attack on Christianity called The True Word around 178 AD. Origen answered Celsus's objections point by point, demonstrating the historical credibility of the Gospels, the fulfillment of messianic prophecy, and the rational coherence of Christian theology. Contra Celsum remains one of the most thorough apologetic works of the ancient world.

In the fourth and fifth centuries, the great Augustine of Hippo — born in 354 AD in Thagaste, in the Roman province of Numidia, and who died in 430 AD — became the most influential Christian thinker of the first millennium. Augustine's masterwork, The City of God, written between 413 and 426 AD, was itself an apologetic work. It was written in response to the sack of Rome by the Visigoth king Alaric on August 24, 410 AD. Pagans blamed the fall of Rome on the Christians, arguing that the abandonment of the old Roman gods had brought divine punishment upon the city. Augustine responded with a massive, sweeping defense of the Christian worldview. In twenty-two books, he demonstrated the failure of pagan religion, the sovereignty of God over human history, and the eternal destiny of the City of God — the community of the redeemed that endures when all earthly cities crumble.

In the medieval period, Thomas Aquinas, born in 1225 in Roccasecca, Italy, and who died on March 7, 1274, synthesized Christian theology and Aristotelian philosophy in his monumental Summa Theologica. Aquinas presented his famous Five Ways — five philosophical arguments for the existence of God drawn from motion, efficient causation, possibility and necessity, the gradation of being, and the governance of the world. He also wrote the Summa Contra Gentiles, a comprehensive apologetic work designed to equip Christian missionaries in their encounters with Muslim and Jewish scholars.

The Protestant Reformation of the sixteenth century brought a renewed emphasis on the authority and sufficiency of Scripture, and with it new forms of apologetic engagement. John Calvin, born on July 10, 1509, in Noyon, France, and who died on May 27, 1564, in Geneva, in the opening chapters of his Institutes of the Christian Religion, first published in 1536, presented powerful arguments for the existence of God and the authority of Scripture. Calvin taught that all human beings possess a sensus divinitatis — an innate sense of the divine — implanted by God in the human heart, which makes the knowledge of God inescapable.

The Puritans, who are the spiritual forebears of this very program, were deeply committed to the defense of the faith. John Owen, born in 1616 in Stadham, Oxfordshire, and who died on August 24, 1683, wrote extensively on the authority and inspiration of Scripture. His work The Reason of Faith argues that faith in Scripture is not a blind leap but is grounded in the self-authenticating testimony of the Holy Spirit working through the Word. Owen wrote, "The Scripture has in itself sufficient evidence of its divine original, and the full persuasion of this in the minds of men is the work of the Holy Spirit."

Stephen Charnock, born in 1628 and who died in 1680, produced The Existence and Attributes of God, one of the most comprehensive treatments of natural theology ever written. Charnock marshaled arguments from the existence of the universe, the design of nature, the testimony of conscience, and the universal consent of mankind to demonstrate the reality and character of God. He wrote, "To deny the being of a God is to unravel all the principles of rational and moral philosophy."

Jonathan Edwards, born on October 5, 1703, in East Windsor, Connecticut, and who died on March 22, 1758, in Princeton, New Jersey, was perhaps the most brilliant philosophical mind America has ever produced. Edwards combined rigorous philosophical argument with passionate evangelical devotion. His Freedom of the Will, published in 1754, is a masterful defense of the compatibility of divine sovereignty and human responsibility.

Blaise Pascal, the great French mathematician and philosopher born on June 19, 1623, in Clermont-Ferrand, and who died on August 19, 1662, left behind his Pensees — a collection of notes for a planned defense of the Christian faith that he never lived to complete. Pascal was a prodigious genius — by the age of sixteen he had written a significant treatise on geometry, and he went on to make groundbreaking contributions to mathematics, physics, and the invention of the mechanical calculator. After a profound conversion experience on the night of November 23, 1654, Pascal devoted himself increasingly to the defense of the Christian faith. He argued that human beings are caught between greatness and misery — capable of extraordinary things yet prone to profound wretchedness — and that only the Christian doctrine of creation and the fall can explain this paradox. He wrote, "The heart has its reasons, which reason does not know," expressing the truth that faith involves not merely the intellect but the whole person. Pascal also proposed his famous "wager" — the argument that believing in God is the only rational bet, since the potential gain of eternal life infinitely outweighs any earthly cost of belief.

In the twentieth century, Clive Staples Lewis, born on November 29, 1898, in Belfast, Ireland, and who died on November 22, 1963 — the same day President Kennedy was assassinated — became perhaps the most widely read Christian apologist in history. Lewis was a professor of medieval and Renaissance literature at Oxford and later at Cambridge. He had been a committed atheist for much of his early life, but through the influence of friends such as J.R.R. Tolkien and Hugo Dyson, and through his own relentless pursuit of truth, he was converted to Christianity in 1931. He later described himself as "the most dejected and reluctant convert in all England." Lewis wrote Mere Christianity, originally delivered as a series of radio broadcasts during the Second World War, The Problem of Pain, Miracles, The Abolition of Man, and many other works that have introduced millions to the rational foundations of the Christian faith. His famous "trilemma" argument — that Jesus must be either Lord, liar, or lunatic — has become one of the most widely known apologetic arguments in the modern world. Lewis wrote with such clarity, warmth, and insight that his books continue to be read and cherished more than sixty years after his death.

Part Five: Why Apologetics Matters — Six Reasons

Now that we have surveyed the rich and remarkable history of Christian apologetics — from the apostles to the Church Fathers to the Reformers to the Puritans to the modern defenders of the faith — let me explain to you why this tradition matters for you personally, right now, in your own life. I will give you six reasons, each of which I want you to consider carefully and remember.

First, apologetics matters because God commands it. The passage from First Peter three, fifteen is not a suggestion. It is an imperative. "Be ready always to give an answer." This is a command given to all believers. God expects His people to be able to explain and defend their faith. Jude three adds urgency: "Earnestly contend for the faith." These are not optional activities for spiritually advanced Christians. They are basic duties of every follower of Christ.

Second, apologetics matters because faith and reason are not enemies. One of the great lies of the modern age is the claim that faith is opposed to reason — that believing in God requires you to abandon your intellect. This is profoundly false. The Christian faith has been defended by some of the greatest minds in history. Christianity invented the university. Roger Bacon, a Franciscan friar born around 1214, pioneered the scientific method. Nicolaus Copernicus, a canon of the Catholic Church born in 1473, revolutionized astronomy. Isaac Newton, born on December 25, 1642, wrote more about theology than about physics. The very concept of rational inquiry — the belief that the universe is orderly and intelligible — is grounded in the Christian conviction that the universe was created by a rational God. As Richard Baxter, born in 1615 and who died in 1691, wrote, "The neglect of reason in religion has been the great cause of all the disorders and evils which have come upon the church."

Third, apologetics matters because you will face challenges to your faith. As you grow and enter the wider world — whether you attend a university, enter the workforce, or simply engage in conversations with neighbors and acquaintances — you will encounter people who challenge Christianity. Some will do so respectfully. Others will do so with contempt. Some will raise objections you have never considered before.

Consider the reality that faces many young Christians. A young man or woman raised in a faithful Christian home enters a university classroom. On the very first day, a professor stands before the class and announces, as one famous philosopher was known to do, "By the end of this semester, I will have disabused you of your religious superstitions." Textbooks present naturalistic explanations for the origin of the universe and of life without even acknowledging that alternative views exist. Fellow students ridicule faith as childish or anti-intellectual. Social media is filled with confident assertions that science has disproved God, that the Bible is full of contradictions, and that religion is the source of the world's problems.

If you have not prepared yourself to respond, these challenges can rattle your confidence, create doubt, and even lead you to abandon the faith that was passed down to you by your parents and grandparents. Statistics tell us that a significant number of young people raised in Christian homes walk away from the faith during their college years. Many of them do so not because they have carefully examined the evidence and found Christianity wanting, but because they were unprepared for the challenges they encountered and had no answers to give when the questions came.

But it does not have to be this way. If you have studied, thought, and prepared — if you have done the work of understanding the evidence and the arguments — these challenges will not weaken your faith. They will strengthen it. Every objection answered becomes a foundation stone of deeper conviction. Every question wrestled with becomes an occasion for greater understanding. Thomas Watson, the Puritan who died in 1686, wrote, "A man may be a great scholar, and yet not wise; but he cannot be a wise man and not a scholar." Study the faith. Know the faith. Be ready.

Fourth, apologetics matters because it is an act of love. When someone raises an objection to Christianity, they are often not merely playing intellectual games. They are wrestling with genuine questions that affect the deepest matters of their lives — the meaning of existence, the reality of death, the hope of eternity. When you take their questions seriously and provide thoughtful answers, you are doing something profoundly loving. You are removing obstacles that stand between them and the truth that could save their souls. Richard Sibbes, born in 1577 and who died in 1635, wrote, "God's children are most loving and tender-hearted." Apologetics done rightly is tender-hearted work.

Fifth, apologetics matters because it strengthens your own faith. As you investigate the evidence for God's existence, the reliability of Scripture, the resurrection of Jesus Christ, and the answers to the great objections, you will find that your own faith deepens and matures in remarkable ways. You will discover that Christianity is not a fragile house of cards that collapses under scrutiny, but a mighty fortress that stands firmer the more closely you examine it. The process of studying apologetics is itself a spiritual discipline — a means of grace that God uses to draw you closer to Himself and to deepen your understanding of His truth. Many of the greatest saints in history have testified that their study of the evidences for the faith was itself an act of worship — that the more they examined the arguments, the more they marveled at the wisdom and power of God. As the Psalmist wrote in Psalm 111:2, "The works of the LORD are great, sought out of all them that have pleasure therein." To study the evidences for the Christian faith is to take pleasure in the works of the Lord.

Sixth, apologetics matters because the honor of God is at stake. When the Christian faith is publicly attacked and no one rises to defend it, the impression is given that it cannot be defended. But when Christians respond with intelligence, humility, and conviction, the honor of God is upheld. The watching world sees that the Christian faith is not only emotionally satisfying but intellectually compelling. John Flavel, born around 1627 and who died in 1691, wrote, "They that know God will be humble; they that know themselves cannot be proud." Apologetics, rightly practiced, glorifies God by demonstrating the truth and beauty of His character.

Part Six: The Three Main Approaches to Apologetics

Now, let me introduce you to the three main approaches to apologetics that Christians have historically employed.

The first approach is called Classical Apologetics. This approach follows a two-step method. First, it establishes the existence of God through philosophical arguments — the cosmological argument from the origin of the universe, the teleological argument from design in nature, the moral argument from objective moral values, and the ontological argument from the very concept of a perfect being. Once the existence of God is established through these philosophical proofs, classical apologetics then moves to the specific evidence for the Christian faith — the reliability of the Bible, the resurrection of Jesus, the fulfillment of prophecy, and so forth. This approach builds a cumulative case, moving from general theism to specific Christianity. Classical apologetics has been practiced by many of the great thinkers of the church, including Thomas Aquinas in the thirteenth century, William Paley in the eighteenth century, and in the modern era, Norman Geisler, R.C. Sproul, and William Lane Craig. Its strength lies in its logical structure and its ability to engage secular philosophy on its own terms.

The second approach is called Evidential Apologetics. This approach focuses primarily on the historical and empirical evidence for the Christian faith. It emphasizes the evidence for the resurrection of Jesus, the archaeological confirmation of the Bible, the testimony of eyewitnesses, the fulfillment of prophecy, and the transformation of individual lives. While evidential apologetics does not ignore philosophical arguments entirely, it tends to lead with the historical facts and let the evidence speak for itself. Practitioners of this approach include Gary Habermas, whose work on the resurrection has been enormously influential, Josh McDowell, whose book Evidence That Demands a Verdict has sold millions of copies since its first publication in 1972, and Lee Strobel, a former journalist and atheist whose book The Case for Christ documents his investigation into the evidence for Christianity. The strength of evidential apologetics lies in its accessibility and its appeal to concrete, verifiable facts.

The third approach is called Presuppositional Apologetics. This approach, developed most fully by the Dutch Reformed theologian Cornelius Van Til, born on May 3, 1895, in Grootegast, the Netherlands, and who died on April 17, 1987, and refined by his student Greg Bahnsen, who was born in 1948 and died in 1995, takes a fundamentally different approach from the other two. Presuppositional apologetics argues that every system of thought rests on presuppositions — foundational assumptions that cannot themselves be proven but that shape everything else. The presuppositionalist argues that only the Christian worldview provides the necessary preconditions for logic, science, morality, and human dignity. Without God, the presuppositionalist contends, you cannot account for the laws of logic, the uniformity of nature, or the existence of objective moral values. Christianity is not merely one option among many; it is the precondition of all intelligible experience. The strength of this approach lies in its ability to challenge the hidden assumptions of secular thought and to demonstrate that Christianity is not merely defensible but necessary for making sense of anything at all.

Each of these approaches has real strengths, and the most effective apologists often draw from all three. You do not need to choose one approach and reject the others. Think of them as different tools in a toolbox. Sometimes a philosophical argument is the right tool for the conversation. Sometimes historical evidence is more persuasive. Sometimes challenging the presuppositions of the person you are speaking with is the most effective strategy. The wise apologist learns to read the situation, understand the person, and select the approach that is most likely to be helpful.

It is also worth noting that these approaches are not mutually exclusive in practice. A classical apologist may use historical evidence to support his philosophical arguments. An evidentialist may employ philosophical reasoning to interpret the evidence. A presuppositionalist may point to specific evidences and arguments even as he challenges the unbeliever's fundamental assumptions. The goal in all three approaches is the same: to demonstrate that the Christian faith is true, reasonable, and worthy of belief.

In this unit, we will explore elements of each approach as we address the major questions and objections that arise in discussions of the Christian faith.

Part Seven: The Spirit of Apologetics

Before we close today's lesson, I want to return to Peter's instruction and emphasize one final, crucial point. Peter says that we are to give our defense "with meekness and fear." The word translated "meekness" is prautes in Greek, which means gentleness, humility, courtesy, and considerateness. The word translated "fear" is phobos, and in this context it refers not to fear of our human questioners but to reverence for God. We are to defend the faith gently and reverently — not with arrogance, not with hostility, not with a desire to humiliate or defeat our opponents, but with genuine love for the people we are speaking to and genuine reverence for the God whose truth we are defending.

Paul echoes this spirit in his letter to the Colossians, chapter four, verses five and six: "Walk in wisdom toward them that are without, redeeming the time. Let your speech be alway with grace, seasoned with salt, that ye may know how ye ought to answer every man." Grace and salt. Truth and love. Conviction and compassion. And in Second Timothy chapter two, verses twenty-four through twenty-six, Paul writes, "And the servant of the Lord must not strive; but be gentle unto all men, apt to teach, patient, In meekness instructing those that oppose themselves; if God peradventure will give them repentance to the acknowledging of the truth; And that they may recover themselves out of the snare of the devil, who are taken captive by him at his will." Notice the goal Paul describes: not that we might win arguments, but that God might give our opponents repentance and bring them to a knowledge of the truth. The goal of apologetics is not intellectual victory. It is spiritual rescue.

Apologetics without love is a clanging cymbal. Arguments without compassion are sterile and cold. You may win every debate and lose every soul. John Owen wisely counseled, "A minister may fill his pews and empty his church." In the same way, an apologist may win arguments and drive people further from the truth by the manner in which he argues. Remember always that the person sitting across from you — the skeptic, the atheist, the agnostic, the confused seeker — is a human being made in the image of God, loved by God, and in desperate need of the gospel. Treat them accordingly.

And remember also that apologetics, while vitally important, has its limits. It is not our arguments that ultimately save people. It is the Holy Spirit working through the Word of God. Paul wrote to the Corinthians, "For after that in the wisdom of God the world by wisdom knew not God, it pleased God by the foolishness of preaching to save them that believe" (1 Corinthians 1:21). And again, "And my speech and my preaching was not with enticing words of man's wisdom, but in demonstration of the Spirit and of power: That your faith should not stand in the wisdom of men, but in the power of God" (1 Corinthians 2:4-5). Our arguments can remove obstacles, answer objections, and demonstrate the reasonableness of the faith. But it is God who opens hearts, illumines minds, and grants the gift of saving faith. We are instruments in His hands — important instruments, but instruments nonetheless. Thomas Goodwin, born in 1600 and who died in 1680, wrote, "God reserves the work of conversion to Himself, but He uses means." Apologetics is one of those means.

As we close this introductory lesson, let me leave you with a charge. Over the course of this unit, you will study the major arguments for the existence of God, the evidence for the reliability of the Bible, the historical case for the resurrection of Jesus Christ, the Christian response to the problem of evil and suffering, and the relationship between science and faith. Each of these topics is a battlefield on which the truth of Christianity is tested and defended. Your task is not merely to memorize the arguments but to understand them, internalize them, and make them your own. Know what you believe. Know why you believe it. And be ready to share those reasons with anyone who asks — with meekness and fear.

Remember the words of the prophet Isaiah, chapter forty-one, verse ten: "Fear thou not; for I am with thee: be not dismayed; for I am thy God: I will strengthen thee; yea, I will help thee; yea, I will uphold thee with the right hand of my righteousness." And remember the words of the Lord Jesus in Matthew chapter ten, verses nineteen and twenty: "But when they deliver you up, take no thought how or what ye shall speak: for it shall be given you in that same hour what ye shall speak. For it is not ye that speak, but the Spirit of your Father which speaketh in you." You do not go into this work alone. God is with you. His truth is on your side. And His Spirit will guide you as you learn to defend the faith once delivered to the saints.

--- Review Questions ---

--- Activity ---

Read 1 Peter 3:13-17 carefully in your King James Bible. Write a one-paragraph paraphrase of this passage in your own words, making sure to capture both the command to defend the faith and the manner in which that defense should be given. Then write a list of three to five questions or objections about Christianity that you have either asked yourself or heard from others. These might include questions like, "How do you know God is real?" or "Why does God allow suffering?" or "Isn't the Bible just a book written by men?" Keep this list and revisit it at the end of this unit to see how your ability to respond has grown. Finally, choose one of the historical apologists mentioned in today's lesson — Justin Martyr, Tertullian, Origen, Augustine, Aquinas, Calvin, Owen, Charnock, Edwards, Pascal, or Lewis — and write a brief biographical sketch of no more than one page, including their birth and death dates, their major works, and their most important contribution to the defense of the Christian faith.

--- Closing ---

Apologetics is a calling for every Christian, not just for scholars and pastors. You are called to know what you believe and why you believe it, and to share those reasons with gentleness and respect. From the apostle Peter writing to persecuted Christians in the first century, to Paul reasoning with philosophers in Athens, to Justin Martyr presenting his case before the Roman emperor, to Augustine defending the faith after the fall of Rome, to the Puritans expounding the glory of God with rigorous minds and tender hearts, to the great apologists of the modern era — the people of God have always been people who could give a reason for the hope that is in them. This unit will equip you for that same calling. It will sharpen your mind, deepen your faith, and prepare you to stand firm when the questions come — and they will come. But you will be ready. Because the truth is on your side, and the God of truth is with you. Until next time, students.
