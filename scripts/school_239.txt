Growing in Christ — Sanctification


Good morning, students. The fear of the Lord is the beginning of knowledge. That ancient truth is the foundation of everything we study here at the Puritan Academy. Let us begin our day with reverence and expectation.

Dear Lord, we come before You at the start of this school day. We are small and the world of knowledge is vast, but You hold it all in Your hand. Guide our thoughts, sharpen our attention, and give us understanding. Through Christ. Amen.

A moment of quiet meditation will prepare our hearts for deeper study.

Our Scripture for today is Galatians 5:22-26; 2 Peter 1:3-11; Colossians 3:12-17; Matthew 5:3-12:

"But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, faith, Meekness, temperance: against such there is no law. And they that are Christ's have crucified the flesh with the affections and lusts. If we live in the Spirit, let us also walk in the Spirit. Let us not be desirous of vain glory, provoking one another, envying one another. According as his divine power hath given unto us all things that pertain unto life and godliness, through the knowledge of him that hath called us to glory and virtue: Whereby are given unto us exceeding great and precious promises: that by these ye might be partakers of the divine nature, having escaped the corruption that is in the world through lust. And beside this, giving all diligence, add to your faith virtue; and to virtue knowledge; And to knowledge temperance; and to temperance patience; and to patience godliness; And to godliness brotherly kindness; and to brotherly kindness charity. For if these things be in you, and abound, they make you that ye shall neither be barren nor unfruitful in the knowledge of our Lord Jesus Christ. But he that lacketh these things is blind, and cannot see afar off, and hath forgotten that he was purged from his old sins. Wherefore the rather, brethren, give diligence to make your calling and election sure: for if ye do these things, ye shall never fall: For so an entrance shall be ministered unto you abundantly into the everlasting kingdom of our Lord and Saviour Jesus Christ. Put on therefore, as the elect of God, holy and beloved, bowels of mercies, kindness, humbleness of mind, meekness, longsuffering; Forbearing one another, and forgiving one another, if any man have a quarrel against any: even as Christ forgave you, so also do ye. And above all these things put on charity, which is the bond of perfectness. And let the peace of God rule in your hearts, to the which also ye are called in one body; and be ye thankful. Let the word of Christ dwell in you richly in all wisdom; teaching and admonishing one another in psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, singing with grace in your hearts to the Lord. And whatsoever ye do in word or deed, do all in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to God and the Father by him."

What does Christ look like living in you? Not in theory but in practice — in the way you treat your family at breakfast, in how you respond when someone cuts you off in traffic, in what you think about when no one is watching, in how you handle disappointment and delay. The fruit of the Spirit is the answer: love that gives without counting the cost, joy that endures even in dark seasons, peace that remains when everything around you is shaking, patience that bears with the failings of others without bitterness, kindness that sees needs and meets them without being asked, goodness that goes out of its way to do right, faithfulness that keeps promises even when it is costly, gentleness that handles fragile people with care, and self-control that says no to what harms and yes to what heals. You cannot produce these by trying harder. But you can receive them by abiding more deeply in Christ — by staying connected to the vine, by drinking from the well of His Word, by breathing the air of prayer. Abide in Him today, and let His life flow through you as fruit that glorifies the Father.

As we prepare for today's lesson, remember that everything we study flows from this one fountain: the Word of God. Our lesson today is part of Growing in Christ — Sanctification, and it is titled: Christian Virtues — Fruit of the Spirit. Let us proceed with minds engaged and hearts open.


Now we come to the heart of today's curriculum.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes in Galatians 5:22-26; 2 Peter 1:3-11; Colossians 3:12-17; Matthew 5:3-12.

If mortification is the negative side of sanctification — putting sin to death — then the cultivation of Christian virtues is its positive side: putting on the graces of Christlikeness. The apostle Paul describes these virtues as "the fruit of the Spirit" in Galatians 5:22-23: "But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, faith, meekness, temperance: against such there is no law." This passage is one of the most beautiful and comprehensive descriptions of the Christian character in all of Scripture, and it repays careful, detailed study.

First, notice that Paul says "fruit," not "fruits." The singular noun is deliberate. The nine qualities he lists are not nine separate fruits that a believer may or may not possess; they are nine facets of a single fruit — the unified character of Christ reproduced in the believer by the Holy Spirit. A Christian who has love but not patience, or joy but not self-control, is exhibiting an incomplete and distorted version of the Spirit's work. The Spirit's fruit is a cluster, and where He truly dwells, all nine qualities will be present in some measure, growing together in progressive maturity.

Second, notice that this is the fruit of the Spirit, not the fruit of human effort. These virtues are not achievements we produce by trying harder but graces that the Holy Spirit produces in us as we abide in Christ. Jesus said, "I am the vine, ye are the branches: He that abideth in me, and I in him, the same bringeth forth much fruit: for without me ye can do nothing" (John 15:5). A branch does not produce fruit by straining and exerting itself; it produces fruit by remaining connected to the vine, drawing nourishment from the vine's life-giving sap. So we produce the fruit of the Spirit not by willpower but by abiding in Christ — by faith, by prayer, by communion with Him through His Word.

The nine virtues form a magnificent portrait of Christlike character. Love (agape) is the foundational virtue — the self-giving, unconditional, sacrificial love that seeks the good of others regardless of cost to self. It is the love that God demonstrated at the cross: "But God commendeth his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8). Joy (chara) is the deep, settled gladness that springs from knowing God and resting in His sovereignty — not the superficial happiness that depends on favorable circumstances but the profound contentment that persists even in suffering. Peace (eirene) is the tranquility of soul that comes from being reconciled to God through Christ — the peace that passes understanding, guarding our hearts and minds (Philippians 4:7).

Longsuffering (makrothumia) is patience with people — the ability to bear with the faults, failures, and provocations of others without retaliating or giving up on them. Gentleness (chrestotes) is the kindly disposition that expresses itself in gracious, considerate, tender treatment of others — the opposite of harshness and severity. Goodness (agathosune) is the active quality of generosity and benevolence that expresses itself in concrete acts of service and giving — not merely refraining from evil but actively doing good. Faith (pistis) here carries the sense of faithfulness, reliability, and trustworthiness — the character of one whose word can be depended upon and whose commitments are kept. Meekness (prautes) is not weakness but controlled strength — the humility that does not insist on its own rights but submits to God's will and treats others with respect and deference. Temperance (enkrateia) is self-control — the mastery of one's appetites, passions, and impulses, the ability to say no to sinful desires and yes to godly discipline.

Paul adds a remarkable observation: "Against such there is no law" (Galatians 5:23). No law, human or divine, condemns these qualities. While the works of the flesh are universally condemned by every moral law, the fruit of the Spirit is universally commended. A person who truly displays these nine qualities needs no external law to constrain them because they are living above the law — not in violation of it but in fulfillment of it. The law was given to restrain sin; where sin is replaced by the Spirit's fruit, the law has accomplished its purpose.

The apostle Peter provides a complementary list of virtues in 2 Peter 1:5-7, presented as a chain of spiritual growth: "Add to your faith virtue; and to virtue knowledge; and to knowledge temperance; and to temperance patience; and to patience godliness; and to godliness brotherly kindness; and to brotherly kindness charity." Peter's list describes a progression — each virtue building on the one before it, with faith as the foundation and love as the crown. Peter emphasizes the believer's responsibility with the phrase "giving all diligence" — while the virtues are produced by the Spirit, we must actively cooperate by exercising diligence, effort, and intentionality in their cultivation.

Peter provides both positive motivation and negative warning. Positively, "If these things be in you, and abound, they make you that ye shall neither be barren nor unfruitful in the knowledge of our Lord Jesus Christ" (2 Peter 1:8). The presence of these virtues produces a fruitful, productive Christian life. Negatively, "He that lacketh these things is blind, and cannot see afar off, and hath forgotten that he was purged from his old sins" (2 Peter 1:9). The absence of growth in virtue is a symptom of spiritual blindness and forgetfulness — a failure to live in the reality of what Christ has done.

In Colossians 3:12-17, Paul provides yet another portrait of the virtuous life, this time using the imagery of clothing. Believers are to "put on" mercy, kindness, humility, meekness, and longsuffering, like putting on garments. And "above all these things put on charity, which is the bond of perfectness" (Colossians 3:14). Love is the overcoat that binds all the other virtues together and completes the outfit. Without love, the other virtues are disconnected and incomplete; with love, they form a unified, beautiful, Christlike character.

Paul concludes with a comprehensive life principle: "And whatsoever ye do in word or deed, do all in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to God and the Father by him" (Colossians 3:17). Every word, every action, every thought is to be offered to God as an act of worship. The cultivation of Christian virtue is not merely about personal development or moral improvement — it is about glorifying God in every dimension of life. The virtuous life is a life lived entirely for Christ, in which every moment becomes an opportunity to display His character and advance His kingdom.

Our Lord Himself provides the supreme picture of blessed character in the Beatitudes of Matthew 5:3-12. The poor in spirit, the mourners, the meek, those who hunger for righteousness, the merciful, the pure in heart, the peacemakers, those who are persecuted for righteousness — these are the citizens of the kingdom of heaven, and these qualities are the fruit of the Spirit working in surrendered human hearts. Let us pursue these virtues with all diligence, knowing that they are the marks of authentic Christianity and the evidence of the Spirit's transforming work.

This biblical foundation is essential. Without it, everything else we study today would be like a house built on sand. But with it, we stand on solid rock.


Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. Thomas Watson wrote extensively on this subject in "The Beatitudes; A Body of Divinity." The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life.

Thomas Watson (c. 1620-1686) devoted substantial portions of his writing to the cultivation of Christian virtue, particularly in his exposition of the Beatitudes and in his comprehensive work "A Body of Divinity," which systematically expounds the Westminster Shorter Catechism. Watson's treatment of Christian virtues is distinguished by his ability to combine theological depth with practical specificity — he does not merely define the virtues but shows exactly how they are cultivated, what they look like in daily life, and how they are distinguished from their counterfeits.

Watson begins his treatment of the fruit of the Spirit by emphasizing that true virtue is always supernatural in origin. He writes that the difference between a virtuous unbeliever and a virtuous Christian is the difference between a painted fire and a real fire. The painted fire may look convincing from a distance, but it produces no heat, no light, and no warmth. Likewise, the natural man may display certain outward virtues — courtesy, generosity, self-discipline — but these are merely natural qualities produced by temperament, upbringing, or social pressure. They do not spring from a renewed heart, they are not empowered by the Holy Spirit, and they do not bring glory to God. True Christian virtue is always the work of the Spirit, flowing from the new nature that is implanted in regeneration.

Watson devotes particular attention to love, which he identifies as the queen of all virtues and the root from which all others grow. He writes that love is to the Christian virtues what the sun is to the solar system — the center around which all others orbit and from which they receive their light and warmth. Without love, patience becomes mere stoicism, kindness becomes mere politeness, and faithfulness becomes mere duty. But when love animates all the other virtues, they come alive with spiritual power and beauty. Watson cites Paul's declaration, "And above all these things put on charity, which is the bond of perfectness" (Colossians 3:14), and argues that this means love is both the supreme virtue and the connecting principle that binds all other virtues into a coherent whole.

Watson's treatment of joy is equally rich. He distinguishes between worldly joy and spiritual joy. Worldly joy depends on favorable circumstances and evaporates when circumstances change. Spiritual joy is rooted in unchanging realities — the love of God, the finished work of Christ, the indwelling of the Spirit, the hope of glory — and therefore persists even in the midst of suffering and loss. Watson writes that the Christian has reason for joy that the world can neither give nor take away: the joy of sins forgiven, the joy of adoption into God's family, the joy of communion with Christ, the joy of knowing that all things work together for good, and the joy of anticipating eternal glory. He argues that joylessness in a Christian is not a sign of humility but of unbelief — a failure to grasp and rest in the magnificent truths of the gospel.

Watson is particularly insightful on the virtue of meekness, which the world consistently misunderstands and despises. He defines meekness not as weakness or passivity but as strength under control — the ability to restrain one's power for the sake of others. He points to Moses, whom Scripture calls "the meekest man on earth" (Numbers 12:3) — yet Moses was also one of the boldest and most decisive leaders in history. Meekness is not the absence of strength but the willingness to use strength in service rather than in self-assertion. Watson observes that meekness is particularly evident in how a person responds to provocation, criticism, and injustice. The meek person does not retaliate, does not bear grudges, and does not seek revenge — not because they are incapable of these things but because they trust God to vindicate them in His own time and way.

Watson's treatment of temperance or self-control addresses one of the most practical challenges of the Christian life. He defines temperance as the governance of all our appetites, passions, and desires by reason illuminated by grace. It is the ability to enjoy God's good gifts without being enslaved by them, to use the things of the world without being consumed by them, to eat without gluttony, to speak without excess, to rest without sloth, and to pursue pleasure without abandoning duty. Watson observes that self-control is one of the rarest and most difficult of all virtues because it requires constant vigilance and immediate obedience to the Spirit's promptings. The moment of temptation is too late to begin developing self-control — it must be cultivated in advance through daily discipline and habitual dependence on the Spirit.

Watson provides extensive guidance on how to cultivate each virtue. He identifies several means by which the Spirit produces His fruit in the believer's life. First, meditation on Scripture — particularly on the life and character of Christ, who is the perfect embodiment of all virtue. The more we contemplate Christ's love, His patience, His gentleness, His self-control, the more these qualities are reproduced in us by the Spirit's transforming work. Second, prayer — specifically asking God for the virtues we lack, since James promises that God gives "liberally, and upbraideth not" to those who ask in faith (James 1:5). Third, practice — deliberately exercising the virtues in daily life, starting with small situations and building toward greater challenges. Watson compares this to how a musician improves by daily practice or how a soldier becomes battle-ready through regular training exercises.

Watson also warns against counterfeit virtues that deceive the soul into false assurance. He identifies false peace (the absence of conviction rather than the presence of reconciliation with God), false joy (excitement based on feelings rather than on truth), false humility (self-deprecation that is really inverted pride), and false love (sentimental affection that refuses to speak truth or confront sin). Watson urges believers to test their virtues by asking whether they spring from a renewed heart, whether they are accompanied by all the other virtues (since the Spirit's fruit is a cluster, not individual items), and whether they produce genuine love for God and obedience to His Word.

Watson concludes with the encouraging truth that every genuine Christian possesses all the virtues of the Spirit in seed form, even if some are more developed than others. The Spirit who dwells in every believer is the Author of all these graces, and His presence guarantees that they will grow. Our duty is to cooperate with His work by using the means of grace faithfully, by exercising the virtues we already possess, and by praying earnestly for those we lack. Watson assures us that the God who has begun a good work in us will carry it on to completion. The fruit may grow slowly, but it will grow — because the Gardener is God Himself, and He never abandons His garden.

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.


Let us now press deeper into the doctrine of Christian virtue by examining additional Scripture passages and Puritan teaching that will strengthen our understanding and application.

The apostle Paul provides one of the most stirring descriptions of love — the foundational virtue of the Spirit's fruit — in 1 Corinthians 13:4-7: "Charity suffereth long, and is kind; charity envieth not; charity vaunteth not itself, is not puffed up, Doth not behave itself unseemly, seeketh not her own, is not easily provoked, thinketh no evil; Rejoiceth not in iniquity, but rejoiceth in the truth; Beareth all things, believeth all things, hopeth all things, endureth all things." This passage is not merely a poetic tribute to love — it is a penetrating description of what love looks like in practice. It is patient when provoked. It is kind when mistreated. It does not envy the success of others. It does not boast of its own achievements. It does not insist on its own way. It does not keep a record of wrongs. It endures everything without breaking. This is the love that the Holy Spirit produces in the hearts of believers, and it is the love that Christ Himself perfectly displayed throughout His earthly ministry — most supremely on the cross, where He loved those who hated Him, forgave those who murdered Him, and interceded for those who mocked Him.

The Puritan divine John Owen, in his meditation on the glory of Christ, connects the cultivation of Christian virtue directly to the contemplation of Christ's character. Owen argues that transformation into Christlikeness occurs primarily through beholding Christ — gazing upon His character in Scripture with the eyes of faith until we are gradually changed into His image. Owen bases this principle on 2 Corinthians 3:18: "But we all, with open face beholding as in a glass the glory of the Lord, are changed into the same image from glory to glory, even as by the Spirit of the Lord." The process is simple but profound: as we behold Christ's love, we become more loving. As we behold His patience, we become more patient. As we behold His gentleness, we become more gentle. The Spirit uses the revelation of Christ's character in Scripture as the primary instrument for producing Christ's character in us.

Richard Sibbes, the beloved Puritan pastor known as "the heavenly Doctor Sibbes," provides a tender and encouraging treatment of the virtue of peace in his work "The Soul's Conflict." Sibbes distinguishes between three dimensions of peace that the Spirit produces in the believer: peace with God (the objective reality of reconciliation through the blood of Christ), the peace of God (the subjective experience of tranquility that guards the heart — Philippians 4:7), and peace with others (the relational harmony that flows from being at peace with God). Sibbes writes that many Christians experience the first dimension — they know they are forgiven and reconciled to God — but struggle with the second — they lack the felt experience of peace in their daily lives. Sibbes counsels such believers that the peace of God is cultivated through trust in God's sovereignty, through meditation on His promises, and through the practice of casting all anxieties upon Him (1 Peter 5:7). He assures them that the God who has made peace with them through the cross will also give them the experience of peace in their hearts, as they learn to rest in His faithfulness.

Thomas Brooks, in "The Mute Christian Under the Smarting Rod," provides an extended treatment of the virtue of patience — or longsuffering — under God's providential dealings. Brooks observes that patience is the virtue most tested and most refined in the furnace of affliction. He writes that patience is not mere passive endurance — the stoic resignation of one who has no hope — but active trust in a sovereign God who works all things for good. Brooks distinguishes between natural patience (which is merely temperamental calmness) and spiritual patience (which is a grace produced by the Holy Spirit through the experience of suffering). He cites James 1:2-4: "My brethren, count it all joy when ye fall into divers temptations; Knowing this, that the trying of your faith worketh patience. But let patience have her perfect work, that ye may be perfect and entire, wanting nothing." Brooks argues that patience is the virtue that brings all other virtues to maturity — it is the fire that refines faith, the hammer that shapes character, and the soil in which every other grace grows to fullness.

The Puritan pastor Jeremiah Burroughs, in his classic work "The Rare Jewel of Christian Contentment," addresses a virtue closely related to the fruit of the Spirit that is often overlooked: contentment. Burroughs defines contentment as "that sweet, inward, quiet, gracious frame of spirit, which freely submits to and delights in God's wise and fatherly disposal in every condition." He argues that contentment is the natural fruit of faith, love, and trust in God's sovereignty. The contented Christian is one who has learned to say, with the apostle Paul, "I have learned, in whatsoever state I am, therewith to be content" (Philippians 4:11). Burroughs observes that Paul says "I have learned" — contentment is not a natural disposition but a learned grace, cultivated through years of experiencing God's faithfulness in every circumstance. The person who is content in God needs nothing the world can offer and fears nothing the world can threaten, for the sovereign God who governs all things is both the source and the guarantee of all true satisfaction.

Jonathan Edwards, in his sermon "Christian Charity, or the Duty of Charity to the Poor," connects the fruit of the Spirit directly to practical acts of mercy and generosity. Edwards argues that genuine love, joy, peace, and goodness are never merely internal feelings — they always express themselves in concrete actions of service to others, particularly to the poor, the sick, the bereaved, and the oppressed. Edwards writes that the person who claims to possess the fruit of the Spirit but refuses to show mercy to those in need is deceiving himself. The apostle John makes the same point with devastating directness: "But whoso hath this world's good, and seeth his brother have need, and shutteth up his bowels of compassion from him, how dwelleth the love of God in him? My little children, let us not love in word, neither in tongue; but in deed and in truth" (1 John 3:17-18). The fruit of the Spirit is never merely theoretical — it always bears practical fruit in the lives of others.

The Puritan Richard Baxter, in his pastoral writings, addresses the virtue of self-control with characteristic directness. Baxter argues that self-control is the guardian virtue — the virtue that protects all the others. Without self-control, love degenerates into sentimentality, joy into frivolity, peace into laziness, and goodness into indiscriminate permissiveness. Baxter compares self-control to the walls of a city — they do not constitute the city, but without them, the city is defenseless against every enemy. He writes that self-control is cultivated through daily discipline: governing the tongue so that it speaks only what is true, kind, and necessary; governing the eyes so that they do not feast on what is impure or covetous; governing the appetite so that it serves the body rather than ruling it; and governing the use of time so that every hour is invested wisely rather than squandered on trivialities. Baxter insists that "a Christian without self-control is like a city without walls — open to every attack and unable to defend what is most precious."


Before we move to our discussion questions, let us apply the doctrine of Christian virtue to daily living.

First, examine your life for the presence or absence of the Spirit's fruit. Since the Spirit's fruit is a cluster — all nine graces growing together — the absence of any one grace is a signal that something is hindering the Spirit's work. Are you loving but impatient? Joyful but lacking self-control? Faithful but unkind? Identify the areas where you are weakest and make them the focus of your prayer and effort.

Second, cultivate the fruit of the Spirit through the means of grace. The Puritans identified three primary means by which God grows His people in virtue: the Word of God (reading, hearing, meditating, memorizing), prayer (private and corporate), and fellowship with other believers (worship, accountability, mutual encouragement). These are the channels through which the Spirit's grace flows into our lives. Neglect them, and the fruit withers. Use them faithfully, and the fruit flourishes.

Third, practice the virtues in small things. Watson counsels believers not to wait for great occasions to exercise virtue but to practice in the daily, mundane situations of ordinary life. Show patience when the line is long. Show kindness to the person who irritates you. Show self-control when you are tempted to speak harshly. Show faithfulness in keeping small promises. These small exercises build spiritual muscles that will serve you in the larger tests that lie ahead. As our Lord taught, "He that is faithful in that which is least is faithful also in much" (Luke 16:10).

Fourth, remember that the fruit of the Spirit is evidence of salvation, not the cause of it. We are not saved by producing virtue but by trusting in Christ. Yet genuine faith always produces genuine virtue, as surely as a living vine produces fruit. If you see no fruit in your life, the problem may not be that you need to try harder but that you need to abide more deeply in Christ. Return to the vine. Draw nourishment from His Word. Rest in His finished work. And the fruit will come — slowly perhaps, unevenly perhaps, but certainly and inevitably, because the Gardener is God Himself.


Now we move from hearing to discussing. This is where knowledge becomes understanding.

Discussion Question 1: Paul says 'fruit' (singular) rather than 'fruits' (plural). What is the significance of this, and what does it teach us about the unity of Christian character?

Discussion Question 2: Watson distinguishes between natural virtues and spiritual virtues. What is the essential difference? Can an unbeliever display genuine Christian virtue?

Discussion Question 3: Watson calls joylessness in a Christian a sign of unbelief rather than humility. Do you agree? How should we cultivate genuine spiritual joy?

Discussion Question 4: How does Peter's 'chain of virtues' in 2 Peter 1:5-7 show us a pathway for spiritual growth? Why does the chain begin with faith and end with love?

Discussion Question 5: Watson warns against counterfeit virtues. What is a counterfeit virtue, and how can we distinguish it from the genuine article?

Take time with each question. The Puritans valued what they called "holy conference," the practice of discussing spiritual truths together. Do not settle for quick, surface answers. Push deeper. Ask follow-up questions. Listen carefully to one another.

Application is where truth becomes life. It is not enough to know what Galatians 5:22-26; 2 Peter 1:3-11; Colossians 3:12-17; Matthew 5:3-12 says. We must ask: how does this change the way I live today? How does this shape my thoughts, my words, my actions? The Puritans insisted that a truth that does not transform is a truth that has not been truly understood.


Now for the practical portion of our day. Here is today's activity.

Students will create a comprehensive 'Fruit of the Spirit Character Study' consisting of three components: (1) A visual 'fruit tree' illustration where each of the nine components of the fruit of the Spirit is represented as a piece of fruit on a single tree, with the roots labeled as 'Union with Christ' and the soil labeled as 'The Means of Grace.' For each fruit, students will write a brief definition, a key Scripture verse, and one practical example of how this virtue is displayed in daily life. (2) A 'Virtue Chain' based on 2 Peter 1:5-7, where students illustrate the progressive nature of spiritual growth by creating a chain of linked paper rings, each labeled with one of Peter's virtues, with a brief explanation on each ring of how that virtue builds on the previous one. (3) A personal assessment in which students rate themselves on each of the nine aspects of the Spirit's fruit and identify two or three areas for focused growth, with specific prayer requests and practical goals for the coming month.

Materials needed: Large drawing paper for fruit tree illustration, colored pencils and markers, strips of colored paper for chain links, glue or tape for chain assembly, printed definitions of each virtue with Scripture references, personal assessment worksheets, pens

As you complete this activity, think about how it connects to what you studied in today's lesson. The goal is not just to do something, but to reinforce the truths you have been learning. The Puritans believed that education should engage the whole person: mind, heart, and hands.

Take your time with this activity. Quality matters more than speed. When you are finished, reflect on what you learned through the process of doing. Often we understand something far better after we have tried to put it into practice.


One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

Thomas Watson ministered during one of the most turbulent periods in English history — the Civil War, the execution of Charles I, the Cromwellian Protectorate, and the Restoration. Through all these upheavals, Watson emphasized the cultivation of stable Christian character as the believer's anchor in unstable times. Study how the political chaos of 17th-century England affected the daily lives of ordinary Christians and how Puritan teaching on virtue provided a framework for navigating constant change and uncertainty. How might Watson's teaching on Christian virtues apply to our own age of rapid cultural and political change?

Literature Connection:

The concept of virtue has been central to Western literature from Homer and Aristotle through the medieval period and into the modern era. Compare the classical (Greek and Roman) concept of virtue — emphasizing courage, justice, wisdom, and temperance — with the biblical concept of virtue as expressed in the fruit of the Spirit. What virtues appear in both lists? What virtues are unique to the biblical list? How does the source of virtue differ between classical philosophy (human reason and discipline) and Christian theology (the Holy Spirit)? Write a comparative essay exploring these questions.

Science Connection:

The study of positive psychology — the scientific investigation of human flourishing and well-being — has identified many of the same character strengths that the Bible describes as the fruit of the Spirit. Researchers have found that qualities like gratitude, kindness, self-control, and hope are strongly correlated with psychological well-being, physical health, and positive relationships. Study the findings of positive psychology and compare them with the biblical teaching on Christian virtue. Where do they agree? Where do they diverge? What does the biblical perspective add that secular psychology cannot provide?

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.

Memory Verse:

"But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, faith, Meekness, temperance: against such there is no law." -- Galatians 5:22-23

Read this verse aloud five times. Then close your eyes and try to recite it from memory. Write it out by hand on a card or in your journal. The Puritans memorized vast portions of Scripture, and it was one of the great sources of their spiritual strength. Even one verse, truly hidden in the heart, can sustain you in times of trial.

Additional Memory Verse:

"And beside this, giving all diligence, add to your faith virtue; and to virtue knowledge; And to knowledge temperance; and to temperance patience; and to patience godliness; And to godliness brotherly kindness; and to brotherly kindness charity." -- 2 Peter 1:5-7

This passage describes the Christian life as a chain of virtues, each building upon the previous one. Faith is the foundation — without it, nothing else is possible. But faith must not remain alone. It must grow into virtue (moral excellence), which must be informed by knowledge (understanding of God's truth), which must be governed by temperance (self-control), which must endure through patience, which must be directed by godliness (reverence for God), which must express itself in brotherly kindness (love for fellow believers), and which must be crowned by charity (self-sacrificing love for all people). Peter's chain is not a ladder where you leave each rung behind but a growing cluster where each virtue enriches and strengthens all the others. As you memorize this passage, let it serve as a roadmap for your spiritual growth — a clear picture of the direction in which the Spirit is taking you and the character He is building in you.

Supplemental Reading Assignment:

For students who wish to go deeper, read Thomas Watson's exposition of the Beatitudes from his work "The Beatitudes: An Exposition of Matthew 5:1-12." Watson provides a detailed analysis of each beatitude, showing how the character qualities Jesus describes — poverty of spirit, mourning over sin, meekness, hunger for righteousness, mercy, purity of heart, peacemaking, and willingness to suffer persecution — correspond to the fruit of the Spirit and constitute the marks of authentic Christian character. Watson is particularly insightful on the first beatitude, "Blessed are the poor in spirit," which he defines not as material poverty but as spiritual humility — the deep, honest recognition of our own sinfulness, weakness, and utter dependence on God's grace. Watson argues that poverty of spirit is the foundation of all other virtues because it is the soil in which grace grows. The proud heart is like packed earth — hard, impenetrable, and barren. But the humble heart is like plowed ground — soft, receptive, and fertile. Every virtue begins with the honest acknowledgment that we are sinners who deserve nothing and have received everything by grace. This humility keeps us dependent on the Spirit, grateful for every grace He produces, and eager for more of His transforming work in our lives.
