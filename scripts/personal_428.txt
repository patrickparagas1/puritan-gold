PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Guided Puritan Reading, Day 28
"Owen's Mortification: A Life Summary"
John Owen — Mortification of Sin, Review and Application


Welcome. Tonight we read together. Not quickly, not casually, but with the kind of attentive, worshipful reading that the Puritans practiced every day. Open your heart as we open this text.

Tonight we draw from the work of John Owen, specifically from "The Mortification of Sin." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Philippians 3:13-14:

"Brethren, I count not myself to have apprehended: but this one thing I do, forgetting those things which are behind, and reaching forth unto those things which are before, I press toward the mark for the prize of the high calling of God in Christ Jesus."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


We have now walked through the major arguments of John Owen's 'The Mortification of Sin in Believers.' Today, we draw together the threads and consider how to carry Owen's teaching forward into the rest of our lives.

Let us review the essential structure of Owen's argument. Owen begins with the universal obligation: every believer is called to the constant mortification of sin (Chapter 1, Romans 8:13). This duty is not optional, not seasonal, and not reserved for the spiritually elite. It is the lifelong calling of every person who belongs to Christ.

Owen then establishes the necessity of this duty (Chapter 2). Unmortified sin produces devastating consequences: loss of peace, loss of strength, loss of usefulness, and the risk of scandalous falls. The famous dictum captures the urgency: 'Be killing sin or it will be killing you.'

The agent of mortification is the Holy Spirit (Chapters 3-4). All human efforts at fighting sin that do not depend upon the Spirit are doomed to produce only counterfeit results. Moral resolution, external discipline, and religious busyness may suppress sin temporarily, but only the Spirit can truly mortify it. The Spirit works by revealing sin, creating new desires, empowering the will, and applying the cross of Christ.

Owen then defines what mortification truly is, distinguishing it from its counterfeits (Chapters 5-6). True mortification involves the habitual weakening of sin, constant fighting against it, and frequent success. It is not the eradication of sin (which is impossible in this life), nor the concealment or transfer of sin (which is self-deception).

The practical heart of the treatise consists of Owen's specific directions for killing sin (Chapters 7-14). These include: knowing your besetting sin and its conditions; developing a deep sense of sin's guilt and evil; cutting off provision for the flesh; resisting sin's first motions; meditating on God's attributes; fixing faith on the crucified Christ; filling the mind with gospel promises; tracing sin to its logical end; watching against occasions of sin; responding immediately to conviction; maintaining comprehensive vigilance; and cultivating the graces of the Spirit.

What emerges from Owen's treatise is not a mechanical system but a spiritual posture. The man who takes Owen seriously will be a man of constant prayer, saturated in Scripture, dependent on the Spirit, and looking always to Christ. He will be a man who knows himself honestly — his weaknesses, his patterns, his vulnerabilities — and who has learned to fight his sin at its root rather than merely pruning its branches.

Let me offer several suggestions for carrying Owen's teaching forward.

First, reread the treatise. Owen rewards multiple readings. Each time you return to this work, you will find new insights that speak to your current struggles. Consider reading it once a year as a spiritual discipline.

Second, apply Owen's framework to your specific situation. Take your besetting sin and work through Owen's directions systematically: identify it, understand its conditions, develop a sense of its evil, cut off its provisions, resist its first motions, and so on. Make it a structured project of sanctification.

Third, share Owen's teaching with others. The church desperately needs this kind of practical theology. If you have been helped by Owen, introduce him to your small group, your Bible study, or your accountability partner. The truths that have blessed you can bless others.

Fourth, and most importantly, remember that Owen's directions are means, not ends. The end is Christ. Every direction points beyond itself to the sufficiency of the Savior. The man who fights sin most effectively is not the man with the best technique but the man with the deepest love for Christ. As Owen himself writes: 'Set your affections upon Christ, and you will find that the world and the flesh grow strangely dim.'

Paul's words in Philippians 3 are the fitting conclusion: 'This one thing I do... I press toward the mark for the prize of the high calling of God in Christ Jesus.' The race is not finished. The battle continues. But the goal is clear, the power is sufficient, and the prize is glorious. Press on.


Now let us turn the lens inward. What is God saying to you through tonight's study?

Question 1: Which of Owen's directions has been most challenging and most helpful to me during this month's study?

Question 2: How will I carry forward the discipline of mortification beyond this reading series — what specific practices will I maintain?

Question 3: Am I pressing toward the mark with the focused determination that Paul describes, or have I become distracted and complacent?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.
