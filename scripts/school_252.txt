PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 12
"No Other Gods: The First Commandment"
Christian Ethics & Moral Philosophy — Lesson 2

---

Welcome back, students. Today we examine the first commandment — the foundation of all morality, the starting point of all true religion, and the commandment upon which every other commandment depends. If we get this one right, everything else falls into its proper place. If we get this one wrong, nothing else can be right. Let us approach this study with the seriousness it demands and the reverence it deserves.

--- Lesson ---

Part One: The Commandment and Its Context

The first commandment declares: "Thou shalt have no other gods before me" (Exodus 20:3).

These nine words in the English Bible — even fewer in the original Hebrew — constitute the most important moral statement ever uttered. They establish the fundamental principle upon which all ethics, all worship, all human flourishing, and all right relationship with the Creator depend. Before God says anything about how we should treat one another — before He addresses murder, theft, adultery, or lying — He addresses the most basic question of all: Whom will you worship? To whom will you give your ultimate allegiance? Who holds the supreme place in your heart?

But before we examine the commandment itself, notice the words that immediately precede it: "I am the Lord thy God, which have brought thee out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage" (Exodus 20:2). This preamble is not incidental. It is essential. God does not introduce Himself as a stranger issuing arbitrary demands. He introduces Himself as the covenant Lord who has already acted on behalf of His people. He is the God who heard their groaning in Egypt. He is the God who sent Moses to deliver them. He is the God who brought ten plagues upon Pharaoh's kingdom, who parted the Red Sea, who drowned the pursuing armies, who fed His people with manna and quail, and who led them through the wilderness by a pillar of cloud by day and a pillar of fire by night.

The commandments, therefore, are not the demands of a tyrant. They are the instructions of a Deliverer. They are given in the context of grace, not law. They are given to a people already redeemed, not as conditions for redemption. This pattern — grace first, then obedience — runs throughout the entire Bible, and the Puritans understood it with crystal clarity.

John Calvin, in his commentary on Exodus, written during his years of ministry in Geneva from 1541 until his death in 1564, emphasized this preamble with great force. Calvin taught that God's self-identification as the Redeemer of Israel establishes the foundation for every command that follows. We obey not in order to be delivered but because we have already been delivered. We obey not to earn God's love but because God has already loved us with an everlasting love. The preamble to the Ten Commandments is, in essence, the gospel in miniature: God acts first, in sovereign grace, and then calls His redeemed people to respond in grateful obedience.

Part Two: What the First Commandment Requires

The Westminster Larger Catechism, Question 104, identifies the duties required by the first commandment with extraordinary comprehensiveness. Let us walk through them carefully, for they reveal the immense scope of this seemingly simple command.

First, the first commandment requires that we know the true God. Not merely know about Him — even the devils know facts about God — but know Him personally, intimately, savingly. "And this is life eternal, that they might know thee the only true God, and Jesus Christ, whom thou hast sent" (John 17:3). This knowledge is not merely intellectual but experiential. It involves the mind, the heart, the will, and the affections. To know God is to encounter the living, personal, infinite Creator of the universe and to be transformed by that encounter.

The Puritans were passionate about the knowledge of God. John Owen, the great theologian who served as Dean of Christ Church, Oxford, from 1651 to 1660, and whose theological works fill twenty-three volumes in the standard edition, wrote extensively about the knowledge of God. In his treatise "Communion with God," published in 1657, Owen argued that the Christian life is fundamentally a life of communion — a personal, ongoing, ever-deepening relationship with each Person of the Trinity. We commune with the Father in His love, with the Son in His grace, and with the Holy Spirit in His comfort and power. This communion is not reserved for monks, mystics, or theological scholars. It is the birthright of every believer, and the first commandment demands that we pursue it with all our might.

Second, the first commandment requires that we acknowledge God as the only true God — that we confess with heart and mouth that "the Lord our God is one Lord" (Deuteronomy 6:4), that there is no other God beside Him, that all other claimants to deity are false and empty. The prophet Isaiah declared this truth with magnificent clarity: "I am the Lord, and there is none else, there is no God beside me: I girded thee, though thou hast not known me: that they may know from the rising of the sun, and from the west, that there is none beside me. I am the Lord, and there is none else" (Isaiah 45:5-6).

Third, the first commandment requires that we worship God alone. "Thou shalt worship the Lord thy God, and him only shalt thou serve" (Matthew 4:10). Worship is the supreme activity of the human soul — the activity for which we were created. It involves the ascription of infinite worth to the One who alone possesses infinite worth. It includes praise, prayer, thanksgiving, adoration, confession, and the yielding of the entire self — body, mind, heart, and will — to the sovereign authority of the living God.

Fourth, the first commandment requires that we love God above all things. This is the duty that Jesus identified as the greatest of all commandments: "Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind" (Matthew 22:37). Thomas Watson noted that the word "all" appears three times in this summary — all thy heart, all thy soul, all thy mind — because God demands the totality of our love. He will not accept a divided heart. He will not be content with a portion of our affection. He claims all, because He alone is worthy of all.

Fifth, the first commandment requires that we trust God completely. "Trust in the Lord with all thine heart; and lean not unto thine own understanding. In all thy ways acknowledge him, and he shall direct thy paths" (Proverbs 3:5-6). Trust means relying on God's wisdom when our own understanding fails, resting on God's promises when circumstances are dark, and committing our lives, our families, our futures, and our eternal destinies to the faithfulness of the God who has never broken a single promise.

Sixth, the first commandment requires that we submit to God's authority in every area of life. He is not one priority among many — He is the priority from which all other priorities derive their meaning and their order. He is not the God of Sunday morning only — He is the God of Monday through Saturday as well. He is not the God of the church building only — He is the God of the marketplace, the classroom, the kitchen, the playing field, and every other square inch of creation. As the great Dutch Reformed theologian Abraham Kuyper declared in the nineteenth century, there is not one square inch of the entire domain of human existence over which Christ, who is sovereign over all, does not cry: "Mine!"

Part Three: The Sins Forbidden by the First Commandment

Thomas Watson, the great Puritan expositor who ministered in London through persecution and upheaval, wrote that the first commandment is violated in more ways than most people realize. The most obvious violation is the worship of false gods — the ancient practice of bowing before idols of wood, stone, or metal. The history of Israel is, in many ways, the history of this sin. Despite all that God had done for them — delivering them from Egypt, parting the sea, providing food in the wilderness — the Israelites repeatedly turned to the gods of the surrounding nations.

The golden calf incident at the very foot of Mount Sinai, recorded in Exodus chapter 32, stands as one of the most shocking episodes in the entire Bible. While Moses was on the mountain receiving the law from God's own hand, the people below were fashioning an idol from their golden earrings and declaring: "These be thy gods, O Israel, which brought thee up out of the land of Egypt" (Exodus 32:4). The speed and ease with which they fell into idolatry — mere weeks after witnessing the greatest display of divine power in human history — reveals the depth of the human heart's addiction to false worship.

Throughout the period of the Judges (roughly 1380-1050 B.C.), the people of Israel repeatedly fell into the worship of Baal, Ashtoreth, Dagon, Molech, and other Canaanite deities. During the divided monarchy (930-586 B.C.), King Ahab and his Phoenician wife Jezebel promoted the worship of Baal in the northern kingdom, provoking the dramatic confrontation with the prophet Elijah on Mount Carmel, recorded in 1 Kings chapter 18. King Manasseh of Judah, who reigned from approximately 697 to 642 B.C., not only worshiped false gods but placed an idol in the temple of the Lord itself — an abomination that was not remedied until the reforms of his grandson Josiah in 621 B.C.

But the Puritans recognized that idolatry takes many forms beyond the worship of physical images. And this is where the first commandment speaks most powerfully to our own age. Anything that occupies the place in our hearts that belongs to God alone is an idol. The Westminster Larger Catechism identifies atheism (the denial of God's existence), ignorance (the willful refusal to know God), heresy (the adoption of false beliefs about God), and self-worship (the elevation of self to the place of God) as violations of the first commandment.

The Puritans would add what they called practical atheism — living as though God does not exist, even while professing belief in Him. This is perhaps the most common and dangerous form of first-commandment violation in the modern world. It is the sin of the person who affirms the existence of God on Sunday but makes every decision from Monday to Saturday without any reference to God's will, God's Word, or God's glory. It is the sin of a nominal Christianity that names the name of Christ but is functionally indistinguishable from the unbelieving world.

Watson, in his exposition of the first commandment, identified several specific forms of modern idolatry that are as relevant today as they were in the seventeenth century. He wrote about the idolatry of money — the sin of trusting in riches rather than in God. "They that will be rich fall into temptation and a snare, and into many foolish and hurtful lusts, which drown men in destruction and perdition. For the love of money is the root of all evil" (1 Timothy 6:9-10). When wealth becomes the primary object of our pursuit, the primary source of our security, and the primary measure of our worth, we have made money our god.

Watson wrote about the idolatry of pleasure — the sin of living for sensual gratification rather than for the glory of God. Paul described those who "are the enemies of the cross of Christ: whose end is destruction, whose God is their belly, and whose glory is in their shame, who mind earthly things" (Philippians 3:18-19). When pleasure — whether physical, emotional, or intellectual — becomes the governing purpose of life, displacing God from the center, we have made pleasure our god.

Watson wrote about the idolatry of reputation — the sin of caring more about what people think of us than about what God thinks of us. Jesus warned against this with devastating force: "How can ye believe, which receive honour one of another, and seek not the honour that cometh from God only?" (John 5:44). The desire for human approval, unchecked and undisciplined, becomes a tyrannical master that controls our decisions, shapes our behavior, and silences our conscience.

And Watson wrote about the idolatry of self — which he considered the root of all other idolatries. Self-love, self-trust, self-will, self-promotion, and self-glorification are the fundamental expressions of the fallen human heart. The essence of sin, as Augustine taught and the Puritans affirmed, is the soul curved in upon itself — incurvatus in se — turned away from God and toward the self as the ultimate point of reference. The first commandment strikes at this root with absolute directness: "Thou shalt have no other gods before me" — not even thyself.

Part Four: The Puritan Understanding of the Heart's Idolatry

John Calvin, writing in his "Institutes of the Christian Religion," Book One, Chapter 11, made an observation that has echoed through the centuries: "The human heart is a perpetual factory of idols." Calvin meant that the fallen human heart is always at work manufacturing substitutes for the true God — images, ideas, desires, and attachments that take the place that God alone should occupy. We do not have to be taught to worship idols. We do it naturally, instinctively, ceaselessly. The question is never whether we will worship but what we will worship.

This insight was developed with great pastoral sensitivity by the Puritans. David Clarkson, a Puritan minister who served alongside John Owen at a congregation in London and who preached Owen's funeral sermon in 1683, delivered a remarkable sermon titled "Soul Idolatry Excludes Men Out of Heaven." In this sermon, Clarkson argued that the most dangerous idols are not the ones that stand in pagan temples but the ones that reside in the secret chambers of the heart. He wrote that a man may destroy every physical idol in the world and still be an idolater if his heart is given to anything other than God.

Clarkson identified several marks of soul idolatry that are penetrating in their application. We make an idol of anything we think about most frequently — when our minds return again and again to some earthly object of desire rather than to God. We make an idol of anything we love most deeply — when our affections are more stirred by a creature than by the Creator. We make an idol of anything we trust most fully — when we rely on our own resources, our own abilities, our own plans rather than on the promises and providence of God. We make an idol of anything we fear most greatly — when the threat of losing some earthly good fills us with more dread than the thought of displeasing God.

These diagnostic questions are of immense practical value for the Christian life. The Puritans did not merely tell people to stop worshiping idols — they taught people how to identify the idols that had already taken root in their hearts. And this is the first step toward obedience to the first commandment: honest, searching, Spirit-assisted self-examination that reveals what actually occupies the throne of the heart.

Part Five: Christ the Fulfillment of the First Commandment

As with every commandment, the first commandment finds its ultimate fulfillment in the person and work of Jesus Christ. Where Adam failed — choosing the creature over the Creator in the garden of Eden — Christ succeeded. Where Israel failed — turning to false gods in the wilderness, during the Judges, and throughout the monarchy — Christ remained perfectly faithful to the Father.

The temptation of Christ in the wilderness, recorded in Matthew chapter 4 and Luke chapter 4, is the supreme demonstration of first-commandment obedience. When Satan offered Jesus all the kingdoms of the world and their glory in exchange for a single act of worship — "All these things will I give thee, if thou wilt fall down and worship me" (Matthew 4:9) — Jesus responded with the words of the first commandment itself: "Get thee hence, Satan: for it is written, Thou shalt worship the Lord thy God, and him only shalt thou serve" (Matthew 4:10).

Christ's entire life was a perfect expression of first-commandment obedience. He loved the Father with all His heart, all His soul, and all His mind. He trusted the Father completely, even when that trust led Him to the cross. He submitted to the Father's authority in every detail of His earthly ministry. "I do always those things that please him," Jesus said (John 8:29). And in the garden of Gethsemane, facing the unspeakable horror of bearing the sins of the world, He prayed: "Not my will, but thine, be done" (Luke 22:42).

For those of us who have believed in Christ, His perfect obedience is credited to our account by faith. This is the glorious doctrine of imputed righteousness that was so precious to the Puritans and to the entire Reformed tradition. We are justified — declared righteous before God — not on the basis of our own obedience to the first commandment, which has been woefully imperfect, but on the basis of Christ's perfect obedience, which is received by faith alone. And having been justified by Christ's righteousness, we are then empowered by the Holy Spirit to pursue our own growing obedience — not perfectly in this life, but genuinely and progressively.

Part Six: The Jealousy of God — A Holy Passion

One of the most striking aspects of the first commandment is the attribute of God it reveals: His jealousy. In Exodus chapter 34, verse 14, God declares: "For thou shalt worship no other god: for the Lord, whose name is Jealous, is a jealous God." And in the second commandment, which is closely linked to the first, God says: "I the Lord thy God am a jealous God" (Exodus 20:5).

The jealousy of God is not the petty, insecure jealousy of a suspicious spouse. It is the holy, righteous, and passionate commitment of a faithful God to the exclusive devotion of His people. When a husband is jealous for his wife's faithfulness, we do not consider that jealousy a vice — we consider it the natural and proper expression of love within the marriage covenant. In the same way, God's jealousy is the natural and proper expression of His love within the covenant He has made with His people.

The prophet Hosea, who ministered in the northern kingdom of Israel around 750 B.C., was given a dramatic and painful illustration of God's jealousy. God commanded Hosea to marry a woman named Gomer who would be unfaithful to him — as an enacted parable of Israel's unfaithfulness to God. When Gomer abandoned Hosea for other lovers, Hosea's grief mirrored the grief of God over His unfaithful people. And when Hosea pursued Gomer, bought her back from slavery, and restored her — that act mirrored the relentless, pursuing love of God for His idolatrous people.

The Westminster Larger Catechism, in Question 105, identifies the reasons annexed to the first commandment, including the statement that God is "a jealous God, visiting the iniquity of the fathers upon the children unto the third and fourth generation of them that hate me; and shewing mercy unto thousands of them that love me, and keep my commandments." This statement reveals both the severity and the mercy of God in relation to the first commandment. Those who persist in idolatry — who "hate" God by giving His worship to others — bring consequences not only upon themselves but upon their descendants. But those who love God and keep His commandments receive mercy that extends to thousands of generations. The disproportion is deliberate and glorious: judgment extends to three or four generations, but mercy extends to thousands. God's mercy always exceeds His judgment.

Ezekiel Hopkins, an Irish bishop who wrote during the Restoration period and whose work "An Exposition of the Ten Commandments," published in 1692, drew out the implications of divine jealousy with great clarity. Hopkins wrote that God's jealousy should produce in us both fear and comfort — fear, because it means that God will not tolerate a rival in His people's hearts; and comfort, because it means that God cares passionately about our devotion. A God who did not care whether we worshiped Him or not would be a God who did not love us. The very jealousy of God is a proof of His love, and the first commandment, with all its seriousness, is ultimately an expression of that love.

Part Seven: Practical Application — Keeping the First Commandment Today

How, then, shall we keep the first commandment in the practical circumstances of daily life? The Puritans were eminently practical theologians, and they offered extensive guidance on this question.

First, we must cultivate the knowledge of God through diligent study of His Word. The Bible is God's self-revelation — the means by which He makes Himself known to us. A Christian who neglects the Bible is a Christian who is drifting toward idolatry, because a heart that does not know the true God will inevitably fill the void with false substitutes. Richard Baxter, in his "Christian Directory" published in 1673 — a massive work of practical theology that runs to over a million words — urged Christians to set aside time every day for the reading, study, and meditation of Scripture. He wrote that the Word of God is the food of the soul, and a soul that is not fed will starve — or, worse, will feed on the poisonous substitutes that the world offers.

Second, we must practice the discipline of daily prayer. Prayer is the expression of our dependence upon God — the acknowledgment that He is God and we are not, that we need Him for everything, and that apart from Him we can do nothing. "Without me ye can do nothing," Jesus said in John chapter 15, verse 5. The first commandment calls us to turn our hearts toward God continually — in the morning, at noon, in the evening, and throughout the day. The Puritans were men and women of extraordinary prayer. Many of them rose before dawn to spend hours in prayer before beginning the work of the day. They understood that prayer is not a religious add-on to an otherwise secular life — it is the beating heart of a life centered on God.

Third, we must practice regular self-examination to identify and mortify the idols that take root in our hearts. John Owen, in his classic treatise "The Mortification of Sin in Believers," published in 1656, taught that the Christian life requires a constant, Spirit-empowered battle against the remaining sin in our hearts — including the sin of idolatry. Owen used the language of Colossians chapter 3, verse 5: "Mortify therefore your members which are upon the earth; fornication, uncleanness, inordinate affection, evil concupiscence, and covetousness, which is idolatry." Notice that Paul explicitly identifies covetousness — the disordered desire for earthly things — as idolatry. The first commandment requires not only that we refrain from bowing before false gods but that we actively put to death the disordered desires that draw our hearts away from the true God.

Fourth, we must order our lives so that God genuinely holds the supreme place — not just in theory but in practice. This means ordering our time so that worship and devotion are not crowded out by lesser things. It means ordering our finances so that God receives the firstfruits rather than the leftovers. It means ordering our relationships so that no human attachment competes with our allegiance to Christ. It means ordering our ambitions so that the glory of God, not the glory of self, is the governing purpose of everything we do.

The Puritans had a phrase for this comprehensive reordering of life around the supremacy of God. They called it "living coram Deo" — living before the face of God. It means conducting every aspect of life — work, study, recreation, conversation, eating, sleeping, buying, selling — in the conscious awareness of God's presence, under the authority of God's Word, and for the advancement of God's glory. This is what the first commandment requires. This is the life to which it calls us. And this is the life that, by the grace of God and the power of the Spirit, we can actually live.

Part Seven: The Sufficiency of God — Why Idolatry Is Always Foolish

One of the most powerful arguments the Puritans made against idolatry was the argument from God's all-sufficiency. If God is who He says He is — infinite in power, wisdom, goodness, mercy, and love — then turning to any other source for what He alone can provide is not merely sinful. It is profoundly foolish. It is like a man dying of thirst who turns away from a clear, pure, inexhaustible spring in order to drink from a muddy puddle.

The prophet Jeremiah expressed this with devastating clarity: "For my people have committed two evils; they have forsaken me the fountain of living waters, and hewed them out cisterns, broken cisterns, that can hold no water" (Jeremiah 2:13). God is the fountain of living waters — the source of everything that sustains, refreshes, and satisfies the human soul. Idols are broken cisterns — cracked containers that promise satisfaction but deliver only emptiness.

Stephen Charnock, the Puritan theologian whose masterwork "The Existence and Attributes of God" was published posthumously in 1682, developed this theme with extraordinary depth. Charnock was born in 1628 and educated at Emmanuel College, Cambridge — one of the great centers of Puritan learning. He served as a chaplain during the Cromwellian period and later pastored a Nonconformist congregation in London alongside Thomas Watson. His treatise on the divine attributes, running to over a thousand pages in most editions, is widely regarded as the most comprehensive and profound work on the nature of God in the English language.

Charnock argued that God possesses infinite self-sufficiency — He lacks nothing, needs nothing, and depends on nothing outside Himself for His existence, His happiness, or His glory. And because God is infinitely self-sufficient, He is infinitely able to satisfy the needs of His creatures. There is no void in the human heart that God cannot fill. There is no longing that He cannot satisfy. There is no need that He cannot meet. The tragedy of idolatry is that it turns from the One who has everything to offer and runs toward things that have nothing to give.

Watson made the same point with his characteristic vividness. He compared the idolater to a man who forsakes a palace to live in a pigsty, who trades a crown of gold for a wreath of thorns, who gives up a feast to gnaw on bones. The objects of our idolatry — money, pleasure, reputation, power — are not evil in themselves. They are good gifts of God when received with gratitude and used in submission to His will. But when they are elevated to the place of God Himself, they become cruel masters. Money, when worshiped, produces anxiety. Pleasure, when worshiped, produces emptiness. Reputation, when worshiped, produces slavery to the opinions of others. Power, when worshiped, produces tyranny and loneliness. Only God, worshiped as God, produces the peace, joy, freedom, and fullness that the human heart was created to experience.

The great Augustine of Hippo, whose "Confessions" written around 397 A.D. remain one of the most influential spiritual autobiographies ever composed, captured this truth in his famous prayer: "Thou hast made us for thyself, O Lord, and our hearts are restless until they rest in thee." Augustine knew this from personal experience. Before his conversion to Christ in 386 A.D. at the age of thirty-one, Augustine had pursued pleasure, philosophy, ambition, and false religion — the Manichaean heresy — in a desperate search for satisfaction. He found that every earthly pursuit left him emptier than before. Only when he surrendered to the God of the first commandment — the true God, the only God, the all-sufficient God — did his restless heart find rest.

The Puritans loved Augustine precisely because his experience confirmed the truth of the first commandment. They saw in his story the universal human pattern: we are created for God, we rebel against God, we pursue substitutes for God, we discover that the substitutes are empty, and we are driven — by grace — back to the God who alone can satisfy. The first commandment is not a restriction on our happiness. It is the road to our happiness. It points us to the only source of lasting joy and says: "Here. Drink from this fountain. It will never run dry."

Part Eight: The Fear of God — The Beginning of First-Commandment Obedience

The Puritans taught that obedience to the first commandment begins with what Scripture calls "the fear of the Lord." This phrase appears over one hundred times in the Bible, and it is one of the most misunderstood concepts in the Christian life. The fear of the Lord is not the terror of a slave before a cruel master. It is the reverent awe of a child before a holy, powerful, and loving Father. It is the response of a creature who has caught a glimpse of the infinite majesty of the Creator and who trembles — not with dread, but with wonder.

"The fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom: and the knowledge of the holy is understanding" (Proverbs 9:10). "The fear of the Lord is the beginning of knowledge: but fools despise wisdom and instruction" (Proverbs 1:7). "The fear of the Lord is a fountain of life, to depart from the snares of death" (Proverbs 14:27). The fear of the Lord is not opposed to the love of God — it is its companion. The person who truly loves God also stands in awe of Him. The person who truly knows God is simultaneously drawn by His grace and humbled by His glory.

John Bunyan, the Puritan author of "The Pilgrim's Progress" — written during his twelve years of imprisonment in Bedford jail from 1660 to 1672 and published in 1678 — explored the nature of godly fear in his lesser-known treatise "A Treatise of the Fear of God," published in 1679. Bunyan distinguished between two kinds of fear: slavish fear, which is the guilty terror of the unconverted sinner who knows he deserves judgment, and filial fear, which is the reverent love of the believer who knows he has been forgiven. Slavish fear drives people away from God. Filial fear draws people toward God. The first commandment, when it says "have no other gods before me," is calling us to filial fear — to the kind of reverent love that makes us want to worship God and God alone, not because we are afraid of what He will do if we do not, but because we have seen who He is and we cannot imagine giving our hearts to anyone else.

The Puritans cultivated this fear of God through what they called "the practice of the presence of God" — the conscious, deliberate awareness of God's nearness in every moment of every day. They rose in the morning with God on their minds. They went about their work with God before their eyes. They spoke with the awareness that God heard every word. They made decisions with the confidence that God's wisdom was available and His will was discoverable. They went to bed at night with prayers on their lips and gratitude in their hearts.

This is what the first commandment looks like in practice. It is not merely a theological proposition to be believed. It is a way of life to be lived — a life oriented entirely around the supreme reality of the living God, a life that acknowledges Him in every circumstance, trusts Him in every trial, obeys Him in every decision, and worships Him with every breath.

Part Nine: Cross-References to Church History and the Other Commandments

The first commandment has been the battleground of the church throughout its entire history. In the earliest centuries, Christians in the Roman Empire faced the demand to worship the emperor as a god — a practice known as the imperial cult. The Roman authorities did not necessarily require Christians to abandon their own God; they merely required them to add Caesar to their pantheon, to burn a pinch of incense before the emperor's image as a sign of political loyalty. To the pagan mind, this seemed a reasonable and harmless request. But to the Christian conscience, informed by the first commandment, it was impossible. "Thou shalt have no other gods before me" admits no exceptions, tolerates no compromises, and allows no additions.

The result was wave after wave of persecution. Under Emperor Nero in 64 A.D., Christians were burned alive as human torches to light the emperor's gardens. Under Emperor Domitian in the 90s A.D., the apostle John was exiled to the island of Patmos, where he received the visions recorded in the book of Revelation. Under Emperor Decius in 250 A.D., a systematic empire-wide persecution demanded that all citizens sacrifice to the gods or face death. Under Emperor Diocletian, beginning in 303 A.D., the Great Persecution destroyed church buildings, burned Scriptures, and executed thousands of believers.

Through all of this, the faithful held firm. Polycarp, the bishop of Smyrna and a disciple of the apostle John, was martyred around 155 A.D. at the age of eighty-six. When the Roman proconsul urged him to save his life by swearing an oath to Caesar and cursing Christ, Polycarp replied with words that have echoed through the centuries: "Eighty and six years have I served Him, and He hath never wronged me; how then can I blaspheme my King who saved me?" These martyrs understood the first commandment. They understood that no earthly authority, no threat of death, no promise of comfort could justify the surrender of the exclusive worship that belongs to God alone.

In the medieval period, the church faced the subtler danger of elevating the saints, the Virgin Mary, and the visible institution of the church itself to a position that competed with the worship of God alone. The practice of praying to saints as intercessors, the veneration of relics as channels of divine power, and the attribution of quasi-divine authority to the pope all represented, in the judgment of the Reformers, violations of the first commandment. The Reformers of the sixteenth century — Luther in Germany, Calvin in Geneva, Zwingli in Zurich, Knox in Scotland — all recognized these practices as encroachments upon the exclusive worship due to God and called the church back to the pure worship of God alone, through Christ alone, by grace alone, through faith alone.

The Puritans continued this reforming work in England. They saw the first commandment not merely as a personal devotional principle but as a standard by which the entire life of the church and the nation must be measured. Every practice, every institution, every tradition must be tested against this foundational command: Does this promote or hinder the exclusive worship of the true and living God?

The first commandment also provides the foundation for every other commandment. If God is not God — if He is not the supreme, sovereign, all-sufficient being who alone is worthy of worship — then there is no ground for any moral obligation whatsoever. Why should I not murder, if there is no God who values human life? Why should I not steal, if there is no God who established the right of property? Why should I not lie, if there is no God who is truth? Every commandment depends on the first. Morality begins with theology. Ethics begins with God.

As we continue through the remaining commandments in the weeks ahead, we will see how each one flows from and depends upon this first, foundational command: "Thou shalt have no other gods before me." The God who claims our exclusive worship also claims our reverent speech, our holy time, our respectful relationships, our care for human life, our marital fidelity, our honest dealings, and our disciplined desires. The first commandment is not merely one rule among many — it is the root from which the entire moral law grows. As Watson wrote, all the commandments are like spokes in a wheel, and the first commandment is the hub. Remove the hub, and the entire wheel collapses. Secure the hub, and the whole structure holds firm. This is why the Puritans gave such passionate attention to this commandment above all others. They knew that a heart rightly ordered toward God would, by the grace of the Spirit, produce a life rightly ordered in every other respect. And they knew that a heart disordered in its worship would produce a life disordered in every dimension — in speech, in relationships, in the use of time and resources, in the governance of desire. The first commandment is the foundation. Let us build well upon it.

--- Review Questions ---

Review Question 1: What is the significance of the preamble to the Ten Commandments ("I am the Lord thy God, which have brought thee out of the land of Egypt")? How does it establish the context of grace for all the commandments that follow?

Review Question 3: What did John Calvin mean when he wrote that "the human heart is a perpetual factory of idols"? How does David Clarkson's teaching on "soul idolatry" help us identify the idols in our own hearts?

Review Question 4: How did Jesus perfectly fulfill the first commandment in His life and ministry? Give specific examples from the Gospels.

Review Question 5: What practical steps can a Christian take to ensure that God holds the supreme place in his or her daily life? Draw on the guidance of the Puritans discussed in today's lesson.

--- Activity ---

Conduct a personal "idol inventory." Begin by reading Clarkson's four diagnostic questions from today's lesson: What do you think about most frequently? What do you love most deeply? What do you trust most fully? What do you fear most greatly? Write honest answers to each question. Then compare your answers with the standard of the first commandment. Write a reflective paragraph (250-400 words) about what you discover — where God rightly holds the supreme place, and where lesser things may have crept in to compete with Him. Finally, identify one specific, practical step you can take this week to ensure that God occupies the throne of your heart more fully.

As an additional exercise, read Psalm 115:1-8 and Isaiah 44:9-20, both of which contain vivid descriptions of the folly of idolatry. Write a brief comparison (150-200 words) of how these two passages portray the foolishness of worshiping anything other than the living God. Consider how these ancient warnings apply to the more subtle forms of idolatry we encounter today.

Materials needed: Paper, pen, Bible (Exodus 20:1-6, Psalm 115, Isaiah 44), quiet time for honest self-reflection.

--- Closing ---

The first commandment is not merely a rule — it is an invitation. It invites us to know the only God who is truly worthy of our worship, to love Him with everything we have, and to build our entire lives upon the unshakable foundation of His supremacy. It calls us away from the exhausting treadmill of serving false gods that can never satisfy, and toward the abundant life that is found only in the worship of the true and living God. As the Psalmist declared: "Whom have I in heaven but thee? and there is none upon earth that I desire beside thee. My flesh and my heart faileth: but God is the strength of my heart, and my portion for ever" (Psalm 73:25-26). Until next time, students.
