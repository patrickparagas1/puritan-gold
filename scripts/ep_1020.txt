"The Legacy of Female Piety — What Godly Women Leave Behind for Generations"
John Angell James — Female Piety

It has been a rich journey — through the character of the pious woman, her calling in the home, her work as mother and wife, her influence upon society, her endurance in suffering, and her devotional life. Today, we close by considering the theme that crowns the whole: the legacy of female piety. What does the godly woman leave behind when her earthly course is run? Let us finish well.

--- Teaching ---

John Angell James lived long enough to see the fruit of his own ministry. He watched children he had baptized grow into mature believers. He witnessed families shaped by the gospel producing generation after generation of faithful Christians. And he observed, with a pastor's keen eye, that the most enduring legacies were almost always traced back to the influence of godly women — mothers, grandmothers, aunts, and friends whose piety had stamped itself upon the souls of those around them with a permanence that outlasted their earthly lives.

James writes with deep conviction that the legacy of a godly woman is one of the most precious gifts that can be bestowed upon the world. It is a legacy not of wealth, status, or accomplishment as the world measures such things, but of faith, love, prayer, and the quiet, persevering influence of a life surrendered to Christ. This legacy does not end at the grave. It ripples outward through time, touching lives the godly woman will never meet, in places she will never visit, in ages she will never see.

Consider the chain of influence that James traces. A godly mother teaches her daughter the fear of the Lord. That daughter grows up, marries, and raises her own children in the faith. Those children do the same. Within three or four generations, a single woman's piety has touched dozens, perhaps hundreds, of souls. And each of those souls, in turn, carries the influence forward into their own spheres — their homes, their churches, their communities, and their nations. This is the multiplication of grace, and it is one of the most powerful forces in the history of the world.

The apostle Paul recognized this chain of influence when he wrote to Timothy: "When I call to remembrance the unfeigned faith that is in thee, which dwelt first in thy grandmother Lois, and thy mother Eunice; and I am persuaded that in thee also" (2 Timothy 1:5). Here is a three-generation legacy of faith — grandmother to mother to son — and through Timothy, that legacy extended into the life of the early church and, through his ministry and Paul's letters about him, into the whole history of Christianity. Lois and Eunice could never have imagined the scope of their influence. They were simply faithful. And God multiplied their faithfulness beyond all human calculation.

James also points to the legacy of the godly woman's prayers. He was deeply convinced that the prayers of faithful women continue to bear fruit long after the women themselves have entered into glory. He tells the story of mothers who prayed daily for the conversion of their children, who died without seeing their prayers answered, but whose children were converted years or even decades later — often tracing their conversion directly to the remembered prayers and faith of their mothers. Prayer is seed sown in the soil of eternity, and it often germinates in God's own time, which is not always our time.

Richard Baxter wrote that the Christian should live and die with eternity in view. James applies this counsel to the godly woman with particular force. He urges her to think often about the legacy she is leaving. Not a legacy of possessions, which moths and rust will destroy, but a legacy of faith, which endures forever. Not a legacy of reputation, which the world may forget, but a legacy of godliness, which God will remember. The question James wants every woman to ask herself is not "What will I leave behind in my bank account?" but "What will I leave behind in the souls of those I have loved?"

Thomas Watson wrote that the saints are God's jewels, and that He will bring them all safely to the treasury of heaven. James expands this image. The godly woman is herself one of God's jewels, polished by affliction and fitted for glory. But she has also been used by God to shape other jewels — her children, her friends, her disciples, and all those who have been touched by her faith. When she enters into the presence of her Lord, she will not come alone. She will come accompanied by the fruit of her influence — souls she helped to bring to Christ, lives she helped to shape for eternity, and prayers she offered that God answered in His perfect time.

James also writes about the legacy of a godly woman's example. Long after she is gone, the memory of her life remains as a source of inspiration and instruction. Her patience in suffering, her joy in worship, her faithfulness in duty, her tenderness in love — these things are not forgotten. They live on in the hearts of those who knew her, and they are passed down from generation to generation as part of the family's spiritual inheritance. "The memory of the just is blessed," says the proverb (Proverbs 10:7), and James insists that this is especially true of godly women, whose influence is often most deeply felt in the intimate circles of home and family where the memory of their lives is most tenderly preserved.

James brings his work to a close by turning the reader's gaze from earth to heaven. He reminds the godly woman that this life, with all its joys and sorrows, is not the end of the story. There is a better country, a heavenly city, where every tear will be wiped away, where every trial will be forgotten, and where the faithful will be welcomed with the words: "Well done, thou good and faithful servant: enter thou into the joy of thy Lord" (Matthew 25:21). This is the ultimate legacy of female piety — not merely a life well lived on earth, but an eternity of joy in the presence of God.

John Flavel, in his Fountain of Life, wrote that Christ is preparing a place of surpassing beauty for His people, and that the glory to be revealed will make all the sufferings of this present time seem as nothing. James invites the godly woman to fix her eyes upon this glory, to press toward the mark, and to finish her course with joy, knowing that her labor in the Lord is not in vain.

And so, dear listener, we come to the end of our study. John Angell James has given us a portrait of female piety that is biblical, practical, searching, and beautiful. He has shown us that the godly woman, in her quiet faithfulness, is one of the most powerful forces for good in the world. He has called us to a piety that is rooted in the grace of God, sustained by the means of grace, tested in the furnace of affliction, and crowned with an everlasting inheritance. May the Lord apply these truths to our hearts and make them fruitful in our lives.

--- Application ---

1. Take time this week to write down the legacy you hope to leave behind. What do you want your children, your grandchildren, and your friends to remember about your faith? What spiritual inheritance do you want to pass on? Let this vision shape your priorities and your daily choices.

2. Identify a younger woman in your church or community whom you can mentor. Share with her the truths you have learned from Scripture, from experience, and from this study. The chain of faith must be passed from generation to generation.

3. Pray specifically for the generations that will follow you. Ask God to extend the influence of your piety beyond your own lifetime, reaching people you will never meet and touching lives in ways you cannot foresee.

4. Fix your eyes upon the heavenly country. Read Hebrews 11 in its entirety and let the example of the saints who have gone before inspire you to persevere in faith, to endure with patience, and to finish your course with joy.
