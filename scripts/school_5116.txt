PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 17
"Is Jesus the Only Way?"
Apologetics for Young Christians — Lesson 7

---

Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we address one of the most culturally sensitive and emotionally charged questions in all of apologetics: Is Jesus really the only way to God? In a world that celebrates diversity, tolerance, and the idea that all paths lead to the same destination, the Christian claim of exclusivity sounds arrogant, narrow-minded, and even dangerous to many people. We will examine the biblical teaching, the reasoning behind the Christian claim of exclusivity, the fundamental differences between Christianity and other major world religions, and how to communicate this truth with both conviction and compassion. Let us pray and begin.

Lord Jesus, Thou hast said, "I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man cometh unto the Father, but by me." We come before Thee asking for courage to affirm this truth in a world that finds it offensive, wisdom to understand why this truth is necessary, compassion to share it with gentleness and respect, and humility to remember that Thy exclusivity is not about our superiority but about Thy uniqueness. Help us to see that the narrowness of the way is not a limitation but a gift — because the way is open to all who will come. In Christ's name, Amen.

--- Lesson ---

In a culture that celebrates diversity and tolerance above almost all other values, the Christian claim that Jesus Christ is the only way to God strikes many people as arrogant, narrow-minded, intolerant, and even morally offensive. "How can you say that your religion is the only right one? Aren't all religions basically the same? Isn't it presumptuous to claim that billions of sincere, devout non-Christians are wrong? Doesn't such a claim lead to intolerance, bigotry, and even violence?" These are common objections, and they deserve thoughtful, careful, and honest responses.

But before we address the objections, let us first establish what the Bible actually teaches. This is the proper starting point for any Christian discussion — not what we wish were true, or what the culture finds acceptable, but what God has revealed in His Word.

Jesus made the exclusive claim Himself, in words that are unmistakably clear. In John 14:6, He declared, "I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man cometh unto the Father, but by me." This statement deserves careful examination. Notice several features of it.

First, Jesus used the definite article — "the way," not "a way." He did not say, "I am one of many paths to God." He said, "I am the way" — the one and only way. The definite article indicates uniqueness and exclusivity.

Second, Jesus claimed to be not merely a teacher of truth but truth itself — "I am the truth." This is an extraordinary claim. Buddha did not claim to be the truth. Muhammad did not claim to be the truth. Confucius did not claim to be the truth. They claimed to teach the truth, or to point the way to the truth. Jesus claimed to be the truth — to embody it, to personify it, to be identical with it. If Jesus is the truth, then any claim that contradicts Him is, by definition, false.

Third, Jesus claimed to be "the life" — the source and sustainer of all genuine spiritual life. Apart from Him, there is no spiritual life, no relationship with God, no hope of eternal blessedness. "He that hath the Son hath life; and he that hath not the Son of God hath not life" (1 John 5:12).

Fourth, Jesus stated the exclusion explicitly: "No man cometh unto the Father, but by me." This is not one interpretation among many. It is not a statement that admits of multiple meanings. It is a direct, unambiguous declaration of exclusivity from the lips of Jesus Christ Himself.

The apostles echoed and amplified this claim throughout the New Testament. The apostle Peter, standing before the Jewish Sanhedrin, declared with boldness, "Neither is there salvation in any other: for there is none other name under heaven given among men, whereby we must be saved" (Acts 4:12). Peter did not say, "Jesus is one name among many." He said there is "none other name" — no other name in the entire world, given to any people, in any culture, at any time in history — by which salvation can be obtained.

The apostle Paul wrote to Timothy, "For there is one God, and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus; Who gave himself a ransom for all, to be testified in due time" (1 Timothy 2:5-6). One God. One mediator. Not many mediators, not a plurality of paths, but one mediator — the man Christ Jesus. Paul could not have been clearer.

Jesus Himself said in John 10:9, "I am the door: by me if any man enter in, he shall be saved." He did not say, "I am one of several doors." He said, "I am the door" — the only entrance into the fold of God.

In Acts 17:30-31, Paul declared to the Athenian philosophers, "And the times of this ignorance God winked at; but now commandeth all men every where to repent: Because he hath appointed a day, in the which he will judge the world in righteousness by that man whom he hath ordained; whereof he hath given assurance unto all men, in that he hath raised him from the dead." God commands all people everywhere — not just people in certain cultures, not just people of certain backgrounds — to repent and believe in the risen Christ.

The New Testament is unanimous: salvation is found in Christ alone, through faith alone, by grace alone. This is not a peripheral doctrine that can be negotiated or modified to accommodate cultural sensibilities. It is the heart of the Christian gospel.

But why? Why would God provide only one way of salvation? Is this not arbitrary or unnecessarily restrictive? The answer lies in the nature of the problem that salvation addresses — a problem so grave, so profound, and so universal that only one solution is adequate to address it.

The human predicament is not merely ignorance — as though better education could solve it. It is not merely moral imperfection — as though a better example could fix it. It is not merely a lack of spiritual technique — as though better meditation practices could remedy it. The human predicament is guilt before a holy God — a guilt so profound, so pervasive, and so insurmountable that no human effort, no religious ritual, no amount of moral improvement, and no quantity of good works can remove it.

The Ten Commandments, which we studied earlier in this unit, reveal the depth of this problem. God's standard is not relative goodness — being better than average, better than most people, better than the worst offenders. God's standard is absolute perfection. Jesus Himself stated this standard: "Be ye therefore perfect, even as your Father which is in heaven is perfect" (Matthew 5:48). This is the standard by which every human being will be judged. And no human being has ever met it. "For all have sinned, and come short of the glory of God" (Romans 3:23).

The Puritan theologian Thomas Goodwin, born in 1600 in Rollesby, Norfolk, England, who served as President of Magdalen College, Oxford, and was one of the leading theologians of the Westminster Assembly, wrote with devastating clarity about the depth of human sinfulness and the impossibility of self-salvation. In his work An Unregenerate Man's Guiltiness before God, in Respect of Sin and Punishment, Goodwin demonstrated from Scripture that every human being stands guilty before God — not merely for individual transgressions but for the corruption of human nature itself. Goodwin argued that the sin of Adam has been imputed to all his descendants, and that the corruption of human nature is so thorough, so pervasive, and so deep that no human remedy can address it. We are not sinners because we sin. We sin because we are sinners. The problem is not merely behavioral but constitutional — it is rooted in the very structure of our fallen nature.

Given the gravity of this problem, only a solution that addresses it at its root can be effective. A moral teacher can give us better instructions, but cannot remove the guilt that already lies upon us. A religious system can provide rituals and disciplines, but cannot atone for the sins we have already committed. A political revolution can change external structures, but cannot change the human heart. Only a substitutionary sacrifice — an innocent party bearing the penalty that the guilty party deserves — can satisfy the demands of divine justice and set sinners free.

And that is exactly what Christ accomplished on the cross. On the cross, Jesus — the sinless Son of God, the only human being who ever lived a perfectly righteous life — bore the penalty for human sin in His own body. He died in our place. He suffered the wrath of God that we deserved. He satisfied the demands of divine justice so that guilty sinners could be forgiven, reconciled to God, and adopted into the family of God. "For he hath made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin; that we might be made the righteousness of God in him" (2 Corinthians 5:21).

No other religious figure in history has claimed to do this or has the credentials to do it. Muhammad did not die for sins. The Quran explicitly denies that Jesus died on the cross (Surah 4:157). Buddha did not atone for guilt — Buddhism teaches that suffering is the result of desire, not of guilt before a holy God, and the solution is the extinction of desire, not the forgiveness of sin. Hindu gurus do not offer substitutionary atonement — Hinduism teaches that the soul is trapped in a cycle of death and rebirth governed by karma, and the solution is the realization that the individual soul (atman) is identical with the universal soul (Brahman). No human moral teacher, however wise and however virtuous, has the ability to bear the penalty for another person's sin before a holy God.

Only Christ can bridge the infinite gap between a holy God and sinful humanity — because only Christ is both God and man. As God, He is infinite — His sacrifice has infinite value, sufficient to atone for the sins of all who believe. As man, He is our representative — able to stand in our place and bear our penalty. The uniqueness of Christ's person — the God-man, fully divine and fully human — is the foundation of the uniqueness of His saving work.

The Puritan pastor and theologian John Owen, born in 1616 in Stadham, Oxfordshire, England, wrote what is arguably the greatest work on the atonement in the history of Christian theology: The Death of Death in the Death of Christ, published in 1647. In this monumental work, Owen argued with relentless logical precision that the death of Christ was not merely an example of love, not merely a moral influence designed to move our hearts, but an actual, definite, effectual atonement — a real payment for real sins, offered by a real substitute to a real God. Owen demonstrated that the exclusivity of Christ as the way of salvation follows necessarily from the nature of His atoning work. If Christ's death actually accomplished salvation — if it actually paid the debt, actually satisfied the justice of God, actually secured the forgiveness of sins — then salvation can be found nowhere else, because the problem it addresses can be solved nowhere else.

Owen wrote with characteristic precision that Christ's sacrifice was "a full, perfect, and sufficient oblation and satisfaction for the sins of the whole world." The sufficiency of the cross makes all other proposed remedies not merely inferior but irrelevant — as irrelevant as a candle is to a man who has the sun.

Now, let us address the common claim that all religions are basically the same. This assertion sounds open-minded and tolerant, and it is repeated so often that many people accept it without examination. But is it true? A brief but careful examination reveals that it is not.

The major world religions differ from one another not merely in peripheral matters but in their most fundamental teachings — their understanding of the nature of God, the nature of the human problem, and the nature of the solution.

Consider the nature of God — or ultimate reality. Christianity teaches that the ultimate reality is a personal, triune God — one God in three persons, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — who is infinite, eternal, omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly holy, and perfectly loving. Hinduism teaches that the ultimate reality — Brahman — is impersonal, beyond all attributes, and identical with the individual soul. Buddhism, in its original form, is agnostic about the existence of God and teaches that the question of God's existence is irrelevant to the quest for enlightenment. Islam teaches that God — Allah — is absolutely one (tawhid), that the doctrine of the Trinity is blasphemy (shirk), and that Jesus is merely a prophet, not the Son of God.

These are not minor differences. They are fundamental, irreconcilable contradictions. God cannot be both personal and impersonal. God cannot be both triune and absolutely unitary. Jesus cannot be both the divine Son of God and merely a human prophet. These claims are mutually exclusive. They cannot all be true. They may all be wrong, but they cannot all be right.

Consider the human problem. Christianity teaches that the fundamental human problem is sin — moral rebellion against a holy God, resulting in guilt, spiritual death, and separation from God. Hinduism teaches that the fundamental problem is ignorance — the failure to realize that the individual soul is identical with Brahman. Buddhism teaches that the fundamental problem is desire (tanha) — the craving and attachment that produce suffering (dukkha). Islam teaches that the fundamental problem is disobedience to Allah's commands.

Again, these are not different ways of saying the same thing. They are fundamentally different diagnoses of the human condition. And as any physician knows, the accuracy of the diagnosis determines the effectiveness of the treatment. If the problem is ignorance, then the solution is knowledge. If the problem is desire, then the solution is the extinction of desire. If the problem is guilt before a holy God, then the solution is atonement — a substitute who bears the penalty on behalf of the guilty. These solutions are not interchangeable, because the problems they address are not the same.

Consider the solution. Christianity teaches that salvation comes through grace alone, through faith alone, in Christ alone — that we are saved not by our own works but by the finished work of Christ on the cross. "For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: Not of works, lest any man should boast" (Ephesians 2:8-9). Hinduism teaches that liberation (moksha) comes through knowledge (jnana), devotion (bhakti), or works (karma) — or some combination of these — over the course of many lifetimes of reincarnation. Buddhism teaches that enlightenment (nirvana) comes through the Eightfold Path — right understanding, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration. Islam teaches that salvation comes through submission to Allah's will, observance of the Five Pillars, and the accumulation of good deeds that outweigh bad deeds on the Day of Judgment.

These are radically different solutions because they address radically different problems. Christianity alone teaches salvation by grace — that we are saved not by what we do but by what Christ has done for us. Every other major religion teaches salvation by works — by human effort, human merit, human achievement. This is not a minor difference. It is the difference between a religion of human accomplishment and a gospel of divine grace.

Consider also the question of what happens after death. Christianity teaches that each person lives once, dies once, and then faces judgment: "It is appointed unto men once to die, but after this the judgment" (Hebrews 9:27). There is no second chance, no reincarnation, no cycle of death and rebirth. The soul's eternal destiny — heaven or hell — is determined in this one life, based on whether a person has trusted in Christ or rejected Him. Hinduism, by contrast, teaches reincarnation — the soul passes through countless lifetimes, ascending or descending based on karma accumulated in previous lives. Buddhism teaches a similar cycle of rebirth (samsara), though without the concept of a permanent soul (anatta). Islam teaches a final judgment based on the balance of good and bad deeds, with paradise (Jannah) for the righteous and hellfire (Jahannam) for the wicked.

These are not merely different cultural expressions of the same underlying belief. They are mutually exclusive metaphysical claims about the nature of reality, the destiny of the soul, and the structure of the universe. One life or many lives? Judgment by grace or judgment by works? Eternal personal existence or the extinction of the self? Heaven and hell or an endless cycle of rebirth? These are questions that admit of only one correct answer, and the answers given by the major religions directly contradict one another.

Furthermore, consider the starkly different views of what constitutes ultimate blessedness. The Christian vision of heaven is fundamentally relational — it is communion with God, face to face, forever: "And they shall see his face; and his name shall be in their foreheads" (Revelation 22:4). The Buddhist ideal of nirvana is the cessation of individual existence — the blowing out of the candle of desire and selfhood. The Hindu concept of moksha is the dissolution of individual identity into the impersonal absolute of Brahman — the drop returning to the ocean. These are not different descriptions of the same experience. They are irreconcilable visions of what it means to be ultimately fulfilled. Christianity promises the eternal enhancement of personal identity in communion with a personal God. Buddhism promises the extinction of personal identity. Hinduism promises the absorption of personal identity into an impersonal absolute. One cannot simultaneously exist as an eternal person in communion with God, cease to exist as a person, and merge into an impersonal absolute. These destinations are as different as three different cities on three different continents.

The philosopher Ravi Zacharias, who spent decades engaging with adherents of different world religions, often observed that while the surface similarities between religions may be superficially impressive, the deeper you go, the more the differences emerge — and it is the differences, not the similarities, that are ultimately decisive. The religions of the world do not merely offer different paths to the same summit. They offer different paths to different summits — and some of those summits are mutually exclusive destinations that cannot coexist.

The Puritan theologian Thomas Watson, born in Yorkshire, England, around 1620, who served as rector of St. Stephen's Church in Walbrook, London, wrote about the uniqueness of the Christian gospel in his Body of Divinity. Watson observed that every false religion in the world is a religion of works — a religion that tells man what he must do to earn God's favor. Only the gospel tells man what God has done to save him. Watson wrote, "The gospel offers that freely which the law demands." The law says, "Do this and live." The gospel says, "It is done — believe and live." This is the glory and the uniqueness of the Christian message.

The Puritan pastor Jeremiah Burroughs, born in 1600 in Essex, England, wrote about the preciousness of Christ as the only Savior in his work The Excellency of a Gracious Spirit. Burroughs argued that the exclusivity of Christ should not be a source of offense but of wonder and gratitude. The fact that there is one way to God means that there is a way to God — a sure, certain, reliable way that does not depend on our own fluctuating efforts but on the finished work of the Son of God. Burroughs wrote that the narrowness of the way is not a burden but a blessing, because the narrow way leads to a wide and glorious destination.

Is the claim of Christian exclusivity arrogant? This is perhaps the most common emotional objection to the Christian position, and it deserves a careful response.

First, it would be arrogant if Christians were saying, "We are better than everyone else." But that is emphatically not the Christian claim. The Christian claim is that all of us — Christians included — are sinners in desperate need of a Savior. The gospel is not a message about the superiority of Christians. It is a message about the grace of God and the sufficiency of Christ. Christians are not people who claim to be better than others. Christians are people who confess that they are sinners saved by grace — beggars who have found bread and want to tell other beggars where to find it.

Second, the charge of arrogance can be turned around. Is it not arrogant to claim that all the great religious teachers in history — Jesus, Muhammad, Buddha, and others — were all basically saying the same thing, when they clearly were not? Is it not patronizing to tell billions of devout believers that the specific doctrines of their faith do not really matter — that their carefully articulated theological convictions are merely different cultural expressions of the same underlying reality? The "all religions are the same" position is not humble. It is deeply disrespectful of the specific truth claims of every major religion. It tells Christians that the deity of Christ does not matter, tells Muslims that tawhid does not matter, tells Buddhists that the Four Noble Truths do not matter, and tells Hindus that the nature of Brahman does not matter. It reduces all of these rich, complex, and mutually contradictory worldviews to a bland, homogenized spirituality that none of them actually teaches.

Third, truth claims are not inherently arrogant. If a doctor tells you that a certain medicine is the only effective treatment for your disease, the doctor is not being arrogant — the doctor is being truthful. If a lifeguard tells you that a certain path is the only safe way through a minefield, the lifeguard is not being narrow-minded — the lifeguard is trying to save your life. The question is not whether the claim is exclusive but whether the claim is true. And if Jesus really is who He claimed to be — the eternal Son of God, the only Savior of the world — then proclaiming His exclusivity is not arrogance. It is the most loving thing anyone can do.

The Puritan theologian John Flavel, born around 1627 in Bromsgrove, Worcestershire, England, who served as minister at Dartmouth until he was ejected from his living by the Act of Uniformity in 1662, wrote about the incomparable worth of Christ in his work The Fountain of Life. Flavel devoted an entire series of sermons to demonstrating the uniqueness, supremacy, and all-sufficiency of Jesus Christ as the Savior of the world. Flavel argued that Christ is unique in His person (the God-man, uniting divine and human natures in one person), unique in His work (the only atoning sacrifice for sin), unique in His resurrection (the only religious founder who conquered death), and unique in His claims (the only teacher who claimed to be God incarnate, the way, the truth, and the life). Flavel wrote, "Christ is the desire of nations, the joy of angels, the delight of the Father. What solace then must that soul be filled with, that hath the possession of Him to all eternity!"

The Puritan pastor Richard Baxter, born in 1615 in Rowton, Shropshire, England, addressed the question of exclusivity with pastoral wisdom in his great work The Reformed Pastor. Baxter argued that the exclusivity of Christ is not a harsh doctrine but a merciful one — because it means that salvation does not depend on our ability to navigate a confusing plurality of paths, but on our willingness to trust the one Savior whom God has provided. The way is narrow, but it is open to all. "Whosoever will, let him take the water of life freely" (Revelation 22:17). The exclusivity of the way does not limit the universality of the invitation. Christ is the only way, and He invites everyone to come.

How should Christians communicate this truth? Peter gives us the answer: "But sanctify the Lord God in your hearts: and be ready always to give an answer to every man that asketh you a reason of the hope that is in you with meekness and fear" (1 Peter 3:15). Notice the qualities Peter emphasizes: meekness and fear — or as other translations render it, gentleness and respect.

We should communicate the exclusivity of Christ with genuine love for the people we are speaking to. We are not trying to win debates. We are trying to introduce people to the Savior who loved them and died for them. We should communicate with humility, remembering that we ourselves were once lost and that we are saved not by our own merit but by grace. We should communicate with respect, acknowledging that the people we are speaking to are sincere, intelligent, and made in the image of God. And we should communicate with clarity, not softening or obscuring the truth in order to avoid offense, but presenting it plainly, honestly, and lovingly.

The Puritan pastor Thomas Brooks, born in 1608 in London, England, who served during the turbulent years of the English Civil War and the Commonwealth, wrote about the art of presenting difficult truths with gentleness and love. In his work Precious Remedies Against Satan's Devices, published in 1652, Brooks warned against two opposite errors: the error of compromising the truth to avoid giving offense, and the error of presenting the truth in such a harsh, abrasive, or self-righteous manner that people are repelled not by the truth itself but by the manner in which it is presented. Brooks counseled that the truth should be presented like a sword wrapped in silk — sharp enough to cut through error, but presented with such gentleness that the hearer feels cared for rather than attacked.

The Puritan theologian Stephen Charnock, born in 1628 in London, England, wrote about the necessity of Christ in his monumental work The Existence and Attributes of God. Charnock argued that the holiness of God makes the exclusivity of Christ inevitable. If God is infinitely holy — and Scripture declares that He is — then no sinful human being can approach Him on the basis of their own merit. The gap between God's holiness and human sinfulness is infinite. Only an infinite sacrifice can bridge an infinite gap. And only an infinite person — the eternal Son of God — can offer an infinite sacrifice. The exclusivity of Christ, therefore, is not arbitrary. It flows necessarily from the nature of God, the nature of sin, and the nature of atonement.

Let us now consider the common philosophical objection that religious exclusivism is logically untenable. Some philosophers, following the parable of the blind men and the elephant attributed to various Eastern traditions, argue that all religions are merely partial perspectives on a reality too vast for any single tradition to comprehend. Each religion, according to this view, grasps a portion of the truth but none possesses the whole truth. The claim that one religion is exclusively true is, in this view, a failure of humility — a refusal to acknowledge the limitations of one's own perspective.

This is a superficially attractive argument, but it contains a fatal flaw. The person who tells the parable of the blind men and the elephant claims to see what the blind men cannot see — namely, that the object they are all touching is an elephant. The person who says, "All religions are partial perspectives on a truth too vast for any single religion to grasp," is claiming to have a perspective that transcends all the particular religions — a God's-eye view that sees the whole truth while the individual religions see only fragments. But this is itself an exclusive claim — a claim to know something that contradicts the specific teachings of every major religion. It is not a humble position at all. It is an extraordinarily arrogant position that claims to know better than Jesus, better than Muhammad, better than Buddha, and better than every other religious teacher in history what the nature of ultimate reality actually is.

The Christian does not claim to have a God's-eye view of reality. The Christian claims that God Himself has spoken — that the infinite, omniscient Creator has revealed Himself in His Word and in His Son. The Christian claim of exclusivity is not a claim of human superiority. It is a claim of divine revelation. We do not say, "We have figured out the truth by our own brilliance." We say, "God has revealed the truth by His grace." The source of our confidence is not our own wisdom but God's self-disclosure.

The Puritan theologian William Perkins, born in 1558 in Marston Jabbet, Warwickshire, England, who was one of the most influential Reformed theologians of the Elizabethan era, wrote about the certainty that comes from divine revelation in contrast to the uncertainty of human speculation. In his work The Arte of Prophecying, published in 1592, Perkins argued that the knowledge of God available through human reason and natural theology, while genuine, is insufficient and unreliable apart from the corrective lens of Scripture. Only God's Word provides certain knowledge of God's nature, God's purposes, and God's way of salvation. Perkins wrote that the Scriptures are "the fountain of all divine truth" and that any claim about God that contradicts Scripture must be rejected, regardless of how philosophically sophisticated or culturally popular it may be.

The Puritan pastor Samuel Rutherford, born in 1600 in Nisbet, Scotland, who was exiled to Aberdeen for his faith in 1636, wrote about the preciousness and exclusivity of Christ with an intensity that is unmatched in devotional literature. In his Letters, written from his exile, Rutherford poured out his love for Christ in language of breathtaking beauty and passion. He wrote, "I see that He was slain before the foundation of the world, and that my name was written in the Lamb's Book of Life." Rutherford understood that the exclusivity of Christ is not a cold, abstract doctrine but a deeply personal reality — the reality that the Son of God loved him, died for him, and secured his salvation by His own blood. For Rutherford, the exclusivity of Christ was not a source of controversy but a source of infinite wonder and gratitude.

The Puritan pastor and evangelist George Whitefield, born in 1714 in Gloucester, England, who became the most famous preacher of the Great Awakening and preached the gospel to enormous crowds on both sides of the Atlantic, proclaimed the exclusivity of Christ with a passion and urgency that moved thousands to faith. Whitefield understood that if Christ is the only way, then preaching Christ to every creature is the most important work in the world. He devoted his entire life to this work, preaching an estimated 18,000 sermons over the course of his ministry. Whitefield's example reminds us that the doctrine of Christ's exclusivity is not merely a theological proposition to be defended in debates. It is a motivating truth that should drive us to share the gospel with every person we can reach, in every way we can, for as long as we live.

Let us also consider a further philosophical point that strengthens the Christian position. The modern virtue of "tolerance" is often invoked against the exclusivity of Christ. But it is important to understand what tolerance actually means. True tolerance does not mean believing that all views are equally true. That is not tolerance — that is indifference. True tolerance means treating people with respect and dignity even when you disagree with their beliefs. You can only tolerate something you disagree with. If you agree with everything, there is nothing to tolerate.

Christians can and should be the most tolerant people in the world — not because we believe all religions are the same, but because we believe every human being is made in the image of God and deserves to be treated with love and respect, regardless of their religious beliefs. We can hold firmly to the exclusivity of Christ while treating people of other faiths with genuine kindness, respect, and friendship. In fact, our conviction that every person is created in God's image and loved by God should make us more respectful of others, not less.

The apostle Paul modeled this approach in Athens (Acts 17). He did not mock the Athenians' religious practices. He acknowledged their spiritual sincerity and used their own cultural artifacts — their altar to the Unknown God — as a bridge to the gospel. He spoke with respect and intelligence. And then he proclaimed the exclusive claims of Christ with clarity and conviction: God "commandeth all men every where to repent" because He has appointed a day of judgment through the risen Christ. Paul was both respectful and exclusive — a model for every Christian apologist.

Let us close with two important practical considerations. First, the question of those who have never heard the gospel. What about people in remote parts of the world, or in previous centuries, who never had the opportunity to hear about Jesus Christ? This is a genuine and sensitive question, and it deserves a thoughtful response.

Scripture teaches that God is perfectly just and that He will judge every person fairly. "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?" (Genesis 18:25). God will not condemn anyone for rejecting a message they never heard. However, Scripture also teaches that every person has sufficient knowledge to be held accountable — the knowledge of God available through creation (Romans 1:19-20) and through conscience (Romans 2:14-15). Those who respond to the light they have been given may be given more light. And those who suppress the truth they have been given are without excuse.

The proper response to this question is not to abandon the exclusivity of Christ but to redouble our efforts to proclaim the gospel to every nation, tribe, and tongue. The urgency of world missions is directly proportional to the exclusivity of Christ. If Jesus is the only way, then telling people about Him is the most important thing we can do. "How then shall they call on him in whom they have not believed? and how shall they believe in him of whom they have not heard? and how shall they hear without a preacher?" (Romans 10:14).

Second, we must remember that the exclusivity of Christ is ultimately good news, not bad news. It means that salvation does not depend on our ability to figure out which of the world's thousands of religions is correct. It does not depend on our moral achievement, our spiritual discipline, or our philosophical acumen. It depends on Jesus Christ — on who He is and what He has done. And what He has done is sufficient. The cross is enough. The blood of Christ is enough. The grace of God is enough. This is the best news the world has ever heard — that there is a way to God, that the way is open, that the way is free, and that the way is Christ.

As the great Puritan pastor and hymn writer Isaac Watts wrote, expressing the heart of the gospel in language that has moved millions of souls, the cross of Christ is the center of all true religion, the only hope of sinners, and the eternal glory of the redeemed. There is no other foundation. There is no other name. There is no other way. And there is no other love so great as the love that sent the Son of God to die for sinners.

The Puritan pastor Richard Sibbes, born in 1577 in Tostock, Suffolk, England, who served as preacher at Gray's Inn, London, and Master of Catherine Hall, Cambridge, wrote about the tenderness and sufficiency of Christ in his beloved work The Bruised Reed and Smoking Flax, published in 1630. Sibbes emphasized that the Christ who is the only way is also the most gentle, compassionate, and welcoming Savior imaginable. He does not break the bruised reed or quench the smoking flax. He receives sinners with open arms. He carries the weak. He binds up the brokenhearted. The exclusivity of Christ does not mean that the way is hard to find or difficult to enter. It means that all who come to Him — regardless of their background, their sins, their failures, or their past — will be received with infinite love and grace. As Jesus Himself promised, "All that the Father giveth me shall come to me; and him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out" (John 6:37).

This is the heart of the matter. The exclusivity of Christ is inseparable from the generosity of Christ. The only way is also the open way. The narrow gate leads to the broad place of God's infinite love. And the invitation goes out to all the world: "Come."

Jesus is the only way. And that is not arrogance — it is the gospel. And the gospel is the best news the world has ever heard.

--- Closing ---

The claim that Jesus is the only way to God is not arrogance — it is the gospel. It is the good news that God has not left us to find our own way through the darkness but has provided a sure, certain, and sufficient way — His own Son. The way is narrow, but it leads to life. The door is singular, but it is open to all who will enter. And the invitation is universal: "Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest" (Matthew 11:28). Share this truth with confidence, with love, with humility, and with joy. It is the best news anyone has ever heard. Until next time, students.
