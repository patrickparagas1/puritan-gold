"Science and the Christian Faith"
Apologetics for Young Christians — Lesson 16

Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we address the common claim that science and Christianity are in conflict. We will discover that the relationship between faith and science is far more harmonious than many people realize. Let us pray and begin.

Lord of all truth, You are the Author of both the book of nature and the Book of Scripture. Help us to see that these two books cannot ultimately contradict, for they come from the same Author. Give us wisdom as we explore this important topic. In Christ's name, Amen.

--- Lesson ---

One of the most persistent myths in our culture is that science and Christianity are at war — that to be a person of science, you must abandon faith, and that to be a person of faith, you must be suspicious of science. This myth has been repeated so often that many people accept it without question. But the historical and intellectual evidence tells a very different story.

The truth is that modern science was born in a Christian civilization, and many of its founders were devout Christians. Consider just a few examples. Nicolas Copernicus, who proposed the heliocentric model of the solar system, was a Catholic clergyman. Johannes Kepler, who discovered the laws of planetary motion, wrote that he was "thinking God's thoughts after Him." Galileo Galilei, often portrayed as a martyr of science against faith, remained a believing Catholic throughout his life. Isaac Newton, who formulated the laws of motion and universal gravitation, wrote extensively about theology and saw his scientific work as revealing the mind of God. Robert Boyle, the father of modern chemistry, was a devout Christian who funded Bible translations. Michael Faraday, who discovered electromagnetic induction, was an elder in his church. And in the modern era, scientists like Francis Collins (who led the Human Genome Project) have been outspoken about their Christian faith.

Why did modern science emerge in the Christian West? Because Christianity provided the intellectual framework that made science possible. Science depends on several assumptions: that the universe is orderly and governed by consistent laws, that the human mind is capable of understanding these laws, and that investigating the natural world is a worthwhile activity. Each of these assumptions is grounded in the Christian worldview.

The universe is orderly because it was created by a rational God who imposed order upon it. The human mind can understand the universe because we are made in the image of the rational God who designed it. And investigating nature is worthwhile because, as the Psalms declare, "The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork" (Psalm 19:1). Studying creation is a form of worship — it reveals the wisdom, power, and beauty of the Creator.

By contrast, worldviews that deny the existence of a rational Creator have difficulty justifying the practice of science. If the universe is the product of blind, purposeless forces, why should we expect it to be orderly? If the human mind is merely the accidental byproduct of evolution, why should we trust its ability to discover truth? These questions are not easily answered within a purely materialistic framework.

What about the specific areas where science and faith are often said to conflict? The most common is the question of origins — how the universe and life came into being. Christians hold a range of views on the specific mechanisms of creation, from young-earth creationism to old-earth creationism to evolutionary creationism. What all orthodox Christians agree on is the essential theological truth: God is the Creator of all things. The universe exists because God willed it into existence. Life exists because God designed it. Human beings are made in God's image and have a unique dignity and purpose.

The important distinction to maintain is between science as a method of investigating the natural world and scientism — the philosophical claim that science is the only way to know anything. Science is a wonderful tool for understanding how the physical world works. But it is not equipped to answer questions about meaning, purpose, morality, or beauty. The question "What is the chemical composition of a sunset?" is a scientific question. The question "Why is the sunset beautiful?" is a philosophical and ultimately theological question. Both questions are legitimate, but they require different methods of investigation.

Christians should be enthusiastic about science. We should encourage our children to study biology, chemistry, physics, and mathematics with the conviction that every discovery reveals something about the mind of God. We should also be discerning, recognizing the difference between scientific findings and the philosophical interpretations that are sometimes attached to them. And we should be humble, acknowledging that our understanding of both Scripture and nature is still growing and that apparent conflicts may be resolved as we learn more.

--- Review Questions ---

Review Question 1: How did the Christian worldview provide the intellectual foundation for the rise of modern science?

Review Question 2: Name three major scientists in history who were devout Christians. How did their faith relate to their scientific work?

Review Question 3: What is the difference between science and scientism? Why is this distinction important?

--- Activity ---

Research one of the Christian scientists mentioned in today's lesson (Copernicus, Kepler, Newton, Boyle, Faraday, or Collins). Write a one-page biographical sketch that includes their major scientific contributions, their Christian faith, and how they understood the relationship between faith and science. Include at least one direct quotation from the scientist about God or faith. Present your findings to your family or study group.

--- Closing ---

Science and Christianity are not enemies. They are allies in the pursuit of truth — one investigating the book of nature, the other reading the Book of Scripture. Both come from the mind of God, and both lead back to Him. Until next time, students.
