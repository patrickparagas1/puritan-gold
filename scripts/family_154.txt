PURITAN GOLD -- Family Devotional, Day 23
"Suffering Produces Glory"
Pilgrim Families — Week 4, Day 2


Hello, dear ones. How wonderful it is to sit together as a family and open the Scriptures. Welcome to Puritan Gold's family devotional. Let us begin with thankful hearts.

Let us pray together. Lord God, we come before You as a family, grateful for Your love and Your provision. We ask that You would quiet our minds and soften our hearts as we hear from You tonight. Help us to understand, to remember, and to obey what You teach us. In Christ's name, Amen.

Our Scripture reading this evening comes from Romans 8:18; 2 Corinthians 4:17. Let us hear the Word of the Lord:

"For I reckon that the sufferings of this present time are not worthy to be compared with the glory which shall be revealed in us. For our light affliction, which is but for a moment, worketh for us a far more exceeding and eternal weight of glory."

That is Romans 8:18; 2 Corinthians 4:17. Let those words settle in your heart for a moment before we continue.


Rutherford's letters are soaked in a paradox: suffering and glory belong together. Not suffering then glory — though that is true — but suffering producing glory. The cross is not merely something we endure on the way to the crown. The cross is the instrument God uses to prepare us for the crown.

Paul states this with mathematical precision in Romans 8:18: "I reckon that the sufferings of this present time are not worthy to be compared with the glory which shall be revealed in us." Paul has done the calculation. He has weighed the suffering on one side and the glory on the other. And the scales are not even close. The glory so far outweighs the suffering that they are "not worthy to be compared." It is like comparing a candle to the sun.

And in 2 Corinthians 4:17, Paul makes an even more astounding claim: our affliction is actually working for us. It is producing something. It is not wasted suffering. It is not meaningless pain. Every moment of trial is building an "eternal weight of glory" that will be revealed in us. The heavier the affliction, the greater the glory. The longer the trial, the deeper the reward.

Rutherford lived this truth. He called the cross of Christ "the sweetest burden that ever I bore" and compared it to "wings on a bird" and "sails to a ship." A bird does not complain about its wings — they are what carry it upward. A ship does not complain about its sails — they are what propel it forward. Rutherford saw his suffering not as an anchor holding him back but as a sail pushing him toward his harbor — the presence of Christ in glory.

This perspective transforms everything. When a child struggles at school, that struggle is producing perseverance. When a parent loses a job, that loss may be producing dependence on God. When a family faces illness, that illness may be producing compassion, faith, and eternal perspective. None of this means suffering is enjoyable. It means suffering is purposeful.

The Puritans distinguished between affliction and punishment. For the unbeliever, suffering may indeed be punitive — the consequence of sin. But for the believer, suffering is never punishment because Christ bore all our punishment on the cross. Every drop of God's wrath against our sin was poured out on Jesus. Therefore, when a Christian suffers, it is not wrath — it is love. It is the Father refining His children, not the Judge condemning criminals.

Rutherford wrote to a friend who had lost a child: "Your dear child has only gone a little before you to heaven; you will see him again." He wrote to a woman enduring persecution: "Christ and you are both going one way; He to a crown, and you to a crown." He constantly reframed suffering in the light of glory, helping his readers see that the present pain is temporary but the coming joy is eternal.

For families, this teaching is not abstract theology — it is practical comfort for real pain. When your family is going through a hard season, remember: this is working for you, not against you. God is not wasting your tears. He is collecting them (Psalm 56:8) and transforming them into glory. The caterpillar in the cocoon feels crushed, but the crushing is producing wings. The olive in the press is broken, but the breaking is producing oil. Your family's trials are producing something beautiful that you may not see yet — but one day you will see, and you will say it was worth it all.

Tonight, if your family is in a season of suffering, take comfort. If your family is in a season of ease, prepare your hearts. Either way, fix your eyes on the glory that is coming. It is a weight of glory so heavy, so magnificent, so eternal that every suffering on earth will seem light by comparison.


The Puritans were men and women who treasured God's Word above all else. Let us hear from one of them now.

Samuel Rutherford, in his work "Letters of Samuel Rutherford," wrote these words:

"The cross of Christ is the sweetest burden that ever I bore; it is such a burden as wings are to a bird, or sails to a ship, to carry me forward to my harbour."

What Samuel Rutherford is reminding us is that the truths we have read tonight in Romans 8:18; 2 Corinthians 4:17 are not new. They have been treasured by faithful Christians for centuries. And they are just as true and just as needed in our family today as they were hundreds of years ago. Let us take these words to heart and let them shape the way we live together as a family.


Let us pause and talk as a family. Here are tonight's discussion questions. Everyone gets a turn to share.

Question 1: How is suffering like 'wings on a bird' or 'sails on a ship,' as Rutherford described?

Question 2: Paul says affliction 'worketh for us' glory. Can you think of a time when something hard in your life produced something good?

Question 3: What is the difference between God punishing us and God refining us? How does knowing Jesus changes the meaning of suffering?

Question 4: How can our family encourage each other when someone is going through a hard time?

Take a few minutes to talk about these questions together. Let everyone share, and listen to one another with patience and love.


Now, here is our memory verse for today. Let us say it together:

"For I reckon that the sufferings of this present time are not worthy to be compared with the glory which shall be revealed in us." -- Romans 8:18

Try to say it out loud three times right now. Then, before bed tonight, see if you can say it from memory. Tomorrow morning, try again. The more we hide God's Word in our hearts, the more it will guide us when we need it most.


To make tonight's lesson come alive, try this activity together as a family:

Place an ice cube in each family member's hand and see how long they can hold it. It is uncomfortable! But when the ice melts, their hand is still there — and they have a story of endurance. Compare this to trials: they are uncomfortable for a moment but produce something lasting. Discuss what 'glory' God might be producing through your family's current challenges.


Let us pray together. Lord God, we thank You for this time as a family. Thank You for speaking to us through Your Word. Plant these truths deep in our hearts, especially in the hearts of our children. Help us to walk in wisdom tomorrow and every day. In Jesus' name, Amen.

Sleep well tonight, knowing that God watches over your family.
