PURITAN GOLD -- Family Devotional, Day 25
"The Godly Person Repents"
Thomas Watson — The Godly Man's Picture

---

Good evening, beloved family. The Puritans believed that the family altar was the most important altar in all the land. Tonight we gather at that altar together. Welcome to Puritan Gold.

Let us come before the Lord. Gracious God, thank You for the gift of family. Thank You that we can come to You together. We pray that You would teach us from Your Word this evening. Let every word sink deep into our hearts. We love You, Lord. In Jesus' name, Amen.

The Word of God is a lamp to our feet and a light to our path. Tonight that light shines from 2 Corinthians 7:10:

"For godly sorrow worketh repentance to salvation not to be repented of: but the sorrow of the world worketh death."

That is 2 Corinthians 7:10. Let those words settle in your heart for a moment before we continue.

--- Teaching ---

Today we come to a topic that many find uncomfortable but that Thomas Watson called essential — repentance. Paul writes in 2 Corinthians 7:10, 'For godly sorrow worketh repentance to salvation not to be repented of: but the sorrow of the world worketh death.'

Paul draws a sharp distinction between two kinds of sorrow. There is 'godly sorrow' and 'the sorrow of the world.' They may look similar on the surface, but they are profoundly different in nature and outcome.

Worldly sorrow is being sorry you got caught. It is regret over consequences — being grounded, losing a friendship, facing punishment. This kind of sorrow does not produce change. A child who is only sorry because they are in trouble will do the same thing again as soon as they think they will not get caught.

Godly sorrow is different. It is grief over the sin itself — not just its consequences but its offense against a holy and loving God. When David sinned with Bathsheba and was confronted by the prophet Nathan, he cried out in Psalm 51:4, 'Against thee, thee only, have I sinned, and done this evil in thy sight.' David had sinned against Bathsheba, against her husband Uriah, and against his own family. But at the deepest level, he recognized that all sin is ultimately against God.

Godly sorrow 'worketh repentance to salvation.' The word 'repentance' in Greek is 'metanoia,' which means a change of mind — a complete turning around. It is not just feeling bad. It is changing direction. It is like a person walking toward a cliff who suddenly realizes where they are heading, stops, turns around, and walks the other way.

Jesus began His public ministry with this call: 'Repent: for the kingdom of heaven is at hand' (Matthew 4:17). Peter preached on the day of Pentecost, 'Repent, and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ for the remission of sins' (Acts 2:38). Repentance is not optional in Christianity. It is the front door.

But repentance is not a one-time event. It is a lifestyle. Martin Luther's first thesis said, 'When our Lord and Master Jesus Christ said, Repent, he willed the entire life of believers to be one of repentance.' Every day, we see ways we fall short. Every day, we turn back to God. The Christian life is a continual cycle of recognizing sin, confessing it, receiving forgiveness, and walking in newness.

First John 1:9 gives us this glorious promise: 'If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness.' God does not reluctantly forgive. He is faithful — He keeps His promise. He is just — because Christ has already paid the penalty. He forgives and cleanses. Both. Completely.

Thomas Watson wrote what many consider the finest Puritan book on repentance — The Doctrine of Repentance. Watson defined repentance as a grace of God's Spirit whereby a sinner is inwardly humbled and visibly reformed. True repentance, he said, is not merely being sorry for the consequences of sin but being grieved at sin's offense against a holy God.

Watson described six ingredients of genuine repentance. First, sight of sin — you see your sin clearly. Second, sorrow for sin — godly grief, not worldly regret. Third, confession of sin — honest, specific acknowledgment before God. Fourth, shame for sin — a healthy sense of humility. Fifth, hatred for sin — not just wishing you had not done it but hating the sin itself. Sixth, turning from sin — actual change of behavior.

Watson warned against counterfeit repentance. Some people shed tears but never change. Some confess but never forsake. Some feel guilty but do not turn to Christ. These are symptoms of worldly sorrow, not godly repentance.

But Watson also offered great comfort. He reminded his readers that God delights to forgive the truly repentant. In Isaiah 55:7, God says, 'Let the wicked forsake his way, and the unrighteous man his thoughts: and let him return unto the LORD, and he will have mercy upon him; and to our God, for he will abundantly pardon.' Not grudgingly pardon — abundantly pardon.

For our youngest ones: when you do something wrong and you feel sorry, that is a good thing! God wants you to tell Him about it and ask for forgiveness. He always forgives when we are truly sorry. For older children: is there something you have been feeling guilty about? Today is the day to bring it to God. He promises to forgive and cleanse. For the whole family: let us be a family where it is safe to say 'I was wrong' and 'I am sorry' — and where forgiveness is given freely because God has freely forgiven us.

--- Puritan Wisdom ---

The wisdom of the Puritans remains as fresh today as when it was first written. Listen to this insight.

Thomas Watson, in his work "The Doctrine of Repentance," wrote these words:

"Repentance is a grace of God's Spirit whereby a sinner is inwardly humbled and visibly reformed. True repentance is not merely being sorry for sin's consequences but being grieved at sin's offense against a holy God."

What Thomas Watson is reminding us is that the truths we have read tonight in 2 Corinthians 7:10 are not new. They have been treasured by faithful Christians for centuries. And they are just as true and just as needed in our family today as they were hundreds of years ago. Let us take these words to heart and let them shape the way we live together as a family.

--- Family Discussion ---

Here are some questions for us to think about and discuss together. There are no wrong answers here, just honest hearts.

Question 1: What is the difference between being sorry you got caught and being truly sorry for what you did?

Question 2: What does 'repentance' mean? Is it just saying sorry, or is it something more?

Question 3: Why does God promise to forgive us when we confess? What makes Him willing to do that?

Question 4: How can our family create a safe space where everyone can admit mistakes and receive grace?

Take a few minutes to talk about these questions together. Let everyone share, and listen to one another with patience and love.

--- Memory Verse ---

Now, here is our memory verse for today. Let us say it together:

"If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness." -- 1 John 1:9

Try to say it out loud three times right now. Then, before bed tonight, see if you can say it from memory. Tomorrow morning, try again. The more we hide God's Word in our hearts, the more it will guide us when we need it most.

--- Family Activity ---

Here is a hands-on way to remember what we have learned tonight:

Use a whiteboard or a piece of paper. Have each person write (or whisper to a parent who writes) something they want to confess or let go of. Then erase or tear up the paper together, symbolizing God's complete forgiveness. Read 1 John 1:9 together as you do it.

--- Closing ---

Father, we have heard Your Word tonight, and we are grateful. We confess that we cannot live it out in our own strength. Fill us with Your Spirit. Give us wisdom for tomorrow, patience with one another, and a deep love for You. Keep our family close to You always. In Jesus' name. Amen.

Rest well tonight. God is faithful, and His wisdom is sure.
