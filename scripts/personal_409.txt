PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Doctrine Deep-Dive, Day 9
"Justified by Faith Alone"
The Doctrine at the Heart of the Gospel


Welcome. Doctrine is not a dry, academic exercise. It is the scaffolding on which the entire Christian life is built. Tonight we examine a foundational truth that, rightly understood, will transform the way you worship, pray, and live.

Tonight we draw from the work of Multiple (Owen, Bunyan, Traill), specifically from "Various works on justification." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Romans 5:1:

"Therefore being justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


The doctrine of justification by faith alone — sola fide — is what Martin Luther called 'the article by which the church stands or falls.' The Puritans inherited this doctrine from the Reformation and developed it with remarkable precision and pastoral warmth. For the man who wants to grow in Reformed theology, there is no more important doctrine to understand than this one.

Justification, in the theological sense, is a legal declaration. It is not a process (that is sanctification) but an act. In justification, God the righteous Judge declares the guilty sinner to be righteous — not on the basis of any righteousness found in the sinner, but solely on the basis of the righteousness of Jesus Christ, received through faith alone.

John Owen, in his monumental work 'The Doctrine of Justification by Faith,' provides the most thorough Puritan treatment of this subject. Owen defines justification as 'the gracious act of God, whereby He declares the sinner righteous through the imputation of Christ's obedience and satisfaction.' Owen is meticulous in his insistence that justification is a forensic (legal) act, not a moral transformation. God does not make the sinner righteous in justification; He declares the sinner righteous. The sinner's actual moral condition is addressed in sanctification, which is a separate (though inseparable) grace.

Owen identifies two components of the righteousness that is imputed to the believer. The first is Christ's passive obedience: His suffering and death on the cross, which satisfies the penalty of the law. Because Christ bore the punishment that sinners deserve, the justified sinner is free from condemnation. The second is Christ's active obedience: His perfect keeping of God's law throughout His earthly life. This positive righteousness is credited to the believer's account, so that he stands before God not merely as 'not guilty' but as 'positively righteous.' Owen insists that both aspects are necessary for a complete justification.

John Bunyan, the beloved author of 'The Pilgrim's Progress,' brings a more experiential perspective to the doctrine. In his spiritual autobiography, 'Grace Abounding to the Chief of Sinners,' Bunyan describes his own agonizing journey to peace with God. For years, Bunyan was tormented by the fear that his sins were too great to be forgiven. He tried to earn God's favor through moral effort and religious devotion, but every attempt left him more despairing. It was only when he understood that his righteousness before God depended entirely on Christ, not on himself, that he found peace.

Bunyan writes that when he finally grasped the truth of imputed righteousness, it was as though chains fell from his soul. He saw that his standing before God did not fluctuate with his daily performance. On his best day, he was no more justified than on his worst, because his justification rested not on his obedience but on Christ's. This is the glorious liberty of the gospel: the believer's peace with God is as stable as the righteousness of Christ, which is to say it is perfectly and eternally stable.

Robert Traill, a Scottish Puritan, wrote a treatise specifically defending justification by faith alone against the errors of his day. Traill argues that any addition of human works to the ground of justification is a denial of the gospel. He writes that the moment we introduce our own righteousness as a contributing factor, we have moved from grace to merit, from Christ to self, from gospel to law. Traill is unsparing: 'If the righteousness by which we are justified is partly Christ's and partly our own, then we are partly saved by grace and partly by works, and the cross of Christ is made of none effect.'

Traill also addresses the common objection that justification by faith alone leads to moral laxity. If we are justified apart from works, why bother with holiness? Traill responds that this objection misunderstands the nature of saving faith. True faith is never alone; it is always accompanied by repentance, love, and a desire for holiness. The man who claims to have faith but shows no evidence of sanctification has reason to doubt whether his faith is genuine. But the ground of his acceptance before God, if he is truly a believer, is never his holiness; it is always and only the righteousness of Christ.

Why does this doctrine matter for your daily life as a Christian man? Because the battle against sin, the burden of failure, the weight of shame — all of these are addressed at the cross. When you fall, and you will, you do not fall from grace. Christ's righteousness still covers you. Your peace with God is not forfeited by your sin, because it was not earned by your obedience. This does not make sin less serious; it makes grace more glorious. And it is this grace that empowers you to get up, repent, and press on toward holiness.


Before we close, take a few moments with these reflection questions.

Question 1: Do I truly rest in the imputed righteousness of Christ, or do I unconsciously rely on my own moral performance for my standing before God?

Question 2: When I sin, does my response reflect a man who understands justification — running to grace rather than running from God?

Question 3: How does a deepening understanding of justification by faith alone fuel rather than diminish my pursuit of holiness?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Father, I thank You that my peace with You rests not on my performance but on the perfect righteousness of Christ imputed to me by faith. When guilt and shame weigh me down, bring me back to the cross. May I never add to or subtract from the finished work of my Savior. Amen.

Until next time. May the Lord deepen your faith with every passing day.
