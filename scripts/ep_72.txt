PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"A Broken and Contrite Heart"
May 11, 2026

---

"Create in me a clean heart, O God; and renew a right spirit within me. Cast me not away from thy presence; and take not thy holy spirit from me. Restore unto me the joy of thy salvation; and uphold me with thy free spirit. Then will I teach transgressors thy ways; and sinners shall be converted unto thee. The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit: a broken and a contrite heart, O God, thou wilt not despise." -- Psalm 51:10-13, 17

We come now to the second half of David's great penitential prayer, and here the tone begins to shift. The first nine verses were dominated by the confession of sin and the plea for cleansing. But from the tenth verse onward, the psalm rises from the depths of contrition to the heights of renewed hope. David, having fully acknowledged the enormity of his guilt, now dares to ask God not merely for forgiveness but for restoration, not merely for pardon but for renewal, not merely for the removal of his sin but for the creation of a new heart within him.

"Create in me a clean heart, O God." We noted previously that the word "create" here is the Hebrew word bara, the same word used in Genesis 1:1 for the original creation of the universe. This is no small matter. David is not asking God to improve his existing heart, to patch it up, to reform it, to educate it into better behaviour. He is asking God to do something entirely new, something that only omnipotent power can accomplish: to create where there was nothing but corruption, to bring purity out of the very depths of defilement.

And this, beloved, is precisely what happens in regeneration. The new birth is not a renovation of the old nature but the creation of a new nature altogether. The old man is not reformed; he is crucified. The new man is not an improvement upon the old; he is a new creation. "If any man be in Christ," says the apostle, "he is a new creature: old things are passed away; behold, all things are become new." This is the work of the Holy Spirit, and it is as miraculous in its own way as the original creation of light out of darkness.

"And renew a right spirit within me." The word "right" here carries the sense of steadfast, firm, established. David's spirit had been unsteady and wavering. His fall into sin had demonstrated the instability of his own heart when left to itself. And now he prays for a spirit that is fixed, settled, grounded in the truth and righteousness of God. It is one thing to repent after a fall. It is another thing altogether to be so established in holiness that the fall does not recur. And this latter blessing is what David seeks.

"Cast me not away from thy presence; and take not thy holy spirit from me." These are among the most agonizing words in the entire psalm. David had seen what happened to King Saul when the Spirit of the Lord departed from him. He had witnessed the madness, the darkness, the torment that descended upon a man whom God had forsaken. And he trembles at the thought that the same fate might befall him. It is worth noting that David does not fear the loss of his kingdom, his wealth, or his reputation as much as he fears the loss of God's presence and God's Spirit. This is the hallmark of a regenerate soul. The unregenerate man fears poverty, disgrace, and death. The regenerate man fears one thing above all others: separation from God.

Now, under the new covenant, the believer has a greater assurance than David possessed. The Lord Jesus has promised that the Holy Spirit shall abide with His people forever. The Spirit's indwelling is not a temporary gift that can be revoked at any moment, but a permanent possession of every true believer. Nevertheless, the felt comfort and joy of the Spirit's presence may be withdrawn as a consequence of unconfessed sin, and this is what David experienced during his long season of concealment. The Spirit did not leave him, but the joy of the Spirit's presence was taken away, and in its absence David's life became a misery.

"Restore unto me the joy of thy salvation." Not the salvation itself, for that had not been lost. David was still a child of God. He was still in covenant with the Almighty. The foundation of his relationship with God had not been destroyed. But the joy that accompanies salvation, the delight of communion with God, the gladness of knowing oneself forgiven and accepted, this had been taken away, and David longs for its return more than a starving man longs for bread.

How many believers today are living in this very condition! They are truly saved. They belong to Christ. Their names are written in the Lamb's book of life. And yet the joy of their salvation has leaked away through some unconfessed sin, some cherished idol, some compromise with the world, and they go through their days with a dull ache in their souls where the joy of the Lord once burned bright. If this describes your present condition, dear listener, then let David's prayer become your own. Confess your sin. Forsake your idol. Return to the Lord with your whole heart. And ask Him to restore unto you the joy of His salvation.

"Then will I teach transgressors thy ways; and sinners shall be converted unto thee." Here is a beautiful and instructive principle. The restored sinner becomes the most effective evangelist. The man who has known the depths of his own corruption and the heights of God's forgiving grace speaks with an authority that the merely theoretical theologian can never possess. He has been there. He has tasted the bitterness of sin and the sweetness of pardon. And when he speaks to other transgressors, they hear in his voice the ring of genuine experience.

"The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit: a broken and a contrite heart, O God, thou wilt not despise." Here we reach the theological summit of the psalm. David, who was himself the king of a nation whose entire worship system was built around animal sacrifices, declares that the sacrifice God truly values is not the blood of bulls and goats but the broken spirit and the contrite heart. This is not a rejection of the sacrificial system, which God Himself had instituted. It is a declaration that the outward sacrifice without the inward reality is worthless. God does not want your rituals if He does not have your heart. He does not want your churchgoing if He does not have your brokenness. He does not want your religious performance if He does not have your genuine repentance.

A broken heart is a heart that has been shattered by the weight of its own sinfulness and the glory of God's holiness. A contrite heart is a heart that has been crushed and ground to powder, until every trace of pride and self-righteousness has been pulverized. And this heart, says the psalmist, God will not despise. He will not turn it away. He will not look upon it with contempt. On the contrary, He treasures it above all the burnt offerings and whole burnt sacrifices that human hands can bring. For the God who inhabits eternity and dwells in the high and holy place has made a second dwelling place for Himself: with him that is of a contrite and humble spirit, to revive the spirit of the humble, and to revive the heart of the contrite ones. Amen.
