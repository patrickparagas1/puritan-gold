Welcome back, students. Today we begin what I believe will be one of the most important and transformative units in all of Puritan Gold Academy. Over the next ten lessons, we will work through the Ten Commandments as the Puritans understood them — not merely as a list of ancient prohibitions carved in stone, but as a comprehensive, living, and deeply practical guide to the moral life. --- Lesson ---

Part One: The Giving of the Law at Sinai

To understand the moral law, we must first return to the place where it was given. The scene at Mount Sinai is one of the most extraordinary events in the entire history of the world. It was approximately 1446 B.C. — though scholars debate the precise date — when the people of Israel, recently delivered from four hundred years of slavery in Egypt, arrived at the foot of a mountain in the wilderness of the Sinai Peninsula. They had crossed the Red Sea on dry ground, they had been fed with manna from heaven, and they had drunk water from a rock that God struck open at the command of Moses. They were a redeemed people — delivered not by their own strength but by the mighty hand of God.

And it was to this redeemed people that God gave His law. This sequence is critically important. The law was not given as a means of earning God's favor. The law was given to a people who had already received God's favor. The Israelites were already saved — delivered from Egypt, brought through the sea, sustained in the wilderness. The law came after redemption, not before it. It was given not as a condition of salvation but as a guide for the saved. This is the pattern that the Puritans grasped with remarkable clarity: grace precedes law, redemption precedes obedience, deliverance precedes duty.

The scene at Sinai was terrifying and majestic. Exodus chapter 19 describes it in vivid detail: "And it came to pass on the third day in the morning, that there were thunders and lightnings, and a thick cloud upon the mount, and the voice of the trumpet exceeding loud; so that all the people that was in the camp trembled. And Moses brought forth the people out of the camp to meet with God; and they stood at the nether part of the mount. And mount Sinai was altogether on a smoke, because the Lord descended upon it in fire: and the smoke thereof ascended as the smoke of a furnace, and the whole mount quaked greatly" (Exodus 19:16-18).

The writer of Hebrews reflects on this scene centuries later: "For ye are not come unto the mount that might be touched, and that burned with fire, nor unto blackness, and darkness, and tempest, and the sound of a trumpet, and the voice of words; which voice they that heard intreated that the word should not be spoken to them any more: for they could not endure that which was commanded" (Hebrews 12:18-20). The giving of the law was an event of overwhelming divine majesty. God was making it unmistakably clear that His moral law was not a human invention, not a social contract, not a philosophical theory — it was the authoritative declaration of the sovereign Creator of heaven and earth.

It was in this context that God spoke the Ten Commandments — the ten "words," as the Hebrew literally says — directly to the people of Israel. He then inscribed them on two tablets of stone with His own finger, a detail that underscores their permanence and divine authority. These were not suggestions. They were not recommendations. They were the unchangeable moral will of the eternal God.

Part Two: The Nature of the Moral Law

Before we examine the individual commandments, we must understand the nature and purpose of the moral law itself. The Puritans and their Reformed predecessors drew a crucial distinction between three types of law found in the Old Testament: the moral law, the ceremonial law, and the judicial (or civil) law. This threefold distinction was articulated by Thomas Aquinas in the thirteenth century, refined by John Calvin in the sixteenth century, and codified by the Westminster Assembly in the seventeenth century. It is essential for understanding how the Old Testament applies to Christians today.

The ceremonial law governed the worship practices of ancient Israel — the sacrifices, the festivals, the dietary regulations, the purification rituals, the priesthood, and the tabernacle services. These laws were types and shadows that pointed forward to Christ. When Christ came and fulfilled what the ceremonies represented, the ceremonial law was abrogated — set aside, no longer binding. This is the clear teaching of the New Testament, particularly the book of Hebrews. We do not offer animal sacrifices because Christ, the Lamb of God, has been offered once for all.

The judicial law governed the civil life of Israel as a nation — laws about property, contracts, punishments for crimes, regulations for warfare, and the administration of justice. These laws were specific to Israel's unique situation as a theocratic nation-state. While the general equity of these laws — the underlying principles of justice they embody — may still be instructive, their specific provisions are no longer binding on other nations.

The moral law, however, is different. The moral law — summarized in the Ten Commandments — is universal, permanent, and unchanging. It reflects the eternal character of God Himself. Murder is wrong not because of a cultural convention but because God is the author of life. Theft is wrong not because society decided to protect property but because God is just. Adultery is wrong not because of a social taboo but because God is faithful. The moral law is grounded in the very nature of God, and because God does not change, His moral law does not change.

The Westminster Confession of Faith, chapter 19, paragraph 5, states this with characteristic precision: "The moral law doth for ever bind all, as well justified persons as others, to the obedience thereof; and that, not only in regard of the matter contained in it, but also in respect of the authority of God the Creator, who gave it. Neither doth Christ, in the gospel, any way dissolve, but much strengthen this obligation." The moral law binds all people, in all times, in all places — not because it was given at Sinai, but because it reflects the eternal character of the God who gave it.

William Ames, the English Puritan theologian who wrote "The Marrow of Theology" in 1623 and later became a professor at the University of Franeker in the Netherlands, put it this way: the moral law is not a new creation of Sinai but the republication of the law of nature originally written on the human heart at creation. When God created Adam and Eve, He inscribed His moral law on their hearts. They knew instinctively what was right and what was wrong. But when sin entered the world, that original knowledge was corrupted — not destroyed entirely, but severely damaged and distorted. The giving of the law at Sinai was God's gracious act of re-inscribing on stone what sin had effaced from the human heart.

The apostle Paul confirms this in Romans chapter 2, verses 14 and 15: "For when the Gentiles, which have not the law, do by nature the things contained in the law, these, having not the law, are a law unto themselves: which shew the work of the law written in their hearts, their conscience also bearing witness, and their thoughts the mean while accusing or else excusing one another." Even the pagan Gentiles, who never stood at Sinai, have a remnant of the moral law written on their consciences. This is why every civilization in human history has recognized certain basic moral principles — the wrongness of murder, theft, and perjury — even when they differ on the details of application.

Part Three: The Three Uses of the Law

The Puritans and their Reformed predecessors distinguished three uses of the law — a distinction first articulated clearly by John Calvin in Book Two of his "Institutes of the Christian Religion," published in its final edition in 1559, and developed extensively by the Westminster Standards, completed in the 1640s.

The first use of the law is the civil or political use, sometimes called the usus politicus. The law restrains evil in society by establishing a standard of justice that civil authorities are responsible to enforce. Even people who do not believe in God are subject to the moral law as it is reflected in civil legislation. This use of the law is essential for maintaining order in a fallen world. Without it, human society would descend into chaos. Calvin wrote that the law acts as a bridle on the wicked, preventing them from carrying out the full extent of their sinful desires and thus preserving a measure of order and decency in human communities.

Consider what happens when this restraining function breaks down. The period of the Judges in Israel — roughly 1380 to 1050 B.C. — is described in the closing verse of the book of Judges with these chilling words: "In those days there was no king in Israel: every man did that which was right in his own eyes" (Judges 21:25). When there is no recognized moral authority, when the law is not upheld, when every person becomes a law unto himself, the result is not freedom but anarchy — and the weak always suffer first and worst.

The Puritans saw this principle confirmed in their own experience. When they arrived in New England in the 1620s and 1630s, one of their first acts was to establish a framework of law based on biblical principles. The Massachusetts Body of Liberties, adopted in 1641, and the New Haven Colony's legal code of 1639 both drew explicitly on the Ten Commandments as the foundation of civil order. They understood that a society without a moral foundation is a society built on sand.

The second use of the law is the pedagogical use — what the Puritans called the "schoolmaster" function, following Paul's language in Galatians chapter 3, verse 24: "Wherefore the law was our schoolmaster to bring us unto Christ, that we might be justified by faith." The law reveals our sin by showing us God's perfect standard and our utter inability to meet it. When we measure ourselves against the Ten Commandments honestly, we discover not our righteousness but our guilt.

Martin Luther, who rediscovered this truth in his study of the book of Romans in the 1510s, called this the "hammer" function of the law. The law hammers away at our self-righteousness, breaking down our pretensions to moral adequacy, and driving us in desperation to the foot of the cross. Without the law, we would never know we were sinners. As Paul wrote in Romans chapter 7, verse 7: "I had not known sin, but by the law: for I had not known lust, except the law had said, Thou shalt not covet." The law is like a mirror: it shows us our true condition. And when we see that condition clearly — stained, guilty, utterly helpless to save ourselves — we cry out for a Savior.

John Owen, the great Puritan theologian who served as vice-chancellor of Oxford University during the 1650s and authored works of unsurpassed theological depth, including "The Death of Death in the Death of Christ" and "The Mortification of Sin," wrote extensively on this second use of the law. Owen taught that the law's power to convict of sin is not cruel but merciful. It is the surgeon's knife that reveals the disease so that the cure can be applied. The law wounds so that grace can heal. The law kills so that the gospel can make alive.

The third use of the law — and this is the one that most concerns us in this unit — is the normative or didactic use, sometimes called the usus in renatis, the use in the reborn. For the believer who has been justified by faith in Christ, the law serves as a guide to grateful obedience. It shows us what God requires, what pleases Him, and how we are to live in the world as His redeemed people.

The Puritans loved the third use of the law with a passion that modern Christians sometimes find surprising. They did not see the commandments as a burden imposed on reluctant sinners but as a gift given to beloved children — a map showing the path of wisdom, holiness, and happiness. Psalm 119, the longest chapter in the Bible — all 176 verses of it — is an extended love song to the law of God. "O how love I thy law! it is my meditation all the day" (Psalm 119:97). "Thy word is a lamp unto my feet, and a light unto my path" (Psalm 119:105). "Great peace have they which love thy law: and nothing shall offend them" (Psalm 119:165).

Samuel Bolton, the Puritan pastor and scholar who served as master of Christ's College, Cambridge, from 1645 until his death in 1654, wrote a remarkable treatise called "The True Bounds of Christian Freedom," published in 1645. In it, Bolton argued that the moral law and Christian liberty are not enemies but friends. The believer is freed from the law as a covenant of works — that is, freed from the impossible demand to earn salvation by perfect obedience. But the believer is not freed from the law as a rule of life. On the contrary, the believer now delights in the law because the Holy Spirit has written it on the heart. Bolton wrote that the law sends us to the gospel for justification, and the gospel sends us back to the law for sanctification. The two work together in beautiful harmony.

Part Four: The Westminster Method of Exposition

The Westminster Larger Catechism, completed in 1648 by the Westminster Assembly of Divines — a gathering of 121 English and Scottish ministers and theologians who met at Westminster Abbey in London from 1643 to 1652 — provides an extraordinarily rich and detailed exposition of the Ten Commandments. Their method of exposition is a masterpiece of theological analysis, and it will guide our study throughout this unit.

For each commandment, the catechism identifies three things: the duty required, the sin forbidden, and the reasons annexed. This threefold analysis reveals that each commandment is far broader and deeper than its surface wording suggests.

Consider, for example, the sixth commandment: "Thou shalt not kill." On the surface, this appears to be a simple prohibition of murder. But the Westminster divines, following a long tradition of Reformed exposition, identified an astonishing range of duties required and sins forbidden by this single commandment. The sins forbidden include not only murder but all forms of injustice, cruelty, anger, hatred, envy, malice, revenge, excessive punishment, provocation, needless risk-taking, gluttony, drunkenness, neglect of the poor, withholding medicine from the sick, and any other action or attitude that harms or endangers human life. And the duty required includes not only the avoidance of killing but the active preservation and promotion of life in all its dimensions — physical, emotional, and spiritual. We are to protect the innocent, feed the hungry, clothe the naked, comfort the afflicted, defend the oppressed, and promote the well-being of every person we encounter.

This expansive method of interpretation rests on several principles that the Westminster Assembly articulated in questions 99 and 100 of the Larger Catechism. These are sometimes called the "Rules for Understanding the Ten Commandments," and they are of immense practical importance. Let me describe six of them.

First, the law is spiritual. It reaches to the understanding, the will, the affections, and all the powers of the soul. It addresses not only outward behavior but inward attitudes, motives, and desires. A man may never have committed murder, but if he harbors hatred in his heart, he has violated the sixth commandment in the court of heaven.

Second, where a duty is commanded, the contrary sin is forbidden; and where a sin is forbidden, the contrary duty is commanded. Thus "Thou shalt not steal" not only forbids theft but requires generosity, honest labor, and the responsible stewardship of resources.

Third, where one sin is forbidden, all sins of the same kind are forbidden; and where one duty is required, all duties of the same kind are required. The prohibition of murder includes the prohibition of all lesser forms of harm — insults, neglect, cruelty, indifference. The command to honor parents includes the duty to respect all legitimate authority.

Fourth, the commandments apply not only to ourselves but to others. We are required not only to avoid sin ourselves but to discourage sin in others, and not only to practice virtue ourselves but to encourage virtue in others. We are our brother's keeper.

Fifth, what God forbids is at no time to be done; what He commands is always a duty, though it may not be possible to fulfill in every particular moment. Circumstances do not create exceptions to the moral law, though they may affect the manner and timing of its application.

Sixth, under every commandment, there is a special sin that is most directly and obviously forbidden and a special duty that is most directly and obviously required. But these are representative, not exhaustive. Each commandment is the tip of an iceberg — a visible point beneath which lies a vast body of moral instruction.

Part Five: The Spirituality of the Law

The Puritans also recognized what they called the "spirituality" of the law — the truth that the commandments address not only outward behavior but inward attitudes and motivations. This was not a Puritan innovation. Jesus Himself taught this with unmistakable clarity in the Sermon on the Mount, recorded in Matthew chapters 5 through 7.

Consider our Lord's teaching in Matthew chapter 5, verses 21 and 22: "Ye have heard that it was said by them of old time, Thou shalt not kill; and whosoever shall kill shall be in danger of the judgment: But I say unto you, That whosoever is angry with his brother without a cause shall be in danger of the judgment: and whosoever shall say to his brother, Raca, shall be in danger of the council: but whosoever shall say, Thou fool, shall be in danger of hell fire." The sixth commandment forbids not only the act of murder but the attitude of anger and contempt that lies behind it.

And again in verses 27 and 28: "Ye have heard that it was said by them of old time, Thou shalt not commit adultery: But I say unto you, That whosoever looketh on a woman to lust after her hath committed adultery with her already in his heart." The seventh commandment forbids not only the act of adultery but the lustful desire that precedes it. The law reaches into the heart, where behavior begins, and demands purity at the deepest level of human experience.

Thomas Watson, in his masterful work "The Ten Commandments," first published in 1692 as part of his larger exposition of the Westminster Shorter Catechism titled "A Body of Divinity," drove this point home with characteristic force. Watson wrote that the law of God is like a net cast into the sea — it catches the great fish and the small, the obvious sins and the hidden ones. No sin is too subtle to escape its reach. No corner of the heart is too dark for its light. The law searches us more thoroughly than any human judge could, because it searches not merely our actions but our thoughts, our desires, our motives, and our dreams.

Watson was born around 1620 and ministered as a Nonconformist pastor in London through some of the most turbulent decades in English history — the English Civil War, the execution of King Charles I in 1649, the Commonwealth under Oliver Cromwell, the Restoration of Charles II in 1660, and the Great Ejection of 1662, when approximately two thousand Puritan ministers were expelled from their pulpits for refusing to conform to the Act of Uniformity. Watson continued to minister faithfully to his congregation in secret, at great personal risk, until the Declaration of Indulgence in 1672 allowed a measure of freedom. His writings, produced during these years of hardship and persecution, are among the most practical, warm-hearted, and spiritually penetrating works in all of Puritan literature.

Part Six: The Two Tablets and the Two Great Commandments

The Ten Commandments were given by God to Moses on Mount Sinai, inscribed on two tablets of stone. The traditional division — recognized by Reformed theologians from Calvin onward — places the first four commandments on the first tablet, dealing with our duties toward God, and the last six on the second tablet, dealing with our duties toward our neighbor.

The first tablet addresses our relationship with God directly: having no other gods, making no graven images, not taking God's name in vain, and keeping the Sabbath holy. These commandments govern our worship, our devotion, our speech about God, and our use of time.

The second tablet addresses our relationships with one another: honoring parents and authorities, preserving life, maintaining marital fidelity, respecting property, speaking truth, and controlling desire. These commandments govern the entire range of human social interaction — from the family to the marketplace, from the courtroom to the inner life of the heart.

Jesus summarized the entire law in two great commandments, as recorded in Matthew chapter 22, verses 37 through 40: "Jesus said unto him, Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind. This is the first and great commandment. And the second is like unto it, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself. On these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets."

This summary reveals the animating principle of the moral law: love. The commandments are not arbitrary rules imposed by a cosmic tyrant. They are expressions of love — God's love for us (in showing us the path of life) and our love for God and neighbor (in walking that path). When we obey the commandments, we are not earning God's favor — that was already secured for us by Christ on the cross. We are expressing our gratitude, reflecting His character, and promoting the welfare of those around us.

Augustine of Hippo, writing in the late fourth and early fifth centuries — a theologian the Puritans deeply admired and frequently cited — articulated this beautifully. He taught that every human action is motivated by love of some kind. The question is not whether we love, but what we love. The moral law directs our love toward its proper objects: God supremely, and neighbor sincerely. When our loves are rightly ordered — when God holds the supreme place and all other loves are subordinated to and directed by our love for Him — then we are living as we were created to live.

Part Seven: The Law and the Gospel — Friends, Not Enemies

One of the most persistent misunderstandings in the history of Christianity is the notion that the law and the gospel are opposed to each other — that the Old Testament God of law and the New Testament God of grace are somehow in conflict. The Puritans rejected this error with vigor and precision.

The apostle Paul addresses this question directly in Romans chapter 3, verse 31: "Do we then make void the law through faith? God forbid: yea, we establish the law." Faith does not abolish the law — it establishes it. And again in Romans chapter 7, verses 12 and 14: "Wherefore the law is holy, and the commandment holy, and just, and good... For we know that the law is spiritual."

The relationship between the law and the gospel is not one of opposition but of cooperation. The law reveals our sin; the gospel provides the remedy. The law shows us what we ought to do; the gospel gives us the power to do it. The law exposes our guilt; the gospel offers forgiveness. The law drives us to Christ; Christ sends us back to the law with new hearts and a new desire to obey. As Samuel Bolton wrote, "The law sends us to the gospel that we may be justified; and the gospel sends us to the law again to inquire what is our duty as those who are justified."

Richard Baxter, the great Puritan pastor of Kidderminster from 1641 to 1660, whose pastoral labors transformed an entire town and whose book "The Reformed Pastor" published in 1656 remains a classic of ministerial literature, wrote that the Christian life is a life of grateful obedience. We do not obey in order to be saved — that would be legalism, the error of the Pharisees. But we obey because we have been saved — and that is the very heart of the gospel. As Paul wrote to Titus: "For the grace of God that bringeth salvation hath appeared to all men, teaching us that, denying ungodliness and worldly lusts, we should live soberly, righteously, and godly, in this present world" (Titus 2:11-12). Grace does not produce lawlessness. Grace produces godliness. Grace teaches us to obey.

The Puritans had a phrase for this: they spoke of the "evangelical use of the law" — the use of the law as it is applied by the Holy Spirit in the hearts of believers. Under this evangelical use, the law is no longer a taskmaster cracking a whip over frightened slaves. It is a loving Father's instruction to beloved children. It is no longer a standard we can never reach but a path we joyfully walk, empowered by the Spirit of God who dwells within us.

Part Eight: The Puritan Approach to Ethics — Practical Wisdom for Daily Life

The Puritans approached the study of the commandments with reverent delight. They saw in them the wisdom of God applied to every dimension of human experience — worship, speech, relationships, property, truth, and the desires of the heart.

Thomas Watson, in "The Ten Commandments," wrote that the law of God is not a chain that binds us but a lamp that guides us. It is the path of true liberty, for the soul that obeys God's law is free from the tyranny of sin and the slavery of self. Watson called the Ten Commandments "a hedge of thorns to keep us from straying into the devil's ground" — a vivid image that captures both the protective and the directive function of the moral law.

William Perkins, who has been called the father of Puritanism, was a fellow of Christ's College, Cambridge, from 1584 until his death in 1602. He was one of the most influential theologians of his generation, and his works were widely read not only in England but throughout Protestant Europe and in the American colonies. Perkins developed what he called "the art of living well and dying well" — a comprehensive system of practical theology that applied the principles of the moral law to every area of human conduct. His "Cases of Conscience" addressed real-life ethical dilemmas with a combination of theological rigor and pastoral warmth that became a model for later Puritan writers.

Perkins taught that the study of the Ten Commandments is not an academic exercise but a practical discipline. The commandments are not meant to be memorized and forgotten — they are meant to be meditated upon, applied, and practiced in the details of daily life. Every decision we make, every word we speak, every dollar we spend, every relationship we enter — all of these are governed by the moral law. The Puritans would have found the modern separation of religion from "real life" incomprehensible. For them, all of life was religion, and the Ten Commandments were the divinely given map for navigating every square inch of it.

Part Nine: The Law Written on the Heart — The New Covenant Promise

Before we close this foundational lesson, we must consider one more vital truth that shaped the Puritans' understanding of the moral law: the new covenant promise that God would write His law on the hearts of His people. This promise, given through the prophet Jeremiah around 627 B.C., during the darkest days before the Babylonian captivity, is one of the most glorious prophecies in all of Scripture.

"Behold, the days come, saith the Lord, that I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel, and with the house of Judah: not according to the covenant that I made with their fathers in the day that I took them by the hand to bring them out of the land of Egypt; which my covenant they brake, although I was an husband unto them, saith the Lord: But this shall be the covenant that I will make with the house of Israel; After those days, saith the Lord, I will put my law in their inward parts, and write it in their hearts; and will be their God, and they shall be my people" (Jeremiah 31:31-33).

Notice carefully what this promise says and what it does not say. It does not say that God will abolish His law. It says He will write it in a new place — not on tablets of stone but on the tablets of the human heart. The content of the law remains the same. What changes is the location and the power. Under the old covenant, the law was external — written on stone, imposed from without. Under the new covenant, the law is internal — inscribed on the heart by the Holy Spirit, embraced from within. Under the old covenant, the law commanded but did not empower. Under the new covenant, the law is accompanied by the transforming power of the Spirit, who gives believers both the desire and the ability to obey.

The prophet Ezekiel, a contemporary of Jeremiah who prophesied from Babylon during the exile, expressed the same truth with different imagery: "A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you: and I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh, and I will give you an heart of flesh. And I will put my spirit within you, and cause you to walk in my statutes, and ye shall keep my judgments, and do them" (Ezekiel 36:26-27). God does not merely command obedience; He creates the conditions for obedience by giving a new heart and a new Spirit.

The Puritans found in these passages the key to their entire understanding of the Christian life. They were not legalists — people who try to earn God's favor by their own moral effort. They were evangelicals — people who know that everything begins with grace, continues by grace, and ends in grace. But they also knew that grace produces holiness. The same Spirit who regenerates the heart also sanctifies the life. The same God who justifies the sinner also transforms the sinner into a saint. And the moral law — written now on the heart by the Spirit — is the pattern of that transformation.

John Flavel, the Puritan minister who served in Dartmouth, Devon, from 1656 until the Great Ejection of 1662, and who continued to preach in secret among the rocks and caves of the Devon coastline during the years of persecution, captured this truth beautifully. Flavel wrote that the new covenant does not give us a new law but a new heart with which to keep the old law. The commandments that once stood over us as condemning judges now walk beside us as friendly guides. The law that once terrified us now delights us, because the Spirit of God has made us lovers of holiness.

This is why the Psalmist could write with such passion: "I delight to do thy will, O my God: yea, thy law is within my heart" (Psalm 40:8). And this is why Paul could write: "For I delight in the law of God after the inward man" (Romans 7:22). The believer who knows Christ, who has been born again by the Spirit, who has experienced the new covenant in its fullness — that believer finds in the moral law not a tyrant's demand but a Father's wisdom, not a prison wall but a garden path, not a burden to be borne but a treasure to be cherished.

This is the spirit in which we approach our study of the Ten Commandments. We come not as slaves trembling before a harsh master but as children eager to learn the ways of a loving Father. We come not to earn our salvation but to express our gratitude for a salvation already given. We come with the confident expectation that the same Spirit who wrote the law on our hearts will illuminate our minds, warm our affections, and strengthen our wills as we seek to understand and obey.

Part Ten: What Lies Ahead — A Preview of the Unit

Over the next nine lessons, we will work through each of the Ten Commandments, examining what it requires, what it forbids, and how it applies to our lives today. We will draw from the Puritan expositors — Watson, Baxter, Owen, Perkins, Bolton, and the Westminster divines — and we will seek to understand the commandments not as historical relics but as living, authoritative, practical guides to the life God calls us to live.

Here is what lies ahead. In Lesson 2, we will study the first commandment: "Thou shalt have no other gods before me." We will examine the nature of idolatry in all its forms — ancient and modern, obvious and subtle — and we will learn what it means to give God the supreme place in our hearts.

In Lesson 3, we will study the second commandment: "Thou shalt not make unto thee any graven image." We will explore the Regulative Principle of Worship, the Puritan understanding of how God is to be worshiped, and the danger of reducing the infinite God to a manageable image.

In Lesson 4, we will study the third commandment: "Thou shalt not take the name of the Lord thy God in vain." We will examine the reverence that is due to God's name in worship, speech, and daily conduct.

In Lesson 5, we will study the fourth commandment: "Remember the sabbath day, to keep it holy." We will explore the Puritan theology of the Sabbath and the radical countercultural practice of setting apart one day in seven for worship and rest.

In Lessons 6 through 10, we will work through the second tablet — the commandments governing our duties to our neighbor — from the honor due to parents and authorities, through the sanctity of life, the sacredness of marriage, the rights of property, the obligation of truthfulness, and the control of desire.

Each lesson will follow the method of the Westminster Larger Catechism: identifying the duty required, the sin forbidden, and the reasons annexed to each commandment. And each lesson will seek to apply the commandments not merely to external conduct but to the inner life of the heart — for, as the Puritans never tired of reminding us, it is from the heart that all behavior flows.

Let me close this foundational lesson with the words of the Psalmist, which express the spirit in which the Puritans approached the moral law and the spirit in which we should approach it as well: "The law of the Lord is perfect, converting the soul: the testimony of the Lord is sure, making wise the simple. The statutes of the Lord are right, rejoicing the heart: the commandment of the Lord is pure, enlightening the eyes. The fear of the Lord is clean, enduring for ever: the judgments of the Lord are true and righteous altogether. More to be desired are they than gold, yea, than much fine gold: sweeter also than honey and the honeycomb" (Psalm 19:7-10).

The law of God is perfect. It is sure. It is right. It is pure. It is true. It is more precious than gold and sweeter than honey. Let us study it with reverence, with delight, and with the earnest desire to live by its light.

Review Question 3: How does Jesus summarize the entire moral law in two commandments? How do these two commandments relate to the two tablets of the Ten Commandments? Why does Jesus say that "on these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets"?

Review Question 4: Explain the difference between the moral law, the ceremonial law, and the judicial law. Why do Christians believe the moral law is still binding while the ceremonial and judicial laws are not?

Review Question 5: What did Samuel Bolton mean when he wrote that "the law sends us to the gospel that we may be justified; and the gospel sends us to the law again to inquire what is our duty as those who are justified"? How does this statement resolve the apparent tension between law and grace?

--- Activity ---

Write the Ten Commandments from memory (or copy them from Exodus 20:1-17), then organize them into the two tablets — duties to God (Commandments 1-4) and duties to neighbor (Commandments 5-10). For each commandment, write one sentence describing the duty it requires and one sentence describing the sin it forbids. Then, using the six rules of interpretation from the Westminster Larger Catechism (Questions 99-100) described in today's lesson, choose any one commandment and write a full paragraph explaining how its meaning extends beyond its surface wording. This exercise will serve as a reference sheet for the remaining nine lessons.

Materials needed: Paper, Bible (Exodus 20:1-17), pen. You may also wish to consult the Westminster Larger Catechism, Questions 91-100, which are available in many free online editions.

--- Closing ---

As we conclude this foundational lesson, let us carry with us the great truths we have learned today. The moral law was given by God at Sinai to a redeemed people — given not as a means of salvation but as a guide for the saved. It reflects the eternal, unchanging character of God Himself. It serves three great purposes: restraining evil in society, driving sinners to Christ for salvation, and guiding believers in the path of grateful obedience. It is spiritual, reaching to the deepest intentions of the heart. It is comprehensive, covering every dimension of human experience. And under the new covenant, it is written on the hearts of believers by the Holy Spirit, so that obedience flows not from fear but from love, not from compulsion but from delight.

The moral law is not the enemy of grace — it is the companion of grace. Grace saves us from the penalty of the law; the law guides us in the life of grace. Together, they form the complete picture of the Christian life — a life of grateful obedience to the God who loved us, redeemed us, and now shows us the path of true and lasting happiness.

As the Puritan pastor Thomas Watson wrote, the law is like the pillar of cloud and fire that led Israel through the wilderness — by day a shade from the burning heat, by night a light in the darkness. It guards us from danger and guides us toward home. Let us follow its light with grateful hearts. Until next time, students.
