PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"I Will Extol Thee, O Lord"
June 13, 2026

---

"I will extol thee, O Lord; for thou hast lifted me up, and hast not made my foes to rejoice over me. O Lord my God, I cried unto thee, and thou hast healed me. O Lord, thou hast brought up my soul from the grave: thou hast kept me alive, that I should not go down to the pit. Sing unto the Lord, O ye saints of his, and give thanks at the remembrance of his holiness." -- Psalm 30:1-4

This is the song of a man snatched from the very brink of destruction, and the gratitude that fills his heart is proportionate to the peril from which he has been delivered. David had been brought so low that the grave seemed ready to swallow him. His enemies had gathered around him like vultures circling a dying animal, waiting for the end so that they might divide the spoil and exult over his ruin. But God intervened. The hand that had seemed so far away reached down at the last possible moment and lifted him up, and those who had prepared their songs of triumph were silenced, and the man they had counted as dead was alive again and praising the Lord.

"I will extol thee, O Lord; for thou hast lifted me up." The word "extol" means to lift up, to exalt, to raise high in praise. And so there is a beautiful reciprocity in this verse: God has lifted David up from the depths of affliction, and David in return lifts God up in the heights of praise. This is the proper response of every redeemed soul. We have been lifted up from the pit of sin and death, from the mire of our own corruption, from the grave of spiritual deadness in which we lay helpless and hopeless. And what can we do in return but lift up the name of the One who rescued us?

Spurgeon observed that the most fervent praise always comes from those who have been delivered from the greatest dangers. The man who has never known serious illness cannot fully appreciate the blessing of health. The man who has never been in peril of his life cannot fully understand the joy of deliverance. And the man who has never felt the weight of God's displeasure cannot fully enter into the ecstasy of restored favour. It is the returning prodigal, not the elder brother, who falls upon his father's neck with tears of unspeakable gratitude. It is the woman who was a sinner, not the self-righteous Pharisee, who washes the feet of Jesus with her tears and wipes them with the hair of her head.

"O Lord my God, I cried unto thee, and thou hast healed me." Prayer and healing are linked here as cause and effect, and this is no coincidence. David did not heal himself. He did not recover by his own strength or by the skill of his physicians. He cried unto God, and God healed him. This is the testimony of every believer who has been brought through the fires of affliction. We did not endure by our own fortitude. We did not overcome by our own wisdom. We cried unto the Lord, and He heard us, and He delivered us out of all our distresses.

Jeremiah Burroughs, in his incomparable treatise on contentment, taught that the believer must learn to cry unto God in every extremity, and not merely as a last resort when all other expedients have failed. Too many Christians treat prayer as a spare tire -- something to be used only when the regular tires have gone flat. But prayer should be the first response of the soul to every circumstance, whether of joy or of sorrow, of prosperity or of adversity. The man who learns to make prayer his constant habit will find that God is a very present help in trouble, and that the healing power of the Almighty is always available to those who call upon His name.

"Sing unto the Lord, O ye saints of his, and give thanks at the remembrance of his holiness." David is not content to praise God alone; he calls upon all the saints to join him. His deliverance is not a private matter but a public testimony to the faithfulness of God, and it demands a public response. And notice what it is that provokes their thanksgiving: not merely the remembrance of God's mercy, though that is sweet indeed, but the remembrance of His holiness. This may seem strange at first, for holiness is an attribute that we associate more with awe and trembling than with joy and thanksgiving. But David understood that the holiness of God is the guarantee of all His other attributes. Because God is holy, His mercy is genuine and not mere sentimentality. Because God is holy, His justice is true and not arbitrary. Because God is holy, His promises are reliable and not empty words.

John Owen meditated at length upon the holiness of God and its relation to the comfort of believers, and he taught that the more deeply we understand the holiness of God, the more profoundly we will appreciate the grace of God. For it is precisely because God is infinitely holy that the forgiveness of our sins is such an astonishing wonder. A God who did not care about holiness could forgive cheaply. But a God who is holy, who cannot look upon sin with the least degree of allowance, who must punish every transgression with perfect justice -- when such a God forgives, it is because He has found a way to satisfy His holiness and exercise His mercy at the same time, and that way is the cross of Jesus Christ.

Let us therefore extol the Lord with grateful hearts, for He has lifted us up from the depths and has not made our enemies to rejoice over us. Let us remember His holiness and give thanks, for it is the holiness of God that makes His grace so precious and His salvation so glorious. Amen.
