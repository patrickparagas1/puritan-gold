PURITAN GOLD -- Study Topic: The Book of Sirach, Part 2 of 10
"The Crown of Wisdom"
Sirach Chapters 1–2 — True Wisdom Begins with the Fear of the Lord

---

Good evening, and welcome back to Puritan Gold. Tonight we open the Book of Sirach in earnest, and what a glorious opening it is. The first two chapters of this book are among the most beautiful meditations on the fear of the Lord to be found anywhere in wisdom literature. If you have your Bible near at hand, I would encourage you to keep it open to Proverbs and the Psalms, for you will hear their echoes on every page.

Let us begin with our Scripture reading. From Sirach chapter 1, verses 1 through 20:

"All wisdom cometh from the Lord, and is with him for ever. Who can number the sand of the sea, and the drops of rain, and the days of eternity? Who can find out the height of heaven, and the breadth of the earth, and the deep, and wisdom? Wisdom hath been created before all things, and the understanding of prudence from everlasting. The word of God most high is the fountain of wisdom; and her ways are everlasting commandments. To whom hath the root of wisdom been revealed? Or who hath known her wise counsels? There is one wise and greatly to be feared, the Lord sitting upon his throne. He created her, and saw her, and numbered her, and poured her out upon all his works. The fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom: and it was created with the faithful in the womb. The fear of the Lord is the fulness of wisdom, and filleth men with her fruits. The fear of the Lord is the crown of wisdom, making peace and perfect health to flourish."

--- Teaching ---

What a magnificent opening. Ben Sira does not ease us gently into his subject. He begins with a thunderclap of theological certainty: "All wisdom cometh from the Lord, and is with him for ever." Every good and perfect gift, James tells us in the New Testament, comes down from the Father of lights. Ben Sira knew this truth centuries before James penned those words, and he builds his entire book upon this foundation.

Notice the structure of the opening hymn. Ben Sira first establishes wisdom's origin — it comes from God and dwells with Him eternally. He then poses a series of rhetorical questions designed to humble us: Who can count the sand, the raindrops, the days of eternity? Who can measure heaven and earth? The implied answer is the same as the answer to God's questions to Job from the whirlwind: no one. No human being can fathom the depths of divine wisdom. It is inexhaustible, unsearchable, beyond all mortal reckoning.

This is precisely where the canonical Scriptures begin their wisdom teaching as well. "The fear of the Lord is the beginning of knowledge," Solomon declares in Proverbs 1:7. "The fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom: and the knowledge of the holy is understanding," he repeats in Proverbs 9:10. The Psalmist agrees: "The fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom: a good understanding have all they that do his commandments" (Psalm 111:10). Ben Sira places himself squarely within this tradition and then expands upon it with a threefold image that is uniquely his own.

He calls the fear of the Lord, first, the root of wisdom. A root is hidden beneath the soil. It is unseen, unimpressive to the eye, but it is the source of all life and fruitfulness in the tree. So the fear of God works invisibly in the heart of the believer, anchoring the soul, drawing up nourishment from the deep places of communion with God. Without this root, all outward displays of wisdom are as cut flowers — lovely for a season but doomed to wither.

Second, he calls it the fulness of wisdom. The fear of the Lord is not merely the starting point from which we graduate to higher things. It is itself the substance of wisdom throughout the believer's life. The Puritan John Flavel taught this same truth when he wrote that "the soul of all improvement in grace lies in the exercise of holy fear." We never outgrow the fear of God. It is not a beginner's grace but the mature believer's constant companion. The more we know of God, the more we stand in awe of Him.

Third — and this is the crowning image — Ben Sira calls the fear of the Lord the crown of wisdom. A crown speaks of glory, honor, and consummation. The fear of God is not only where wisdom begins and what sustains it; it is wisdom's final ornament and highest achievement. To live an entire life in reverential awe of the Almighty — this is the crown that adorns the head of the wise. As Proverbs 16:31 says, "The hoary head is a crown of glory, if it be found in the way of righteousness." The fear of the Lord is that way.

Now let us turn to chapter 2, where Ben Sira addresses the testing of faith. This chapter is addressed to those who would serve the Lord, and its opening counsel is bracing:

"My son, if thou come to serve the Lord, prepare thy soul for temptation. Set thy heart aright, and constantly endure, and make not haste in time of trouble. Cleave unto him, and depart not away, that thou mayest be increased at thy last end. Whatsoever is brought upon thee take cheerfully, and be patient when thou art changed to a low estate. For gold is tried in the fire, and acceptable men in the furnace of adversity."

Here is counsel that every Puritan would have recognized and embraced. The Christian life is not a holiday excursion. It is a warfare, a pilgrimage, a refining fire. Ben Sira tells the aspiring servant of God: prepare yourself. Expect trial. Do not be surprised when affliction comes, as though some strange thing had happened to you. The apostle Peter would later write almost identical words: "Beloved, think it not strange concerning the fiery trial which is to try you, as though some strange thing happened unto you" (1 Peter 4:12).

The image of gold tested in fire is one that appears throughout Scripture. Malachi 3:3 speaks of the Lord sitting as a refiner of silver. Job declared, "When he hath tried me, I shall come forth as gold" (Job 23:10). Peter himself uses the same image: "That the trial of your faith, being much more precious than of gold that perisheth, though it be tried with fire, might be found unto praise and honour and glory at the appearing of Jesus Christ" (1 Peter 1:7). Ben Sira stands firmly in this tradition. Suffering is not a sign of God's displeasure; it is the furnace in which acceptable men and women are refined.

The Puritans understood this deeply. Thomas Watson wrote in All Things for Good that "God's people are like stars, which shine brightest in the darkest night." Samuel Rutherford, writing from his prison in Aberdeen, declared that he had never found Christ so sweet as in suffering. These men had read their Bibles, but they had also lived the truth of Sirach 2 — they knew from experience that the furnace of adversity is where faith is proved genuine.

Ben Sira then gives a series of exhortations to trust: "Believe in him, and he will help thee; order thy way aright, and trust in him. Ye that fear the Lord, wait for his mercy; and go not aside, lest ye fall. Ye that fear the Lord, believe him; and your reward shall not fail. Ye that fear the Lord, hope for good, and for everlasting joy and mercy."

Notice the repetition: "Ye that fear the Lord... ye that fear the Lord... ye that fear the Lord." Three times the phrase rings out like a bell, calling the faithful to trust, to believe, to hope. This is not passive resignation. This is active, muscular faith that looks to God in the midst of trial and refuses to let go. It is the faith of Jacob wrestling at Peniel: "I will not let thee go, except thou bless me" (Genesis 32:26).

Chapter 2 closes with a remarkable statement about the nature of God's mercy: "For as his majesty is, so is his mercy." This is a truth that should make every trembling soul take heart. The God whose majesty fills the heavens and whose power sustains the universe — that same God possesses a mercy proportionate to His infinite greatness. His mercy is not small, not limited, not grudging. It is as vast as His majesty. The Puritan Thomas Goodwin loved to dwell on this theme, writing that "the mercies of God are like the sea — boundless, bottomless, and endless." Ben Sira would have said amen.

The opening chapters of Sirach, then, lay down the foundation for everything that follows. All wisdom begins with God. All wisdom is sustained by the fear of God. And the life of wisdom is proved genuine not in comfort but in the furnace of affliction, where the faithful cling to the Lord and find His mercy as great as His majesty.

--- Application ---

1. Examine the root. Ask yourself honestly: is the fear of the Lord the root of my daily decisions? Not the fear of man, not the fear of failure, not the fear of missing out — but a living, reverent awe of the holy God who sees all, knows all, and judges righteously. Let this fear be the unseen root that nourishes everything you do.

2. Do not despise the furnace. If you are in a season of trial, take heart from Sirach 2. You are not abandoned. You are being refined. Acceptable men and women are proved in the furnace of adversity. Ask God for grace to endure patiently and to come forth as gold.

3. Meditate on the proportion of God's mercy to His majesty. When your sins feel overwhelming and your failures loom large, remember: "As his majesty is, so is his mercy." The God who flung the stars into space loves you with an infinite love. Let that truth anchor your soul.

4. Memorize the threefold image. The fear of the Lord is the root, the fulness, and the crown of wisdom. Carry these three words with you this week. Let them shape your prayers: "Lord, let Thy fear be my root, my sustenance, and my crown."

--- Closing ---

Let us pray. O Lord our God, how majestic is Thy name in all the earth. We confess that our fear of Thee is often shallow and our trust in trial is often weak. Deepen in us, we pray, a holy reverence that is the very root of wisdom. When the furnace of affliction heats around us, grant us grace to cling to Thee and not depart. And let us ever remember that as Thy majesty is, so is Thy mercy — boundless, unfailing, and eternal. Through Jesus Christ our Lord, in whom are hid all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge. Amen.

Go now in the peace of Christ. In our next session, we will turn to Sirach chapter 3 and the sacred duty of honoring father and mother. Until then, may the fear of the Lord be your crown and your delight.
