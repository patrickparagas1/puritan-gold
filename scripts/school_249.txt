PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 9
"The Great Ejection of 1662"
The Reformation -- Lesson 9


Welcome back, students. Today we come to one of the most painful and consequential events in the history of the English church -- a day that has been called Black Bartholomew's Day, or simply the Great Ejection. On August 24, 1662 -- the feast day of St. Bartholomew -- approximately two thousand ministers were forced out of the Church of England because they could not, in good conscience, conform to the requirements of the newly enacted Act of Uniformity. These men gave up their pulpits, their incomes, their homes, and their legal standing in society rather than violate their convictions. Their ejection was a catastrophe for the English church, a triumph of political vindictiveness over Christian charity, and a watershed moment in the history of English-speaking Christianity. Today we will examine the causes of the Great Ejection, the experiences of the ejected ministers, and the profound consequences of this event for the church and the nation.


To understand the Great Ejection, we must go back to the events that preceded it. The death of Oliver Cromwell on September 3, 1658, threw England into political chaos. Cromwell had held the Commonwealth together through the force of his personality, his military prestige, and his genuine commitment to godly government. His son Richard, who succeeded him as Lord Protector, lacked his father's abilities and his authority. Within months, the Protectorate collapsed. The army quarreled with Parliament, Parliament quarreled with itself, and the nation drifted toward anarchy. By early 1660, it was clear that the only solution was the restoration of the monarchy.

On May 29, 1660, King Charles II entered London in triumph, greeted by cheering crowds who were weary of military rule, political instability, and the moral austerity associated (fairly or unfairly) with the Puritan regime. Charles was the son of the executed Charles I, and he had spent his years of exile at the courts of France and the Netherlands, where he had acquired tastes and habits very different from those of the Puritans. He was charming, intelligent, witty, and thoroughly worldly. He was also, in religious matters, personally indifferent -- he had little genuine piety of any kind, though he would convert to Roman Catholicism on his deathbed in 1685.

Before his return, Charles had issued the Declaration of Breda (April 4, 1660), in which he promised, among other things, "liberty to tender consciences" -- that is, religious toleration for those who could not conform to the established church. Many Puritans took this promise at face value and hoped that the Restoration settlement would include a place for them within a broad, comprehensive Church of England. Some Puritan leaders, including Richard Baxter, actively supported the Restoration, believing that a constitutional monarchy under a reasonable king was preferable to the instability of the last two years.

But the promise of toleration was not kept. The Convention Parliament that welcomed Charles home was succeeded in 1661 by the Cavalier Parliament -- so called because it was dominated by Royalist supporters of the crown, many of them young men who had suffered during the Civil War and the Interregnum and who burned with desire for revenge against the Puritans. The Cavalier Parliament saw the Puritans not as fellow Christians with different views on church government and worship, but as dangerous rebels who had torn the nation apart, murdered the king's father, and imposed a tyranny of saints upon the English people. They were determined to ensure that such a thing could never happen again, and their instrument of vengeance was the Act of Uniformity.

The Act of Uniformity, passed by Parliament on May 19, 1662, and taking effect on August 24, 1662 (St. Bartholomew's Day), imposed three requirements on every minister in the Church of England. First, every minister must declare his "unfeigned assent and consent" to everything contained in the newly revised Book of Common Prayer -- every word, every ceremony, every rubric. The 1662 Prayer Book had been deliberately revised to make it more difficult for Puritans to conform. Ceremonies that the Puritans found objectionable -- kneeling at communion, the sign of the cross in baptism, the use of the surplice -- were retained and even emphasized. The ordination service was modified to require episcopal ordination -- ordination by a bishop -- effectively invalidating the ministerial orders of every Puritan who had been ordained by a presbytery rather than a bishop during the Civil War and Interregnum years.

Second, every minister must be ordained or re-ordained by a bishop. This requirement struck at the heart of Puritan ecclesiology. The Puritans believed that ordination by a presbytery was the scriptural model, and that episcopal ordination -- ordination by a bishop in the apostolic succession -- was a human invention with no basis in the New Testament. To accept episcopal re-ordination would be to deny the validity of their existing ministry, to repudiate the Presbyterian understanding of the church that they had taught and practiced, and to submit to a form of church government that they believed was contrary to the Word of God.

Third, every minister must renounce the Solemn League and Covenant. The Solemn League and Covenant, adopted by Parliament and the Church of Scotland in 1643, had bound the two nations together in a common cause of Reformed religion and constitutional government. Many Puritan ministers had sworn this covenant as a sacred oath before God, and they regarded it as binding upon their consciences for life. To renounce it would be, in their eyes, to commit perjury -- to break a solemn vow made to the Almighty.

The deadline was absolute: August 24, 1662. Any minister who had not complied by that date would be automatically deprived of his living -- his parish, his income, his home (which was usually the parsonage attached to the parish), and his legal right to preach. There was no provision for gradual compliance, no room for negotiation, no possibility of conscientious exception. It was conform or be cast out.

The timing of the deadline was itself a calculated cruelty. St. Bartholomew's Day was the traditional date by which tithes for the year were collected. By setting the deadline on August 24, the Act ensured that ministers who refused to conform would forfeit not only their future livings but also the tithes for the current year -- the income they had already earned. They would walk out of their parishes with nothing. Many of the ejected ministers had wives and children depending on them, and they faced the prospect of destitution. The cruelty was deliberate. The framers of the Act wanted to make nonconformity as costly as possible, in the hope that economic pressure would compel ministers to conform even if conscience would not.

The revised Book of Common Prayer of 1662 was itself a major obstacle to Puritan conformity. The Puritans had hoped that the Prayer Book would be substantially revised in a direction more acceptable to Reformed scruples. Instead, the revisions moved in the opposite direction. The new Prayer Book retained all the ceremonies that the Puritans had long opposed and added new elements that were even more objectionable. The baptismal service still included the sign of the cross. The marriage service still included the ring ceremony. The communion service still required kneeling to receive the elements. The ordination service explicitly stated that episcopal ordination was necessary for the ministry, effectively unchurching every minister who had been ordained by a presbytery during the previous two decades. The preface to the new Prayer Book was dismissive of Puritan objections, and the overall tone of the document made clear that the bishops intended no accommodation whatsoever.

Moreover, the Act required every minister to read the revised Prayer Book in its entirety before his congregation on the Sunday before August 24, and to declare publicly his "unfeigned assent and consent" to everything in it. This was not merely a matter of formal subscription -- it was a public, personal declaration of agreement with every word, every rubric, every ceremony in the book. For a Puritan minister who believed that some of these ceremonies were unscriptural and that the episcopal form of ordination was not the New Testament pattern, such a declaration would be a lie. And for men whose entire lives had been devoted to truth-telling and to the authority of conscience under God, lying was simply impossible. As the ejected minister Oliver Heywood wrote in his diary: "It is a sad thing to subscribe what a man does not believe, and a sadder thing to be cast out for not subscribing what a man cannot believe."

The situation was made even more painful by the fact that many of the Puritan ministers who faced ejection had served their parishes faithfully for years or even decades. They had baptized the children, married the young, buried the dead, and preached the Word of God through plague, war, and political upheaval. They knew their parishioners by name. They had prayed at their bedsides in sickness and counseled them in affliction. The bonds between pastor and people were deep and tender, and the prospect of severing them was agonizing for both sides.

In the months leading up to August 24, there were desperate attempts to find a compromise. Richard Baxter, the most prominent Puritan minister in England, negotiated extensively with the bishops, particularly with Edward Reynolds (the Bishop of Norwich, who was himself sympathetic to the Puritan cause) and with the Lord Chancellor, Edward Hyde, Earl of Clarendon. Baxter proposed a modified form of the Prayer Book that would accommodate Puritan scruples -- removing the most objectionable ceremonies, making episcopal ordination optional rather than compulsory, and allowing ministers to exercise their own judgment on matters of indifference. But the bishops, backed by the vengeful Cavalier Parliament, refused to make any significant concessions. The Savoy Conference, held at the Savoy Palace in London in April-July 1661, brought twelve bishops and twelve Puritan ministers together to discuss the revision of the Prayer Book, but it ended in failure. The bishops had no incentive to compromise -- they had the votes in Parliament, and they had the backing of the king. The Puritans had nothing to bargain with except their good faith and their appeals to Christian charity, and these proved insufficient.

As St. Bartholomew's Day approached, each Puritan minister faced an agonizing decision. Compliance meant security, income, continued ministry, and the ability to care for one's family. It also meant violating one's conscience, repudiating one's theological convictions, and accepting a form of church government and worship that one believed to be contrary to the Word of God. Nonconformity meant poverty, homelessness, the loss of one's vocation, social disgrace, and the prospect of persecution. It also meant keeping faith with one's convictions, maintaining integrity before God, and setting an example of principled courage for one's congregation and for future generations.

The great Puritan divine John Owen, who had served as Cromwell's chaplain and as Vice-Chancellor of Oxford, never hesitated. Owen had long since concluded that episcopal government and the ceremonies of the Prayer Book were not merely matters of indifference but were positive hindrances to true worship and true godliness. He was among the first to declare his inability to conform, and he spent the remaining twenty-one years of his life as a Nonconformist minister, writing, preaching when he could, and suffering the legal disabilities that attached to dissent. When asked why he could not conform, Owen replied with characteristic precision: "I would not that any man should judge of my present nonconformity from what I suffered in former days; but this I must say, that I know of no such thing as a human authority in matters of religion and worship, that can bind the consciences of men."

Richard Baxter, too, found himself unable to conform, though his decision was more anguished than Owen's. Baxter was a man of moderate temperament who genuinely desired the unity of the church. He had worked harder than any other Puritan leader to find a middle way. But the Act of Uniformity left no middle way. In the end, Baxter concluded that he could not declare his "unfeigned assent and consent" to a Prayer Book that contained elements he believed to be unscriptural, and he could not accept episcopal re-ordination without implicitly denying the validity of his previous ministry. He later wrote of this period: "I was most frequently and vehemently tempted to think that the whole course of my ministry, and all my sufferings, were nothing but hypocrisy and delusion." But he held firm. He preached his farewell sermon at Kidderminster -- the parish he had served with such extraordinary devotion for nearly twenty years -- and walked out of the Church of England forever.

Some ministers agonized for weeks, swinging back and forth between conformity and nonconformity, unable to resolve the conflict between their desire to continue serving their people and their inability to make the required declarations. Some ministers who initially intended to conform found that when the moment came -- when they stood before their congregations and tried to read the words of assent -- they could not do it. The words stuck in their throats. Their consciences, bound by the Word of God, would not permit them to speak what they did not believe. Others who had initially resolved to refuse found themselves tempted, in the final days, by the thought of their wives and children and the prospect of poverty and persecution. It was a furnace of testing, and every minister who passed through it was marked by the experience for the rest of his life.

The final Sundays before August 24 were heartbreaking occasions across England. Puritan ministers preached their farewell sermons to weeping congregations. These farewell sermons were among the most powerful and moving discourses in the history of English preaching. Many of them were recorded by listeners and published in collections that circulated widely among the Nonconformist community for decades. The themes were remarkably consistent -- the ministers urged their people to remain faithful to the truths they had been taught, to continue in prayer and in the study of the Scriptures, to love one another, and to trust in the sovereign providence of God who works all things together for good. They quoted the words of the apostle Paul in Acts 20:32: "And now, brethren, I commend you to God, and to the word of his grace, which is able to build you up, and to give you an inheritance among all them which are sanctified." They wept, their congregations wept, and then they walked out of their churches for the last time.

Thomas Watson, the minister of St. Stephen's Walbrook in London and one of the most gifted preachers of his generation, delivered a farewell sermon that has been preserved and widely read. Watson was known for his sparkling, epigrammatic style -- his sermons were full of memorable phrases and vivid illustrations that lodged in the memory and illuminated the understanding. In his farewell, Watson urged his people: "Let not religion die in your families. Let the worship of God be kept up, morning and evening. Read the Scriptures. Pray in your closets. God hath many dishes at His feast." Watson would go on to write some of the most beloved devotional works in the Puritan tradition, including "A Body of Divinity," "The Ten Commandments," "The Lord's Prayer," "The Beatitudes," and "All Things for Good" -- works that have comforted and instructed believers for over three centuries. His ejection from St. Stephen's was the church's loss and the literary world's gain.

The stories of individual ejected ministers are deeply moving, and we should pause to hear a few more of them. Philip Henry (1631-1696), the father of the great Bible commentator Matthew Henry, was the minister of Worthenbury in Flintshire, Wales. He was beloved by his congregation, and his pastoral labors had transformed the parish. When the Act of Uniformity forced him from his pulpit, his parishioners wept. Henry himself was calm but resolute. In his diary, he recorded his reason for nonconformity with simple clarity: "I could not satisfy my conscience that the things required were according to the mind of Christ." For the next thirty-four years, until his death in 1696, Philip Henry continued to minister to his people as best he could -- preaching in private homes, in barns, in fields, and wherever a small congregation could gather without attracting the attention of the authorities. His son Matthew later recalled that his father "never repented of his nonconformity" but "often said he should have repented of his conformity."

Thomas Manton (1620-1677), one of the most learned and eloquent preachers in England, was the rector of St. Paul's, Covent Garden, one of the most prestigious parishes in London. Manton had preached before Parliament, had served as one of Cromwell's chaplains, and had helped draft the preface to the Westminster Confession of Faith. He was a moderate, irenic man who sought to avoid extremes. But the Act of Uniformity left him no room for moderation. He preached his farewell sermon to his congregation, resigned his living, and spent the rest of his life in relative obscurity, writing and preaching to small gatherings of Nonconformists. His collected works, published after his death, fill twenty-two volumes and include some of the finest expository preaching in the English language, including a magnificent series of 190 sermons on Psalm 119.

Edmund Calamy (1600-1666), one of the leading Presbyterian ministers in London and a member of the Westminster Assembly, was ejected from his parish of St. Mary Aldermanbury. His grandson, also named Edmund Calamy (1671-1732), later compiled a massive biographical register of the ejected ministers, providing the most important historical source for the events of 1662. The younger Calamy estimated the total number of ejected ministers at approximately two thousand, though some historians place the figure slightly lower (around 1,760 from parish livings) and others slightly higher when university fellows, schoolmasters, and military chaplains are included.

The total number is less important than the quality of the men who were lost. The Great Ejection removed from the Church of England many of its best-educated, most devout, and most effective ministers. The parishes that lost their Puritan ministers were in many cases left with inferior replacements -- conforming clergy who owed their positions to political loyalty rather than spiritual gifts. The standard of preaching in the established church declined sharply after 1662, and the spiritual life of many parishes suffered for generations. As the Puritan Thomas Brooks, himself one of the ejected, wrote with mingled grief and defiance: "God hath many ways to take care of His saints. He that feeds the ravens will not starve His children."

John Flavel (c. 1627-1691), the minister of Dartmouth in Devon, was another of the ejected who continued his ministry in spite of every obstacle. Flavel was a preacher of immense power and warmth, known especially for his ability to reach the hearts of ordinary working people. After his ejection, he preached in secret -- in private homes, in forests, on beaches, and on one famous occasion on a rocky island in the sea, accessible only at low tide, where a congregation gathered beyond the reach of the constables. His works, including "The Mystery of Providence," "Keeping the Heart," and "The Fountain of Life," are treasures of Puritan devotional literature. Flavel's courage in the face of persecution was rooted in a deep trust in God's providence. He wrote: "The providence of God is like Hebrew words -- it can be read only backwards."

Samuel Annesley (1620-1696), the minister of St. Giles, Cripplegate -- one of the largest parishes in London -- was ejected from his living and continued to minister to Nonconformists for the rest of his life. He is remembered not only for his own ministry but as the grandfather of John and Charles Wesley, the founders of Methodism. The spiritual heritage of the ejected ministers flowed through the generations in ways they could not have imagined.

William Bates (1625-1699), known as the "silver-tongued" preacher for his eloquence, was ejected from St. Dunstan's-in-the-West, Fleet Street. Bates was one of the most respected and moderate of the ejected ministers, and his friendship with prominent men on both sides of the divide gave him a continuing influence in English religious life. His funeral sermons for Richard Baxter and Thomas Manton are masterpieces of the genre, combining biographical detail with theological reflection and pastoral encouragement.

Let us also consider the experiences of the congregations who lost their ministers. In many parishes, the departure of a beloved Puritan minister left a spiritual vacuum that was never adequately filled. The replacement clergy, often chosen for their political reliability rather than their spiritual gifts, frequently lacked the preaching ability, the pastoral devotion, and the personal godliness that had characterized the ejected ministers. Some parishes that had been centers of vibrant spiritual life under Puritan leadership sank into spiritual torpor under their new incumbents. Parishioners who had been accustomed to searching, applicatory sermons and diligent pastoral care now received perfunctory homilies and indifferent shepherding. Many of these parishioners quietly drifted away from the established church and sought out the Nonconformist conventicles where the old Puritan faith was still preached with power and conviction.

The consequences of the Great Ejection were far-reaching and enduring. In the immediate term, the ejected ministers and their families suffered severely. They were forbidden to preach, forbidden to come within five miles of any city or borough (under the Five Mile Act of 1665), and subject to fines and imprisonment for violating these restrictions. Many lived in poverty, dependent on the charity of sympathetic laypeople. Some were imprisoned -- John Bunyan, as we have noted, spent twelve years in Bedford jail for unlicensed preaching. Others moved from place to place, staying one step ahead of the informers and constables who enforced the penal laws.

The so-called Clarendon Code -- a series of acts passed by the Cavalier Parliament between 1661 and 1665 -- tightened the noose further. The Corporation Act (1661) required all holders of municipal office to take the sacrament according to the rites of the Church of England, effectively barring Nonconformists from local government. The Act of Uniformity (1662) ejected them from the church. The Conventicle Act (1664) made it illegal for more than five people (beyond the immediate household) to gather for any religious worship not conducted according to the Book of Common Prayer, with penalties of fines, imprisonment, and transportation for repeated offenses. The Five Mile Act (1665) prohibited ejected ministers from coming within five miles of any city, town, or parish where they had previously served, unless they took an oath of nonresistance and declared that it was unlawful to take up arms against the king.

These laws were not uniformly enforced -- much depended on the zeal of local magistrates, the presence or absence of informers, and the political mood of the moment -- but they created a climate of constant anxiety and intermittent persecution that lasted for over twenty-five years. Some periods were worse than others. The years immediately following the Great Plague of 1665 and the Great Fire of London in 1666 saw particularly fierce persecution, as the government blamed Nonconformists for the national calamities and sought scapegoats for its own failures. The so-called Conventicle Act of 1670, a strengthened version of the earlier act, imposed heavier penalties and provided financial incentives for informers -- anyone who reported an illegal religious meeting could claim a share of the fines imposed on the participants. This created a class of professional informers who made their living by hunting down Nonconformist gatherings, and it turned neighbor against neighbor in a way that poisoned social relationships across England.

The Nonconformists responded with extraordinary ingenuity and courage. They developed a sophisticated system of secret worship, using coded signals, lookouts, and rotating meeting places to evade detection. Meetings were held in cellars, attics, barns, private houses, forests, caves, and even on boats anchored in rivers. Ministers preached in disguise, traveled at night, and used back roads to reach their scattered flocks. Congregations posted sentries at the doors and windows of their meeting places, ready to give warning at the approach of constables or informers. The experience of secret worship fostered a deep sense of fellowship and mutual dependence among the Nonconformists -- they were a community forged in the fire of persecution, bound together by shared suffering and shared faith.

The persecution was not limited to England. In Scotland, the Restoration government imposed episcopacy on the Presbyterian Church of Scotland, and hundreds of Scottish ministers were ejected from their parishes in circumstances parallel to the English ejection. The Scottish Covenanters -- those who held firm to the National Covenant of 1638 and the Solemn League and Covenant of 1643 -- endured a persecution even more severe than their English counterparts. The "Killing Time" of the 1680s, when government troops hunted Covenanters across the moors and mountains of southern and western Scotland, produced scenes of heroic suffering and martyrdom that became foundational to Scottish national identity. The stories of the Covenanting martyrs -- men like Richard Cameron, Donald Cargill, James Renwick, and the two Margarets (Margaret Wilson and Margaret McLachlan), who were tied to stakes in the Solway Firth and drowned by the rising tide -- belong to the same chapter of church history as the Great Ejection in England. Both were expressions of the same conviction: that the crown rights of Jesus Christ over His church cannot be surrendered to any earthly power.

Let us also consider the theological legacy of the Great Ejection and its aftermath. The years of persecution forced the Nonconformists to think deeply about the nature of the church, the meaning of Christian liberty, and the proper relationship between church and state. The experience of being cast out of the established church led many Nonconformists to conclude that a national church, controlled by the state and including everyone in the parish regardless of their spiritual condition, was not the biblical model. They developed instead a vision of the church as a voluntary community of believers -- men and women who had experienced genuine conversion and who had freely covenanted together to worship God, to submit to church discipline, and to walk together in the faith. This "gathered church" ecclesiology, which had been present in embryonic form within Puritanism from the beginning, now became the dominant model for Nonconformist churches, and it would eventually shape the ecclesiology of Baptists, Congregationalists, and most evangelical churches in the English-speaking world.

The Nonconformists also developed a robust theology of religious liberty. John Owen, in his later writings, argued that civil magistrates have no authority over the worship and belief of their subjects, and that the attempt to impose religious uniformity by force is contrary to the gospel of Christ. Owen grounded his argument in the nature of faith itself: genuine faith is the work of the Holy Spirit in the heart, and it cannot be compelled by external force. "The authority of Christ alone is the formal reason of our believing," Owen wrote. "We cannot believe anything with divine faith upon any authority but that of God speaking in His Word." This argument -- that religious belief is by nature voluntary and cannot be coerced -- would become a cornerstone of the Anglo-American tradition of religious liberty. The period from 1662 to 1688 is sometimes called the "Great Persecution" of English Nonconformity. Thousands of Nonconformists were fined, hundreds were imprisoned, and some died in prison. John Bunyan's twelve-year imprisonment (1660-1672) is the most famous case, but it was far from unique. Joseph Alleine (1634-1668), the author of "An Alarm to the Unconverted" -- one of the most widely read Puritan evangelistic works -- was imprisoned in Ilchester jail under such harsh conditions that his health was permanently broken, and he died at the age of thirty-four. His wife Theodosia continued his ministry of writing and pastoral care after his death.

Yet the Great Ejection, for all its cruelty, had consequences that the persecutors did not intend and could not have foreseen. The ejection created a permanent body of Nonconformist or Dissenting churches -- Presbyterians, Congregationalists, and Baptists -- that existed outside the established Church of England. These Dissenting churches became centers of intense spiritual life, theological learning, and social activism. Freed from the constraints of the parish system and the formularies of the Prayer Book, they developed their own distinctive traditions of worship, preaching, and church government. They established their own academies for the education of their ministers -- the Dissenting Academies, which in many cases provided a broader, more modern, and more rigorous education than the ancient universities of Oxford and Cambridge, which were restricted to members of the Church of England. Figures like Charles Morton, whose academy at Newington Green educated Daniel Defoe, and Philip Doddridge, whose academy at Northampton trained a generation of evangelical ministers, represent the extraordinary intellectual vitality of the Dissenting tradition.

The Dissenting Academies deserve particular attention, for they represent one of the most remarkable educational achievements of the Nonconformist tradition. When the Act of Uniformity and the Five Mile Act barred Nonconformists from the universities and from teaching in the established schools, the Dissenters responded by creating their own institutions of higher education. These academies, typically run by a single learned minister or by a small faculty, provided instruction not only in theology but in philosophy, science, mathematics, modern languages, history, and practical subjects like commerce and navigation. In many respects, the Dissenting Academies were more progressive and more intellectually stimulating than Oxford and Cambridge, which had become intellectually stagnant under the weight of enforced Anglican conformity and outdated scholastic curricula.

The academy at Newington Green, run by Charles Morton, was one of the most famous. Morton, who later emigrated to Massachusetts and became vice-president of Harvard College, taught his students logic, philosophy, natural science, and modern languages alongside the traditional classical curriculum. Among his students was Daniel Defoe, who would become one of the greatest English novelists and who always spoke with gratitude of the education he had received at Morton's academy. Philip Doddridge's academy at Northampton, which operated from 1729 to 1751, was another celebrated institution. Doddridge, himself a descendant of the ejected ministers (his grandfather had been one of the two thousand), trained a generation of Dissenting ministers in a curriculum that combined evangelical piety with intellectual rigor. His devotional work "The Rise and Progress of Religion in the Soul" (1745) was one of the most influential books of the eighteenth century, and it played a role in the conversion of William Wilberforce, the great evangelical politician who led the campaign to abolish the slave trade.

The academies also contributed to the development of modern science. Joseph Priestley, the discoverer of oxygen and one of the greatest chemists of the eighteenth century, was educated at a Dissenting Academy and later taught at the Warrington Academy. John Dalton, the father of atomic theory, was also a product of the Dissenting educational tradition. The intellectual freedom of the Dissenting Academies -- their willingness to engage with new ideas, to question received wisdom, and to pursue knowledge wherever it led -- was a direct legacy of the Puritan commitment to education and to the life of the mind. The Puritans had always believed that all truth is God's truth, and that the study of God's creation is a form of worship. This conviction, passed down through the generations of Nonconformist educators, made a disproportionate contribution to the scientific, literary, and intellectual life of Britain and the world.

The ejected ministers also produced a remarkable body of literature. Deprived of their pulpits, they turned to the pen. The decades after 1662 saw the publication of some of the greatest works of Puritan theology, devotion, and literature: Bunyan's "Pilgrim's Progress" (1678) and "Holy War" (1682); Owen's "Communion with God" (1657), "The Death of Death in the Death of Christ" (1647), and "On the Mortification of Sin" (1656); Baxter's "Reformed Pastor" (1656) and "Saints' Everlasting Rest" (1650); Thomas Watson's "A Body of Divinity" (1692) and "The Art of Divine Contentment" (1653); Thomas Brooks' "Precious Remedies Against Satan's Devices" (1652); and scores of other works that have nourished the souls of believers for centuries. As the Psalmist wrote: "It is good for me that I have been afflicted; that I might learn thy statutes" (Psalm 119:71). The affliction of the ejected ministers bore fruit in a harvest of spiritual wisdom that continues to bless the church to this day.

The Great Ejection also planted the seeds of religious liberty in England. The spectacle of two thousand godly ministers being cast out of the church for conscience's sake shocked many Englishmen and provoked a growing sentiment in favor of toleration. John Locke, the great philosopher who would become the most influential theorist of religious liberty in the English-speaking world, was deeply influenced by his friendship with Nonconformist ministers and by his observation of the injustice of the penal laws. His "Letter Concerning Toleration" (1689) argued that the civil magistrate has no authority over matters of conscience and that religious persecution is both unjust and ineffective -- arguments that the ejected ministers had been making from their prison cells for a quarter century.

The Toleration Act of 1689, passed in the wake of the Glorious Revolution that brought William and Mary to the throne, finally granted legal recognition to Protestant Nonconformists, allowing them to worship freely (though not to hold public office). It was a limited toleration by modern standards, but it was a genuine breakthrough, and it owed much to the patient suffering and principled witness of the ejected ministers. The broader principle of religious liberty -- the conviction that the state has no right to dictate the worship and belief of its citizens -- would continue to develop through the eighteenth century and would be enshrined in the First Amendment to the United States Constitution, which declares that "Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof."

The spiritual legacy of the ejected ministers is incalculable. The Dissenting tradition that they founded gave rise to the great evangelical revival of the eighteenth century. George Whitefield, the most powerful preacher of the Great Awakening, was deeply influenced by Puritan writers -- he read Baxter, Owen, and Bunyan voraciously. John Wesley, the founder of Methodism, was formed by Puritan devotional literature and sought to recover the Puritan emphasis on personal holiness. Jonathan Edwards, the greatest American theologian, was steeped in Puritan theology and regarded himself as the heir of the Puritan tradition. The missionary movement of the nineteenth century -- the movement that carried the gospel to Africa, Asia, and the Pacific -- was launched largely from Nonconformist churches that traced their origins to the Great Ejection. William Carey, the "father of modern missions," was a Baptist minister in the Dissenting tradition. The stream of spiritual life that flowed from the suffering of the ejected ministers has watered the entire evangelical world.

Let us reflect for a moment on what the ejected ministers gave up and why they gave it up. They gave up security, comfort, income, social standing, and the ability to serve their congregations openly. They received in return poverty, persecution, imprisonment, and social marginalization. Why? Because they believed that the authority of Scripture and the freedom of conscience are more precious than any earthly comfort. They believed, with the apostle Peter, that "we ought to obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29). They believed, with the apostle Paul, that "our light affliction, which is but for a moment, worketh for us a far more exceeding and eternal weight of glory" (2 Corinthians 4:17). They believed, with Moses, that it was better to suffer affliction with the people of God than to enjoy the pleasures of sin for a season, "esteeming the reproach of Christ greater riches than the treasures in Egypt: for he had respect unto the recompence of the reward" (Hebrews 11:25-26).

Let us also reflect, students, on the personal cost of the Great Ejection to the individual ministers and their families. We have spoken in general terms about poverty and persecution, but consider what this meant in concrete, daily reality. A minister who was ejected lost his salary -- typically between fifty and one hundred pounds per year, enough to support a family in modest comfort. He lost his home -- the parsonage that came with the living. He was forbidden to preach, which was not only his livelihood but his calling, the work to which God had set him apart. He was forbidden to teach, so he could not earn a living as a schoolmaster. He was forbidden to come within five miles of his former parish, so he was cut off from the people he loved and had served. If he violated these restrictions, he faced fines that he could not pay and imprisonment in conditions that frequently destroyed men's health. And through all of this, he had to provide for his wife and children -- to put food on the table and a roof over their heads in a society that offered no social safety net and that regarded Nonconformists as social pariahs.

Some ejected ministers were reduced to the most humble occupations to survive. Some worked as farm laborers, some as tutors in private households (often disguising the fact that they were ejected ministers), some depended entirely on the charity of wealthy sympathizers. Their wives took in sewing, washing, and other domestic work to supplement their meager incomes. Their children grew up in poverty, marked by the stigma of dissent. Yet the testimony of the ejected ministers and their families is overwhelmingly one of trust in God's providence and gratitude for His sustaining grace. Philip Henry wrote in his diary: "Though we are cast out from serving God in the way we would, we are not cast out from serving Him in the way we may." And Oliver Heywood recorded: "I bless God for this day's experience of His goodness and mercy, that when all outward comforts fail, He is a present help."

The Great Ejection of 1662 is one of the defining events of the tradition we study in Puritan Gold. It reminds us that faithfulness to Christ often comes at a cost -- that the narrow way is indeed narrow, and that those who walk it must be prepared to sacrifice everything for the sake of conscience and truth. But it also reminds us that God honors those who honor Him, and that the sufferings of His servants are never wasted. The ejected ministers sowed in tears, and their spiritual descendants have reaped in joy. As the Psalmist promised: "They that sow in tears shall reap in joy. He that goeth forth and weepeth, bearing precious seed, shall doubtless come again with rejoicing, bringing his sheaves with him" (Psalm 126:5-6).
