PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Pastoral Encouragement, Day 29
"A Man Given to the Church"
Puritan Counsel on Faithful Church Membership


Good evening, dear brother or sister. Tonight I speak to you not as a lecturer but as a pastor, as a fellow traveler on the road of faith. Wherever you are tonight, whatever burden you carry, you are not alone. God is near, and He has a word for you.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Hebrews 10:24-25:

"And let us consider one another to provoke unto love and to good works: Not forsaking the assembling of ourselves together, as the manner of some is; but exhorting one another: and so much the more, as ye see the day approaching."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


The writer of Hebrews commands believers not merely to attend church but to be actively involved in provoking one another to love and good works. Richard Baxter, though most famous for his counsel to pastors, was equally passionate about the duties of ordinary church members. Baxter believed that the health of a congregation depended not only on the faithfulness of its ministers but on the commitment of its members.

Baxter identifies several duties of the faithful church member. The first and most basic is regular attendance. The command 'not forsaking the assembling of ourselves together' is plain and admits of few exceptions. Baxter writes that the man who is casual about church attendance has a casual view of his own soul's health. Public worship is not a supplement to the Christian life; it is essential to it. In the gathered assembly, God speaks through His Word, the sacraments are administered, prayers are joined, and the body of Christ functions as it was designed to function.

But Baxter goes beyond mere attendance. He calls for active participation. The faithful member does not merely sit in the pew and consume; he contributes. He uses his gifts for the building up of the body. He offers encouragement to the discouraged, correction to the erring, and support to the burdened. Baxter writes that 'every member of the body has a function, and the body suffers when any member fails to perform it.'

Baxter also stresses the duty of supporting the pastor. The minister who serves faithfully is often isolated, exhausted, and under spiritual attack. The faithful member prays for his pastor by name, speaks well of him to others, receives his instruction with humility, and addresses concerns privately and respectfully rather than through gossip or public criticism.

Thomas Watson adds that the faithful church member is one who pursues peace in the congregation. Watson observes that churches are often damaged not by external persecution but by internal conflict: petty grievances, personality clashes, power struggles, and doctrinal disputes handled without grace. Watson counsels the godly man to be a peacemaker, not a peace-breaker: 'Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called the children of God.'

Watson also commends the practice of hospitality within the congregation: welcoming visitors, integrating newcomers, and building genuine friendships across generational and social lines. The church that is characterized by warm, authentic relationships is a powerful testimony to the reality of the gospel.

The Puritan tradition also strongly emphasized accountability within the church. The faithful member submits to the authority of the elders, welcomes loving admonition, and does not resist biblical discipline. This is counter-cultural in an age of radical individualism, but the Puritans understood that without mutual accountability, the church cannot fulfill its purpose of sanctifying its members.

Baxter adds a word about giving. The faithful member gives generously and consistently to support the work of the church. This is not a burden but a privilege. Baxter writes that the man who withholds from the church withholds from God, and the man who gives generously sows into an eternal harvest.

Finally, Baxter commends the virtue of long-term commitment. In his day, as in ours, some believers moved from church to church seeking the perfect congregation. Baxter writes that there is no perfect church, and the man who seeks one will never settle. Instead, commit to a faithful church and invest there for the long term. Put down roots. Build relationships. Serve through the difficult seasons as well as the easy ones.

The challenge for you today is to evaluate your relationship with your church. Are you a consumer or a contributor? Are you committed or casual? Are you a source of peace or a source of friction? The man who gives himself to the church discovers that the church gives back far more than he could imagine.


Let us pause and reflect. Consider these questions in the quiet of your heart.

Question 1: Am I a consumer or a contributor in my local church? What gifts am I using to build up the body?

Question 2: Do I pray for my pastor regularly, support him actively, and address concerns respectfully?

Question 3: Am I committed to my church for the long term, investing through both easy and difficult seasons?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Lord, thank You for the gift of the church. Forgive me for the ways I have been casual about my membership, passive in my participation, and critical in my spirit. Make me a man who gives to the church as Christ gave to her: sacrificially, consistently, and with joy. Amen.

Goodnight, friend. Walk with God tomorrow.
