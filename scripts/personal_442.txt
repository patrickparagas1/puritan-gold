PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Pastoral Encouragement, Day 11
"Raising Children in the Fear of God"
John Flavel — Pastoral Counsel for Fathers


Welcome. Tonight's devotional is personal. It is a word spoken from heart to heart, from one sinner saved by grace to another. Let us lay aside pretense and come before God as we truly are.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Deuteronomy 6:6-9:

"And these words, which I command thee this day, shall be in thine heart: And thou shalt teach them diligently unto thy children, and shalt talk of them when thou sittest in thine house, and when thou walkest by the way, and when thou liest down, and when thou risest up. And thou shalt bind them for a sign upon thine hand, and they shall be as frontlets between thine eyes. And thou shalt write them upon the posts of thy house, and on thy gates."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


Moses' words in Deuteronomy 6 constitute the most comprehensive parenting instruction in all of Scripture. The command is total: God's Word is to pervade every moment of family life — sitting, walking, lying down, rising up. It is to be the atmosphere of the home, not merely an occasional lesson. This is not religious education in the modern sense, a weekly class or a structured curriculum. It is life lived coram Deo — before the face of God — with every ordinary moment becoming an opportunity to point children toward their Creator.

John Flavel, the beloved pastor of Dartmouth, England, was a man who understood suffering and the necessity of faith in the midst of it. He buried three wives and numerous children, and his ministry was conducted under the constant threat of persecution during the ejection of Nonconformist ministers after 1662. Yet Flavel's writings glow with a warmth and tenderness that reveal a heart deeply shaped by the fatherhood of God.

Flavel writes about the duty of fathers with a combination of urgency and tenderness. He argues that the father is the primary spiritual instructor of his children — not the pastor, not the Sunday school teacher, not the school. These may supplement, but the father bears the chief responsibility. Flavel writes that God has placed children in the home, under the father's authority and care, precisely so that they might be instructed in His ways during the most formative years of their lives.

Flavel offers several principles for faithful fathering. First, teach your children the whole counsel of God. Do not reduce Christianity to a list of moral rules — 'do this, don't do that' — but unfold for them the grand narrative of Scripture: creation, fall, redemption, and restoration. Let them see that the Bible is not a rulebook but a love story — the story of God pursuing and rescuing His people at infinite cost to Himself.

Second, Flavel emphasizes the power of example. Children are far more influenced by what they see than by what they hear. The father who preaches patience but practices anger, who talks about faith but lives by worry, who extols the Scriptures but never opens them, is teaching his children a devastating lesson: religion is for Sundays, not for real life. Flavel writes that a father's life is the commentary on his words, and if the commentary contradicts the text, the children will believe the commentary.

Third, Flavel counsels fathers to pray earnestly and specifically for each child. Not generic prayers for 'the children' but specific, individual prayers that reflect knowledge of each child's unique temperament, struggles, and needs. Flavel himself was known for his fervent prayer life, and he believed that a father's prayers are among the most powerful instruments God uses in the conversion and sanctification of children.

Fourth, Flavel addresses the importance of discipline. Not harsh, angry, reactionary discipline, but consistent, loving, purposeful correction that aims at the heart, not merely the behavior. Flavel writes that the father who disciplines his children is imitating the Heavenly Father, who chastens those He loves. But the discipline must always be administered in love, explained clearly, and followed by assurance of continued affection.

Patrick, you have been given a sacred trust in your children. They are not merely mouths to feed and bodies to clothe; they are souls entrusted to your care by God Himself. Are you teaching them diligently? Not merely sending them to church, not merely ensuring they behave, but actively, intentionally, daily shaping their understanding of God, themselves, and the world through the lens of Scripture? This is a calling that requires sacrifice — of time, energy, and comfort — but it is a calling that bears fruit for eternity. The seeds planted in a child's heart today will blossom long after you are gone.


The Puritans kept spiritual journals. Consider writing your answers to these questions.

Question 1: Am I the primary spiritual instructor of my children, or have I delegated that responsibility to the church or school?

Question 2: Does my daily example reinforce or contradict the spiritual truths I want my children to learn?

Question 3: Do I pray specifically and regularly for each of my children by name, bringing their individual needs before God?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Father in heaven, You have entrusted my children to my care, and I tremble at the weight of this responsibility. Give me wisdom to teach them Your Word, faithfulness to model it in my life, and perseverance to pray for them without ceasing. Let my home be a place where Your truth is spoken, Your love is demonstrated, and Your presence is felt. I ask this in the name of Jesus, who welcomed the little children into His arms. Amen.

Go in peace. The God who began a good work in you will bring it to completion.
