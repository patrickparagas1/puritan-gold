PURITAN GOLD -- Study Devotional, Day 8
Series: Romans
"Against Hope, Believed in Hope"
Abraham's faith in the God who raises the dead

---

Good evening, and welcome to our study devotional from Puritan Gold. We are continuing our journey through one of the most important books in all of Scripture. The Puritans treasured careful, verse-by-verse study, and tonight we follow in their footsteps.

We are studying the book of Romans together. Tonight's lesson is titled "Against Hope, Believed in Hope." Each session builds on what came before, so if you have been following along, you will see how tonight's passage connects to the larger argument of the book.

--- Scripture Reading ---

Let us read our passage for tonight. It comes from Romans 4:13-25:

"For the promise, that he should be the heir of the world, was not to Abraham, or to his seed, through the law, but through the righteousness of faith... Therefore it is of faith, that it might be by grace; to the end the promise might be sure to all the seed; not to that only which is of the law, but to that also which is of the faith of Abraham; who is the father of us all... Who against hope believed in hope, that he might become the father of many nations, according to that which was spoken, So shall thy seed be. And being not weak in faith, he considered not his own body now dead, when he was about an hundred years old, neither yet the deadness of Sara's womb: He staggered not at the promise of God through unbelief; but was strong in faith, giving glory to God; And being fully persuaded that, what he had promised, he was able also to perform. And therefore it was imputed to him for righteousness. Now it was not written for his sake alone, that it was imputed to him; But for us also, to whom it shall be imputed, if we believe on him that raised up Jesus our Lord from the dead; Who was delivered for our offences, and was raised again for our justification."

Read that passage again to yourself. Mark any words or phrases that stand out. The Puritans read with a pen in hand, always ready to note what God was showing them.

--- Key Verse ---

Let us focus on one verse that captures the heart of tonight's passage.

"Who against hope believed in hope, that he might become the father of many nations, according to that which was spoken, So shall thy seed be." -- Romans 4:18

This verse is the lens through which we will examine the rest of tonight's passage. Keep it in the front of your mind as we proceed.

--- Exposition ---

Now let us move into the exposition of this passage.

Charles Hodge offers valuable commentary on this passage. The Puritans were expositors of the highest order, and their insights remain remarkably fresh.

Charles Hodge, in his exhaustive commentary on Romans, gave careful attention to the nature of Abraham's faith as described in Romans 4:13-25. Hodge argued that this passage defines saving faith by its object, its character, and its result. The object of Abraham's faith was the promise of God — specifically, the promise that he would be the father of many nations. Abraham did not believe in faith itself, or in his own ability to believe; he believed in God and His word. Hodge insisted that this distinction is critical: saving faith is not confidence in one's own faith but confidence in the God who speaks.

Hodge was deeply moved by the phrase 'against hope believed in hope' and considered it one of the most beautiful descriptions of faith in all of Scripture. Abraham's situation was humanly hopeless — his body was 'now dead' and Sarah's womb was barren. Every natural circumstance screamed impossibility. Yet Abraham believed because the God who made the promise is the God who 'quickeneth the dead, and calleth those things which be not as though they were' (verse 17). Hodge saw in this a pattern for all Christian faith: we believe not because circumstances are favorable but because God is faithful.

Hodge gave particular emphasis to the concluding verses (23-25), arguing that Paul's account of Abraham's justification is not merely historical but paradigmatic. 'Now it was not written for his sake alone' — the principle by which Abraham was justified is the same principle by which all believers are justified. We believe 'on him that raised up Jesus our Lord from the dead.' The resurrection of Christ is the foundation of our justification. Verse 25 is a masterful summary of the atonement: Christ 'was delivered for our offences' — He died because of our sins — 'and was raised again for our justification' — He rose to secure our acquittal. Hodge argued that the resurrection is God's public declaration that the debt has been paid, the sentence has been served, and the justified sinner is free forever.

Romans 4:13-25 continues and completes Paul's argument from Abraham's example, moving from the fact of Abraham's justification by faith to the nature and quality of that faith. This passage is not only theological argumentation but also one of the most inspiring portraits of faith in the entire Bible.

Verse 13 establishes a crucial point: 'For the promise, that he should be the heir of the world, was not to Abraham, or to his seed, through the law, but through the righteousness of faith.' The promise came 430 years before the law (Galatians 3:17). The law cannot be the means of receiving what was promised before the law existed. The promise is received through faith, not through law-keeping.

Verses 14-15 explain why: 'For if they which are of the law be heirs, faith is made void, and the promise made of none effect: Because the law worketh wrath: for where no law is, there is no transgression.' The law and the promise operate on completely different principles. The law says 'do this and live.' The promise says 'believe and receive.' The law brings knowledge of sin and therefore wrath. The promise brings grace and therefore life. If inheritance comes through law-keeping, then the promise is meaningless and faith is pointless.

Verse 16 is one of the most important verses in the chapter: 'Therefore it is of faith, that it might be by grace; to the end the promise might be sure to all the seed.' Why did God make justification depend on faith rather than works? So that it might be by grace. And why by grace? So that the promise might be sure — certain, guaranteed, unshakeable. If salvation depended on works, it would never be sure, because our works are always imperfect. But because it depends on grace received through faith, it is as certain as God Himself. The security of the believer rests not on the strength of their faith but on the faithfulness of the One in whom they believe.

Notice also the breadth: 'to all the seed; not to that only which is of the law, but to that also which is of the faith of Abraham; who is the father of us all.' Abraham is not merely the father of the Jewish nation but the father of all who believe — every Gentile Christian in every nation throughout history claims Abraham as their spiritual ancestor.

Verse 17 introduces the character of the God in whom Abraham believed: He 'quickeneth the dead, and calleth those things which be not as though they were.' This is the God of resurrection and creation. He brings life from death and existence from nothing. This is the foundation of Abraham's faith. He was not believing in natural possibilities but in supernatural power.

Verses 18-21 give us the magnificent portrait of Abraham's faith. 'Who against hope believed in hope' — when all human hope was gone, Abraham found a higher hope in God's promise. He was nearly a hundred years old; his body was 'now dead' as far as reproductive ability was concerned. Sarah's womb was equally past hope. Every visible circumstance proclaimed impossibility. But Abraham 'considered not his own body now dead.' This does not mean he was ignorant of the facts; it means the facts did not determine his faith. He looked beyond what he could see to what God had spoken.

'He staggered not at the promise of God through unbelief; but was strong in faith, giving glory to God.' The word 'staggered' means to waver, to be divided in mind. Abraham was not pulled between doubt and faith; he settled on faith. And notice how faith gives glory to God: by trusting God's word, Abraham declared God to be faithful, powerful, and true. Unbelief dishonors God by implying He cannot or will not keep His word. Faith honors God by taking Him at His word.

Verse 21 gives the core of saving faith: 'And being fully persuaded that, what he had promised, he was able also to perform.' Saving faith is full persuasion — not perfect understanding, not absence of questions, but settled confidence in God's ability and willingness to do what He has said. Abraham did not understand how God would give him a son at a hundred years old. He did not need to understand the how. He trusted the who.

Verses 23-25 make the critical application to all believers. Abraham's story was 'not written for his sake alone' but for us. The same principle of justification by faith applies to everyone who believes 'on him that raised up Jesus our Lord from the dead.' The resurrection of Christ is the object of our faith, just as the promise of a son was the object of Abraham's faith. Both require believing in the God who gives life to the dead.

Verse 25 is a compressed summary of the entire gospel: Christ 'was delivered for our offences, and was raised again for our justification.' His death dealt with our sins; His resurrection secured our acquittal. The resurrection is not an afterthought or a bonus; it is essential to our salvation. If Christ is not raised, our faith is vain and we are still in our sins (1 Corinthians 15:17). But because He is risen, we know that His sacrifice was accepted, the debt was paid, and our justification is complete.

This passage reveals that the faith which justifies is not a vague spiritual feeling or a mere intellectual assent to propositions. It is personal trust in the living God, grounded in His promises, sustained by His power, and directed ultimately toward the crucified and risen Christ. It is the faith of Abraham — believing God against all odds, resting on His word when circumstances contradict it, and giving Him glory by refusing to stagger at His promises.

--- Application ---

Now let us consider: how does this truth apply to our lives? The Puritans never separated doctrine from practice. Every truth they studied was immediately applied.

Abraham's faith is our model. Here are practical ways to cultivate the same kind of faith.

First, feed your faith on God's promises. Abraham's faith had specific content — it was faith in what God had spoken. Your faith needs the same nourishment. Identify three specific promises of God from Scripture and meditate on them daily this week. Write them out. Speak them aloud. When doubt assails, answer it with 'God has said...' Faith grows by hearing, and hearing by the word of God (Romans 10:17).

Second, stop staring at your circumstances and start staring at God. Abraham 'considered not his own body now dead.' He did not deny the reality of his situation, but he refused to let it determine his belief. What impossible situation are you facing? A broken relationship? A financial crisis? A health diagnosis? A besetting sin? Look away from the problem and look to the God who raises the dead. 'Fully persuaded that, what he had promised, he was able also to perform.'

Third, practice faith-filled prayer. Instead of praying anxious, doubt-filled prayers that simply catalog your problems, begin praying God's promises back to Him. 'Lord, You have said You would never leave me or forsake me — I trust You in this lonely season.' 'Lord, You have said all things work together for good — I believe this even though I cannot see how.' This is the kind of prayer that gives God glory.

Fourth, remember the resurrection. Your faith is grounded in the historical, bodily resurrection of Jesus Christ. When everything seems hopeless, remember: God raised His Son from the dead. If He can conquer death, He can handle your problem. Let the empty tomb be the anchor of your hope.

Write down one concrete action you will take this week in response to tonight's study. Do not let this truth remain theoretical. The Puritans measured the success of a sermon not by how much the hearer learned but by how much the hearer changed.

--- Closing ---

God of Abraham, who quickens the dead and calls things that are not as though they were — we believe Your promises. Forgive us for staggering through unbelief, for staring at our circumstances instead of at Your faithfulness. Give us the faith of Abraham, who against hope believed in hope. We trust in the Christ who was delivered for our offences and raised again for our justification. Make us fully persuaded that what You have promised, You are able to perform. Strengthen our faith, not for our glory, but for Yours. Amen.

Until next time, keep studying. The Word of God will never run dry.
