PURITAN GOLD -- Family Devotional, Day 18
"Unmasking Hypocrisy"
William Secker — The Nonsuch Professor


Good evening, beloved family. The Puritans believed that the family altar was the most important altar in all the land. Tonight we gather at that altar together. Welcome to Puritan Gold.

Let us come before the Lord. Gracious God, thank You for the gift of family. Thank You that we can come to You together. We pray that You would teach us from Your Word this evening. Let every word sink deep into our hearts. We love You, Lord. In Jesus' name, Amen.

The Word of God is a lamp to our feet and a light to our path. Tonight that light shines from Matthew 23:27-28:

"Woe unto you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! for ye are like unto whited sepulchres, which indeed appear beautiful outward, but are within full of dead men's bones, and of all uncleanness. Even so ye also outwardly appear righteous unto men, but within ye are full of hypocrisy and iniquity."

That is Matthew 23:27-28. Let those words settle in your heart for a moment before we continue.


Today we confront one of the most serious warnings Jesus ever gave. In Matthew 23, Jesus pronounces seven 'woes' — pronouncements of grief and judgment — against the scribes and Pharisees. These were the religious leaders of His day, and Jesus' words to them are among the harshest in the Gospels.

In verses 27-28, Jesus says, 'Woe unto you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! for ye are like unto whited sepulchres, which indeed appear beautiful outward, but are within full of dead men's bones, and of all uncleanness.'

To understand this picture, you need to know a custom in ancient Israel. Before the Passover festival, all the tombs around Jerusalem were whitewashed with lime. This made them clearly visible so that people would not accidentally walk over them and become ceremonially unclean. The whitewashed tombs looked clean and bright — even beautiful — on the outside. But inside? Dead bones and decay.

Jesus says this is exactly what the Pharisees are like. On the outside, they look righteous, pious, and holy. They wear the right clothes, say the right prayers, keep the right rules. But on the inside — hypocrisy and iniquity. Their hearts are full of pride, greed, jealousy, and self-righteousness.

The word 'hypocrite' comes from the Greek word 'hypokrites,' which means 'actor' — someone who wears a mask on stage and pretends to be someone they are not. The Pharisees were actors playing the role of holy men while their real selves were very different.

Now, we must be careful here. Jesus is not condemning all outward religious practice. Going to church, reading the Bible, praying — these are all good and important. The problem is when the outward practice becomes a mask that hides an unchanged heart. When people use religion to look good while their private lives tell a different story.

Jesus says in Matthew 23:3 about the Pharisees, 'All therefore whatsoever they bid you observe, that observe and do; but do not ye after their works: for they say, and do not.' Their teaching was often correct. But their lives did not match their teaching. They loaded heavy burdens on others but would not lift a finger to help (verse 4). They did their religious works 'to be seen of men' (verse 5).

In Luke 18:9-14, Jesus tells the parable of the Pharisee and the publican (tax collector). The Pharisee stood and prayed, 'God, I thank thee, that I am not as other men.' The tax collector, by contrast, would not even lift his eyes to heaven but beat his breast and said, 'God be merciful to me a sinner.' Jesus says the tax collector went home justified — right with God — rather than the Pharisee. Why? Because God values genuine humility over proud performance.

William Secker had much to say about hypocrisy. He wrote that a hypocrite may have the tongue of an angel and the heart of a devil. He is like a mirror that presents a face that is not its own. Better, Secker said, to be a humble sinner who confesses than a proud professor who pretends.

Secker observed that the hypocrite often fools other people but never fools God. He compared the hypocrite to a gilded nutshell — golden on the outside but empty within. The hypocrite's religion is like a winter sun that shines but gives no warmth.

But Secker's purpose was not merely to condemn hypocrisy. It was to urge genuine Christianity. He wanted his readers to examine themselves honestly. Do I love God in my heart, or am I just performing for an audience? Am I the same person on Monday that I appear to be on Sunday? Do I seek God's approval or man's approval?

This is a vital lesson for families. Our homes are the place where our masks come off. Our children see who we really are — not who we pretend to be at church. If there is a large gap between our public persona and our private behavior, our children will notice, and it will undermine their faith. But if they see genuine, honest, imperfect but sincere faith at home, it will nurture their faith.

For our youngest: God wants us to be real, not pretend. It is better to say, 'God, help me — I am struggling' than to pretend everything is fine. For older children: can you think of times when you acted one way in front of others but felt differently inside? God invites us to be honest with Him. For the whole family: let us be a home where it is safe to be real, where we do not have to wear masks, and where honest struggles are met with grace.


The wisdom of the Puritans remains as fresh today as when it was first written. Listen to this insight.

William Secker, in his work "The Nonsuch Professor in His Meridian Splendour," wrote these words:

"A hypocrite may have the tongue of an angel and the heart of a devil. He is like a looking glass, which presents a face that is not its own. Better to be a humble sinner who confesses than a proud professor who pretends."

What William Secker is reminding us is that the truths we have read tonight in Matthew 23:27-28 are not new. They have been treasured by faithful Christians for centuries. And they are just as true and just as needed in our family today as they were hundreds of years ago. Let us take these words to heart and let them shape the way we live together as a family.


Here are some questions for us to think about and discuss together. There are no wrong answers here, just honest hearts.

Question 1: What is a 'whitewashed tomb'? Why did Jesus compare the Pharisees to one?

Question 2: What does the word 'hypocrite' mean? (An actor who wears a mask!)

Question 3: In the parable of the Pharisee and the tax collector, why did God accept the tax collector's prayer?

Question 4: How can we build a home where everyone feels safe to be honest and real instead of pretending?

Take a few minutes to talk about these questions together. Let everyone share, and listen to one another with patience and love.


Now, here is our memory verse for today. Let us say it together:

"Even so ye also outwardly appear righteous unto men, but within ye are full of hypocrisy and iniquity." -- Matthew 23:28

Try to say it out loud three times right now. Then, before bed tonight, see if you can say it from memory. Tomorrow morning, try again. The more we hide God's Word in our hearts, the more it will guide us when we need it most.


Here is a hands-on way to remember what we have learned tonight:

Make simple masks out of paper plates. On the outside, draw a smiling, perfect face. On the inside, write or draw honest feelings — things like 'sometimes I am scared' or 'sometimes I get angry.' Discuss how God wants us to take off our masks and be real with Him and with each other.


Father, we have heard Your Word tonight, and we are grateful. We confess that we cannot live it out in our own strength. Fill us with Your Spirit. Give us wisdom for tomorrow, patience with one another, and a deep love for You. Keep our family close to You always. In Jesus' name. Amen.

Rest well tonight. God is faithful, and His wisdom is sure.
