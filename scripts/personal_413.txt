PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Guided Puritan Reading, Day 13
"Knowing the Enemy Within"
John Owen — Mortification of Sin, Chapters 7-8


Good evening. We are continuing our journey through one of the treasures of the Puritan library. These are not dusty old words. They are living truths that have the power to transform your walk with God.

Tonight we draw from the work of John Owen, specifically from "The Mortification of Sin." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Hebrews 12:1:

"Wherefore seeing we also are compassed about with so great a cloud of witnesses, let us lay aside every weight, and the sin which doth so easily beset us, and let us run with patience the race that is set before us."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


Owen now turns to one of his most practical directions: before you can mortify a sin, you must know it thoroughly. The writer of Hebrews speaks of 'the sin which doth so easily beset us,' and Owen takes up this language to describe what he calls 'the particular besetting sin' of each believer.

Owen's argument in these chapters is that while every believer struggles with the general corruption of indwelling sin, there is typically one sin or cluster of sins that has a particular strength in a particular person. For some, it is pride. For others, lust. For others, anger, covetousness, laziness, or fear of man. This besetting sin is the one that attacks most frequently, prevails most easily, and does the most damage to the soul.

Owen's first direction is to identify your besetting sin. This requires honest self-examination, and Owen provides searching questions: What sin do you think about most often? What sin do you most quickly excuse or rationalize? What sin recurs despite repeated resolutions to overcome it? What sin brings you the most pleasure in the contemplating of it? What sin are you most reluctant to confess to another person? The answers to these questions will typically reveal the sin that most easily besets you.

Owen warns against self-deception at this stage. The heart, as Jeremiah warns, is 'deceitful above all things, and desperately wicked.' A man may think his besetting sin is one thing when it is actually another. The man who focuses on his struggle with anger may be blind to the deeper sin of pride that fuels it. The man who laments his laziness may not see the fear of failure that underlies it. Owen counsels the use of several aids in self-examination: the mirror of God's Word, the searchlight of the Spirit in prayer, and the honest feedback of trusted friends.

Owen's second direction is to understand the conditions that give your besetting sin its advantage. Every sin has particular circumstances in which it thrives. Owen instructs the believer to study the patterns: When are you most tempted? Where? With whom? In what emotional state? Under what pressures? The man who knows his sin's preferred battlefield can take measures to avoid it or prepare for it.

Owen identifies several conditions that empower besetting sins. Physical conditions: fatigue, hunger, illness, and the effects of substance use all weaken the soul's resistance. Emotional conditions: loneliness, discouragement, anxiety, and boredom create vulnerabilities that sin exploits. Relational conditions: certain people, environments, or social dynamics serve as catalysts for particular temptations. Spiritual conditions: seasons of dryness, doubt, or distance from the means of grace leave the soul exposed.

Owen's third direction is to take the danger of your besetting sin seriously. He warns against the fatal tendency to underestimate a familiar sin. Because your besetting sin is the one you have lived with the longest, it can feel almost normal. You have learned to accommodate it, to manage it, to coexist with it. Owen shatters this complacency: your besetting sin is your greatest spiritual danger. It is the enemy who knows you best, who has studied your defenses most carefully, and who will be the most likely instrument of your downfall if you do not take it seriously.

Owen also warns against comparing your besetting sin to others' sins. 'My sin is not as bad as his' is the reasoning of a man who does not understand the gravity of any sin before a holy God. Owen writes that the man who compares himself to worse sinners has abandoned the only standard that matters: the holiness of God.

The practical application of these chapters is straightforward but demanding: do the hard work of self-knowledge. Name your besetting sin. Study its patterns. Understand its conditions. And refuse to treat it as a manageable companion rather than a mortal enemy.


Reflection is where truth becomes personal. Consider these questions carefully.

Question 1: What is my besetting sin — the one that recurs most frequently and prevails most easily?

Question 2: What conditions — physical, emotional, relational, spiritual — give this sin its greatest advantage in my life?

Question 3: Have I been underestimating this sin because of its familiarity, treating it as a companion rather than an enemy?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Lord, give me the honesty to name my besetting sin and the wisdom to understand its power over me. I do not want to be deceived by familiarity. Show me the conditions that make me vulnerable, and prepare me for the fight. In the name of Christ who conquered all sin, Amen.

May the Lord guard your mind and your heart tonight. Until we meet again.
