PURITAN GOLD -- Study Topic: Female Piety, Part 5 of 10
"The Christian Mother — Raising Children in the Nurture and Admonition of the Lord"
John Angell James — Female Piety

---

Welcome back, dear listener, to Puritan Gold. We are in the heart of our study of Female Piety by John Angell James. Today we come to what James considered perhaps the most sacred of all female callings: the work of the Christian mother. The souls of children are entrusted to a mother's care, and the eternal consequences of that stewardship are beyond calculation. Let us attend with reverence and seriousness.

Let us read from Ephesians 6:4, in the King James Version:

"And, ye fathers, provoke not your children to wrath: but bring them up in the nurture and admonition of the Lord."

And from 2 Timothy 1:5:

"When I call to remembrance the unfeigned faith that is in thee, which dwelt first in thy grandmother Lois, and thy mother Eunice; and I am persuaded that in thee also."

And from Proverbs 22:6:

"Train up a child in the way he should go: and when he is old, he will not depart from it."

--- Teaching ---

John Angell James regarded the Christian mother as one of the most powerful agents of spiritual influence in the world. In his eyes, the mother who raises her children in the fear of the Lord is doing a work comparable to the missionary who carries the gospel to distant lands, the pastor who feeds the flock of God, or the reformer who shapes the conscience of a nation. Indeed, James argues, she may be doing a work greater than all of these, for she is shaping immortal souls at the very point where they are most impressionable, most receptive, and most in need of the tender hand of a godly guide.

James writes with deep feeling about the unique bond between mother and child. The mother is the child's first teacher, first comforter, first example, and first image of the love of God. Before the child can read a single word of Scripture, the mother has been teaching it — by her prayers at the bedside, by her songs in the nursery, by her tears of gratitude at the Lord's Table, by the reverence with which she speaks the name of Christ. These earliest impressions, James insists, are often the most lasting. They sink down into the soil of the young soul and take root there, and even when the storms of adolescence and the temptations of the world sweep over them, they are not easily uprooted.

The Puritans understood this principle profoundly. Cotton Mather, in his Bonifacius, urged parents — and especially mothers — to begin the religious instruction of their children as soon as the children were capable of understanding anything at all. He recommended that mothers take every opportunity to point their children to God: at meals, at bedtime, during walks, during moments of discipline, and during moments of delight. The whole of daily life was to be a catechism, a continuous instruction in the ways of the Lord.

James echoes Mather's counsel. He urges the Christian mother to make the instruction of her children her highest domestic priority. This instruction, he says, must be both doctrinal and practical. The child must be taught the great truths of the Christian faith: the being and attributes of God, the fall of man, the person and work of Christ, the necessity of repentance and faith, the reality of heaven and hell, and the call to holy living. These truths must not be communicated as dry abstractions but as living realities, woven into the texture of everyday experience. When the child sees a beautiful sunset, the mother points to the glory of the Creator. When the child tells a lie, the mother explains the holiness of God and the seriousness of sin. When the child is frightened, the mother speaks of the everlasting arms that are underneath.

But doctrinal instruction alone is not sufficient. James insists that the mother must also model the faith she teaches. Children are remarkably perceptive. They see the difference between a religion that is spoken and a religion that is lived. If a mother talks about prayer but never prays, if she speaks of the Bible but never reads it, if she commends patience but is constantly irritable, the child will draw the conclusion that religion is a matter of words rather than of reality. The most powerful sermon a mother can preach is the sermon of her own life.

James draws particular attention to the mother's role in prayer for her children. He writes movingly about mothers who prayed daily, earnestly, and persistently for the conversion of their children — and who lived to see those prayers answered. He cites the example of Monica, the mother of Augustine, who wept and prayed for her wayward son for years before God answered her supplication in the most dramatic fashion. James reminds his readers that God has promised to hear the prayers of His people, and that the believing mother who brings her children before the throne of grace is engaging the mightiest power in the universe on their behalf.

Richard Baxter, in The Saints' Everlasting Rest, wrote that one of the heaviest responsibilities of Christian parents is the knowledge that they are shepherding souls that will exist forever. The mother who truly grasps this reality will not treat her parenting as a casual or secondary matter. She will approach it as a sacred trust, a stewardship given by God Himself, for which she will one day give an account.

James also addresses the mother's responsibility to discipline her children with wisdom and love. He is no advocate of harshness or tyranny. Drawing upon the language of Ephesians 6, he urges mothers to avoid provoking their children to wrath while maintaining firm, consistent, and loving discipline. The goal of discipline, James writes, is not merely to secure outward obedience but to reach the heart — to help the child understand the nature of sin, the beauty of righteousness, and the grace of God that alone can transform the inner person.

The apostle Paul's mention of Timothy's grandmother Lois and mother Eunice in 2 Timothy 1:5 is a text that James treasures. Here we see the fruit of maternal piety extending across generations. Lois taught Eunice; Eunice taught Timothy; and Timothy became one of the most faithful ministers of the gospel in the history of the church. This is the legacy that James holds before every Christian mother: the possibility that the faith you plant in your child's heart today may bear fruit for centuries to come, in ways you cannot foresee and in places you will never visit.

James closes this section of his work with a tender appeal to mothers who feel inadequate to the task. He reminds them that the work of raising children in the Lord is not accomplished by human strength or wisdom alone. It is a work of grace, sustained by the power of the Holy Spirit, directed by the wisdom of the Word, and undergirded by the promises of a faithful God. The mother who feels her weakness most keenly is often the mother who depends most fully upon God — and she is the mother through whom God does His greatest work.

"I can do all things through Christ which strengtheneth me" (Philippians 4:13). This is the promise that sustains the Christian mother in every season — in the exhaustion of sleepless nights, in the anxiety of watching a wayward child, in the quiet joy of seeing a young soul turn to Christ. Her sufficiency is not in herself. It is in God.

--- Application ---

1. If you are a mother, take time this week to pray individually for each of your children by name. Pray for their salvation, their character, their future callings, and their walk with the Lord. Make this a daily habit if it is not already.

2. Identify one area of your children's spiritual instruction that needs strengthening. Perhaps it is family worship, Scripture memorization, catechism, or simply the regular habit of talking about God's Word throughout the day. Take one concrete step to improve in that area this week.

3. Examine the consistency between what you teach and what you model. Ask the Lord to reveal any areas of hypocrisy or inconsistency, and seek His grace to bring your life into alignment with your words.

4. If your children are grown, do not cease to pray for them. The promise of Proverbs 22:6 extends beyond childhood. Continue to entrust them to the Lord and to be a living example of faith for as long as He gives you breath.

--- Closing ---

Let us pray.

Lord God, Thou who hast entrusted the souls of children to the care of mothers, we bow before Thee with a deep sense of the weight and the privilege of this calling. We confess that we are insufficient for these things in our own strength. We need Thy wisdom, Thy patience, Thy love, and Thy grace. We ask Thee to make us faithful in the instruction of our children, consistent in the example of our lives, and fervent in the prayers of our hearts. May our children rise up and call Thee blessed, and may the faith we plant in their hearts bear fruit unto eternal life. Through Jesus Christ our Lord, who took little children in His arms and blessed them. Amen.

Go in peace, dear listener. The work of a Christian mother is the work of eternity. May God sustain you in it with His unfailing grace. Until next time.
