PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Pastoral Counsel, Day 13
"The Discipline of Bible Reading"
Establishing a Daily Scripture Habit

---

Good evening, friend. Tonight we turn to one of the most foundational disciplines of the Christian life: the daily, deliberate reading of Holy Scripture. The Puritans were, above all else, people of the Book. They read it, memorized it, meditated upon it, preached it, sang it, and shaped every corner of their lives by it. And they would urge us — with great affection and great urgency — to do the same.

--- Scripture ---

Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Psalm 119:97 and 105:

"O how love I thy law! it is my meditation all the day."

"Thy word is a lamp unto my feet, and a light unto my path."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.

--- Teaching ---

It is a strange and troubling thing that Christians who profess to believe the Bible is the very Word of God so often leave it unread. We say it is our authority, our comfort, our guide — and then we let it gather dust on the nightstand. The Puritans recognized this inconsistency and addressed it with both compassion and candor. Thomas Watson wrote that the Scriptures are the breeze of the Spirit by which the soul is carried forward. To neglect the Word is to becalm the soul and leave it drifting upon still waters with no wind to fill the sails.

The first thing to understand about daily Bible reading is that it is a discipline, not a feeling. You do not read Scripture because you always feel like it. You read it because it is the appointed means by which God feeds your soul. Some mornings the Word will burst with light and every verse will seem written for you. Other mornings the words will feel flat and your mind will wander. Read anyway. John Owen taught that faithfulness in the means of grace, even when we feel nothing, is itself an act of faith — and God honors it. The farmer sows the seed whether or not the sky looks promising. The harvest belongs to the Lord.

The second principle is regularity. It is far better to read a small portion of Scripture every day than to read five chapters in a burst of enthusiasm and then nothing for a week. The Puritans understood the power of daily habit. Richard Baxter counseled his people to set aside a fixed time each day — morning was his strong preference — and to guard that time as they would guard an appointment with a king. Because that is exactly what it is: an audience with the King of kings, who speaks to us through His Word.

The third principle is attentiveness. Do not merely read the words; hear them. The Puritans distinguished between reading and meditation. Reading covers ground; meditation digs deep. After you have read a passage, stop and ask: What does this teach me about God? What does it reveal about my own heart? What command must I obey? What promise may I claim? What sin must I confess? Thomas Manton compared meditation to the digestion of food. Reading puts the food in the mouth; meditation chews it, breaks it down, and draws out its nourishment. A man who eats without chewing gains little strength from his meal.

The fourth principle is prayer. The Puritans never separated Bible reading from prayer. They read with the Bible in one hand and their hearts lifted to God in the other. Before you open the Scriptures, ask the Holy Spirit to open your eyes. "Open thou mine eyes, that I may behold wondrous things out of thy law" (Psalm 119:18). As you read, let the text shape your prayers. If you read of God's holiness, worship Him. If you read of His promises, thank Him. If you read of His commands, ask for grace to obey. The Word and prayer are the two lungs of the Christian life. Neither functions well without the other.

Finally, the Puritans would remind us that the goal of Bible reading is not mere knowledge but transformation. We read not to become scholars but to become more like Christ. The Scriptures are not an end in themselves; they are a mirror that shows us the face of our Savior and a window through which His light enters our souls. As William Bridge wrote, the Word of God is the instrument the Spirit uses to conform us to the image of Christ. Without it, we have no pattern and no power.

--- Application ---

First, if you do not have a daily habit of Scripture reading, begin one tonight. Choose a time, choose a place, and choose a starting point. The Gospel of John is a wonderful place to begin if you are unsure. Start small — even ten minutes — and be consistent.

Second, add meditation to your reading. After each passage, pause and ask the five questions: What does this teach about God? About me? What must I obey? What may I claim? What must I confess?

Third, pray the Scriptures. Let the passage you have read become the script of your prayers. This will transform both your Bible reading and your prayer life.

--- Closing ---

O Lord, forgive us for neglecting the treasure Thou hast placed in our hands. Thy Word is a lamp and a light, and we have too often walked in the dark by our own choice. Give us hunger for the Scriptures, discipline to read them daily, and hearts soft enough to be changed by them. In the name of Christ, who is the living Word, Amen.

Goodnight, friend. Open the Book tonight. He is waiting to speak.
