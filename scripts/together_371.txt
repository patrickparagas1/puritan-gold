PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 10
"Thou Art All Fair"
Song of Solomon 4:1-7 — The Bridegroom Beholds His Bride


Welcome, beloved couple. Tonight the bridegroom speaks at length, and his words are an extended, detailed, deeply personal declaration of his bride's beauty. This is one of the most lavish passages of praise in all of Scripture. Let it teach us how to see and celebrate each other.

Song of Solomon 4:1-7:

"Behold, thou art fair, my love; behold, thou art fair; thou hast doves' eyes within thy locks: thy hair is as a flock of goats, that appear from mount Gilead. Thy teeth are like a flock of sheep that are even shorn, which came up from the washing; whereof every one bear twins, and none is barren among them. Thy lips are like a thread of scarlet, and thy speech is comely: thy temples are like a piece of a pomegranate within thy locks. Thy neck is like the tower of David builded for an armoury, whereon there hang a thousand bucklers, all shields of mighty men. Thy two breasts are like two young roes that are twins, which feed among the lilies. Until the day break, and the shadows flee away, I will get me to the mountain of myrrh, and to the hill of frankincense. Thou art all fair, my love; there is no spot in thee."


"Behold, thou art fair, my love; behold, thou art fair." The bridegroom begins and ends with the same declaration: You are beautiful. And between these bookends, he catalogues her beauty in exquisite, patient detail. He notices her eyes, her hair, her teeth, her lips, her speech, her temples, her neck, her breasts. He does not rush past her. He does not give a generic compliment. He pays attention. He sees her — really sees her — and tells her what he sees.

The Puritans believed that this kind of attentive appreciation is a duty and a delight within marriage. William Gouge wrote that a husband should "study his wife" as a scholar studies a text — with careful attention, growing understanding, and increasing wonder. The same applies to a wife studying her husband. When was the last time you looked at your spouse — really looked — and told them specifically what you find beautiful?

The imagery here is drawn from the natural world of ancient Israel. Her hair is like a flock of goats cascading down Mount Gilead — flowing, abundant, alive. Her teeth are like freshly shorn sheep — even, white, and perfect. Her lips are scarlet thread — vivid and inviting. Her temples are like pomegranate halves — flushed with life and color. Her neck is like the tower of David — strong, dignified, adorned with the shields of warriors. These are not modern compliments, but they are deeply personal. The bridegroom draws his imagery from the landscape they share. He uses the world they both know to describe the world he sees in her.

Richard Sibbes saw in this passage a lesson about the language of love. Each marriage, he wrote, develops its own vocabulary — private jokes, pet names, shared references that no one else understands. This private language is one of the treasures of marriage. It creates an intimacy that excludes the rest of the world and says, "This is ours. This belongs to us alone." Cultivate your private language. It is the mortar between the bricks of your relationship.

"Thy speech is comely." This is a detail that modern readers sometimes overlook, but the Puritans did not. The bridegroom praises not only her physical beauty but also her speech — her words, her conversation, her way of expressing herself. Thomas Gataker wrote that a spouse whose conversation is pleasant, whose words are kind, and whose speech builds up rather than tears down is a treasure beyond price. Beauty of speech outlasts beauty of face. When the years have changed everything else, the way you speak to each other will remain.

"Thy neck is like the tower of David builded for an armoury." This is not a compliment about size but about strength and dignity. The tower of David was a fortress, a place of defense, hung with the shields of mighty warriors. The bride carries herself with a dignity that speaks of inner strength. The Puritans prized this quality in women and in men — what they called "spiritual carriage," a way of moving through the world that reflects the dignity of being made in God's image and redeemed by Christ's blood.

"Thou art all fair, my love; there is no spot in thee." This is the climactic declaration. Not "thou art mostly fair" or "thou art fair despite a few flaws." All fair. No spot. Is this realistic? In one sense, no. Every human being has flaws. But in another sense — the covenantal sense — it is profoundly true. When a husband looks at his wife through the eyes of covenant love, he chooses to see her as God sees the church: washed, sanctified, presented without spot or wrinkle. This does not mean ignoring real problems. It means that the fundamental posture of a spouse's heart toward their partner is one of acceptance, delight, and unconditional welcome.

Baxter wrote that the spouse who is always cataloguing their partner's faults has forgotten the nature of covenant love. Love does not keep a record of wrongs. Love covers a multitude of sins. Love says, "Thou art all fair, my love; there is no spot in thee."

Tonight, practice the art of beholding. Look at your spouse and name what you see — not flaws, not frustrations, but beauty. Specific, detailed, personal beauty.


Turn to each other and consider these questions.

Question 1: The bridegroom praises specific details about his bride. Take turns naming three specific things you find beautiful about each other — not generic compliments but particular, personal observations.

Question 2: The bridegroom praises her speech as "comely." How would you describe the way your spouse speaks? What about their words or voice do you find attractive?

Question 3: "Thou art all fair; there is no spot in thee." What would change in your marriage if this became your default posture toward each other — choosing to see beauty rather than cataloguing faults?


Tonight's prayer focus: Pray that God would give you eyes to behold each other's beauty — physical, spiritual, and relational. Ask Him to replace the critical eye with the admiring eye, and to fill your mouths with words of specific, genuine praise for one another.


Thou art all fair, my love. There is no spot in thee. Let these words be your gift to each other tonight. Goodnight, beloved couple. Rest in the knowledge that you are seen, known, and adored.
