PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Guided Puritan Reading, Day 25
"Final Directions: Sustained Warfare"
John Owen — Mortification of Sin, Chapter 14


Good evening. There is a reason the Puritans read so many books alongside their Bibles. They knew that God raises up teachers in every generation to illuminate His Word. Tonight we sit with one of those teachers and listen carefully.

Tonight we draw from the work of John Owen, specifically from "The Mortification of Sin." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is 1 Peter 5:8-9:

"Be sober, be vigilant; because your adversary the devil, as a roaring lion, walketh about, seeking whom he may devour: Whom resist stedfast in the faith, knowing that the same afflictions are accomplished in your brethren that are in the world."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


Owen brings his treatise to a conclusion with final directions that emphasize the sustained, lifelong nature of the mortification of sin. There is no retirement from this battle. There is no furlough. The fight continues until the day you see Christ face to face.

Owen's ninth direction (continuing from his earlier numbering): Watch against the occasions of sin with particular diligence. Owen distinguishes between occasions and temptations. An occasion is an external circumstance that provides an opportunity for sin. A temptation is the internal response to that occasion. While we cannot always avoid occasions (we live in a fallen world), we can avoid placing ourselves unnecessarily in their path.

Owen provides specific counsel. Know your weaknesses. If you are prone to covetousness, do not linger over advertisements and catalogs. If you are prone to gossip, do not spend time with people who feed that habit. If you are prone to anger, take steps to address the circumstances that trigger it. Owen writes that the wise man does not test his armor by standing in front of the cannon; he takes cover and waits for the right moment to advance.

Owen's tenth direction: When conscience is troubled by sin, act immediately. Do not delay repentance. Do not wait for a more convenient time. Do not tell yourself you will deal with it tomorrow. Owen writes that delay is one of sin's most effective strategies: it allows the initial conviction to fade, the heart to grow harder, and the will to grow weaker. The moment the Spirit convicts you of sin, respond. Confess it. Renounce it. Turn to Christ.

Owen's eleventh direction: Set a watch over your whole life. This is the direction of comprehensive vigilance. Owen writes that many believers are diligent about mortifying one sin while neglecting others. A man may be fighting lust with great intensity while allowing pride to grow unchecked. Or he may be combating anger while neglecting laziness. Owen insists that mortification must be comprehensive. Every area of life, every thought, every affection, every habit must be brought under the scrutiny of the Spirit.

Owen uses the image of a garden. The gardener who weeds one section while allowing weeds to grow in another will eventually find his entire garden overrun. The weeds from the neglected section will spread to the tended section. So it is with sin: the unmortified sin in one area of your life will eventually corrupt areas that you thought were healthy.

Owen's twelfth and final direction: Cultivate and cherish the graces of the Spirit. Mortification is not only a negative work (killing sin) but a positive one (growing in grace). The soul that is full of love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control has less room for sin. Owen writes that 'the best defense against the power of sin is the cultivation of the power of grace.'

Owen commends specific practices for cultivating grace: regular meditation on Scripture, extended times of prayer, participation in the Lord's Supper, fellowship with mature believers, and the diligent use of all the means of grace that God has appointed. He also commends the practice of thanksgiving: the grateful soul is a fortified soul. The man who counts his blessings finds his heart drawn toward God and away from the empty promises of sin.

As we conclude our journey through Owen's 'Mortification of Sin,' let us hear his final word: 'Mortification is the constant duty of believers, to be carried on all their days. The vigor and comfort and power of our spiritual life depend on it. If ye through the Spirit do mortify the deeds of the body, ye shall live.'

You are not called to fight this battle alone. The Spirit who raised Christ from the dead dwells in you. The same power that conquered death is available to you for the conquest of sin. Go forward in this confidence: the One who began a good work in you will be faithful to complete it.


The Puritans kept spiritual journals. Consider writing your answers to these questions.

Question 1: Am I watching over my whole life with comprehensive vigilance, or am I fighting sin in one area while neglecting others?

Question 2: When the Spirit convicts me of sin, do I respond immediately, or do I delay and allow the conviction to fade?

Question 3: What specific grace of the Spirit do I most need to cultivate as a positive weapon against sin?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Lord, as I conclude this journey through Owen's treatise, I commit to sustained, lifelong warfare against sin. Forgive my neglect. Sharpen my vigilance. Fill me with the graces of Your Spirit. I go forward not in my own strength but in the power of the One who raised Christ from the dead. Amen.

Go in peace. The God who began a good work in you will bring it to completion.
