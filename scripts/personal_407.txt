PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Guided Puritan Reading, Day 7
"The Spirit Alone Mortifies Sin"
John Owen — Mortification of Sin, Chapters 3-4

---

Welcome. Tonight we read together. Not quickly, not casually, but with the kind of attentive, worshipful reading that the Puritans practiced every day. Open your heart as we open this text.

Tonight we draw from the work of John Owen, specifically from "The Mortification of Sin." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.

--- Scripture ---

Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Galatians 5:16-17:

"This I say then, Walk in the Spirit, and ye shall not fulfil the lust of the flesh. For the flesh lusteth against the Spirit, and the Spirit against the flesh: and these are contrary the one to the other: so that ye cannot do the things that ye would."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.

--- Guided Reading ---

Owen now turns to what he considers the most critical point in the entire treatise: the role of the Holy Spirit in mortification. His thesis is uncompromising: only the Spirit of God can mortify sin. All other attempts, no matter how sincere or strenuous, will fail.

Owen begins by addressing why human effort alone is insufficient. He identifies several methods of fighting sin that people employ apart from the Spirit, and he shows why each of them falls short.

The first inadequate method is moral resolution. A man discovers a sin in his life and resolves to stop. He grits his teeth, clenches his fists, and determines by sheer willpower to overcome. Owen does not deny that willpower can produce temporary results. A man can, for a time, suppress a particular sin through force of will. But Owen argues that this is not mortification; it is mere suppression. The sin is not killed; it is caged. And a caged beast is still alive. The desire remains, the affection remains, the root remains. The man has changed his behavior without changing his heart, and the heart will eventually reassert itself.

The second inadequate method is external discipline. This includes things like accountability partnerships, internet filters, dietary restrictions, and rigid schedules. Again, Owen does not despise these tools; they have a place. But he insists that they are instruments, not sources. An accountability partner can help you fight sin, but he cannot give you a new heart. An internet filter can block temptation, but it cannot purify desire. These external measures deal with symptoms while leaving the disease untouched.

The third inadequate method is the most dangerous: religious activity as a substitute for mortification. Owen saw this in the monasteries and in the elaborate penitential systems of Roman Catholicism, but he also saw it in Protestant churches. A man who is troubled by sin throws himself into religious busyness: more church attendance, more committee work, more Bible reading, more fasting. But if these activities are undertaken as a way of avoiding the deep heart-work of mortification, they become a cover for sin rather than a cure. Owen writes that 'mortification from a self-strength, carried on by ways of self-invention, to the end of a self-righteousness, is the soul and substance of all false religion in the world.'

Having demolished false methods, Owen turns to the true method: the work of the Spirit. He grounds his argument in Romans 8:13: 'If ye through the Spirit do mortify the deeds of the body.' The Spirit mortifies sin in several ways.

First, the Spirit reveals sin. The natural man is blind to the true nature of his sin. He minimizes it, rationalizes it, and compares himself favorably to others. The Spirit opens the eyes of the heart to see sin as God sees it: offensive, dangerous, and worthy of death. This sight of sin is not mere intellectual awareness; it is a spiritual conviction that reaches the depths of the soul.

Second, the Spirit creates new desires. Mortification is not merely the removal of sinful desires but the replacement of them with holy desires. The Spirit gives the believer a love for God, a delight in holiness, a hunger for righteousness, and a taste for spiritual things that competes with and gradually overcomes the desires of the flesh.

Third, the Spirit empowers the will. The believer's will, though renewed, is still weak. The Spirit strengthens the will to choose what is right even when the flesh pulls toward what is wrong. This is not an irresistible force that removes all struggle; it is a sufficient power that enables victory in the midst of struggle.

Fourth, the Spirit applies the cross. Owen is deeply Christological in his understanding of sanctification. The Spirit mortifies sin by bringing the believer again and again to the cross of Christ, where the power of sin was broken. The more we behold Christ crucified, the more our affections are weaned from the world and drawn to Him. Owen writes that 'there is no death of sin without the death of Christ.'

The practical implication is this: if you want to kill sin, you must go to the Spirit. You must pray for His illumination, His power, His comfort, and His sanctifying work. You must use the means of grace — Word, prayer, sacraments, fellowship — because it is through these means that the Spirit ordinarily works. But you must never trust the means themselves; you must trust the Spirit who works through them.

--- Reflection ---

Now let us turn the lens inward. What is God saying to you through tonight's study?

Question 1: Which of Owen's 'inadequate methods' do I most tend to rely upon — moral resolution, external discipline, or religious busyness?

Question 2: Am I daily asking the Holy Spirit to do in me what I cannot do in myself?

Question 3: How does contemplating the cross of Christ weaken the power of sin in my life?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.

--- Closing Prayer ---

Holy Spirit, I confess that I have tried to fight sin in my own strength and with my own methods. I need Your illuminating, empowering, sanctifying work. Open my eyes to see my sin clearly. Create in me new desires. Strengthen my will. Bring me again and again to the cross. Amen.

Press on, dear saint. The race is worth running, and the prize is Christ Himself.
