"The Thundering Scot — John Knox"
The Reformation — Lesson 5

---

Welcome back, students. Today we travel to Scotland to meet a man whose fiery preaching and iron will brought the Reformation to an entire nation — John Knox, the founder of Scottish Presbyterianism and one of the most remarkable figures of the sixteenth century. In our previous lessons, we have studied the great continental Reformers — Luther, Calvin, and Zwingli — and we have followed the story of William Tyndale, the father of the English Bible. Today we cross the border into Scotland, where the Reformation took a form all its own — fiercer, more democratic, more uncompromising — shaped by the character of the man who led it.

Knox was not a polished theologian like Calvin. He was not a university professor like Luther. He was a prophet — a man consumed by the fire of God's Word, who feared the face of the Almighty more than the face of any earthly monarch. He stood before queens and told them they were wrong. He stood before parliaments and told them their duty. He stood before nations and called them to repentance. And by the grace of God, he prevailed. The Scotland that Knox left behind at his death in 1572 was a radically different nation from the Scotland into which he had been born — and the Presbyterian church he established would shape not only Scotland but the entire English-speaking world for centuries to come.

--- Lesson ---

Part One: Scotland Before Knox — A Nation in Darkness

To understand the magnitude of Knox's achievement, we must first understand the Scotland of his birth. Scotland in the early sixteenth century was a small, poor, and politically unstable kingdom on the northern fringe of Europe. Its population numbered perhaps half a million — a fraction of England's. Its economy was largely agricultural. Its politics were dominated by rival noble families — the Hamiltons, the Douglases, the Campbells — who competed for power, often violently, and who regarded the weak Scottish monarchy as a prize to be seized rather than an institution to be served.

The church in Scotland was, by nearly all accounts, in a deplorable condition. The medieval Catholic church controlled vast wealth — estimates suggest that the church owned roughly half of all the land in Scotland — but much of that wealth was concentrated in the hands of a small number of senior churchmen who treated their positions as sources of income rather than as spiritual responsibilities. Bishops and abbots lived in luxury while the parish clergy — those who actually served the people — were often poorly educated, poorly paid, and morally lax.

The Archbishop of St Andrews, the highest ecclesiastical office in Scotland, was frequently held by men whose primary qualifications were political connections rather than spiritual fitness. Cardinal David Beaton, who held the office from 1539 to 1546, was a worldly and ruthless politician who used his ecclesiastical power to advance his own ambitions and to persecute those who challenged the authority of Rome. The Scottish Parliament itself acknowledged the problems within the church. In 1549 — just three years after Beaton's death — a provincial council of the Scottish church admitted that "the corruption of morals and the profane lewdness of life in churchmen of almost all ranks" was a major cause of the contempt in which the church was held.

The spiritual condition of the Scottish people reflected the failures of their clergy. The mass was celebrated in Latin — a language the people did not understand. The Bible was largely unavailable in the vernacular. Superstition was widespread: the veneration of saints' relics, pilgrimages to holy sites, the purchase of indulgences, and the belief in the intercessory power of the Virgin Mary and the saints were deeply embedded in popular piety. The gospel of salvation by grace alone through faith alone in Christ alone — the heart of the Reformation message — was virtually unknown.

Yet even in this darkness, there were glimmers of light. The Lollard movement — the followers of John Wycliffe — had reached Scotland in the fifteenth century, and small communities of believers who questioned the teachings of Rome survived in scattered pockets. Lutheran ideas began filtering into Scotland through trade connections with the Continent, particularly through the east coast ports of Edinburgh, Dundee, and St Andrews. And a handful of courageous preachers — Patrick Hamilton, George Wishart, and others — would pay with their lives for proclaiming the truths that Knox would later carry to victory.

The words of our Lord apply with full force to this period in Scottish history: "The people which sat in darkness saw great light; and to them which sat in the region and shadow of death light is sprung up" (Matthew 4:16). The light was coming — and the man who would carry it was John Knox.

Part Two: Knox's Early Life and Conversion

John Knox was born around the year 1514 in or near the town of Haddington, in East Lothian, on the eastern coast of Scotland, south of Edinburgh. His family was of modest standing — respectable but not wealthy. His father, William Knox, may have been a farmer or small landowner. Little is known of his mother, and nothing is known of his childhood, but his intellectual abilities were recognized early, and he was sent to study at the University of St Andrews, Scotland's oldest and most prestigious seat of learning, founded in 1413.

At St Andrews, Knox studied under the distinguished scholar John Major (or Mair), a theologian and philosopher of considerable European reputation. Major was a moderate Catholic reformer who advocated for the reform of abuses within the church without breaking from Rome. His political thought, however, was remarkably progressive for the era: Major taught that political authority derives from the people, not from the monarch alone, and that a tyrannical ruler may lawfully be resisted and even deposed. These ideas — radical in the sixteenth century — would profoundly influence Knox's own political theology and his willingness to challenge royal authority in the name of God's Word.

Knox was ordained as a Catholic priest, probably in the late 1530s, and served as a papal notary — a legal functionary of the church. The precise date and circumstances of his conversion to Protestant convictions are not known with certainty, but the process appears to have been gradual, influenced by several factors: the writings of the early church fathers, particularly Augustine and Jerome; the growing availability of Lutheran literature in Scotland; and, above all, the personal influence of George Wishart.

George Wishart was a Scottish scholar who had studied in Switzerland and Germany, where he had absorbed the Reformed theology of the Continental Reformation. Returning to Scotland in 1543, Wishart embarked on a preaching tour that carried him across the lowlands, proclaiming the doctrine of justification by faith alone with a power and clarity that drew large crowds. Knox became Wishart's devoted disciple and bodyguard — literally carrying a two-handed sword to protect the preacher from arrest. Knox later described Wishart as a man of "such graces as before him were never heard within this realm" — a man whose preaching, life, and death bore witness to the truth of the gospel.

Wishart's ministry was brief and his end was violent. In January 1546, he was arrested on the orders of Cardinal Beaton, who saw the Protestant movement as a threat to both ecclesiastical and political stability. On March 1, 1546, George Wishart was burned at the stake in front of the castle of St Andrews, while Cardinal Beaton reportedly watched from a window, reclining on cushions. Wishart's last words were a prophecy: he predicted that God would send someone to avenge his death and continue the work of reformation. The prophecy would prove to be devastatingly accurate.

Wishart's martyrdom was the crucible in which Knox's calling was forged. The sight of a godly man condemned to death for preaching the truth — while a corrupt and worldly cardinal looked on from a position of luxury and power — crystallized Knox's conviction that the institutional church had become the enemy of the gospel and that radical reform was not merely desirable but divinely commanded. As the apostle Peter declared before the Sanhedrin: "We ought to obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29). Knox took those words as his life's motto.

Part Three: The Castle of St Andrews and the Galley Slaves

Within three months of Wishart's execution, Cardinal Beaton was dead. On May 29, 1546, a group of Protestant sympathizers — some motivated by religious conviction, others by personal grievances — broke into St Andrews Castle and assassinated Beaton in his bedchamber. They then fortified the castle and held it as a Protestant stronghold.

Knox, who had been serving as a tutor to the sons of several Protestant families, joined the castle garrison in April 1547. It was here that the pivotal moment of his early ministry occurred. The castle's Protestant chaplain, John Rough, publicly called upon Knox to take up the preaching ministry, declaring that Knox's gifts and calling were evident to all. Knox was overwhelmed — he wept publicly and withdrew in anguish, struggling with the weight of the call. But he accepted, and his first sermon — preached in the parish church of St Andrews — was a thunderbolt. Knox attacked the papacy as the Antichrist foretold in Scripture, declared that the Roman church had departed from the apostolic faith, and called upon his hearers to repent and believe the gospel. His listeners were stunned. Even those who disagreed with his conclusions acknowledged the power of his preaching. One observer noted that while other preachers "lopped the branches of the papistry," Knox "struck at the root."

The garrison of St Andrews Castle held out for over a year, but in July 1547, a French fleet arrived in support of the Scottish Catholic establishment and bombarded the castle into submission. Knox and the other defenders were captured. Rather than being tried and executed — the usual fate of rebels and heretics — they were condemned to serve as galley slaves on French warships. For the next nineteen months, Knox labored at the oar of a French galley, chained to his bench, exposed to the elements, beaten by his overseers, and subjected to conditions of unimaginable brutality.

The experience of the galleys was a furnace of suffering that forged Knox's character into steel. His health was permanently damaged — he would suffer from kidney ailments and chronic pain for the rest of his life. But his faith was unbroken. John Foxe records that when a painted image of the Virgin Mary was passed among the galley slaves for them to kiss, Knox seized the image, declared, "Let our Lady now save herself; she is light enough — let her learn to swim," and threw it into the sea. The anecdote — whether strictly historical or partially legendary — captures the essence of Knox's character: defiant, fearless, and absolutely unwilling to compromise with what he regarded as idolatry.

Knox later testified that throughout his months in the galleys, he never lost confidence that God would eventually bring him back to preach in Scotland. The words of the psalmist sustained him: "I had fainted, unless I had believed to see the goodness of the Lord in the land of the living. Wait on the Lord: be of good courage, and he shall strengthen thine heart: wait, I say, on the Lord" (Psalm 27:13-14). Knox waited, and God delivered.

Part Four: The Years of Exile — England, Frankfurt, and Geneva

Knox was released from the galleys in early 1549, probably through the diplomatic intervention of the English government under Edward VI, the young Protestant king of England. Knox made his way to England, where he spent the next five years as a licensed preacher and chaplain. He was stationed at Berwick-upon-Tweed and later at Newcastle, where his powerful preaching drew large congregations and attracted the attention of the English government. He was offered the bishopric of Rochester — a position of considerable prestige and income — but refused it, partly because he had already developed serious reservations about the episcopal system of church government and partly because he sensed that the English Reformation under Edward VI was dangerously incomplete.

Knox's years in England were formative but also deeply frustrating. He recognized that the English Reformation, as conducted under Henry VIII and continued under Edward VI, was fundamentally a political reformation — driven more by the crown's desire for independence from Rome than by a genuine commitment to Reformed theology. The Church of England retained many elements of Catholic worship — vestments, altars, the kneeling reception of communion — that Knox regarded as unbiblical compromises. The second Book of Common Prayer, published in 1552, included a rubric allowing kneeling at communion — a practice Knox had vigorously opposed. A "Black Rubric" was inserted at the last minute, declaring that kneeling did not imply adoration of the elements, largely in response to Knox's objections. This controversy foreshadowed the later Puritan critique of the Church of England and established Knox as a precursor of the Puritan movement.

In July 1553, the young King Edward VI died, and the Catholic Mary Tudor ascended the English throne. The brief Protestant experiment in England was over. Mary began a systematic campaign to restore Catholicism, and Protestant ministers faced a stark choice: conform, flee, or die. Knox chose to flee. He left England and made his way to the Continent, beginning a period of exile that would last nearly six years and would prove to be the most intellectually formative period of his life.

Knox spent time first in Dieppe, France, and then traveled to Geneva, where John Calvin had established what Knox would later describe as "the most perfect school of Christ that ever was in the earth since the days of the Apostles." In Geneva, Knox witnessed firsthand the Reformed model of church and society — a community governed by the Word of God, with a church structured not around bishops and archbishops but around pastors, teachers, elders, and deacons. He attended Calvin's lectures, absorbed Reformed theology at its source, and developed the vision of church government that he would later implement in Scotland.

From Geneva, Knox traveled to Frankfurt am Main, where he served as pastor to a congregation of English Protestant refugees. The Frankfurt congregation became embroiled in a bitter dispute over worship — specifically, over whether to use the Book of Common Prayer or to adopt a simpler, more Reformed order of worship. Knox advocated for the simpler form, arguing that the Prayer Book retained too many Catholic elements. His opponents, led by Richard Cox, accused Knox of disloyalty to the English church and had him expelled from Frankfurt. The "Troubles at Frankfurt," as the episode became known, was a rehearsal for the later conflicts between Puritans and conformists in the Church of England.

Knox returned to Geneva and immersed himself in Calvin's Reformed theology. He served as pastor to the English-speaking congregation in the city from 1556 to 1559. During this period, he was deeply involved in the production of the Geneva Bible — the first English Bible with verse numbers and extensive marginal notes, which would become the household Bible of Elizabethan England and the Bible of the Puritans, the Pilgrims, and Shakespeare. Knox also contributed to the development of the Geneva liturgy — a Reformed order of worship that would serve as the model for Presbyterian worship in Scotland.

It was also during the Geneva period that Knox produced his most famous — and most controversial — literary work: "The First Blast of the Trumpet Against the Monstrous Regiment of Women," published in 1558. The word "regiment" in Knox's usage meant "rule" or "government," and the treatise argued, on scriptural and natural-law grounds, that the rule of women over men was contrary to the will of God. Knox's primary targets were the Catholic queens Mary Tudor of England (who was persecuting Protestants) and Mary of Guise, the regent of Scotland (who was suppressing the Protestant movement there). Knox argued from passages such as 1 Timothy 2:12 — "But I suffer not a woman to teach, nor to usurp authority over the man" — that female sovereignty was an inversion of the divinely ordained order.

The timing of the publication was catastrophic. Mary Tudor died in November 1558, and the Protestant Elizabeth I ascended the English throne. Elizabeth, understandably, took Knox's treatise as an attack on her own right to rule, and she never forgave him. Knox's relationship with Elizabeth remained poisoned for the rest of his life, depriving the Scottish Reformation of what might have been a powerful political ally. Calvin himself disapproved of the "First Blast," not because he disagreed with Knox's theological conclusions but because he regarded the publication as politically unwise. The episode illustrates both Knox's courage — his willingness to say what he believed regardless of consequences — and his occasional lack of political prudence.

Part Five: The Return to Scotland and the Crisis of 1559-1560

Knox returned to Scotland on May 2, 1559, landing at Leith, the port of Edinburgh. He was forty-five years old, hardened by years of suffering and exile, armed with the theology of Geneva, and consumed by a single ambition: to bring the Reformation to his homeland.

The Scotland to which Knox returned was a nation in crisis. Mary of Guise, the French-born mother of the young Mary Queen of Scots, served as regent, ruling on behalf of her daughter, who was being raised at the French court. French troops were stationed in Scotland, effectively making the country a satellite of France. The Catholic hierarchy remained in nominal control of the church, but the Protestant movement had been growing steadily, fueled by the preaching of men like John Willock and Paul Methven, and organized through a network of "privy kirks" — secret Protestant congregations that met in private homes.

The Protestant lords — the Lords of the Congregation, as they styled themselves — had signed a covenant in December 1557 committing themselves to the cause of reform. They included some of the most powerful nobles in Scotland: the Earl of Argyll, the Earl of Glencairn, Lord Lorne, and others. But they lacked a leader — a man of sufficient spiritual authority, rhetorical power, and personal courage to unite the movement and carry it to victory. Knox was that man.

Within days of his arrival, Knox preached a sermon at Perth that ignited a firestorm. Preaching on the text of Christ's cleansing of the temple (John 2:13-17), Knox denounced the idolatry of the Mass and the corruption of the monastic orders with such passion that a mob of supporters ransacked the local friaries, destroying altars, images, and ornaments. Knox was dismayed by the violence — he had called for the reform of worship, not for the destruction of property — but the incident demonstrated the explosive power of his preaching and the depth of popular discontent with the old order.

The conflict escalated rapidly. Mary of Guise declared the Protestant preachers outlaws and summoned French military reinforcements. The Lords of the Congregation raised an army. For the next year, Scotland was convulsed by a low-level civil war in which religious reform and political independence were inextricably intertwined. The Protestants sought not only the reformation of the church but the expulsion of the French troops who propped up the Catholic regime.

Knox served as the spiritual leader and chief propagandist of the Protestant cause. He preached with relentless energy, traveling from town to town, rallying congregations, stiffening the resolve of wavering lords, and writing letters and pamphlets that framed the conflict in biblical terms. He compared Scotland to ancient Israel, called upon the nobility to act as the godly magistrates of the Old Testament, and warned that God would judge the nation if it failed to purge itself of idolatry. His rhetoric was drawn directly from the prophets of the Old Testament — Isaiah, Jeremiah, and Ezekiel — whose denunciations of apostasy and calls for repentance provided the template for Knox's own preaching.

The turning point came with English military intervention. Elizabeth I, despite her personal antipathy toward Knox, recognized that a Protestant Scotland was strategically preferable to a French-dominated Catholic one. In February 1560, she dispatched an English fleet and army to assist the Lords of the Congregation. The combined Protestant forces besieged the French garrison at Leith. The death of Mary of Guise in June 1560 removed the last obstacle to a negotiated settlement. The Treaty of Edinburgh, signed on July 6, 1560, ended French military presence in Scotland and opened the way for the Reformation Parliament.

Part Six: The Reformation Parliament of 1560

The Scottish Parliament that met in August 1560 accomplished one of the most dramatic religious transformations in European history. In a single session — lasting less than two weeks — Parliament abolished papal authority in Scotland, banned the celebration of the Mass (making it a criminal offense punishable by death for a third offense), and adopted a Reformed confession of faith — the Scots Confession — written primarily by Knox and five other ministers (all named John, leading to the nickname "the six Johns").

The Scots Confession of 1560 was a robust and uncompromising statement of Reformed theology. It affirmed the authority of Scripture as the supreme rule of faith and life, the doctrines of election and predestination, justification by faith alone, and the two sacraments of baptism and the Lord's Supper. It rejected the Mass as idolatrous, denied the authority of the pope, and repudiated the veneration of saints, purgatory, and the merit of good works as grounds of salvation. The confession's language was direct, vigorous, and polemical — characteristic of Knox's literary style.

The political circumstances of the Reformation Parliament were extraordinary. The queen — Mary Queen of Scots — was in France and had not authorized the Parliament to meet. Many of the bishops and abbots who would normally have attended did not, either because they opposed the proceedings or because they feared for their safety. The legality of the Parliament's actions was therefore questionable by the standards of the time. But Knox and the Lords of the Congregation were acting on a principle that transcended questions of constitutional legality: the principle that the Word of God is the supreme authority in all matters of faith and that the reformation of religion is a duty that falls upon the entire nation — not merely upon the clergy or the monarch.

The apostle Paul's words to the Corinthians capture the spirit of Knox's enterprise: "For the weapons of our warfare are not carnal, but mighty through God to the pulling down of strong holds; casting down imaginations, and every high thing that exalteth itself against the knowledge of God, and bringing into captivity every thought to the obedience of Christ" (2 Corinthians 10:4-5). The strongholds of papal authority, medieval superstition, and ecclesiastical corruption had been pulled down in a single parliamentary session — a testimony to the power of God's Word when it is faithfully preached and fearlessly applied.

Part Seven: The Book of Discipline and the Organization of the Reformed Church

Following the Reformation Parliament, Knox and his fellow ministers turned to the practical task of organizing the Reformed church in Scotland. The result was the First Book of Discipline, completed in January 1561 — one of the most comprehensive and visionary documents of the entire Reformation.

The Book of Discipline addressed three areas: the government of the church, the provision of education, and the relief of the poor.

In matters of church government, the Book of Discipline established a Presbyterian system — governed not by bishops appointed by the monarch but by elders elected by the congregations. The basic unit of church government was the kirk session, composed of the minister, the elders, and the deacons of each local congregation. Above the kirk session stood the presbytery, the synod, and the General Assembly — a hierarchy of church courts that provided oversight, accountability, and a mechanism for resolving disputes. This system was designed to prevent the concentration of ecclesiastical power in the hands of any single individual — whether pope, bishop, or monarch — and to ensure that the church was governed according to the Word of God by men chosen by the people of God.

The Presbyterian system of church government was one of Knox's most enduring contributions to the life of the church. It was based on the conviction that Christ alone is the Head of the Church, that all ministers are equal in authority and dignity, and that the government of the church should be exercised collectively through councils of elders rather than individually through prelates. As Paul wrote to the elders of the church at Ephesus: "Take heed therefore unto yourselves, and to all the flock, over the which the Holy Ghost hath made you overseers, to feed the church of God, which he hath purchased with his own blood" (Acts 20:28). The elders were overseers — not lords — and their authority was a stewardship entrusted to them by the Holy Spirit for the spiritual care of God's people.

In matters of education, the Book of Discipline was extraordinarily ambitious. Knox proposed a national system of education that would provide schooling for every child in Scotland, regardless of social class. Each parish was to have a school. Each major town was to have a grammar school. Each principal city was to have a college. The purpose of this educational system was both spiritual and practical: to ensure that every Scottish man and woman could read the Bible in their own language and to train the ministers, teachers, and leaders that the new Reformed church would need.

Knox's educational vision was centuries ahead of its time. Universal public education would not become a reality in England until the nineteenth century, but Knox proposed it for Scotland in 1561. Although the Book of Discipline's educational provisions were never fully implemented — the Scottish nobility appropriated much of the church's former wealth rather than redirecting it to schools and poor relief as Knox had intended — the ideal remained alive and eventually bore fruit. Scotland became one of the most literate nations in Europe, with a tradition of public education that would later influence educational systems around the world.

In matters of poor relief, the Book of Discipline called for the redistribution of the former wealth of the Catholic church to provide for the poor, the sick, and the aged. Knox argued that the vast revenues of the monasteries, which had been used to support the luxurious lifestyles of abbots and monks, should instead be used to fund schools, pay ministers, and care for the destitute. The biblical mandate was clear: "Pure religion and undefiled before God and the Father is this, To visit the fatherless and widows in their affliction, and to keep himself unspotted from the world" (James 1:27). Knox envisioned a church that was not merely a preaching institution but a caring community — a body of believers who took practical responsibility for the physical as well as the spiritual needs of their neighbors.

Part Eight: Knox and Mary Queen of Scots

The most dramatic episodes of Knox's later career were his confrontations with Mary Queen of Scots, who returned to Scotland from France in August 1561. Mary was eighteen years old, beautiful, cultured, and devoutly Catholic. She had been raised at the French court, married to the French king Francis II (who died in December 1560), and was accustomed to the elegance and deference of French courtly life. Scotland — rough, poor, and newly Protestant — was a rude shock.

Mary's arrival in Scotland created an immediate constitutional crisis. The queen was Catholic; the country had just become Protestant. Mary insisted on her right to celebrate the Mass privately in her chapel at Holyrood Palace. Knox denounced this concession from the pulpit, declaring that "one Mass is more fearful to me than if ten thousand armed enemies were landed in any part of the realm." The queen summoned Knox to a private audience — the first of several famous interviews between the two.

The encounters between Knox and Mary are among the most dramatic scenes in the history of the Reformation. Mary alternately pleaded, reasoned, wept, and raged. Knox remained unmoved. He told the queen that he had no objection to her ruling Scotland, provided she ruled according to God's Word. He told her that the Mass was idolatry and that her persistence in celebrating it was a provocation that would bring God's judgment upon the nation. When Mary wept, Knox replied — with a bluntness that strikes modern readers as shockingly insensitive — that he could better endure her tears than he could be silent and betray his conscience.

Knox's treatment of Mary has been criticized by historians of every persuasion. Even sympathetic biographers have acknowledged that his manner was often harsh, his language often intemperate, and his attitude toward the young queen often lacking in the courtesy and compassion that the gospel demands. Knox was a prophet, not a diplomat; his strength was the uncompromising proclamation of truth, not the gentle art of persuasion.

Yet Knox's theological principle was sound, even if his manner was deficient. He believed — as Calvin had taught, and as the Westminster Confession would later affirm — that the civil magistrate is subject to the law of God and that no ruler, however exalted, has the right to impose false worship upon the people. Knox's question to Mary was essentially the question that every Reformed Christian must ask of every ruler: Will you govern according to the Word of God, or will you govern according to your own will? The answer to that question determines whether a ruler is a servant of God or a tyrant, and whether the people owe obedience or resistance.

The crisis between Knox and Mary deepened over the following years. Mary's personal life became increasingly scandalous — her marriage to Lord Darnley, Darnley's murder, her marriage to the Earl of Bothwell (who was widely suspected of having murdered Darnley), and her eventual abdication and flight to England in 1568 — and Knox did not hesitate to pronounce judgment on the queen's conduct from the pulpit. His sermons became increasingly apocalyptic in tone, warning that God's patience with an idolatrous and immoral court would not last forever.

Part Nine: Knox's Theology — The Sovereignty of God and the Right of Resistance

Knox's theology was thoroughly Reformed — grounded in the doctrines of God's sovereignty, human depravity, unconditional election, and salvation by grace alone through faith alone. He learned these doctrines from Calvin in Geneva, but he applied them with a distinctively Scottish emphasis on the practical implications of God's sovereignty for political and social life.

Knox's most original theological contribution was his development of the right of resistance to tyranny. Calvin had taught that Christians must generally submit to the civil magistrate, even when the magistrate was unjust or ungodly, and that active resistance was permissible only through the agency of "lesser magistrates" — subordinate governmental officials who had a duty to restrain the tyranny of a sovereign. Knox went further. Drawing on the political philosophy of his teacher John Major, on the examples of the Old Testament prophets, and on the dire situation of the Scottish Reformation, Knox argued that the people as a whole — not merely the nobles or the magistrates — had the right and the duty to resist a ruler who sought to impose false religion upon the nation.

This doctrine of popular resistance was revolutionary. It challenged the prevailing theory of the divine right of kings — the notion that monarchs rule by God's direct appointment and that their subjects have no right to resist them, even when they act tyrannically. Knox argued from Scripture that God's law is above the king's law, that rulers who violate God's law forfeit their claim to obedience, and that the people are the ultimate guardians of true religion and political liberty.

Knox's political theology would have far-reaching consequences. It influenced the French Huguenot resistance theorists (Philippe du Plessis-Mornay and the author of the "Vindiciae contra tyrannos"), the Dutch revolt against Spain, the English Puritan revolution, the Covenanting movement in Scotland, and ultimately the American Revolution. The principle that government derives its authority from the consent of the governed, and that a tyrannical government may lawfully be overthrown — a principle enshrined in the American Declaration of Independence — has deep roots in Knox's Reformed political theology.

The Scriptural foundations of Knox's position were drawn from throughout the Old Testament. He pointed to the example of Moses, who confronted Pharaoh and demanded the liberation of God's people (Exodus 5:1). He pointed to the example of Elijah, who challenged King Ahab and the prophets of Baal on Mount Carmel (1 Kings 18). He pointed to the example of Daniel, who refused to obey the king's decree to worship a golden image (Daniel 3). And above all, he pointed to the words of the apostles: "We ought to obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29). When human law conflicts with divine law, the Christian's duty is clear — and Knox never flinched from applying that principle to the most powerful rulers of his age.

Part Ten: Knox's Final Years and Death

The last years of Knox's life were marked by physical decline and political turbulence. After Mary's abdication in 1567, Scotland was governed by a series of regents on behalf of the young King James VI (the future James I of England). Knox continued to preach and to exercise influence over Scottish affairs, but his health was failing. The years in the galleys had permanently weakened his constitution, and the relentless demands of his ministry had taken a further toll.

In 1571, Knox suffered a stroke that partially paralyzed him and weakened his voice — a devastating blow for a man whose power lay in his preaching. Yet he continued to preach, even when he had to be helped into the pulpit and when his voice could barely be heard beyond the first few rows. An eyewitness recorded that despite his physical frailty, Knox still preached with such spiritual power that "he was like to ding the pulpit in blads and fly out of it" — that is, he seemed ready to smash the pulpit to pieces and leap out of it with the force of his proclamation.

John Knox died on November 24, 1572, at the age of approximately fifty-eight. His final days were spent in prayer, Scripture reading, and conversation with friends and colleagues. On his deathbed, he asked his wife to read aloud from the seventeenth chapter of the Gospel of John — Christ's high priestly prayer — and from the passage in 1 Corinthians 15 where Paul proclaims the resurrection of the dead: "O death, where is thy sting? O grave, where is thy victory? The sting of death is sin; and the strength of sin is the law. But thanks be to God, which giveth us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ" (1 Corinthians 15:55-57). Knox died with these words in his ears — the words of victory, of resurrection, and of eternal hope.

At his funeral, the Earl of Morton, the regent of Scotland, delivered the famous epitaph: "Here lies a man who neither flattered nor feared any flesh." It was a fitting tribute — perhaps the finest epitaph ever spoken over a minister of the gospel. Knox had spent his entire life proclaiming the truth without regard for the consequences. He had confronted cardinals, queens, and regents. He had endured imprisonment, galley slavery, exile, and chronic illness. He had never wavered, never compromised, and never retreated. He feared the face of God, and therefore he feared the face of no man.

Part Eleven: The Legacy of John Knox

Knox's legacy extends far beyond Scotland, though Scotland itself was transformed by his work. The Kirk — the Church of Scotland — became one of the most thoroughly Reformed churches in Europe. Its Presbyterian system of government, its commitment to an educated ministry, its emphasis on preaching and catechetical instruction, and its vision of a godly commonwealth shaped Scottish culture for centuries.

The Scottish emphasis on education — which produced some of the finest universities in Europe, including St Andrews, Glasgow, Edinburgh, and Aberdeen — owes much to Knox's educational vision in the Book of Discipline. Scotland's reputation as a nation of thinkers, theologians, scientists, and engineers is rooted in the Reformation conviction that every person should be able to read the Bible and that education is a divine calling.

Knox's influence on the wider English-speaking world was equally profound. The Presbyterian system of church government that he established in Scotland was carried by Scottish emigrants to Ulster, to the American colonies, and eventually to every continent. The Scotch-Irish Presbyterians of the American frontier were the direct spiritual descendants of John Knox, and their fierce independence, their democratic instincts, and their unshakeable conviction that no earthly authority may override the authority of God's Word are traits that bear the stamp of Knox's character.

The Puritan movement in England, though it developed independently, shared many of Knox's convictions about worship, church government, and the duty of resistance to ungodly rulers. The Westminster Assembly of the 1640s — which produced the Westminster Confession of Faith, the Larger and Shorter Catechisms, and the Directory for Public Worship — was deeply influenced by Scottish Presbyterian theology. When the Assembly completed its work, the Church of Scotland adopted the Westminster Standards as its own — and they remain the confessional standard of Presbyterian churches worldwide to this day.

Richard Baxter, the great Puritan pastor and theologian, described Knox as a man who "shook the very foundations of the papacy in Scotland." Thomas M'Crie, Knox's most distinguished biographer, wrote in the early nineteenth century that Knox was "a man who, in the most difficult and perilous times, displayed a fortitude and constancy of mind which no dangers could appal, no sufferings could subdue." And the Puritan tradition that we study in these lessons — with its passion for the Word of God, its commitment to godly worship, its insistence on the lordship of Christ over every sphere of life — is, in significant measure, the legacy of the Thundering Scot.

Each participant should prepare at least five arguments based on the historical positions of their character, drawing on specific events, Scripture passages, and theological principles discussed in today's lesson. --- Closing ---

Knox reminds us that true reformation requires not only theological conviction but personal courage — the willingness to speak truth to power, even when power responds with tears and threats. He reminds us that the church of Christ is not the possession of any monarch, any prelate, or any political system — it belongs to Christ alone, and it must be governed by His Word alone. And he reminds us that God uses broken vessels — men who have been through the galleys, who bear scars, who struggle with their own weaknesses — to accomplish His sovereign purposes in the world.

As the Earl of Morton said over Knox's grave: "Here lies a man who neither flattered nor feared any flesh." May we, by the grace of God, develop something of that same holy fearlessness — not the fearlessness of arrogance or self-will, but the fearlessness of faith, the fearlessness that comes from knowing that the God who called us is greater than any power that opposes us. "If God be for us, who can be against us?" (Romans 8:31). Until next time, students.
