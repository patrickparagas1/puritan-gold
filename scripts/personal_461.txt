PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Doctrine Deep-Dive, Day 30
"The Beatific Vision — Seeing God Face to Face"
The Beatific Vision Explored Through the Puritans


Welcome. Doctrine is not a dry, academic exercise. It is the scaffolding on which the entire Christian life is built. Tonight we examine a foundational truth that, rightly understood, will transform the way you worship, pray, and live.

Tonight we draw from the work of Jonathan Edwards, specifically from "The End for Which God Created the World / Charity and Its Fruits." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is 1 John 3:2:

"Beloved, now are we the sons of God, and it doth not yet appear what we shall be: but we know that, when he shall appear, we shall be like him; for we shall see him as he is."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


We close this month's theological exploration with the doctrine that is, in many ways, the consummation of all other doctrines: the beatific vision — the promise that believers will one day see God face to face. This is the ultimate hope of the Christian faith, the final destiny of every redeemed soul, the 'end for which God created the world.' Everything else — creation, providence, redemption, sanctification — is a means to this end: bringing the people of God into the direct, unmediated, eternal enjoyment of God Himself.

Jonathan Edwards, the greatest theologian America has produced, devoted sustained attention to this doctrine. Edwards was not merely a philosopher of religion or a revival preacher; he was a mystic in the best sense of the word — a man who had tasted the sweetness of God and who longed for the day when taste would give way to sight. His writings on the beatific vision are among the most exalted pages in all of Christian literature.

Edwards begins with the nature of the vision itself. What does it mean to 'see God as he is'? Edwards argues that this is not merely an intellectual comprehension of God's attributes, though it includes that. It is not merely an emotional experience of God's love, though it includes that as well. It is a direct, intuitive, all-encompassing apprehension of the beauty, goodness, and glory of God in the Person of Jesus Christ. Edwards writes that in the beatific vision, the believer will know God not through the mediating lens of Scripture, creation, or providence — not 'through a glass, darkly' — but directly, face to face, with an immediacy and a fullness that no present experience can approach.

Edwards explores the transformative effect of this vision. John writes, 'We shall be like him; for we shall see him as he is.' The vision produces likeness. To gaze upon infinite beauty is to be made beautiful. To see infinite holiness is to be made holy. To behold infinite love is to be filled with love. The beatific vision does not merely satisfy the soul; it perfects it. All the remaining corruption, all the lingering weakness, all the shadows of sin that have clung to the believer throughout his earthly pilgrimage will be burned away in the blazing light of God's unmediated presence.

Edwards makes the remarkable argument that the beatific vision is not a static experience but a dynamic, ever-increasing one. God is infinite, and the finite soul can never exhaust the knowledge of an infinite Being. Edwards envisions an eternity of discovery — an endless progression of deeper knowledge, greater love, and more intense delight as the glorified saint moves ever deeper into the inexhaustible riches of God. There will be no boredom in heaven, no satiation, no reaching the end. God is an ocean without a shore, and the redeemed will forever be sailing into deeper waters.

The Puritan Richard Baxter, whose 'Saints' Everlasting Rest' we explored earlier this month, adds his own voice to this meditation. Baxter writes that the beatific vision will bring perfect satisfaction to every faculty of the soul. The mind will know God perfectly — not exhaustively, but without error or confusion. The will will choose God freely and exclusively — with no competing desires, no internal warfare, no remaining inclination toward sin. The affections will love God supremely — with a love that is pure, intense, and unending. Every dimension of the human soul that was broken by the fall will be perfectly restored and perfectly oriented toward its Creator.

Edwards also connects the beatific vision to the present life. He argues that the Christian's growth in grace in this life is a foretaste and a preparation for the beatific vision. Every genuine experience of God's beauty in Scripture, every moment of worship in which the heart is truly engaged, every act of obedience in which the will is genuinely submitted — these are anticipations of the eternal vision, previews of the glory that is coming. The more we taste God now, the more we long for the day when taste will give way to sight.

Thomas Goodwin, whose 'Heart of Christ' has guided our reading this month, closes the circle by connecting the beatific vision to Christ's present heart toward His people. Christ Himself longs for the day when His people will see His glory. In His high priestly prayer, He asked, 'Father, I will that they also, whom thou hast given me, be with me where I am; that they may behold my glory' (John 17:24). The beatific vision is not merely our desire; it is Christ's desire. He wants to share His glory with us. He is preparing a place for us. And He will come again to receive us unto Himself, that where He is, we may be also.

Patrick, as this month draws to a close, let your heart be lifted toward the ultimate hope. Whatever you are facing — joy or sorrow, triumph or struggle, clarity or confusion — it is all moving toward a day when you will see Him as He is, and in seeing Him, you will be made like Him. Every tear will be wiped away. Every question will be answered. Every longing will be fulfilled. For the beatific vision is not merely the end of suffering; it is the beginning of an eternal, ever-increasing joy that the heart of man has not yet conceived.


Before we close, take a few moments with these reflection questions.

Question 1: How often do I meditate on the hope of seeing God face to face, and how does that hope shape my daily priorities and affections?

Question 2: Do I experience foretastes of the beatific vision in my present communion with God — moments when His beauty becomes real to my heart — and am I cultivating those experiences?

Question 3: How does Edwards's vision of an eternity of ever-increasing knowledge and delight in God change my understanding of heaven from a static destination to a dynamic adventure?

Question 4: What has this month's study revealed about God, about Christ, and about myself that I want to carry forward into the months ahead?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — my eternal hope and my present joy — I thank You for this month of growth. I have seen more deeply the tenderness of Christ's heart, the vastness of Your attributes, the richness of Your grace, and the glory of Your purposes. But all that I have seen is but a shadow of what is to come. I long for the day when I shall see You as You are — when faith shall become sight, when knowledge shall become vision, when longing shall become possession. Until that day, keep me faithful. Fan the flame of my desire for You. And when at last I stand before Your throne, let me fall at Your feet in wonder, love, and praise that will never, never end. Through Christ my Lord, who has gone to prepare a place for me, Amen.

Until next time. May the Lord deepen your faith with every passing day.
