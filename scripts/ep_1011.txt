PURITAN GOLD -- Study Topic: Female Piety, Part 1 of 10
"Introduction — John Angell James and His Vision for Godly Womanhood"
John Angell James — Female Piety

---

Welcome, dear listener, to a new study series on Puritan Gold. Over the next ten episodes, we shall walk together through one of the most searching and tender works of the nineteenth century: Female Piety, by John Angell James. This is a book written by a pastor who loved the souls of women, who saw in the godly woman an instrument of extraordinary power for the kingdom of Christ, and who believed that the cultivation of female piety was essential not only for the home but for the whole of society. I invite you to settle in, open your heart to the Word of God, and let us begin.

Let us open with a reading from the book of Proverbs, chapter 31, verses 10 and 30, in the King James Version:

"Who can find a virtuous woman? for her price is far above rubies... Favour is deceitful, and beauty is vain: but a woman that feareth the LORD, she shall be praised."

--- Teaching ---

John Angell James was born in 1785 in Blandford Forum, Dorset, and became the pastor of Carrs Lane Congregational Chapel in Birmingham, England, where he served for over fifty years until his death in 1859. He was a man of deep evangelical conviction, a prolific author, and a pastor whose influence extended far beyond the walls of his own chapel. His works include The Anxious Enquirer After Salvation, A Help to Domestic Happiness, and the volume we now study together — Female Piety, or the Young Woman's Friend and Guide Through Life to Immortality.

James wrote Female Piety not as a detached theologian but as a shepherd who had spent decades counseling, teaching, and observing the spiritual lives of women in his congregation. He had seen the power of a godly mother's influence upon her children. He had watched young women wrestle with the temptations of vanity, worldliness, and spiritual indifference. And he had witnessed, again and again, that when a woman gave her heart wholly to Christ, the effects rippled outward through her family, her church, and her community in ways that could scarcely be measured.

The burden of James's work is stated plainly in his opening pages. He believed that religion is of supreme importance to women — not merely as a private comfort, but as the very foundation upon which all true female excellence is built. Without genuine piety, James argued, a woman might possess beauty, accomplishment, wit, and social grace, and yet lack the one thing that gives lasting dignity and usefulness to her life. As he wrote, the ornament of a meek and quiet spirit, which is in the sight of God of great price, is worth more than all the jewels of the earth.

This conviction did not arise in a vacuum. James stood in a rich tradition of Protestant and Puritan thought on the dignity and calling of women. Richard Baxter, in his Christian Directory, had spoken at length about the duties and privileges of the Christian wife and mother. Thomas Watson, in his exposition of the Ten Commandments, had emphasized the mutual obligations of husbands and wives and the spiritual nurture of children. William Gouge, in his massive work Of Domesticall Duties, had laid out a comprehensive vision of the Christian household in which the godly woman played an indispensable role. James inherited this tradition, refined it, and applied it with pastoral warmth to the women of his own generation.

But James was also responding to the particular challenges of his age. The early nineteenth century in England was a time of rapid social change. Industrialization was reshaping the family. New philosophies were challenging traditional views of womanhood. The Romantic movement, with its emphasis on feeling and individual expression, was influencing how young women understood their own identity. James saw that many women were being drawn away from the simplicity and depth of true religion by the glittering distractions of a changing world. He wrote Female Piety as a corrective — a call back to the ancient paths, to the fear of the Lord, and to the beauty of holiness.

What makes James's approach so valuable is the combination of doctrinal substance and pastoral tenderness. He does not lecture women from above; he speaks to them as a father speaks to his daughters, with affection, earnestness, and deep respect. He takes their spiritual lives seriously. He believes that women are capable of the highest attainments in godliness, and he calls them upward with both urgency and gentleness.

James begins his work by establishing the fundamental importance of personal religion. He writes that true piety is not a mere external conformity to religious duties, but a transformation of the heart by the grace of God. It is the implantation of a new nature, the indwelling of the Holy Spirit, and the living union of the soul with Christ by faith. This is the foundation upon which everything else in the book is built. Without this inward reality, all outward religion is mere show — a whitened sepulchre, as our Lord Himself warned.

The apostle Peter echoes this when he writes in his first epistle, chapter 3, verses 3 and 4: "Whose adorning let it not be that outward adorning of plaiting the hair, and of wearing of gold, or of putting on of apparel; But let it be the hidden man of the heart, in that which is not corruptible, even the ornament of a meek and quiet spirit, which is in the sight of God of great price." James returns to this passage again and again throughout his work, for it captures the very essence of what he means by female piety — a beauty that is not of the body but of the soul, not of the world but of God, not corruptible but eternal.

John Flavel, the great Puritan divine of Dartmouth, once wrote that the greatest work in all the world is the work of God upon the heart. James would have said amen to that with all his soul. For it is precisely this inward work of grace that transforms a woman from within, that gives her strength to endure trials, wisdom to govern her household, tenderness to nurture her children, and courage to witness for Christ in a hostile world.

As we embark upon this series, I want to encourage you to approach it not merely as an intellectual exercise but as a devotional journey. James wrote for the heart as much as the mind. He wanted his readers to be moved, convicted, comforted, and changed. Whether you are a young woman just beginning your walk with Christ, a mother in the thick of family life, or a grandmother looking back on years of service, the truths we will explore together are for you. They are grounded in the eternal Word of God, tested by centuries of Christian experience, and applied with the pastoral wisdom of a man who truly loved the souls he served.

Let us pray that the Holy Spirit would use these studies to deepen our understanding, strengthen our faith, and kindle in our hearts a fresh love for the Lord Jesus Christ, in whom all true piety finds its source and its goal.

--- Application ---

1. Begin this series with prayer. Ask the Lord to open your heart to whatever He would teach you through the words of John Angell James and the Scriptures we will study together. Set aside a regular time for these episodes, treating them as a devotional appointment with God.

2. Read 1 Peter 3:1-6 this week and meditate on what the apostle means by "the hidden man of the heart." Ask yourself: Am I more concerned with outward appearance or with the cultivation of inward grace? Write down your reflections.

3. Consider the legacy of godly women in your own life — a mother, grandmother, teacher, or friend whose faith has shaped you. Give thanks to God for their influence, and consider how you might carry that legacy forward.

4. If you have access to a copy of James's Female Piety, begin reading the introductory chapters alongside these episodes. Let the original text enrich your study. Many editions are freely available in the public domain.
