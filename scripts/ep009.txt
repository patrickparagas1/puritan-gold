PURITAN GOLD — Episode 9
"The Sovereignty of God"
Week 2, Day 2 (Tuesday) — A.W. Pink's The Attributes of God

---

Have you ever made a plan... a really good plan... and watched it fall completely apart? Maybe it was a career plan. A five-year vision for your life. A carefully charted course that seemed so certain, so right, so well-considered — and then something happened that you never could have predicted. An illness. A job loss. A relationship that ended. A door that closed when every sign had pointed to it swinging wide open.

If you've been alive for any length of time, you know exactly what I'm talking about. And in those moments — those disorienting, bewildering moments — there's a question that rises from somewhere deep in the human heart: Is anyone actually in charge here? Does anyone have a plan? Or are we all just... drifting?

This is Puritan Gold, and today we're stepping away from our walk through Proverbs — just for a day — to sit at the feet of one of the twentieth century's most uncompromising theologians: Arthur Walkington Pink. And we're going to wrestle with a doctrine that Pink considered absolutely foundational to everything else in the Christian faith. The sovereignty of God.

Now, before we dive in, let me give you two verses from Proverbs that will anchor our conversation today. The first is Proverbs 16:9:

"A man's heart deviseth his way: but the Lord directeth his steps."

And the second is Proverbs 19:21:

"There are many devices in a man's heart; nevertheless the counsel of the Lord, that shall stand."

Hold those verses in your mind. We'll come back to them.

A.W. Pink published The Attributes of God as a series of articles in the 1930s and later compiled them into a book that has become, I believe, one of the most important theological works of the last century. And he begins — not with God's love, not with God's mercy, not even with God's wisdom — but with God's sovereignty. Why? Because Pink understood that if you don't get this right, nothing else will make sense. If God is not sovereign, then His love is merely sentimental. His promises are merely hopeful. His salvation is merely attempted. But if God is truly, absolutely, comprehensively sovereign... then everything changes.

So what does it mean that God is sovereign?

Pink put it plainly. He wrote that the sovereignty of God means that God is God. That He is on the throne. That He is absolutely free to do as He pleases, when He pleases, with whom He pleases. There is no power above Him, beside Him, or beneath Him that can thwart His will, frustrate His purposes, or overturn His decrees. He governs the universe He created — not in broad, general strokes, but in precise, particular detail. From the rise and fall of nations to the number of hairs on your head. From the orbit of galaxies to the outcome of a coin toss. Nothing — absolutely nothing — happens apart from either the directive or the permissive will of God.

Now... I know that for some of you, this is deeply comforting. For others, it may be deeply troubling. And I want to speak to both responses, because they're both natural, and they both matter.

Let's start with the comfort.

If God is truly sovereign, then your life is not random. The things that have happened to you — even the painful things, even the confusing things, even the things that felt like the bottom falling out — none of them caught God off guard. None of them disrupted His plan. None of them happened in some cosmic blind spot where God wasn't paying attention.

Go back to Proverbs 16:9. "A man's heart deviseth his way: but the Lord directeth his steps." You make your plans. You chart your course. And you should — planning is wise and good. But underneath your planning, beneath your strategizing, behind every decision you make... the Lord is directing your steps. Not in a vague, general sense. Not in a "I hope it all works out" sense. Directing. Guiding. Steering. Governing.

Pink illustrated this beautifully. He pointed to Joseph in the Old Testament — sold into slavery by his own brothers, falsely accused, imprisoned for years, forgotten by the very man he helped. If you had been standing beside Joseph in that Egyptian prison, you would have seen nothing but tragedy. Nothing but injustice. Nothing but a life gone horribly wrong. But God... God was working. Quietly. Invisibly. Sovereignly. And when the full story was revealed, Joseph himself could say to his brothers: "Ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good."

Do you hear that? Not "God made the best of a bad situation." Not "God cleaned up the mess." God meant it. From the beginning. The evil intentions of men were real — and they were culpable for those intentions. But God's sovereign purpose was working through it all, weaving even human wickedness into His tapestry of redemption.

Friend, if you're in a season of confusion right now — if you're in the prison and you can't see the palace — hold onto this. The Lord directs your steps. The counsel of the Lord shall stand. Your pain is not pointless. Your suffering is not random. Your story is not over.

Now let me speak to the other response — the trouble. Because the sovereignty of God does raise hard questions. If God is in control of everything, then what about evil? What about suffering? What about the child who gets sick, the marriage that falls apart, the nation that descends into tyranny? If God could prevent these things and doesn't... what do we make of that?

Pink did not shy away from this question. He was, frankly, a theologian who refused to soften the edges of Scripture to make people comfortable. And he would say — and I believe Scripture supports him — that we must hold two truths in tension. First: God is sovereign over all things, including evil. Second: God is never the author of sin. He governs it. He permits it. He limits it. He directs it toward His purposes. But He does not commit it, and those who do commit it bear full moral responsibility.

This is a mystery. I won't pretend it isn't. The relationship between divine sovereignty and human responsibility is one of the deepest paradoxes in all of theology. But Pink would argue — and I think he's right — that the mystery is not a contradiction. It's a limitation of our finite minds trying to comprehend an infinite God. We see in part. We know in part. And there are depths of God's wisdom and knowledge that we simply cannot plumb from this side of eternity.

What we can know — what we must know — is that God's sovereignty is not cold. It is not mechanical. It is not the indifferent hand of fate. It is the wise, loving, purposeful governance of a Father who knows exactly what He's doing, even when we can't see it. Even when it hurts. Even when every fiber of our being cries out, "Why?"

Proverbs 19:21 speaks to this beautifully. "There are many devices in a man's heart; nevertheless the counsel of the Lord, that shall stand." How many plans have you made in your life? Hundreds? Thousands? Your heart has been a factory of devices — schemes, strategies, ambitions, blueprints for how life should go. And how many of those plans have actually come to pass exactly as you envisioned?

Not many, I'd wager. And that's not a failure. That's sovereignty. The counsel of the Lord — His plan, His purpose, His design — that is what stands. Not your plan. His.

And here is where the sovereignty of God doesn't just comfort us — it humbles us. Because if God is truly sovereign, then we are not. If His counsel stands, then ours does not — at least, not unless it aligns with His. And that means we must hold our plans loosely. We must hold our ambitions with open hands. We must say, with genuine submission, "Not my will, but Thine be done."

Pink was relentless on this point. He argued that the great disease of the modern church was an exaltation of human will at the expense of divine sovereignty. We have, he said, created a god in our own image — a god who suggests but doesn't decree, who wishes but doesn't will, who hopes but doesn't govern. And that god is an idol. The God of Scripture is not wringing His hands in heaven, hoping things work out. He is seated on His throne, and His purposes will not be moved.

Now, someone might say, "Well, if God is sovereign over everything, then what's the point of prayer? What's the point of effort? What's the point of making any plans at all?"

And this is a fair question, but it reveals a misunderstanding. God's sovereignty doesn't nullify human action — it gives it meaning. When you pray, you are not trying to change the mind of a reluctant deity. You are participating in the means God has ordained to accomplish His ends. When you work, you are not fighting against fate. You are cooperating with the very purposes God has set in motion. Prayer matters precisely because God is sovereign. Your obedience matters precisely because God uses means to accomplish His will — and you, friend, are one of those means.

Pink put it this way in Studies in the Scriptures: God has not only ordained the end, but He has also ordained the means to that end. He has decreed that prayer shall be answered, that faith shall be rewarded, that obedience shall be blessed. And so we pray, we believe, we obey — not because we're uncertain whether God will act, but because we know He will act, and He has invited us into the story.

Let me bring this to a very practical place as we close.

How should the sovereignty of God change the way you live today — this very day?

First, it should give you peace. Whatever you're facing — a difficult diagnosis, a broken relationship, an uncertain future — God is not absent. He has not lost control. The very thing that frightens you most is held firmly in His hands. You can rest. Not because the situation isn't hard, but because the God who governs it is good.

Second, it should make you humble. Stop trying to be God. Stop trying to control outcomes that are beyond your reach. Stop white-knuckling your plans and demanding that life conform to your blueprint. Hold your plans loosely. Surrender your agenda to the One who actually knows the future — because He's the One who's writing it.

Third, it should fuel your worship. A sovereign God is a God worthy of praise. Not a God who tries hard and sometimes succeeds. Not a God who does His best in a chaotic universe. A God who reigns. A God who governs. A God whose counsel stands forever. That kind of God deserves not just your acknowledgment but your adoration.

And finally, it should give you courage. If God is sovereign — if nothing can thwart His purposes — then you are free to take risks for His kingdom. You're free to step out in faith. You're free to speak the truth even when it's costly. Because the outcome doesn't rest on your shoulders. It rests on His. And His purposes... will not... be... moved.

I want to leave you with one more thought from Pink. He wrote that the sovereignty of God is the pillow on which the child of God rests his head at night. What a beautiful image. When the day has been long, when the questions have been many, when the future is unclear... the believer lays his head down on this truth: God is on the throne. And he sleeps in peace.

Let's pray.

Sovereign Lord, we bow before You — not before an idea, not before a doctrine, but before the living God who reigns over all things. Forgive us for the times we've tried to sit on Your throne. Forgive us for the arrogance of thinking we know better than You. Forgive us for the anxiety that reveals our lack of trust in Your governance. Teach us to rest in Your sovereignty. Teach us to trust what we cannot see. And give us the humility to say, from the depths of our hearts, "Thy will be done." We pray this in the name of Jesus, our sovereign King. Amen.
