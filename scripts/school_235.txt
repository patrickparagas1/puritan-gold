Good morning, students. Welcome to the Puritan Academy. Let us begin this day as the Puritans did, by acknowledging God as the source of all knowledge and wisdom. Before we open our textbooks, let us open our hearts.

Almighty God, the fountain of all wisdom, we ask You to bless our studies today. Open our minds to understand, our hearts to receive, and our wills to obey the truth. Grant us diligence, humility, and a spirit of inquiry that honors You. Through Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.

Let us meditate on a portion of Scripture before we begin our main lesson.

Our Scripture for today is 1 Timothy 2:1-6; James 5:16; Exodus 32:9-14; Ezekiel 22:30; John 17:20-23:

"I exhort therefore, that, first of all, supplications, prayers, intercessions, and giving of thanks, be made for all men; For kings, and for all that are in authority; that we may lead a quiet and peaceable life in all godliness and honesty. Who needs your prayers today? Not the vague, general, habitual prayers that cost nothing, but the specific, fervent, faith-filled intercession that storms the gates of heaven on behalf of a particular soul? Is there a family member wandering from God? Intercede for them by name. Is there a friend carrying a burden too heavy to bear alone? Bring that burden to the throne of grace. Is there a pastor laboring under the weight of ministry, a missionary serving in a hostile land, a neighbor who does not know Christ? They need your prayers more than they need your advice, more than they need your money, more than they need your sympathy. They need you to stand in the gap, to make up the hedge, to plead their cause before the God who moves mountains. Samuel said it would be sin to stop praying for God's people. Let it not be said of you what God said through Ezekiel: I looked for an intercessor, but found none. Be that intercessor. Pray now.

As we prepare for today's lesson, remember that everything we study flows from this one fountain: the Word of God. Our lesson today is part of The Life of Prayer, and it is titled: Intercession — Praying for Others. Let us proceed with minds engaged and hearts open.


Now let us turn to our main lesson for today.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes in 1 Timothy 2:1-6; James 5:16; Exodus 32:9-14; Ezekiel 22:30; John 17:20-23.

Intercessory prayer — prayer offered on behalf of others — is one of the most sacred privileges and solemn responsibilities of the Christian life. When we intercede for others, we are doing nothing less than standing before the throne of the Almighty God and presenting the needs of fellow human beings to the One who alone can meet those needs. The apostle Paul makes clear that intercession is not an optional addition to our prayer life but a primary obligation. Writing to Timothy, he says, "I exhort therefore, that, first of all, supplications, prayers, intercessions, and giving of thanks, be made for all men" (1 Timothy 2:1). The phrase "first of all" does not merely indicate sequence but priority — intercession for others should occupy a central place in our prayers.

Paul specifies that this intercession should extend to "kings, and for all that are in authority" (1 Timothy 2:2). At the time Paul wrote these words, the Roman emperor was Nero — one of the most cruel and godless rulers in human history, a man who would eventually order Paul's execution. Yet Paul commands the church to pray for this man and for all in authority. This demonstrates a crucial principle: intercession is not limited to those we approve of or those who share our faith. We are to pray for the salvation of all people, for the welfare of our communities and nations, and for the wisdom of those who govern — regardless of their personal character or political alignment.

The theological foundation for intercession is laid out in 1 Timothy 2:5-6: "For there is one God, and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus; Who gave himself a ransom for all." Jesus Christ is the supreme Intercessor — the One who stands between holy God and sinful humanity, who gave His life as a ransom to bridge the infinite gap. When we intercede for others, we are participating in the intercessory ministry of Christ Himself. We do not approach God in our own name or on the basis of our own worthiness; we come in the name of Christ and on the basis of His sacrifice. Our intercession derives its power not from our own righteousness but from the mediation of our great High Priest.

The book of James declares that "the effectual fervent prayer of a righteous man availeth much" (James 5:16). The word translated "effectual fervent" in the King James Version is the Greek word energoumene, from which we derive the English word "energy." It speaks of prayer that is energized, activated, empowered — not by the human spirit alone but by the Holy Spirit working through the believing heart. Such prayer "availeth much" — it accomplishes great things, produces real results, moves the hand that moves the world. James illustrates this with the example of Elijah, who "prayed earnestly that it might not rain: and it rained not on the earth by the space of three years and six months. And he prayed again, and the heaven gave rain" (James 5:17-18). One righteous man's prayer changed the weather patterns of an entire nation for three and a half years. This is the power of intercessory prayer.

Perhaps the most dramatic example of intercession in the Old Testament is Moses standing in the gap for Israel at Mount Sinai, recorded in Exodus 32. While Moses was on the mountain receiving the law from God, the people below had fashioned a golden calf and were engaged in idolatrous worship. God's response was terrifying: "Let me alone, that my wrath may wax hot against them, and that I may consume them: and I will make of thee a great nation" (Exodus 32:10). God offered Moses an extraordinary deal — destroy Israel and start over with Moses as the new Abraham. But Moses refused. Instead, he interceded for the people who had just betrayed God and him.

Moses's intercession in Exodus 32:11-13 is a masterclass in how to pray for others. He does not defend Israel's sin or minimize their guilt. He appeals entirely to God's own character and purposes. First, he appeals to God's power: these are the people "which thou hast brought forth out of the land of Egypt with great power, and with a mighty hand." The implication is that God's reputation is tied to the people He has redeemed. Second, he appeals to God's glory: "Wherefore should the Egyptians speak, and say, For mischief did he bring them out?" God's name would be dishonored among the nations if He destroyed His own people. Third, he appeals to God's faithfulness: "Remember Abraham, Isaac, and Israel, thy servants, to whom thou swarest by thine own self." God had made covenant promises to the patriarchs, and Moses holds God to His own word.

The result is one of the most remarkable statements in Scripture: "And the LORD repented of the evil which he thought to do unto his people" (Exodus 32:14). This does not mean that God changed His mind in the way a human changes his mind — God is immutable and His purposes are eternal. Rather, it means that God, in His sovereign design, had purposed to use Moses's intercession as the means by which His mercy would be extended to Israel. God ordained both the prayer and the answer. Moses's intercession was part of God's plan from the beginning, but it was genuine intercession nonetheless — real prayer producing real results through the real power of a sovereign God.

The prophet Ezekiel reveals the divine longing for intercessors: "And I sought for a man among them, that should make up the hedge, and stand in the gap before me for the land, that I should not destroy it: but I found none" (Ezekiel 22:30). God was looking for an intercessor — someone to stand between His judgment and His people — and found no one. The implication is staggering: nations may perish, communities may be destroyed, individuals may be lost, because no one stood in the gap for them in prayer. Intercessory prayer is not a luxury; it is a necessity. The absence of intercessors has consequences.

The supreme model of intercessory prayer is found in John 17, where the Lord Jesus Christ prays for His disciples and for all who would believe through their testimony: "Neither pray I for these alone, but for them also which shall believe on me through their word" (John 17:20). This means that Jesus prayed for you and for me — centuries before we were born, He held us before the Father in intercession. And what did He pray for? "That they all may be one; as thou, Father, art in me, and I in thee, that they also may be one in us" (John 17:21). Christ's intercession was for the unity, holiness, and preservation of His church. His prayer was heard and continues to be answered as the church endures through all the centuries of history.

The apostle Paul practiced what he preached about intercession. He writes to the Romans, "God is my witness, whom I serve with my spirit in the gospel of his Son, that without ceasing I make mention of you always in my prayers" (Romans 1:9). To the Philippians he says, "Always in every prayer of mine for you all making request with joy" (Philippians 1:4). To the Thessalonians: "We give thanks to God always for you all, making mention of you in our prayers" (1 Thessalonians 1:2). And to Timothy, his spiritual son: "I thank God, whom I serve from my forefathers with pure conscience, that without ceasing I have remembrance of thee in my prayers night and day" (2 Timothy 1:3). Paul did not merely teach about intercession — he practiced it with relentless consistency, mentioning specific churches and individuals by name in his prayers. His example challenges us to move beyond vague, general intercession to specific, persistent, name-by-name prayer for those God has placed in our lives.

The book of Acts records an extraordinary instance of corporate intercession in Acts 12:5-17. When King Herod arrested Peter and intended to execute him after Passover, the church responded not with political lobbying, not with military rescue, but with prayer: "Prayer was made without ceasing of the church unto God for him" (Acts 12:5). The Greek phrase "without ceasing" suggests fervent, sustained, agonizing prayer — the kind of prayer that Epaphras practiced. And God answered dramatically: an angel appeared in the prison, Peter's chains fell off, and he walked out a free man. When Peter arrived at the house where the church was praying, they were so astonished that they thought it was his ghost. They had been praying for his deliverance, but the answer still took them by surprise. This is both a rebuke to our lack of faith and an encouragement to our practice of intercession — God answers even the prayers of those whose faith is imperfect.

As believers, we are called to follow Christ's example and to intercede for others — for our families, our churches, our pastors, our neighbors, our enemies, our nation, and the whole world. We are called to stand in the gap, to make up the hedge, to plead before God on behalf of those who cannot or will not pray for themselves. This is not a passive activity but an aggressive spiritual warfare. When we intercede, we are contending with the powers of darkness for the souls of men and women. We are partnering with God's purposes in history. We are wielding the most powerful weapon in the Christian arsenal — prayer in the name of Jesus Christ.

This biblical foundation is essential. Without it, everything else we study today would be like a house built on sand. But with it, we stand on solid rock.


Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. Matthew Henry wrote extensively on this subject in "A Method for Prayer." The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life.

Matthew Henry (1662-1714) is best known for his monumental Commentary on the Whole Bible, a work that remains one of the most widely consulted Bible commentaries in the English language more than three centuries after its completion. But Henry also produced a deeply practical guide to prayer titled "A Method for Prayer," in which he provides a comprehensive framework for the different elements of prayer — adoration, confession, petition, thanksgiving, and intercession. It is in his treatment of intercession that Henry's pastoral wisdom and biblical depth are particularly evident.

Henry begins his discussion of intercession by establishing its scriptural warrant. He notes that the Bible is filled with examples of believers praying for others, and that God has repeatedly commanded His people to make intercession a regular part of their prayer life. He cites Abraham's intercession for Sodom (Genesis 18), Moses's intercession for Israel (Exodus 32), Samuel's declaration that it would be a sin against God to cease praying for the people (1 Samuel 12:23), and Paul's constant intercession for the churches (Ephesians 1:16; Colossians 1:9; 1 Thessalonians 1:2). From these and many other passages, Henry concludes that intercession is not merely a generous addition to our prayers but a binding duty — a responsibility that we neglect at our own spiritual peril and to the detriment of those around us.

Henry organizes intercessory prayer into several concentric circles, moving outward from those closest to us to those farthest away. The first circle is our own family — spouse, children, parents, siblings. Henry writes that no one is more responsible for praying for our families than we are, and no prayers are more effective than those of a loving parent, spouse, or child who brings their beloved ones before God daily. He particularly emphasizes the duty of parents to pray for their children, noting that the prayers of a godly mother or father often follow children through their entire lives, bearing fruit long after the one who prayed has gone to glory.

The second circle is our local church — the pastor, the elders, the congregation. Henry is particularly insistent on the duty of church members to pray for their ministers. He writes that the work of the ministry is so demanding, so spiritually dangerous, and so dependent on the power of God that no pastor can survive, much less thrive, without the sustained intercession of his flock. He points to Paul's urgent request for prayer in Romans 15:30-31 and Ephesians 6:19-20 as evidence that even an apostle felt the need for the prayers of ordinary believers. Henry observes that churches that pray faithfully for their pastor invariably hear better preaching, receive more faithful pastoral care, and experience greater spiritual vitality than churches that neglect this duty.

The third circle encompasses the broader church of Christ throughout the world. Henry urges believers to pray for persecuted Christians in other lands, for missionaries carrying the gospel to unreached peoples, for churches struggling with false doctrine or internal division, and for the advance of Christ's kingdom in every nation. He writes that intercessory prayer is the way ordinary believers can participate in the great work of global evangelization, even if they never leave their own town. A praying grandmother in a rural village may have a greater impact on the spread of the gospel than a thousand uninspired preachers, because her prayers move the arm of the Almighty.

The fourth circle is the civil authorities and the wider society. Following Paul's instruction in 1 Timothy 2:1-2, Henry counsels believers to pray regularly for kings, magistrates, judges, and all who hold positions of public authority. He argues that the moral and spiritual condition of a nation's leaders profoundly affects the welfare of its citizens, and that prayer is the most effective means the church possesses for influencing the course of public affairs. Henry is realistic about the corruption of earthly governments, but he is also optimistic about the power of prayer to restrain evil, promote justice, and open doors for the gospel.

The fifth and widest circle is the unconverted — those who do not know Christ and are perishing in their sins. Henry writes with great tenderness about the duty of praying for the salvation of unbelievers. He argues that every Christian should have a list of specific individuals — friends, neighbors, coworkers, family members — for whose salvation they pray regularly. He notes that many of the great conversions in church history began not with a dramatic sermon or a miraculous sign but with the quiet, persistent prayers of a faithful intercessor who refused to give up on a lost soul.

Henry provides extensive practical guidance on how to intercede effectively. He teaches that intercession should be informed — we should seek to understand the specific needs of those for whom we pray, not merely offering vague, general requests but bringing specific situations, challenges, and opportunities before God. He teaches that intercession should be earnest — not perfunctory or half-hearted but passionate and urgent, reflecting a genuine love for those we are praying for. He teaches that intercession should be persistent — not a one-time effort but a sustained campaign of prayer that continues until the answer comes.

Henry also addresses the theological question of how intercession works in light of God's sovereignty. If God has already determined all things, why should we pray? Henry answers that God has ordained prayer as the means by which His purposes are accomplished. When we intercede, we are not informing God of something He does not know or persuading Him to do something He was unwilling to do. Rather, we are participating in His eternal plan — we are the instruments through which He has chosen to bestow His blessings. Just as God ordains the preaching of the Word as the means of conversion, so He ordains the prayers of His people as the means of dispensing grace. Prayer does not change God's mind; it is the channel through which God's unchanging purpose flows into the lives of men and women.

Henry's most powerful appeal comes in his treatment of Samuel's declaration: "God forbid that I should sin against the LORD in ceasing to pray for you" (1 Samuel 12:23). Henry writes that to cease praying for others when we have the ability and the opportunity to intercede is itself a sin — a failure of love, a neglect of duty, a squandering of the privilege that the blood of Christ has purchased for us. He challenges believers to examine their prayer lives and ask whether intercession occupies the prominent place that Scripture assigns to it. If our prayers are entirely focused on our own needs, our own problems, our own desires — then our prayer life is impoverished, self-centered, and falling short of the biblical standard.

Henry concludes by pointing to the ongoing intercession of Christ at the right hand of the Father as the ultimate encouragement for our own intercessory ministry. Hebrews 7:25 declares that Christ "ever liveth to make intercession" for His people. He did not cease interceding when He ascended to heaven; He continues to present our needs before the Father with perfect love and perfect knowledge. When we intercede for others, we are joining our feeble voices to the mighty intercession of our exalted Savior. We pray in His name, by His authority, through His blood, and in the power of His Spirit. And because He ever lives and never fails, our intercession can never be in vain.

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.

Take time with each question. The Puritans valued what they called "holy conference," the practice of discussing spiritual truths together. Do not settle for quick, surface answers. Push deeper. Ask follow-up questions. Listen carefully to one another.

Application is where truth becomes life. It is not enough to know what 1 Timothy 2:1-6; James 5:16; Exodus 32:9-14; Ezekiel 22:30; John 17:20-23 says. We must ask: how does this change the way I live today? How does this shape my thoughts, my words, my actions? The Puritans insisted that a truth that does not transform is a truth that has not been truly understood.


Let us explore additional Scripture and Puritan wisdom on the subject of intercessory prayer to deepen our understanding.

The apostle Paul's own intercessory prayers, recorded throughout his epistles, provide a rich model for our own intercession. In Ephesians 1:17-19, Paul prays for the believers at Ephesus: "That the God of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of glory, may give unto you the spirit of wisdom and revelation in the knowledge of him: The eyes of your understanding being enlightened; that ye may know what is the hope of his calling, and what the riches of the glory of his inheritance in the saints, And what is the exceeding greatness of his power to us-ward who believe." Notice what Paul does not pray for. He does not pray for their health, their finances, their comfort, or their ease. He prays for spiritual illumination — that they would know God better, understand their calling more deeply, and grasp the power available to them in Christ. This teaches us that the highest and most important intercession is spiritual intercession — prayer for the spiritual growth, understanding, and maturity of those we love.

In Colossians 1:9-12, Paul offers another model intercessory prayer: "For this cause we also, since the day we heard it, do not cease to pray for you, and to desire that ye might be filled with the knowledge of his will in all wisdom and spiritual understanding; That ye might walk worthy of the Lord unto all pleasing, being fruitful in every good work, and increasing in the knowledge of God; Strengthened with all might, according to his glorious power, unto all patience and longsuffering with joyfulness; Giving thanks unto the Father, which hath made us meet to be partakers of the inheritance of the saints in light." Again, Paul's intercession focuses on spiritual realities: knowledge of God's will, worthy conduct, fruitfulness, strength, patience, and gratitude. These Pauline prayers should reshape our intercessory priorities. While there is nothing wrong with praying for physical health, material provision, and earthly blessings, the deepest needs of those we love are always spiritual.

The Puritan pastor David Clarkson (1622-1686), who succeeded John Owen as pastor of the Leadenhall Street congregation in London, wrote with great insight about the duty and privilege of intercessory prayer. Clarkson was a scholar of remarkable depth — he studied at Trinity College, Cambridge, and was a Fellow of Clare Hall — but his learning never made him cold or abstract. His sermons and writings pulse with pastoral warmth and practical application. Clarkson teaches that intercession is one of the primary ways in which believers fulfill the law of love. When we pray for others, we are loving them in the most effective way possible, because we are bringing their needs to the One who alone can truly help them. A friend who prays for you is doing more for you than a friend who gives you money, because money can only address temporal needs while prayer addresses eternal ones.

Clarkson also teaches that intercessory prayer produces a remarkable spiritual effect in the one who prays. When we regularly intercede for others, our hearts are enlarged, our self-centeredness is broken, and our love for our brothers and sisters in Christ deepens. Clarkson observes that it is nearly impossible to pray sincerely for someone and simultaneously harbor bitterness, resentment, or indifference toward them. Intercession melts hard hearts, heals broken relationships, and binds believers together in bonds of mutual love and concern.

Consider the striking example of Job's intercession at the end of his great trial. After Job endured devastating losses and the misguided counsel of his three friends, God instructed Eliphaz, Bildad, and Zophar to go to Job and offer sacrifices: "And my servant Job shall pray for you: for him will I accept" (Job 42:8). And then we read one of the most beautiful statements in the entire book: "And the LORD turned the captivity of Job, when he prayed for his friends" (Job 42:10). Job's own deliverance came when he interceded for others — even for the friends who had added to his suffering with their accusations and bad theology. This teaches us that intercession is not merely a duty but a means of our own spiritual liberation. When we stop focusing exclusively on our own problems and begin to carry the burdens of others before God, something breaks free in our own souls.

The prophet Samuel declared to the people of Israel: "Moreover as for me, God forbid that I should sin against the LORD in ceasing to pray for you: but I will teach you the good and the right way" (1 Samuel 12:23). Samuel regarded the failure to intercede as a sin against the Lord. This is a sobering declaration that should cause every Christian to examine their prayer life. How much of our praying is devoted to the needs of others? How many people depend on our intercession, whether they know it or not? And what blessings are being withheld from our families, our churches, and our communities because we have ceased to stand in the gap?


Let us now consider practical steps for developing a consistent ministry of intercessory prayer.

First, create an intercessory prayer list organized by the categories Matthew Henry identifies — family, local church, global church, civil authorities, and the unconverted. Write specific names and specific requests under each category. Update the list regularly as circumstances change and prayers are answered.

Second, assign different categories to different days of the week. For example, pray for your family on Monday, your local church on Tuesday, missionaries and the global church on Wednesday, civil authorities on Thursday, and unsaved friends and neighbors on Friday. This prevents the feeling of being overwhelmed by trying to pray for everyone every day while ensuring that all categories receive regular attention.

Third, follow Paul's model by making spiritual growth the primary focus of your intercession. Pray for the faith, holiness, wisdom, and perseverance of those you love, even more than you pray for their health and material needs. The greatest gift you can give another person through prayer is not a comfortable life but a deeper knowledge of Christ.

Fourth, intercede for your pastor and church leaders. As Henry emphasized, the work of the ministry is impossible without the prayer support of the congregation. Your pastor faces spiritual attacks, emotional burdens, and intellectual challenges that you may never see. Your faithful intercession may be the unseen support that sustains his ministry through the darkest seasons.


Now we move to our afternoon activity, where we put truth into practice.

Students will create a personal 'Intercessory Prayer Guide' using Matthew Henry's concentric circles framework. On a large sheet of paper, students will draw five concentric circles labeled (from inner to outer): Family, Local Church, Global Church, Civil Authorities, and the Unconverted. In each circle, they will write the names of specific people they intend to pray for, along with specific prayer requests for each person based on Scripture. Students will also compose a model intercessory prayer for each circle, incorporating Scripture promises and following the pattern of Moses's intercession (appealing to God's character, glory, and faithfulness). The completed guide will serve as a daily prayer tool for the remainder of the school year.

Materials needed: Large poster paper or card stock, colored pens and markers, printed Scripture promise cards, compass or circular templates for drawing concentric circles, lined paper for writing model prayers, plastic sleeves or laminating sheets to protect the finished guide

As you complete this activity, think about how it connects to what you studied in today's lesson. The goal is not just to do something, but to reinforce the truths you have been learning. The Puritans believed that education should engage the whole person: mind, heart, and hands.

Take your time with this activity. Quality matters more than speed. When you are finished, reflect on what you learned through the process of doing. Often we understand something far better after we have tried to put it into practice.


One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

Matthew Henry's father, Philip Henry, was one of the ministers ejected in the Great Ejection of 1662. Matthew grew up witnessing his father's faithfulness under persecution and the power of his intercessory prayers for their family and nation. Study the Henry family as a case study in generational faithfulness: how did Philip Henry's example of piety and prayer shape his son Matthew's life and ministry? Trace the broader pattern of how the Puritan commitment to family prayer and intercession influenced subsequent generations of English Christianity.

Literature Connection:

Matthew Henry's Bible commentary is renowned for its devotional warmth, practical application, and memorable phrases. Many well-known sayings are attributed to Henry. Analyze Henry's commentary style: how does he move from exegesis (what the text means) to application (what it means for us)? How does his writing differ from modern academic commentaries? Compose your own brief commentary on a short passage of Scripture, using Henry's method of combining careful exposition with warm, practical devotion.

Science Connection:

Modern research on social networks and the 'ripple effect' of human behavior provides an interesting parallel to the biblical concept of intercession. Studies have shown that actions, attitudes, and behaviors spread through social networks in ways that affect people several degrees of separation away. Consider how this 'network effect' parallels the biblical teaching that one person's prayer can affect the lives of many others. What does social network science suggest about the interconnectedness of human communities, and how does this relate to the biblical call to pray for one another?

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.

Summary of Key Truths:

Let us gather the central teachings from today's study. First, intercessory prayer is not optional but obligatory — Samuel declared it would be a sin to cease praying for others. Second, the theological foundation for intercession is the mediation of Christ — we pray for others through the same Mediator who gave His life as a ransom for all. Third, Moses's intercession at Sinai demonstrates that our prayers should be grounded in God's character and promises, not in the merit of those we pray for. Fourth, God actively seeks intercessors — the tragedy of Ezekiel 22:30 is that He looked for someone to stand in the gap and found no one. Fifth, Henry's concentric circles provide a comprehensive framework for organizing our intercessory ministry — from family to local church to global church to civil authorities to the unconverted. Sixth, the highest intercession is spiritual intercession — praying for the faith, growth, and perseverance of those we love, following the model of Paul's epistolary prayers. Seventh, intercession transforms the one who prays — breaking selfishness, enlarging the heart, and deepening love for others. And eighth, Christ Himself is the supreme Intercessor who "ever liveth to make intercession" for us — and when we intercede for others, we are joining our weak voices to His mighty advocacy at the right hand of the Father.

Let this final truth sustain you: you are never more like Christ than when you are on your knees praying for someone else.

Memory Verse:

"Confess your faults one to another, and pray one for another, that ye may be healed. The effectual fervent prayer of a righteous man availeth much." -- James 5:16

Read this verse aloud five times. Then close your eyes and try to recite it from memory. Write it out by hand on a card or in your journal. The Puritans memorized vast portions of Scripture, and it was one of the great sources of their spiritual strength. Even one verse, truly hidden in the heart, can sustain you in times of trial.

Additional Memory Passage:

"Moreover as for me, God forbid that I should sin against the LORD in ceasing to pray for you." -- 1 Samuel 12:23

Let Samuel's solemn declaration ring in your ears as a constant reminder that intercession is not merely a suggestion but a sacred responsibility. To cease praying for those God has placed in your life is to sin against the Lord who appointed you as their intercessor. If the prophet Samuel — a man of unparalleled integrity and faithfulness — considered it a sin to stop interceding, how much more should we, who have received so much grace, devote ourselves to praying for others?
