PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 9
"Of Justification and Adoption"
The Westminster Standards — Lesson 9


Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we study two of the most glorious doctrines in all of Christian theology — justification and adoption. In justification, God declares guilty sinners to be righteous in His sight. In adoption, God receives condemned rebels into His family as beloved children. Together, these doctrines describe the astonishing transformation that takes place when a sinner is united to Christ by faith. Let us pray and begin.

O righteous Father, we come before Thee as those who have no righteousness of our own. We deserve only Thy wrath and curse. Yet through the gospel, Thou hast offered us the righteousness of Thy Son and the privilege of being called Thy children. As we study these doctrines today, fill us with wonder, gratitude, and joy. Help us to understand the riches of Thy grace, that we might rest more fully in Christ and live more faithfully for Thy glory. In the name of Jesus Christ, our righteousness and our elder Brother, Amen.


Part One: The Application of Redemption — How Salvation Reaches Us

Before we examine justification and adoption specifically, we must understand where these blessings fit within the broader framework of salvation. Christ accomplished redemption on the cross. But how does that redemption become ours? How does the work performed on a hill outside Jerusalem two thousand years ago reach into the twenty-first century and transform individual human hearts?

The Westminster Standards answer this question by distinguishing between the accomplishment of redemption (what Christ did for us) and the application of redemption (what the Holy Spirit does in us). The Shorter Catechism, Question 29, provides an overview: "We are made partakers of the redemption purchased by Christ, by the effectual application of it to us by his Holy Spirit."

Salvation is Trinitarian from beginning to end. The Father plans it in His eternal decree of election. The Son accomplishes it through His life, death, and resurrection. And the Spirit applies it to the hearts of the elect, bringing them into living, personal union with Christ. Without the Spirit's work, the cross would remain a historical event with no personal impact. It is the Spirit who takes the blessings purchased by Christ and actually delivers them to individual sinners.

The Shorter Catechism, Question 30, teaches: "The Spirit applieth to us the redemption purchased by Christ, by working faith in us, and thereby uniting us to Christ in our effectual calling." Faith is the instrument by which we are united to Christ. And union with Christ is the fountainhead from which all the blessings of salvation flow — including justification and adoption.

The Larger Catechism, Question 69, elaborates on this union: "The communion in grace which the members of the invisible church have with Christ, is their partaking of the virtue of his mediation, in their justification, adoption, sanctification, and whatever else in this life manifests their union with him." Every blessing of salvation is a manifestation of our union with Christ. We are justified because we are in Christ. We are adopted because we are in Christ. We are sanctified because we are in Christ. Apart from Him, we have nothing. In Him, we have everything.

The order in which the Spirit applies redemption is important. The Westminster Standards present it as follows: effectual calling leads to faith and repentance, which result in justification and adoption, which are followed by sanctification, which culminates in glorification. This is sometimes called the ordo salutis — the order of salvation. While these blessings are logically distinct, they are temporally simultaneous in many cases. The moment a person is effectually called and believes in Christ, that person is immediately justified and adopted. There is no interval of time between faith and justification.

Part Two: Effectual Calling — The First Step

The Shorter Catechism, Question 31, asks: "What is effectual calling?" and answers: "Effectual calling is the work of God's Spirit, whereby, convincing us of our sin and misery, enlightening our minds in the knowledge of Christ, and renewing our wills, he doth persuade and enable us to embrace Jesus Christ, freely offered to us in the gospel."

Effectual calling is the sovereign, irresistible work of the Holy Spirit by which He brings the elect from spiritual death to spiritual life. It is distinguished from the general, external call of the gospel — the invitation that goes out to all who hear the preaching of the Word. The general call may be heard and rejected. The effectual call is always successful. Those whom God effectually calls are not merely given an opportunity to believe — they are given the ability and the desire to believe.

Notice the four elements of effectual calling. First, the Spirit convinces us of our sin and misery — showing us that we are guilty before God, helpless to save ourselves, and in desperate need of a Saviour. Second, the Spirit enlightens our minds in the knowledge of Christ — opening our eyes to see the beauty, sufficiency, and suitability of Jesus Christ as our Redeemer. Third, the Spirit renews our wills — changing our hearts so that we actually desire to come to Christ and submit to Him as Lord. Fourth, the Spirit persuades and enables us to embrace Christ — giving us the faith by which we lay hold of Christ and all His benefits.

The great Puritan theologian Thomas Goodwin, in his work on the Object and Acts of Justifying Faith, explored the nature of effectual calling with extraordinary depth. Goodwin described the Spirit's work as a divine illumination — like the rising of the sun that dispels the darkness of night. Before the Spirit's call, the sinner is in darkness — unable to see Christ, unable to desire Christ, unable to come to Christ. But when the Spirit shines His light into the darkened heart, everything changes. Christ is seen in His true glory. Sin is seen in its true horror. Grace is seen in its true wonder. And the sinner, drawn irresistibly by the beauty of what he now sees, comes willingly and joyfully to Christ.

The Apostle Paul describes his own effectual calling in these terms: "For God, who commanded the light to shine out of darkness, hath shined in our hearts, to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ" (2 Corinthians 4:6). Just as God created physical light by His sovereign command in Genesis 1, so He creates spiritual light in the hearts of His elect by the same sovereign power. Effectual calling is nothing less than a new creation.

Part Three: Justification — The Doctrine That Shook the World

We come now to the doctrine of justification — the doctrine that Martin Luther called "the article by which the church stands or falls," and which the Puritans regarded as the very heart and soul of the gospel.

The Shorter Catechism, Question 33, asks: "What is justification?" and answers: "Justification is an act of God's free grace, wherein he pardoneth all our sins, and accepteth us as righteous in his sight, only for the righteousness of Christ imputed to us, and received by faith alone."

The Larger Catechism, Question 70, provides a fuller definition: "Justification is an act of God's free grace unto sinners, in which he pardoneth all their sins, accepteth and accounteth their persons righteous in his sight; not for any thing wrought in them, or done by them, but only for the perfect obedience and full satisfaction of Christ, by God imputed to them, and received by faith alone."

The Westminster Confession, Chapter 11, Section 1, states: "Those whom God effectually calleth, he also freely justifieth: not by infusing righteousness into them, but by pardoning their sins, and by accounting and accepting their persons as righteous: not for any thing wrought in them, or done by them, but for Christ's sake alone: not by imputing faith itself, the act of believing, or any other evangelical obedience, to them as their righteousness; but by imputing the obedience and satisfaction of Christ unto them, they receiving and resting on him and his righteousness by faith: which faith they have not of themselves; it is the gift of God."

This is one of the richest and most carefully worded paragraphs in the entire Confession. Let us unpack it piece by piece.

First, justification is "an act of God's free grace." It is not a process but an act — a single, instantaneous, once-for-all divine declaration. Unlike sanctification, which unfolds gradually over the course of the Christian life, justification happens in a moment. The moment a sinner believes in Christ, that sinner is justified — fully, completely, and irrevocably.

And it is an act of "free grace." It is not earned. It is not deserved. It is not the reward for good behavior. It is a gift — freely given by a gracious God to undeserving sinners.

Second, justification involves two distinct elements: the pardon of sin and the imputation of righteousness. In pardoning our sins, God removes the guilt of every offense we have ever committed or will ever commit. Past sins, present sins, future sins — all are covered by the blood of Christ. The record of debt that stood against us has been nailed to the cross (Colossians 2:14). In imputing Christ's righteousness to us, God credits to our account the perfect obedience of Jesus Christ. Christ's lifelong, flawless keeping of God's law is counted as ours. When God looks at the justified sinner, He sees not the sinner's guilt but Christ's righteousness.

This double aspect of justification was a point of particular emphasis for the Reformers and the Puritans. It is not enough merely to have our sins forgiven. Forgiveness alone would leave us morally neutral — no longer guilty, but with no positive righteousness to commend us to God. What we need is not merely the absence of guilt but the presence of righteousness. And that is precisely what justification provides. In Christ, we have both: the removal of our sins and the provision of a perfect righteousness.

Third, justification is "not for any thing wrought in them, or done by them, but for Christ's sake alone." The ground of justification — the reason God declares us righteous — is not anything in us. It is not our faith, our repentance, our good works, our religious observances, or our moral improvement. It is Christ alone — His obedience and His sacrifice. The Confession is emphatic on this point, rejecting every form of human merit as a basis for justification.

Fourth, justification is received "by faith alone." Faith is the instrument — the means — by which we receive the gift of justification. But faith itself is not the ground of justification. It is not our faith that makes us righteous but Christ's righteousness received through faith. Faith is like the empty hand of a beggar that receives the gift of a king. The value is in the gift, not in the hand that receives it.

And even this faith, the Confession adds, "they have not of themselves; it is the gift of God." Even the instrument of reception is a divine gift. We cannot produce saving faith by our own effort. It is worked in us by the Holy Spirit. "For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: Not of works, lest any man should boast" (Ephesians 2:8-9).

Part Four: The Historical Importance of Justification

The doctrine of justification by faith alone was the material cause of the Protestant Reformation. When Martin Luther, an Augustinian monk and professor of theology at the University of Wittenberg in Saxony, discovered this truth through his study of Paul's Epistle to the Romans in the years between 1513 and 1517, it transformed his life and eventually transformed the world.

Luther had spent years trying to earn God's favor through monastic discipline, fasting, confession, and good works. But the more he tried, the more he despaired. He knew that God's standard was perfection, and he knew that he fell grievously short. The phrase "the righteousness of God" in Romans 1:17 filled him with dread, for he understood it as the righteousness by which God judges and condemns sinners.

But then, through careful study, Luther came to understand that "the righteousness of God" in Romans 1:17 is not the righteousness by which God condemns but the righteousness that God freely gives to sinners through faith. "For therein is the righteousness of God revealed from faith to faith: as it is written, The just shall live by faith." Luther later wrote that when he understood this truth, he felt as if the gates of paradise had been opened to him. The entire Scripture appeared in a new light. The gospel — the good news that God justifies the ungodly through faith in Christ — became the most precious truth in the universe.

John Calvin, in his Institutes of the Christian Religion, described justification as "the main hinge on which religion turns." Calvin devoted extensive attention to the doctrine, arguing that the righteousness of Christ is not merely exemplary (a model for us to follow) but substitutionary (a righteousness performed in our place and credited to our account). Calvin insisted that the distinction between justification and sanctification must be maintained, even though the two are inseparable in experience. Justification changes our legal status before God. Sanctification changes our moral character. Both are essential, but they must not be confused.

The Council of Trent, convened by the Roman Catholic Church from 1545 to 1563 in response to the Reformation, explicitly rejected the Protestant doctrine of justification by faith alone. Trent defined justification as a process involving both God's grace and human cooperation — including faith, works, and sacramental participation. The Council anathematized (pronounced a curse upon) anyone who taught that justification is by faith alone or that the righteousness of Christ is imputed rather than infused.

The Westminster Assembly, meeting less than a century after Trent, crafted its teaching on justification in deliberate contrast to the Roman position. The Confession's insistence that justification is "not by infusing righteousness into them, but by pardoning their sins, and by accounting and accepting their persons as righteous" was a direct rejection of the Tridentine doctrine. And the Confession's repeated emphasis on "faith alone," "Christ's sake alone," and "not for any thing wrought in them, or done by them" was designed to close every door to human merit.

Part Five: The Imputation of Christ's Righteousness — A Deeper Look

The doctrine of imputation is the heart of the Reformed understanding of justification. Imputation means "to reckon to one's account." In justification, the righteousness of Christ is imputed — reckoned, credited, counted — to the believer. It is not a righteousness that the believer possesses inherently. It is a righteousness that belongs to Christ and is graciously transferred to the believer's account.

The Apostle Paul develops this doctrine most fully in Romans chapters 3 through 5 and in his letters to the Galatians and Philippians. In Romans 3:21-22, Paul writes: "But now the righteousness of God without the law is manifested, being witnessed by the law and the prophets; Even the righteousness of God which is by faith of Jesus Christ unto all and upon all them that believe." This righteousness is not achieved by keeping the law. It is received by faith in Jesus Christ.

In Romans 4, Paul uses the example of Abraham to illustrate justification by faith. "For what saith the scripture? Abraham believed God, and it was counted unto him for righteousness. Now to him that worketh is the reward not reckoned of grace, but of debt. But to him that worketh not, but believeth on him that justifieth the ungodly, his faith is counted for righteousness" (Romans 4:3-5). Abraham was not justified by his works. He was justified by faith — and that faith was "counted" (imputed) to him as righteousness.

In Philippians 3:8-9, Paul makes his own confession: "Yea doubtless, and I count all things but loss for the excellency of the knowledge of Christ Jesus my Lord: for whom I have suffered the loss of all things, and do count them but dung, that I may win Christ, And be found in him, not having mine own righteousness, which is of the law, but that which is through the faith of Christ, the righteousness which is of God by faith."

The Puritan theologian Thomas Gataker, who served as a member of the Westminster Assembly and was known for his learning and his piety, wrote extensively on the imputation of Christ's righteousness. Gataker argued that imputation involves a "wonderful exchange" — a concept rooted in the teaching of the early church fathers and developed by the Reformers. In this exchange, Christ takes our sin and we receive His righteousness. As Paul expresses it in 2 Corinthians 5:21: "For he hath made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin; that we might be made the righteousness of God in him."

This exchange is not a legal fiction, as critics have sometimes charged. It is a real, covenantal transaction based on the union between Christ and His people. Because believers are united to Christ by faith, what is His becomes theirs, and what is theirs was borne by Him. His righteousness is truly credited to their account, and their sins were truly laid upon Him at the cross.

Part Six: Faith and Works — Resolving the Apparent Tension

One of the most persistent objections to the doctrine of justification by faith alone is the apparent tension between Paul and James. Paul writes, "Therefore we conclude that a man is justified by faith without the deeds of the law" (Romans 3:28). James writes, "Ye see then how that by works a man is justified, and not by faith only" (James 2:24). Do these two apostles contradict each other?

The Westminster Confession, Chapter 11, Section 2, addresses this question: "Faith, thus receiving and resting on Christ and his righteousness, is the alone instrument of justification; yet is it not alone in the person justified, but is ever accompanied with all other saving graces, and is no dead faith, but worketh by love."

The resolution is that Paul and James are addressing different questions. Paul is asking: On what basis are we declared righteous before God? His answer: By faith alone, apart from works. James is asking: What kind of faith saves? His answer: A living faith that produces works — not a dead, barren, merely intellectual faith. Paul is concerned with the ground of justification (Christ's righteousness received by faith). James is concerned with the evidence of justification (good works that demonstrate the reality of faith).

The Puritans were careful to maintain this balance. They insisted with all their might that justification is by faith alone — that no human work of any kind contributes to our acceptance before God. But they also insisted that true faith is never alone — it is always accompanied by repentance, love, obedience, and good works. As the Reformers liked to say: "We are justified by faith alone, but the faith that justifies is never alone."

Thomas Watson, in his commentary on the Shorter Catechism titled A Body of Divinity, published in 1692, illustrated this with a vivid image. He compared justifying faith to the hand that receives a gift. The hand that receives a gift may also be a hand that works, writes, and serves. But it is not the working, writing, or serving that receives the gift — it is the empty hand, open and extended. So faith, while it produces works, does not justify by producing works. It justifies by receiving Christ.

Part Seven: Justification and the Roman Catholic Controversy

It is important for homeschool students to understand the historical controversy surrounding justification, for it remains one of the most significant theological disagreements in the history of Christianity.

The Roman Catholic Church, as defined at the Council of Trent (1545-1563), teaches that justification is a process by which God infuses righteousness into the sinner, gradually making the sinner righteous through the cooperation of divine grace and human effort. According to Rome, justification begins at baptism, can be increased by good works and participation in the sacraments, can be lost through mortal sin, and must be restored through the sacrament of penance. Good works, in the Roman system, are meritorious — they contribute to the sinner's justification and earn an increase of grace.

The Protestant Reformers — Luther, Calvin, Zwingli, Bucer, Cranmer, Knox, and the rest — rejected this understanding root and branch. They insisted that justification is not a process of making sinners righteous but a declaration that sinners are righteous — a forensic, legal act in which God, the righteous Judge, pronounces a verdict of "not guilty" over the believing sinner. The basis of this verdict is not the sinner's own righteousness (whether infused or achieved) but the alien righteousness of Christ — a righteousness that belongs to another and is credited to the sinner's account through faith.

The Westminster Assembly sided decisively with the Reformers. The Confession's language is carefully crafted to exclude the Roman Catholic position at every point. Justification is "not by infusing righteousness" (rejecting Rome's doctrine of infused grace). It is "not for any thing wrought in them, or done by them" (rejecting the merit of human works). It is "for Christ's sake alone" (rejecting every mediator other than Christ). It is received "by faith alone" (rejecting the addition of works as a condition of justification). And even faith itself is "the gift of God" (rejecting the idea that the sinner contributes anything to the process).

The Puritan Richard Baxter, who ministered at Kidderminster in Worcestershire from 1647 to 1661, provoked considerable controversy within the Reformed camp by proposing a modified view of justification that appeared to some to compromise the doctrine of sola fide. Baxter argued that faith includes within it an element of obedience — that the "faith" by which we are justified is not mere intellectual assent but a comprehensive act of surrender to Christ as Lord. While Baxter's intentions were pastoral (he wanted to prevent the abuse of justification as a license for lawlessness), many of his fellow Puritans — notably John Owen, Thomas Gataker, and the theologians of the Antinomian controversy — insisted that any introduction of obedience into the definition of justifying faith was a dangerous step toward Rome.

This historical debate reminds us of the importance of precision in theology. The words we use matter. The distinctions we draw matter. The difference between "justified by faith that works" and "justified by faith and works" may seem small, but it is the difference between the gospel and a counterfeit. The Westminster Standards stand as a monument to the importance of getting this doctrine exactly right.

The great Scottish theologian James Buchanan, whose magisterial work The Doctrine of Justification was published in 1867, surveyed the entire history of the doctrine from the apostolic age to his own day and concluded that the Protestant doctrine of justification by faith alone, as set forth in the Westminster Confession, represents the teaching of Scripture, the faith of the early church, and the rediscovery of the Reformers. Buchanan wrote that justification by faith alone is not a theological innovation but a return to the original gospel preached by the apostles and received by the first Christians.

Part Eight: The Blessings of Justification

The Shorter Catechism, Question 36, teaches: "The benefits which in this life do accompany or flow from justification, adoption, and sanctification, are, assurance of God's love, peace of conscience, joy in the Holy Ghost, increase of grace, and perseverance therein to the end."

Let us consider some of these blessings as they relate specifically to justification.

Peace with God: "Therefore being justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ" (Romans 5:1). Before justification, the sinner was at war with God — an enemy, an object of wrath. After justification, the war is over. Peace has been established — not a fragile truce but an eternal, unbreakable peace founded upon the finished work of Christ.

No condemnation: "There is therefore now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1). The justified sinner can never be condemned. The verdict has already been rendered: "Not guilty." And because it is God's verdict — the verdict of the supreme Judge of the universe — it can never be overturned.

Access to God: "By whom also we have access by faith into this grace wherein we stand, and rejoice in hope of the glory of God" (Romans 5:2). The justified sinner has access to God — not as a terrified criminal before a wrathful judge, but as a beloved child before a gracious Father.

Assurance of final glory: "Moreover whom he did predestinate, them he also called: and whom he called, them he also justified: and whom he justified, them he also glorified" (Romans 8:30). The golden chain of salvation is unbreakable. Those whom God justifies, He will certainly glorify. There are no missing links. Justification is the guarantee of eternal glory.

Part Eight: Adoption — Received into God's Family

We turn now to the second great doctrine of this lesson: adoption. The Shorter Catechism, Question 34, asks: "What is adoption?" and answers: "Adoption is an act of God's free grace, whereby we are received into the number, and have a right to all the privileges, of the sons of God."

The Larger Catechism, Question 74, provides a fuller definition: "Adoption is an act of the free grace of God, in and for his only Son Jesus Christ, whereby all those that are justified are received into the number of his children, have his name put upon them, the Spirit of his Son given to them, are under his fatherly care and dispensations, admitted to all the liberties and privileges of the sons of God, made heirs of all the promises, and fellow-heirs with Christ in glory."

The Westminster Confession, Chapter 12, states: "All those that are justified, God vouchsafeth, in and for his only Son Jesus Christ, to make partakers of the grace of adoption: by which they are taken into the number, and enjoy the liberties and privileges of the children of God; have his name put upon them; receive the Spirit of adoption; have access to the throne of grace with boldness; are enabled to cry, Abba, Father; are pitied, protected, provided for, and chastened by him as by a father; yet never cast off, but sealed to the day of redemption, and inherit the promises, as heirs of everlasting salvation."

Adoption is one of the most neglected and underappreciated doctrines in the Christian faith. In justification, we are pardoned criminals. In adoption, we are welcomed children. In justification, God acts as Judge. In adoption, God acts as Father. In justification, we receive a new legal status. In adoption, we receive a new family identity. Both are acts of God's free grace. Both are based entirely on the work of Christ. Both are received through faith. But they address different dimensions of our salvation and provide different blessings.

The Apostle Paul develops the doctrine of adoption in several of his letters. In Galatians 4:4-7 he writes: "But when the fulness of the time was come, God sent forth his Son, made of a woman, made under the law, To redeem them that were under the law, that we might receive the adoption of sons. And because ye are sons, God hath sent forth the Spirit of his Son into your hearts, crying, Abba, Father. Wherefore thou art no more a servant, but a son; and if a son, then an heir of God through Christ."

And in Romans 8:14-17: "For as many as are led by the Spirit of God, they are the sons of God. For ye have not received the spirit of bondage again to fear; but ye have received the Spirit of adoption, whereby we cry, Abba, Father. The Spirit itself beareth witness with our spirit, that we are the children of God: And if children, then heirs; heirs of God, and joint-heirs with Christ; if so be that we suffer with him, that we may be also glorified together."

Part Nine: The Privileges of Adoption

The Westminster Confession and Catechisms enumerate several specific privileges that belong to adopted children of God.

First, the name of God is put upon them. They bear their Father's name. They are identified as His. They belong to His family. "Behold, what manner of love the Father hath bestowed upon us, that we should be called the sons of God" (1 John 3:1).

Second, they receive the Spirit of adoption. The Holy Spirit is given to believers as the Spirit of adoption — the divine witness within their hearts that they truly are God's children. The Spirit produces in them the cry "Abba, Father" — the intimate, trusting, affectionate address of a child to its parent. This is not a cry that unregenerate people can produce. It is the supernatural work of the Spirit in the hearts of those who have been born again.

Third, they have access to the throne of grace with boldness. Adopted children can come to God at any time, in any place, with any need. They are not turned away. They are not ignored. They are welcomed — warmly, eagerly, lovingly — by a Father who delights to hear their prayers. "Let us therefore come boldly unto the throne of grace, that we may obtain mercy, and find grace to help in time of need" (Hebrews 4:16).

Fourth, they are pitied, protected, provided for, and chastened by God as by a Father. God treats His adopted children with the tenderness of a perfect Father. He pities them in their weaknesses: "Like as a father pitieth his children, so the LORD pitieth them that fear him" (Psalm 103:13). He protects them from their enemies. He provides for their needs: "But my God shall supply all your need according to his riches in glory by Christ Jesus" (Philippians 4:19). And He disciplines them when they stray — not out of anger, but out of love: "For whom the Lord loveth he chasteneth, and scourgeth every son whom he receiveth" (Hebrews 12:6).

Fifth, they are never cast off. This is one of the most comforting of all the privileges of adoption. An adopted child of God can never be disowned. The relationship is permanent, irrevocable, and eternal. God does not adopt children only to abandon them later. He seals them by His Spirit for the day of redemption (Ephesians 4:30). He keeps them by His power through faith unto salvation (1 Peter 1:5). Nothing in all creation can separate them from the love of God which is in Christ Jesus our Lord (Romans 8:38-39).

Sixth, they are heirs of everlasting salvation. As children of God, believers are heirs — they have a right to the Father's estate. And what an estate it is! Not earthly riches that perish, but "an inheritance incorruptible, and undefiled, and that fadeth not away, reserved in heaven for you" (1 Peter 1:4). They are heirs of God and joint-heirs with Christ — sharing in the same inheritance that belongs to the eternal Son of God Himself.

Part Eleven: The Biblical Background of Adoption

To fully appreciate the doctrine of adoption, it helps to understand its background in the ancient world. In the Greco-Roman world of the first century, adoption was a well-established legal practice with profound implications. When a Roman father adopted a son, the adopted child received all the rights, privileges, and responsibilities of a natural-born child. The adopted son received a new name, became heir to the father's estate, and was treated in every legal respect as if he had been born into the family. Previous debts were canceled. Previous legal obligations to the birth family were dissolved. The adopted son was, in the eyes of the law, a new person with a new identity.

The Apostle Paul, writing to churches in the Roman world, drew upon this cultural background to explain the spiritual reality of the believer's adoption by God. When God adopts sinners into His family, they receive a new name — they are called children of God. Their previous debts (their sins) are canceled. Their previous bondage (to sin, Satan, and the law's condemnation) is dissolved. They become heirs of God's estate — an inheritance that is incorruptible, undefiled, and that fadeth not away. They are, in the deepest possible sense, new people with a new identity.

Part Twelve: Puritan Reflections on Adoption

The Puritan who wrote most extensively and most beautifully about adoption was the Scottish theologian Thomas Boston, who ministered in the rural parish of Ettrick in the Scottish Borders from 1707 until his death in 1732. Boston's great work, Human Nature in Its Fourfold State, devotes profound attention to the privileges of the believer's adoption. Boston urged his readers to meditate upon the wonder of their new identity: "Consider what ye were by nature — children of wrath, children of the devil, heirs of hell. And consider what ye are by grace — children of God, heirs of heaven, brethren of Christ."

John Owen, in his Communion with God, published in 1657, wrote about the believer's fellowship with the Father specifically in terms of the Father's love for His adopted children. Owen distinguished between the love of the Father, the grace of the Son, and the fellowship of the Holy Spirit (drawing on 2 Corinthians 13:14). The Father's love, Owen argued, is the love of a parent for a child — tender, watchful, protective, and enduring. To commune with the Father is to rest in His love, to trust His care, and to delight in His presence.

Jeremiah Burroughs, the Puritan preacher who served in London during the 1640s, observed that one of the greatest evidences of adoption is the "filial spirit" — the spirit of a child rather than a slave. A slave obeys out of fear. A child obeys out of love. A slave dreads punishment. A child trusts in the father's goodness. A slave looks for ways to escape. A child runs toward the father. When believers find within their hearts a genuine love for God — not merely a fear of His punishment but a desire for His presence, a longing for His smile, a grief at displeasing Him — they may take it as evidence that the Spirit of adoption is at work within them.

Part Thirteen: The Eternal Security of the Adopted Child

One of the most precious aspects of the doctrine of adoption is its permanence. Human adoptions can sometimes be disrupted or contested. But divine adoption is irreversible. The Confession states that adopted children are "never cast off, but sealed to the day of redemption."

This permanence rests upon the character and purpose of God Himself. The Father who adopted us did so in His eternal decree, before the foundation of the world (Ephesians 1:4-5). The Son who secured our adoption did so through His blood — a price of infinite value that cannot be refunded. The Spirit who seals our adoption is the earnest, the down-payment, of our inheritance (Ephesians 1:13-14). If the triune God has determined to make us His children, who can reverse His decree?

The Puritan William Bridge, who served as one of the five dissenting brethren at the Westminster Assembly and later ministered in Great Yarmouth until his death in 1670, preached a series of sermons on the believer's security that were later published under the title A Lifting Up for the Downcast. In these sermons, Bridge addressed every conceivable reason for spiritual discouragement and directed his hearers to the unshakable promises of God. Bridge reminded his listeners that their adoption was not based on their performance but on God's grace, not on their faithfulness but on God's faithfulness, not on their love for God but on God's love for them. And God's love, Bridge insisted, is immutable — it cannot change, it cannot waver, and it cannot fail.

Part Fourteen: Justification and Adoption Together — The Fullness of Grace

Justification and adoption, though distinct, are inseparable. Every justified person is also adopted, and every adopted person is also justified. Together, they provide a comprehensive description of the believer's new standing before God.

In justification, we learn that we are accepted. In adoption, we learn that we are loved. In justification, our guilt is removed. In adoption, our loneliness is ended. In justification, we are declared righteous. In adoption, we are welcomed home. In justification, we are freed from the penalty of sin. In adoption, we are freed from the spirit of fear.

These two doctrines together provide the foundation for the entire Christian life. Because we are justified, we do not need to earn God's favor — it has already been freely given. Because we are adopted, we do not need to prove ourselves worthy — we have already been accepted as beloved children. This is the freedom of the gospel: the freedom to serve God not out of slavish fear but out of grateful love, not to earn acceptance but because we have already been accepted in the Beloved.

The Apostle Paul captures this freedom magnificently in Romans 8:31-34: "What shall we then say to these things? If God be for us, who can be against us? He that spared not his own Son, but delivered him up for us all, how shall he not with him also freely give us all things? Who shall lay any thing to the charge of God's elect? It is God that justifieth. Who is he that condemneth? It is Christ that died, yea rather, that is risen again, who is even at the right hand of God, who also maketh intercession for us."

No charge can stand against the justified. No condemnation can touch the adopted. God is for us. Christ has died for us. The Spirit dwells in us. And nothing — absolutely nothing — can separate us from the love of God which is in Christ Jesus our Lord.
