PURITAN GOLD — Episode 29
"A Friend Loves at All Times"
Proverbs 17 | Day 29 of 31

---

[INTRO]

There's something about a fire on a cold evening... the way the wood cracks and settles, the way the warmth finds your bones before you even realize you were cold. And I think that's what real friendship is like. It finds you. It warms you. Before you even know how much you needed it.

This is Puritan Gold. I'm glad you're here with me tonight. We're nearing the end of our month-long walk through Proverbs, and today — day twenty-nine — we land in Proverbs chapter 17. And the theme the Spirit sets before us is one of the most tender in all of Scripture: the nature of true friendship, genuine loyalty, and the kind of love that doesn't pack up and leave when the weather turns.

So settle in. Let's sit with this chapter for a while and see what the Lord would teach us.

---

[PART ONE — A FRIEND LOVES AT ALL TIMES]

Proverbs 17:17 says this — and I want you to hear every word of it:

"A friend loveth at all times, and a brother is born for adversity."

Now... that's a short verse. Just a handful of words. But the weight of it — the sheer gravity of what Solomon is saying — is enough to reshape how you think about every relationship in your life.

"A friend loveth at all times." Not some of the times. Not when it's convenient. Not when the other person is agreeable or successful or useful to you. At all times. In the bright seasons and in the dark ones. When your friend is flourishing and when your friend is falling apart.

And then that second clause — "and a brother is born for adversity." Do you see what he's saying? There is a kind of bond — a brotherhood, a sisterhood — that doesn't merely survive hardship... it was made for it. It was born for it. Some relationships only reveal their true depth when the storm comes.

You know, we live in an age of shallow connections. We have hundreds of "friends" on social media, and yet loneliness is an epidemic. People are more connected digitally than any generation in history, and yet they are more isolated than ever. And I think it's because we've lost this biblical understanding of what friendship actually is. We've traded covenant for convenience. We've traded the kind of love that bears burdens for the kind that simply exchanges pleasantries.

But the Proverbs vision of friendship is thick. It's substantial. It costs something. It's the kind of love where you show up at someone's door not because they invited you, but because you knew they needed you. It's the kind of love where you speak truth even when it stings — and receive truth even when it humbles you.

Charles Spurgeon once said something I've never been able to shake. He said, "Friendship is one of the sweetest joys of life. Many might have failed beneath the bitterness of their trial had they not found a friend." And he was right. God, in His kindness, does not leave us to walk through this world alone. He gives us companions. Fellow pilgrims. People who will sit with us in the ashes and say nothing... or say exactly the right thing... or simply weep with us, which is sometimes the most eloquent sermon of all.

Think about David and Jonathan. There's a friendship that was born for adversity if there ever was one. Jonathan had every earthly reason to view David as a rival, a threat to his father's throne. And yet — "the soul of Jonathan was knit with the soul of David, and Jonathan loved him as his own soul" (1 Samuel 18:1). That wasn't a friendship of convenience. That was a friendship forged by God Himself, tested by fire, and proven faithful.

So let me ask you tonight... who are those people in your life? Who has God given you as a true companion? And — perhaps more importantly — are you being that kind of friend to someone else? Are you the one who loves at all times? Are you the one born for adversity?

---

[PART TWO — COVERING OFFENSES]

Now, earlier in this same chapter, Solomon gives us one of the most practical and searching proverbs on friendship you'll find anywhere. Proverbs 17:9:

"He that covereth a transgression seeketh love; but he that repeateth a matter separateth very friends."

Oh, friend... this one cuts, doesn't it? Because we all know what it's like to have someone repeat a matter. To have a failure thrown back in your face. To have a sin you confessed become gossip on someone else's lips. And we all know — if we're honest — what it's like to be the one doing the repeating.

Solomon is telling us that love covers. Now, he's not talking about enabling sin or pretending evil doesn't exist. He's not talking about sweeping abuse under the rug or refusing to confront what needs confronting. No — the Bible is clear elsewhere that we are to speak truth and pursue holiness. But what this proverb addresses is something different. It's about what you do with someone's failure after it's been dealt with. After repentance has come. After the matter has been resolved.

Do you bury it? Or do you keep digging it up?

Love buries it. Love says, "That's been covered by grace, and I will not unearth it." Love refuses to weaponize someone's past against them. Love does not keep a ledger of wrongs. As the apostle Paul wrote, love "beareth all things, believeth all things, hopeth all things, endureth all things" (1 Corinthians 13:7).

And here's the danger Solomon warns about — "he that repeateth a matter separateth very friends." The word "very" there in the old English means "close" or "intimate." In other words, gossip doesn't just damage casual acquaintances. It destroys the deepest bonds. It separates the dearest of friends. One careless word. One repeated matter. And trust that took years to build crumbles in an afternoon.

Spurgeon put it pointedly when he spoke of how believers ought to guard one another's reputations with the same jealousy they guard their own. He urged Christians to be slow to believe evil of one another and swift to cover what love can rightly cover. And he was echoing Solomon.

So here's the application, and it's simple: guard your tongue when it comes to other people's failures. If someone has repented, let it be finished. Don't bring it up again. Don't mention it to others. Don't hold it in reserve for the next argument. Let grace have the final word.

---

[PART THREE — A CHEERFUL HEART]

Now, before we close, I want to take us to one more verse in this chapter. Proverbs 17:22:

"A merry heart doeth good like a medicine: but a broken spirit drieth the bones."

I love that Solomon includes this here, right alongside his teaching on friendship and loyalty. Because it reminds us that the life of faith is not meant to be grim. It's not meant to be joyless. Yes, we are pilgrims. Yes, we carry crosses. Yes, there is a seriousness to the Christian life that the world cannot understand. But there is also a gladness — a deep, settled, unshakeable gladness — that the world cannot take away.

A merry heart does good like a medicine. Think about that. Joy heals. Laughter restores. A cheerful spirit — not a foolish one, not a flippant one, but a genuinely glad heart — is medicinal. It ministers to the body. It ministers to the soul. It ministers to everyone around you.

And the contrast is stark — "a broken spirit drieth the bones." Prolonged despair, unrelieved sorrow, a spirit crushed under the weight of this fallen world... it withers a person from the inside out. It's not just a spiritual problem — it becomes a physical one. The bones themselves feel it.

Now, I want to be careful here, because I know some of you listening tonight are in a season of deep sorrow. And I don't want to heap guilt on top of grief by suggesting that you simply need to "cheer up." That's not what Solomon is saying. He's not offering a platitude. He's offering a principle — and embedded in that principle is a gentle invitation. The invitation is this: come to the God who is the source of all joy. Come to the One who turns mourning into dancing and gives beauty for ashes. You don't manufacture a merry heart. You receive it. It's a gift of grace.

And you receive it, often, through the very friendships we've been talking about tonight. Through a brother born for adversity. Through a friend who loves at all times. Through a fellow believer who sits with you in your sorrow and — at just the right moment — makes you laugh again. Reminds you of God's goodness. Points you back to the promises.

This is the Christian life lived in community. This is what the body of Christ is meant to be. Not a collection of isolated individuals grimly enduring their way to heaven... but a fellowship of forgiven sinners, bearing one another's burdens, covering one another's faults, and reminding one another — again and again — that there is a God who is faithful, and a Savior who is near, and a joy that no darkness can extinguish.

---

[CLOSE AND PRAYER]

So as we bring this twenty-ninth day to a close, let me leave you with this thought. The friendships you have — the real ones, the ones that cost something, the ones that have weathered storms — those are not accidents. They are gifts from the hand of a generous God. Steward them. Invest in them. Be the friend who loves at all times. Be the brother, the sister, born for adversity. Cover offenses. Guard reputations. And let the joy of the Lord flow through you like medicine to a weary world.

Let's pray together.

Father in heaven, we thank You that You have not left us alone. You have given us one another — brothers and sisters in the faith, companions on this narrow road. Forgive us for the times we have been careless with these gifts. Forgive us for gossip, for disloyalty, for fair-weather love that abandons when the cost gets high. Teach us, O Lord, to love as You love — at all times, in all seasons, through all adversity. Give us merry hearts — not the shallow laughter of fools, but the deep, abiding gladness that comes from knowing You. Knit our souls together in genuine fellowship, that the world might see our love and know that we belong to You. We ask it in the name of Christ, who is Himself the truest Friend — the One who laid down His life for us. Amen.
