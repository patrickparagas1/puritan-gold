PURITAN GOLD -- My Growth, Day 13
"The Mortification of Sin — John Owen"
Reading Day — Owen's Classic on Killing Sin


Welcome. Today is a Reading day. We engage with one of the most important Puritan works on practical holiness: John Owen's The Mortification of Sin — a treatise that is as relevant today as when it was first published in 1656.

Let us read Romans 8:13 (KJV):

"For if ye live after the flesh, ye shall die: but if ye through the Spirit do mortify the deeds of the body, ye shall live."


John Owen (1616-1683) is widely regarded as the greatest theologian among the English Puritans. His Mortification of Sin is built on a single verse — Romans 8:13 — and a single thesis: if sin is not being killed in your life, it is killing you. There is no middle ground. No truce with sin. No peaceful coexistence. Either you are mortifying sin, or sin is mortifying you.

Owen begins by identifying the enemy. Indwelling sin is not an occasional visitor. It is a permanent resident in the believer — a remaining corruption that will not be fully eradicated until glorification. The new nature given in regeneration does not eliminate the old nature; it opposes it. The Christian life is therefore a perpetual civil war — the Spirit warring against the flesh, and the flesh against the Spirit.

Owen identifies several characteristics of indwelling sin that make it so dangerous. First, it is subtle. Sin rarely announces itself openly. It disguises itself as legitimate desire, reasonable self-interest, or harmless pleasure. The Christian who is not vigilant will not even recognize sin's approach until it has gained a foothold.

Second, it is persistent. Sin does not give up after being resisted once. It returns — often in a different form, from a different angle, at a different time. Owen writes that sin is like a river that, when dammed in one place, simply finds another channel. The Christian who blocks one avenue of temptation must immediately watch for sin to appear elsewhere.

Third, it is deceitful. Sin promises what it cannot deliver and conceals what it will inevitably produce. It promises pleasure and delivers guilt. It promises freedom and delivers bondage. It promises satisfaction and delivers emptiness. The wise Christian learns to look past the promise to the product.

Owen then outlines his method of mortification — the practical steps for killing sin.

Step one: consider whether a particular sin has become habitual. Is there a pattern? Is it a besetting sin — one that returns again and again? If so, it requires sustained, focused attention. You cannot fight it casually.

Step two: develop a clear and abiding sense of the guilt, danger, and evil of sin. Do not minimize it. Do not make excuses for it. See it for what it is — an offense against a holy God, a defiance of His law, a grieving of His Spirit, a crucifying afresh of His Son. Let the horror of sin drive you to action.

Step three: do not speak peace to yourself while sin remains unmortified. Many Christians soothe their consciences prematurely — telling themselves that God's grace covers their sin, so they need not be too concerned. Owen calls this false peace. Grace is real, and it does cover sin. But grace also empowers the killing of sin. To appeal to grace as an excuse for complacency is to pervert grace.

Step four: consider whether the sin is rooted in your temperament or constitution. Some sins are related to your particular personality, your upbringing, your emotional wiring. Knowing the root helps you address it more effectively.

Step five: watch for the occasions that trigger the sin. Where do you typically fall? What circumstances, people, or situations lower your defenses? Avoid those triggers ruthlessly. Owen is blunt: if you will not flee from the occasions of sin, you are not serious about mortification.

Step six: rise up with all holy violence against the first movements of sin. Do not wait for sin to develop into a full temptation. Kill it in the cradle. The thought that says "just this once" or "no one will know" or "it is not that serious" — kill that thought immediately, before it becomes a plan, and the plan becomes an action, and the action becomes a habit.

Step seven: fix your heart upon Christ. This is Owen's ultimate counsel. You do not kill sin primarily by looking at sin. You kill it by looking at Christ. The beauty, the holiness, the sacrifice of Christ — when beheld with the eyes of faith — make sin ugly by comparison. The more you see of Christ, the less you want of sin.


If sin is not being killed in your life, it is killing you. There is no truce. Take up arms today. Lord, I come with all my indwelling sin — the subtle, persistent, deceitful enemy that dwells within me. By Your Spirit, help me to kill what is killing me. Fix my eyes on Christ, in whose beauty sin loses its appeal. Amen.
