PURITAN GOLD -- Study Topic: The Book of Enoch, Part 10 of 10
"Jude and Enoch"
The Book of Enoch — How the New Testament references Enoch (Jude 14-15)

---

Dear friends, we have arrived at the final episode of our study of the Book of Enoch. Over the past nine sessions, we have journeyed through the Watchers and their fall, the cosmic travels of Enoch, the messianic Son of Man, the luminaries of heaven, the dream visions of Israel's history, and the moral exhortations of the Epistle. Today we come full circle to the question with which we began: What is the relationship between the Book of Enoch and the canonical Scriptures? And specifically, what are we to make of the fact that the apostle Jude quotes from it directly?

Let us read the full passage in context. Jude verses 14 through 16:

"And Enoch also, the seventh from Adam, prophesied of these, saying, Behold, the Lord cometh with ten thousands of his saints, To execute judgment upon all, and to convince all that are ungodly among them of all their ungodly deeds which they have ungodly committed, and of all their hard speeches which ungodly sinners have spoken against him. These are murmurers, complainers, walking after their own lusts; and their mouth speaketh great swelling words, having men's persons in admiration because of advantage."

--- Teaching ---

The quotation in Jude 14-15 is drawn from 1 Enoch chapter 1, verses 9, where the text reads in translation: "And behold! He cometh with ten thousands of His holy ones to execute judgment upon all, and to destroy all the ungodly, and to convict all flesh of all the works of their ungodliness which they have ungodly committed, and of all the hard things which ungodly sinners have spoken against Him."

The verbal correspondence between Jude and 1 Enoch is unmistakable. Jude is not merely alluding to a shared tradition; he is directly quoting from the text of 1 Enoch. And he attributes the quotation to "Enoch, the seventh from Adam" — the biblical patriarch himself. This raises several important questions that we must address with care and theological precision.

First, does Jude's quotation mean that 1 Enoch is canonical Scripture? The answer, according to the historic Protestant and Reformed position, is no. The church has always recognized a distinction between quoting a source and endorsing it as inspired Scripture. The apostle Paul quoted the Cretan poet Epimenides in Titus 1:12, saying, "One of themselves, even a prophet of their own, said, The Cretians are alway liars, evil beasts, slow bellies." Paul calls Epimenides a "prophet" — but not in the sense of a biblical prophet. He was recognizing that even a pagan writer could speak a truth. Paul also quoted the Greek poet Aratus in Acts 17:28 and Menander in 1 Corinthians 15:33 without endorsing the entirety of their works.

Similarly, Jude's quotation of 1 Enoch does not elevate the entire work to the status of inspired Scripture. It indicates that Jude recognized a particular statement in the text as true and relevant to his argument. The truth of the statement — that the Lord will come with His holy ones to execute judgment — is confirmed by many canonical passages (Deuteronomy 33:2, Zechariah 14:5, Matthew 25:31, 2 Thessalonians 1:7-10). Jude is drawing upon a tradition that his readers would have recognized, and he is affirming that the core truth contained in that tradition is consistent with the apostolic message.

Second, did Jude believe that the historical Enoch actually spoke these words? Jude introduces the quotation by saying Enoch "prophesied." The Greek word is epropheteuse — a strong verb indicating genuine prophetic activity. It is possible that Jude believed the Book of Enoch preserved an authentic saying of the patriarch. It is also possible that Jude was using a literary convention — attributing the words to Enoch because the work bearing his name contained them, without necessarily making a historical claim about their origin. Either way, the inspiration of Jude's epistle guarantees the truth of what Jude writes, not the inspiration of his sources.

John Owen addressed this principle in his work on the canon of Scripture. Owen distinguished between the divine authority of the canonical books and the usefulness of extra-canonical writings. A canonical author could draw upon non-canonical sources without thereby canonizing those sources. The Holy Spirit, who inspired the apostles, could superintend their use of various materials — canonical, extra-canonical, and even pagan — to communicate His truth infallibly.

Third, what other New Testament passages may reflect Enochic traditions? Beyond Jude's direct quotation, several passages in the New Testament appear to draw upon or reflect traditions found in the Enochic literature.

The most significant is 2 Peter 2:4: "For if God spared not the angels that sinned, but cast them down to hell, and delivered them into chains of darkness, to be reserved unto judgment." As we discussed in earlier episodes, this verse uses the unusual Greek verb tartaroo — "cast down to Tartarus" — and describes the binding of fallen angels in language closely paralleling the Book of the Watchers.

First Peter 3:19-20 is another passage often connected to the Enochic tradition: "By which also he went and preached unto the spirits in prison; Which sometime were disobedient, when once the longsuffering of God waited in the days of Noah." The identity of these "spirits in prison" has been debated throughout church history, but many scholars — including a number of Puritan commentators — understood them as the fallen angels of the Watchers tradition, to whom Christ proclaimed His victory after His death and resurrection.

The phrase "Son of Man" as used by Jesus throughout the Gospels, while rooted primarily in Daniel 7:13, would have carried additional resonances for anyone familiar with the Parables of Enoch. When Jesus said "the Son of man hath power on earth to forgive sins" (Mark 2:10), or "Hereafter shall ye see the Son of man sitting on the right hand of power, and coming in the clouds of heaven" (Matthew 26:64), His hearers — those who knew the Enochic tradition — would have heard overtones of the preexistent, heavenly judge described in the Parables.

The language of Revelation also shows points of contact with the Enochic tradition. The binding of Satan for a thousand years (Revelation 20:1-3) parallels the binding of Azazel in 1 Enoch. The new Jerusalem descending from heaven (Revelation 21:2) echoes the new house built for the righteous in the Animal Apocalypse. The tree of life available to the redeemed (Revelation 22:2) recalls the fragrant tree shown to Enoch in his journeys.

None of this means that the Book of Revelation depends upon 1 Enoch. Rather, both works draw upon a common pool of prophetic imagery and theological expectation — a pool fed by the canonical Old Testament and enriched by centuries of Jewish reflection and anticipation. The Book of Enoch is one witness among many to the hope that burned in Israel's heart: the hope of a coming judgment, a coming deliverer, and a coming kingdom that would never pass away.

As we conclude this series, let us remember the principles that have guided us throughout. The Book of Enoch is not Scripture. It is not infallible. It contains elements that are speculative, fanciful, and inconsistent with the canonical revelation. But it is a valuable and fascinating witness to the theological world of Second Temple Judaism — the world into which our Lord was born and in which His gospel was first proclaimed. To study Enoch is to deepen our understanding of the Bible, not to replace it. To read Enoch with discernment is to exercise the kind of Christian scholarship that the Puritans modeled — rigorous, reverent, and always tethered to the Word of God.

--- Application ---

1. Read the Bible with an awareness of its historical context. The New Testament was written in a world shaped by centuries of Jewish thought and expectation. Understanding that world helps us understand the Scriptures more deeply.

2. Hold fast to the sufficiency of Scripture. The sixty-six books of the Protestant canon contain everything necessary for salvation and godly living. Extra-canonical writings can be helpful servants but must never become competing authorities.

3. Practice discernment in all your reading. The Puritans read widely but critically, testing everything by the standard of God's Word. Follow their example — read broadly, think carefully, and believe only what Scripture confirms.

4. Let the certainty of Christ's return shape your priorities. Jude quoted Enoch's prophecy of the Lord's coming to call the church to vigilance and holiness. Let that same truth stir your heart: the Lord is coming. Are you ready?
