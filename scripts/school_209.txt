SEGMENT 1: MORNING DEVOTION
----------------------------------------

Welcome to a new day of learning at the Puritan Academy. The Puritans believed that all truth is God's truth, and that the pursuit of knowledge is a form of worship. Let us worship Him through our studies today.

Heavenly Father, we dedicate this day of learning to You. We confess that apart from You we can understand nothing rightly. Illuminate our minds with the light of Your truth. Make us faithful students who study to show ourselves approved. In Christ's name. Amen.

Before we open our lesson books, let us sit quietly with a passage from God's Word.

Our Scripture for today is Mark 9:23-24:

"Jesus said unto him, If thou canst believe, all things are possible to him that believeth. And straightway the father of the child cried out, and said with tears, Lord, I believe; help thou mine unbelief."

If doubt has visited you in the night, take courage this morning. You carry a key in your bosom — the key of Promise. The God who spoke the universe into existence has spoken promises to your soul, and not one of them will fail. When your faith feels weak, remember that it is not the strength of your faith that saves you but the object of your faith — the Lord Jesus Christ, who is the same yesterday, today, and forever. Cry out with honest tears, 'Lord, I believe; help thou mine unbelief.' He will not turn away a doubting soul that comes to Him in sincerity. He will strengthen your faith, open the doors of your prison, and set your feet once more upon the solid path.

As we prepare for today's lesson, remember that everything we study flows from this one fountain: the Word of God. Our lesson today is part of The Pilgrim's Journey, and it is titled: Doubting Castle — Overcoming Doubt. Let us proceed with minds engaged and hearts open.

SEGMENT 2: MAIN LESSON PART 1 -- BIBLICAL FOUNDATION
----------------------------------------

With our hearts prepared, let us move into the substance of today's lesson.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes in Mark 9:23-24.

Doubt is one of the most common and most distressing experiences in the Christian life. Nearly every believer, at some point, wrestles with questions, uncertainties, and wavering confidence. The Bible does not ignore this reality but addresses it with honesty, compassion, and practical wisdom. Mark 9:23-24 records one of the most authentic and relatable prayers in all of Scripture — the cry of a desperate father whose son is tormented by an unclean spirit.

When Jesus tells the father, "If thou canst believe, all things are possible to him that believeth," the father's response is a masterpiece of spiritual honesty: "Lord, I believe; help thou mine unbelief." In a single sentence, this man expresses both faith and doubt simultaneously. He believes — genuinely, sincerely, but imperfectly. And he knows that his faith is weak and insufficient, so he asks Jesus to help his unbelief. This prayer teaches us that faith and doubt can coexist in the same heart, and that the proper response to doubt is not to pretend it does not exist but to bring it honestly to Christ and ask Him to strengthen our faith.

James 1:5-8 addresses doubt in the context of seeking wisdom: "If any of you lack wisdom, let him ask of God, that giveth to all men liberally, and upbraideth not; and it shall be given him. But let him ask in faith, nothing wavering. For he that wavereth is like a wave of the sea driven with the wind and tossed. For let not that man think that he shall receive any thing of the Lord. A double minded man is unstable in all his ways." James distinguishes between honest doubt (which seeks answers and brings its questions to God) and willful unbelief (the "double-minded" man who refuses to commit himself fully to God). The doubter who comes to God in honesty will receive help; the double-minded man who tries to serve both God and the world will receive nothing.

Hebrews 11:1 provides the classic definition of faith: "Now faith is the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen." Faith is not blind belief without evidence. It is "substance" — something real, weighty, and solid. It is "evidence" — the conviction that rests on reliable testimony. The testimony of Scripture, the witness of the Holy Spirit, the evidence of changed lives, the fulfillment of prophecy, the resurrection of Christ — these are the grounds of Christian faith. Doubt often arises when we lose sight of these grounds and begin to rely on our feelings, our circumstances, or our own reasoning instead of on the Word of God.

Psalm 42:5-6 gives us a window into the experience of a godly man wrestling with doubt and discouragement: "Why art thou cast down, O my soul? and why art thou disquieted in me? hope thou in God: for I shall yet praise him for the help of his countenance. O my God, my soul is cast down within me: therefore will I remember thee from the land of Jordan, and of the Hermonites, from the hill Mizar." The psalmist's method of dealing with doubt is instructive. He does not deny his feelings — he acknowledges that his soul is "cast down" and "disquieted." But he does not allow his feelings to have the final word. He speaks to his own soul, directing it to hope in God. He deliberately remembers God's past faithfulness. He chooses to praise God even when he does not feel like praising. This is the discipline of faith in the face of doubt — not the suppression of honest questions but the redirection of the heart toward the character and promises of God.

Thomas, one of the twelve disciples, provides another instructive example of doubt. When the other disciples told Thomas that they had seen the risen Lord, Thomas famously declared, "Except I shall see in his hands the print of the nails, and put my finger into the print of the nails, and thrust my hand into his side, I will not believe" (John 20:25). A week later, Jesus appeared to Thomas and invited him to touch His wounds. Thomas responded with one of the greatest confessions of faith in the entire New Testament: "My Lord and my God!" (John 20:28). Jesus did not reject Thomas for his doubt. He met Thomas in his doubt and provided the evidence his weak faith needed. But He also said, "Thomas, because thou hast seen me, thou hast believed: blessed are they that have not seen, and yet have believed" (John 20:29). There is a higher blessing for those who trust God's Word without requiring visible proof.

The book of Habakkuk records a prophet who wrestled with profound doubt about God's justice and God's methods. Habakkuk could not understand why God would use the wicked Babylonians to punish His own people. But by the end of the book, Habakkuk arrives at one of the most magnificent declarations of faith in all of Scripture: "Although the fig tree shall not blossom, neither shall fruit be in the vines; the labour of the olive shall fail, and the fields shall yield no meat; the flock shall be cut off from the fold, and there shall be no herd in the stalls: Yet I will rejoice in the LORD, I will joy in the God of my salvation" (Habakkuk 3:17-18). This is faith that has passed through doubt and emerged stronger — faith that rejoices not because circumstances are favorable but because God is faithful regardless of circumstances.

The key to overcoming doubt is not intellectual argument alone but personal trust in the living God. Faith grows not by eliminating all questions but by knowing the One who holds all answers. As Paul declared, "I know whom I have believed, and am persuaded that he is able to keep that which I have committed unto him against that day" (2 Timothy 1:12). Paul did not say "I know what I have believed" but "I know whom I have believed." Faith is ultimately relational — it is trust in a Person, not mere assent to propositions.

This biblical foundation is essential. Without it, everything else we study today would be like a house built on sand. But with it, we stand on solid rock.

SEGMENT 3: MAIN LESSON PART 2 -- PURITAN TEACHING
----------------------------------------

Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. John Bunyan wrote extensively on this subject in "The Pilgrim's Progress." The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life.

John Bunyan's episode of Doubting Castle in "The Pilgrim's Progress" is one of the most psychologically penetrating and pastorally valuable passages in all of Christian literature. After leaving Vanity Fair, Christian and his new companion Hopeful grow weary of the hard path and decide to take an easier way through a pleasant meadow called By-path Meadow. This seemingly harmless detour leads them into the territory of Giant Despair, who captures them and imprisons them in the dungeon of Doubting Castle.

The significance of this episode cannot be overstated. Bunyan is teaching that doubt often enters the Christian life not through dramatic spiritual failure but through small, seemingly reasonable compromises. Christian and Hopeful did not set out to find Doubting Castle. They simply chose an easier path when the right path seemed too hard. This is how doubt typically works — it begins not with a frontal assault on faith but with a gradual drifting away from strict obedience to God's Word. A missed devotion here, a compromised conviction there, a small concession to the flesh — and before long, the believer finds himself in the dungeon of doubt, wondering how he got there.

Giant Despair is one of Bunyan's most terrifying creations. He represents not ordinary sadness or discouragement but the spiritual condition of despair — the utter loss of hope. Giant Despair beats Christian and Hopeful mercilessly, starves them, and — most chillingly — advises them to kill themselves, since they will never escape. His wife, Diffidence (which means distrust or lack of confidence), eggs him on, suggesting ever more cruel torments. Bunyan drew these images from his own experience. In "Grace Abounding," he describes periods when he was so overwhelmed by despair that he was tempted to curse God, to blaspheme, and even to take his own life. Bunyan knew the dungeon of Doubting Castle from the inside.

The turning point comes when Christian suddenly remembers that he has a key called Promise in his bosom. "What a fool am I," he cries, "thus to lie in a stinking dungeon when I may as well walk at liberty! I have a key in my bosom called Promise, that will, I am persuaded, open any lock in Doubting Castle." He takes out the key, and indeed, it opens every lock — the dungeon door, the outer gate, and the iron gate of the castle itself. Christian and Hopeful escape and return to the King's highway.

The theological point is magnificent in its simplicity. The key that unlocks Doubting Castle is the promises of God. When a believer is imprisoned by doubt and despair, the way of escape is not through human reasoning, positive thinking, or sheer willpower. It is through laying hold of the promises of God in Scripture and trusting them as the unchanging Word of the unchanging God. Bunyan was teaching what all the Puritans taught: the remedy for doubt is the Word of God applied to the heart by the Holy Spirit.

Bunyan was careful to show that even mature, sincere believers can fall into doubt. Christian is not a new convert or a careless professor — he is a pilgrim who has already passed through many trials and victories. Yet even he can be captured by Giant Despair. This is a humbling and comforting truth. Humbling, because it warns us that no level of spiritual maturity makes us immune to doubt. Comforting, because it assures us that falling into doubt does not mean we have lost our salvation or that God has rejected us. It means we are human, and we need to remember our promises.

The Puritan tradition provided extensive pastoral counsel for dealing with doubt. They identified several common causes of spiritual doubt. First, unconfessed sin. When a believer harbors known sin, the Holy Spirit withdraws His comforting presence, and the soul is left in darkness and uncertainty. The remedy is repentance and confession (1 John 1:9). Second, spiritual neglect. When a believer neglects prayer, Bible reading, fellowship, and the means of grace, faith weakens and doubt creeps in. The remedy is a return to faithful use of the means of grace. Third, physical and emotional exhaustion. The Puritans recognized that the body affects the soul. Elijah fell into despair after his great victory on Mount Carmel, and God's remedy was food and sleep (1 Kings 19:5-8). Sometimes the most spiritual thing a doubting believer can do is eat a good meal and get a good night's rest.

Fourth, the direct attacks of Satan. The devil is called "the accuser of the brethren" (Revelation 12:10), and one of his primary strategies is to whisper accusations and doubts into the believer's mind. "You are not really saved." "God does not really love you." "Your sins are too great to be forgiven." "You have committed the unpardonable sin." Bunyan himself was tormented by these whispers for years. The Puritan remedy was to meet Satan's accusations with the promises of God. When the devil says "You are not saved," the believer responds with Romans 8:1: "There is therefore now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus." When the devil says "Your sins are too great," the believer responds with 1 John 1:7: "The blood of Jesus Christ his Son cleanseth us from all sin."

The Puritans also distinguished between doubt and unbelief. Doubt is the weakness of faith; unbelief is the absence of faith. Doubt is a condition that afflicts believers; unbelief is the state of those who have never come to Christ. The doubting believer cries out with the father in Mark 9:24, "Lord, I believe; help thou mine unbelief." The unbeliever has no such cry. The very fact that a person is troubled by their doubt is evidence that they have genuine faith — for if they had no faith at all, they would not care about losing it. This pastoral insight has comforted countless believers throughout the centuries and continues to bring light into the dungeons of Doubting Castle.

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.

SEGMENT 4: DISCUSSION AND APPLICATION
----------------------------------------

Let us take time to discuss and apply what we have studied.

Discussion Question 1: The father in Mark 9:24 said 'Lord, I believe; help thou mine unbelief.' How can faith and doubt coexist in the same heart? Is this prayer one that you can identify with?

Discussion Question 2: In Pilgrim's Progress, Christian and Hopeful ended up in Doubting Castle because they took a seemingly harmless shortcut. How do small compromises in the Christian life lead to larger spiritual problems?

Discussion Question 3: The key that opened Doubting Castle was called 'Promise.' What specific promises of God have helped you (or could help you) overcome doubt?

Discussion Question 4: The Puritans identified several causes of doubt: unconfessed sin, spiritual neglect, physical exhaustion, and satanic attack. Which of these do you think is most common among believers today?

Discussion Question 5: How does Habakkuk's journey from questioning God to declaring 'Yet I will rejoice in the LORD' encourage you in your own struggles with doubt?

Take time with each question. The Puritans valued what they called "holy conference," the practice of discussing spiritual truths together. Do not settle for quick, surface answers. Push deeper. Ask follow-up questions. Listen carefully to one another.

Application is where truth becomes life. It is not enough to know what Mark 9:23-24 says. We must ask: how does this change the way I live today? How does this shape my thoughts, my words, my actions? The Puritans insisted that a truth that does not transform is a truth that has not been truly understood.

SEGMENT 5: AFTERNOON ACTIVITY
----------------------------------------

Learning by doing was central to Puritan education. Here is today's hands-on activity.

Students will create a 'Key of Promise' booklet. Using cardstock cut into the shape of an old-fashioned key, students will create a small booklet where each page contains one promise of God that can unlock the dungeon of doubt. Students should find and write out at least ten promises from Scripture, covering various types of doubt: doubt about salvation (John 10:28-29), doubt about forgiveness (1 John 1:9), doubt about God's love (Romans 8:38-39), doubt about God's presence (Hebrews 13:5), doubt about the future (Jeremiah 29:11), doubt about provision (Philippians 4:19), and others. Each page should include the full verse in KJV, a one-sentence summary of the promise, and a brief note about when this promise would be especially helpful. This booklet becomes a practical tool students can carry with them.

Materials needed: Cardstock (preferably in gold or brown to look like a key), scissors, hole punch, string or small ring for binding, fine-tipped markers or pens, Bibles, template for key shape (optional)

As you complete this activity, think about how it connects to what you studied in today's lesson. The goal is not just to do something, but to reinforce the truths you have been learning. The Puritans believed that education should engage the whole person: mind, heart, and hands.

Take your time with this activity. Quality matters more than speed. When you are finished, reflect on what you learned through the process of doing. Often we understand something far better after we have tried to put it into practice.

SEGMENT 6: CROSS-SUBJECT CONNECTIONS
----------------------------------------

One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

Throughout church history, many of the greatest Christian leaders have struggled with doubt and spiritual depression. Martin Luther experienced what he called 'Anfechtungen' — periods of intense spiritual trial and doubt. Charles Spurgeon, the great Victorian preacher, battled depression throughout his ministry. Study how these leaders overcame their struggles and how their experiences of doubt actually deepened their ministry and their understanding of God's grace. Discuss how the historical experience of doubt among great Christians can encourage us in our own struggles.

Literature Connection:

Doubt and its resolution are central themes in some of the greatest works of literature. Fyodor Dostoevsky's novels — particularly 'The Brothers Karamazov' — wrestle profoundly with questions of faith and doubt. The poems of Gerard Manley Hopkins, a Victorian Jesuit, include both ecstatic praise and agonized doubt. Study how these authors express the experience of doubt and what resolution (if any) they reach. Compare their approach with Bunyan's more straightforward allegorical treatment.

Science Connection:

The scientific method itself involves a form of productive doubt — scientists question existing theories and test hypotheses through careful experimentation. Discuss the difference between scientific skepticism (which seeks truth through evidence and testing) and spiritual doubt (which often arises from emotional or spiritual causes rather than from a lack of evidence). Consider how the intellectual discipline of scientific inquiry can actually complement rather than undermine Christian faith, since both seek truth and both require a form of trust in the reliability of evidence.

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.

SEGMENT 7: EVENING REVIEW AND MEMORY WORK
----------------------------------------

We have reached the final segment of today's lesson. The Puritans placed great value on review and memorization. They knew that what is not reviewed is soon forgotten. Let us take time now to consolidate what we have learned.

Review Questions:

Review Question 1: What was the father's prayer in Mark 9:24, and what does it teach us about the nature of faith?

Review Question 2: How does Hebrews 11:1 define faith, and why is this definition important for combating doubt?

Review Question 3: In Pilgrim's Progress, how did Christian and Hopeful end up in Doubting Castle?

Review Question 4: What was the 'key of Promise' that opened the doors of Doubting Castle, and what does it represent?

Review Question 5: What distinction did the Puritans make between doubt and unbelief?

Write out your answers to these questions. Use complete sentences and support your answers with what you learned in today's lesson. If you are unsure of an answer, go back and review that section of the lesson.

Memory Verse:

"Lord, I believe; help thou mine unbelief." -- Mark 9:24

Read this verse aloud five times. Then close your eyes and try to recite it from memory. Write it out by hand on a card or in your journal. The Puritans memorized vast portions of Scripture, and it was one of the great sources of their spiritual strength. Even one verse, truly hidden in the heart, can sustain you in times of trial.

Well done, students. Today you have engaged with deep truths from Scripture and from the great Puritan tradition. Carry these truths with you. Let them shape the way you think, the way you act, and the way you worship. Until tomorrow.
