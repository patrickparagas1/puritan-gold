PURITAN GOLD -- Study Devotional, Day 23
Series: Romans
"A Living Sacrifice"
Presenting your body, renewing your mind, and using your gifts


Welcome, friend. Tonight we open our Bibles again for another session of deep, prayerful study. The Puritans believed that the richest treasures of Scripture are found not on the surface but beneath it, by those willing to dig.

We are studying the book of Romans together. Tonight's lesson is titled "A Living Sacrifice." Each session builds on what came before, so if you have been following along, you will see how tonight's passage connects to the larger argument of the book.


Let us read our passage for tonight. It comes from Romans 12:1-8:

"I beseech you therefore, brethren, by the mercies of God, that ye present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable unto God, which is your reasonable service. And be not conformed to this world: but be ye transformed by the renewing of your mind, that ye may prove what is that good, and acceptable, and perfect, will of God. For I say, through the grace given unto me, to every man that is among you, not to think of himself more highly than he ought to think; but to think soberly, according as God hath dealt to every man the measure of faith. For as we have many members in one body, and all members have not the same office: So we, being many, are one body in Christ, and every one members one of another. Having then gifts differing according to the grace that is given to us, whether prophecy, let us prophesy according to the proportion of faith; Or ministry, let us wait on our ministering: or he that teacheth, on teaching; Or he that exhorteth, on exhortation: he that giveth, let him do it with simplicity; he that ruleth, with diligence; he that sheweth mercy, with cheerfulness."

Read that passage again to yourself. Mark any words or phrases that stand out. The Puritans read with a pen in hand, always ready to note what God was showing them.


Within tonight's passage, one verse stands out as a key that unlocks the rest.

"I beseech you therefore, brethren, by the mercies of God, that ye present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable unto God, which is your reasonable service." -- Romans 12:1

This verse is the lens through which we will examine the rest of tonight's passage. Keep it in the front of your mind as we proceed.


With our key verse before us, let us examine the broader teaching.

John Owen offers valuable commentary on this passage. The Puritans were expositors of the highest order, and their insights remain remarkably fresh.

John Owen saw Romans 12:1-2 as the great hinge of the epistle, the point where Paul transitions from doctrinal exposition to practical exhortation. Owen stressed that the 'therefore' of verse 1 connects everything that follows to everything that precedes. The mercies of God — justification, sanctification, adoption, glorification, the sovereignty and faithfulness of God — these are the foundation and motivation for the Christian life. We do not obey in order to earn mercy; we obey because we have received mercy.

Owen gave careful attention to the nature of the sacrifice Paul commands. Under the old covenant, sacrifices were slain — dead animals placed on the altar. But Christians are called to be living sacrifices — daily laying their lives on the altar of God's service while still alive and active. Owen observed that the difficulty of a living sacrifice is that it keeps crawling off the altar. A dead sacrifice stays put; a living sacrifice must be continually re-offered. This is why Paul uses the present tense: 'present your bodies' — keep presenting, continually offering, daily surrendering.

Owen also emphasized the renewal of the mind in verse 2 as the key to transformation. He argued that the world (the age, the system of values opposed to God) constantly presses believers into its mold through media, culture, peer pressure, and the residual patterns of the old nature. The counter-strategy is the renewal of the mind through the word of God and the work of the Spirit. As the mind is renewed, the entire life is transformed — not from the outside in (which is mere conformity) but from the inside out (which is genuine transformation). The Greek word metamorphoo (from which we get metamorphosis) describes a change of essential nature, like a caterpillar becoming a butterfly. This is what God is doing in every believer through the renewal of the mind.

Romans 12 marks the great transition in the epistle. Chapters 1-11 laid the doctrinal foundation; chapters 12-16 build the practical structure. The word 'therefore' in verse 1 is the bridge between doctrine and duty, between belief and behavior. Paul never separates the two. What you believe determines how you live, and how you live reveals what you believe.

Verse 1 issues the foundational command of the Christian life: 'I beseech you therefore, brethren, by the mercies of God, that ye present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable unto God, which is your reasonable service.' Notice how Paul frames it. He does not command like a tyrant but beseeches like a brother. His motivation is not threat but mercy — 'by the mercies of God.' After eleven chapters of mercy — justification, redemption, propitiation, adoption, preservation, glorification — Paul says: in light of all this, here is the fitting response.

The call is to present our bodies. Not just our souls, not just our spirits, but our physical bodies — our hands, feet, eyes, tongues, minds. Christianity is not Gnosticism; it does not despise the physical. The body is the temple of the Holy Spirit (1 Corinthians 6:19) and will be raised and glorified on the last day. Present it — offer it, yield it, surrender it — as a sacrifice. But not a dead sacrifice. A living sacrifice. The old covenant sacrifices died once; we die daily (1 Corinthians 15:31). Every morning, we climb back on the altar and say, 'Lord, this day I belong to You — all of me.'

This sacrifice is 'holy' — set apart for God's exclusive use. It is 'acceptable unto God' — pleasing to Him. And it is 'your reasonable service' — literally, your logical worship (logike latreia). Given what God has done for you, total self-offering is not extravagant but logical. It is the only reasonable response to infinite mercy.

Verse 2 gives the method: 'And be not conformed to this world: but be ye transformed by the renewing of your mind.' Two imperatives, one negative and one positive. Negatively: stop being shaped by the world's pattern. The Greek suschematizo means to be squeezed into a mold — the mold of the world's values, priorities, and perspectives. Positively: be transformed (metamorphoo — a deep, organic change) by the renewing of your mind. The mind is the battlefield. As the mind goes, so goes the life. When we fill our minds with God's truth, the Spirit transforms us from within.

The result: 'that ye may prove what is that good, and acceptable, and perfect, will of God.' A renewed mind can discern God's will. It develops spiritual taste — the ability to distinguish between good and evil, between God's way and the world's way. You do not need miraculous signs to know God's will; you need a renewed mind.

Verses 3-8 apply this to life within the body of Christ. Verse 3 addresses the fundamental problem of pride: 'not to think of himself more highly than he ought to think; but to think soberly.' The renewed mind produces sober self-assessment — not self-deprecation (which denies God's gifts) and not self-exaltation (which claims credit for God's work), but sober, honest, grace-based self-knowledge.

Verses 4-5 present the body analogy that Paul develops more fully in 1 Corinthians 12. The church is one body with many members, each with a different function. No member is the whole body, and no member is unnecessary. 'We, being many, are one body in Christ, and every one members one of another.' We belong to each other. We need each other. The eye cannot say to the hand, 'I have no need of thee.'

Verses 6-8 list seven spiritual gifts and instruct believers to exercise them faithfully. Prophecy — speaking God's truth — should be exercised 'according to the proportion of faith.' Ministry (service, Greek diakonia) should be served diligently. Teaching should be given with care. Exhortation should be offered with encouragement. Giving should be done 'with simplicity' — with generosity and sincerity, without ulterior motives. Leading should be done 'with diligence' — not lazily or carelessly. Showing mercy should be done 'with cheerfulness' — not grudgingly but with genuine joy.

This passage teaches that every believer has been given gifts by grace, and every gift is meant to be exercised for the good of the body. There are no spectators in the church. Every member is a minister. The question is not whether you have a gift but whether you are using the gift you have been given.


Application is where truth meets life. Let us consider what this passage demands of us.

Romans 12:1-8 calls for total surrender and active service. Here are specific applications.

First, present your body to God this morning. Get out of bed and consciously say: 'Lord, today my body belongs to You. My eyes will look at what glorifies You. My tongue will speak words that honor You. My hands will do work that serves You. My feet will go where You direct. I am a living sacrifice.' Make this a daily practice — the first act of every morning.

Second, identify one area where the world is squeezing you into its mold and take action to resist. Is it materialism — the belief that more possessions equals more happiness? Is it sexual ethics — the culture's redefinition of morality? Is it self-promotion — the social media obsession with personal brand? Is it anxiety — the world's response to uncertainty? Name it, and then counter it with specific Scripture. Replace the world's lie with God's truth.

Third, renew your mind deliberately. The mind is renewed through Scripture, prayer, and worship — not passively but actively. Set aside time this week to memorize a passage, to meditate on a chapter, to listen to solid biblical preaching. Cut back on media consumption that fills your mind with the world's perspective. Your transformation depends on what fills your mind.

Fourth, identify your spiritual gift and use it. Do not hide behind false humility — 'I do not have any gifts.' You do. God has given every believer at least one gift for the building up of the body. If you are not sure what yours is, ask a mature Christian who knows you well. Then exercise it faithfully, whether it is teaching, serving, giving, leading, showing mercy, or encouraging. The church needs your contribution.

Write down one concrete action you will take this week in response to tonight's study. Do not let this truth remain theoretical. The Puritans measured the success of a sermon not by how much the hearer learned but by how much the hearer changed.


Father, we present our bodies to You as living sacrifices — holy, acceptable, our reasonable service. Forgive us for the many times we have crawled off the altar and taken back what we surrendered. Renew our minds by Your word and Spirit. Deliver us from conformity to this world and transform us from the inside out. Give us sober self-assessment — neither inflated nor deflated, but grace-based honesty. Show us our gifts and give us courage to use them for the good of the body. May the mercies of eleven chapters produce a lifetime of grateful obedience. Through Christ our Lord. Amen.

Goodnight. May the truths you have studied tonight take deep root in your heart.
