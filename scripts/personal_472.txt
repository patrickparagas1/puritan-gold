PURITAN GOLD -- My Growth, Day 11
"The Rare Jewel of Christian Contentment"
Pastoral Day — Jeremiah Burroughs on Contentment

---

Welcome. Today is a Pastoral day. We draw from Jeremiah Burroughs's beloved classic The Rare Jewel of Christian Contentment, which teaches us the art of being satisfied in God regardless of circumstances.

Let us read Philippians 4:11-13 (KJV):

"Not that I speak in respect of want: for I have learned, in whatsoever state I am, therewith to be content. I know both how to be abased, and I know how to abound: every where and in all things I am instructed both to be full and to be hungry, both to abound and to suffer need. I can do all things through Christ which strengtheneth me."

--- Teaching ---

Jeremiah Burroughs (c. 1600-1646) defined Christian contentment as that sweet, inward, quiet, gracious frame of spirit, which freely submits to and delights in God's wise and fatherly disposal in every condition. Notice the words: sweet, inward, quiet, gracious. Contentment is not resignation. It is not gritting your teeth and enduring. It is a genuine, heartfelt delight in God's ordering of your life.

Paul says he has learned to be content. The word learned is significant. Contentment does not come naturally. It is a skill acquired through practice, discipline, and the ongoing work of the Holy Spirit. Burroughs identifies several lessons that the contented Christian has mastered.

First, the contented Christian has learned to draw satisfaction from God rather than from circumstances. This is the foundation. If your happiness depends on your circumstances — your income, your health, your reputation, your relationships — then you will always be at the mercy of events you cannot control. But if your happiness is rooted in God — who never changes, never fails, never disappoints — then you possess a contentment that no circumstance can shake.

Second, the contented Christian has learned to see God's hand in every condition. Burroughs writes that the Christian who sees only second causes — bad luck, unfair people, unfortunate timing — will be perpetually discontent. But the Christian who sees God's hand behind every circumstance — ordering, permitting, directing, sustaining — will find peace even in difficulty.

Third, the contented Christian has learned to distinguish between needs and wants. Much of our discontent arises from treating wants as needs. We think we need a larger house, a better job, a more attentive spouse, a different body, a more exciting life. But these are wants, not needs. What we truly need — forgiveness, grace, the presence of God, daily bread — we already have. Burroughs writes that the secret of contentment is the subtraction of desire, not the addition of possessions.

Fourth, the contented Christian has learned the discipline of meditation. When discontentment arises, the remedy is not to change your circumstances but to change your thinking. Meditate on God's faithfulness. Recall His past mercies. Consider the cross — the ultimate proof that God loves you and will not abandon you. Burroughs calls this exercise the soul's arithmetic — adding up God's mercies until the sum overwhelms the deficit of your complaints.

Fifth, the contented Christian has learned to view present suffering in light of eternal glory. Paul writes that our light affliction, which is but for a moment, worketh for us a far more exceeding and eternal weight of glory. Burroughs points out that Paul — who was beaten, stoned, shipwrecked, and imprisoned — called his sufferings light and momentary. How? Because he had learned to weigh them against the eternal glory that awaited him. The scales are not even close.

Burroughs also warns against the sin of murmuring — the habit of complaining about God's provisions. Murmuring, he writes, is a more serious sin than most Christians realize. It is essentially a charge against God's wisdom and goodness — an accusation that He has not ordered your life correctly. The Israelites murmured in the wilderness, and God treated it not as a minor irritation but as a grave offense.

The remedy for murmuring is thanksgiving. In everything give thanks, for this is the will of God in Christ Jesus concerning you. Thanksgiving is the antidote to discontent. It reorients the heart from what is lacking to what has been given. And as Burroughs insists, what has been given is always, always, always more than what is lacking.

--- Closing ---

Contentment is a rare jewel — precious, valuable, and worth the effort of learning. Begin today. Lord, I confess the sin of murmuring. I have complained about Your provisions instead of receiving them with gratitude. Teach me the secret that Paul learned. Let me find in You a satisfaction so deep that no circumstance can disturb it. Amen.
