PURITAN GOLD -- Study Topic: The Book of Sirach, Part 9 of 10
"Let Us Now Praise Famous Men"
Sirach Chapters 44–50 — The Heroes of Faith from Enoch to Simon

---

Good evening, and welcome back to Puritan Gold. Tonight we come to what many consider the crowning glory of the entire Book of Sirach — the magnificent hymn to the heroes of Israel's faith found in chapters 44 through 50. This passage, which begins with the unforgettable words "Let us now praise famous men," is one of the most celebrated pieces of ancient literature. It walks through the great figures of Israel's history — from Enoch and Noah through Abraham, Moses, David, Elijah, and onward to the high priest Simon — and it does so with a grandeur that directly foreshadows the great "hall of faith" in Hebrews chapter 11.

Let us read from Sirach chapter 44, verses 1 through 15:

"Let us now praise famous men, and our fathers that begat us. The Lord hath wrought great glory by them through his great power from the beginning. Such as did bear rule in their kingdoms, men renowned for their power, giving counsel by their understanding, and declaring prophecies: Leaders of the people by their counsels, and by their knowledge of learning meet for the people, wise and eloquent in their instructions: Such as found out musical tunes, and recited verses in writing: Rich men furnished with ability, living peaceably in their habitations: All these were honoured in their generations, and were the glory of their times. There be of them, that have left a name behind them, that their praises might be reported. And some there be, which have no memorial; who are perished, as though they had never been; and are become as though they had never been born; and their children after them. But these were merciful men, whose righteousness hath not been forgotten. With their seed shall continually remain a good inheritance, and their children are within the covenant. Their seed standeth fast, and their children for their sakes. Their seed shall remain for ever, and their glory shall not be blotted out. Their bodies are buried in peace; but their name liveth for evermore."

--- Teaching ---

There is something deeply human about the desire to remember the great figures of the past. We are creatures of memory, and our identity is shaped by the stories we tell about those who came before us. Ben Sira understood this. His hymn to the famous men of Israel is not mere nostalgia or hero worship — it is an act of spiritual formation. By remembering the faithful who went before, we are drawn to imitate their faith.

The prologue to the hymn contains a remarkable observation that deserves careful attention. Ben Sira notes that some great figures "have left a name behind them, that their praises might be reported" — but he immediately adds: "And some there be, which have no memorial; who are perished, as though they had never been." There are unnamed heroes of the faith. There are godly men and women whose names history has forgotten but whose righteousness God remembers. "These were merciful men, whose righteousness hath not been forgotten." This is a profound consolation for every ordinary believer who will never be famous, never write a book, never lead a nation. God forgets nothing. The faithful life lived in obscurity is just as precious to Him as the life that changes the course of history.

Let us walk briefly through the great gallery of heroes. Ben Sira begins with Enoch, "who walked with God, and was not; for God took him" (Genesis 5:24). Then Noah, who "in the time of the flood was taken in exchange for the world." Abraham, "the great father of a multitude of nations: in glory was there none like unto him; who kept the law of the most High, and was in covenant with him." Moses receives special honor as the lawgiver "beloved of God and man" who was "sanctified in his faithfulness and meekness." Aaron the priest, Joshua the warrior, Samuel the judge, David the king, Elijah the prophet — each receives a portrait painted with reverence and theological insight.

The portrait of David is especially beautiful. Sirach 47:8-10 declares: "In all his works he praised the Holy One most high with words of glory; with his whole heart he sung songs, and loved him that made him. He set singers also before the altar, that by their voices they might make sweet melody, and daily sing praises in their songs. He beautified their feasts, and set in order the solemn times until the end, that they might praise his holy name, and that the temple might sound from morning." David is remembered not primarily as a warrior or king but as a worshipper. His greatest legacy is the Psalms — songs of praise that have been sung by the faithful in every generation for three thousand years.

Elijah receives one of the most vivid portraits: "Then stood up Elias the prophet as fire, and his word burned like a lamp" (48:1). What an image! A man who was himself like fire — burning with holy zeal, consuming falsehood, illuminating truth. The prophet who called down fire from heaven was himself on fire with the Spirit of God.

The hymn reaches its climax with Simon the high priest, a contemporary of Ben Sira himself. Sirach 50:1-21 describes Simon's ministry with breathless admiration — how he fortified the city, repaired the temple, and led the worship of Israel with magnificence. When Simon emerged from the Holy of Holies and lifted his hands to bless the people, it was as glorious as "the morning star in the midst of a cloud" and "the sun shining upon the temple of the most High."

The parallels with Hebrews 11 are unmistakable. The author of Hebrews walks through many of the same heroes — Abel, Enoch, Noah, Abraham, Moses, Rahab, Gideon, David, Samuel — and draws the same conclusion: these all lived and died by faith. "These all died in faith, not having received the promises, but having seen them afar off, and were persuaded of them, and embraced them, and confessed that they were strangers and pilgrims on the earth" (Hebrews 11:13).

The Puritans loved this passage in Sirach. Cotton Mather's great biographical work Magnalia Christi Americana was modeled on Ben Sira's pattern — a catalogue of godly lives meant to inspire the next generation. Jonathan Edwards preached biographical sermons on the great saints of the past, believing that their example was a powerful means of grace. The principle is the same: we become what we admire. If we fill our minds with the stories of the faithful, we are more likely to walk faithfully ourselves.

There is also a warning embedded in the hymn. Between the heroes of faith, Ben Sira notes the failures — Rehoboam who "drove Israel away through his foolish counsel" and Jeroboam "who made Israel to sin." The gallery of faith includes cautionary tales as well as inspiring ones. Not every king was David. Not every leader was Moses. Some used their power for destruction rather than blessing. The lesson is clear: greatness is not guaranteed by position, but by faithfulness.

--- Application ---

1. Read Hebrews 11 alongside Sirach 44-50 this week. Notice the parallels and the common thread: faith in the promises of God, even when those promises are not yet fulfilled.

2. Identify two or three "heroes of faith" in your own life — people whose godliness inspired you. Give thanks to God for their example and consider how you might pass that inspiration to the next generation.

3. Remember that most of the faithful are unnamed. Your ordinary faithfulness matters more than you know. God remembers what the world forgets.

4. Consider whose story you are telling with your life. Will your children and grandchildren remember you as someone who feared God and walked in His ways?

--- Closing ---

Let us pray. Almighty God, who art surrounded by a great cloud of witnesses — the patriarchs and prophets, the apostles and martyrs, the saints of every generation — we give Thee thanks for their faithful example. By their lives they pointed to Thee. By their faith they overcame the world. Now grant us grace to follow in their steps, running with patience the race that is set before us, looking unto Jesus, the author and finisher of our faith. And when our race is run and our story told, may we be found faithful — whether our names are remembered or forgotten by men, known and honored forevermore by Thee. Through Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.

Go in peace, dear friend. In our final session on Sirach, we come to chapter 51 — Ben Sira's deeply personal farewell prayer and his invitation to seek wisdom. Until then, let the heroes of faith inspire your journey.
