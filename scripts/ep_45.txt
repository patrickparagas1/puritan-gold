PURITAN GOLD -- Study Devotional, Day 14
Series: Romans
"O Wretched Man That I Am!"
The inner struggle with indwelling sin and the cry for deliverance


Good evening. Whether this is your first time joining our study series or you have been with us from the beginning, you are welcome. God's Word is inexhaustible, and there is always more to discover.

We are studying the book of Romans together. Tonight's lesson is titled "O Wretched Man That I Am!." Each session builds on what came before, so if you have been following along, you will see how tonight's passage connects to the larger argument of the book.


Let us read our passage for tonight. It comes from Romans 7:14-25:

"For we know that the law is spiritual: but I am carnal, sold under sin. For that which I do I allow not: for what I would, that do I not; but what I hate, that do I. If then I do that which I would not, I consent unto the law that it is good. Now then it is no more I that do it, but sin that dwelleth in me. For I know that in me (that is, in my flesh,) dwelleth no good thing: for to will is present with me; but how to perform that which is good I find not. For the good that I would I do not: but the evil which I would not, that I do... I find then a law, that, when I would do good, evil is present with me. For I delight in the law of God after the inward man: But I see another law in my members, warring against the law of my mind, and bringing me into captivity to the law of sin which is in my members. O wretched man that I am! who shall deliver me from the body of this death? I thank God through Jesus Christ our Lord."

Read that passage again to yourself. Mark any words or phrases that stand out. The Puritans read with a pen in hand, always ready to note what God was showing them.


The Puritans called the key verse of a passage its 'doctrine text.' Tonight, ours is this:

"O wretched man that I am! who shall deliver me from the body of this death? I thank God through Jesus Christ our Lord." -- Romans 7:24-25

This verse is the lens through which we will examine the rest of tonight's passage. Keep it in the front of your mind as we proceed.


The text is before us. Let us dig into its riches.

John Owen offers valuable commentary on this passage. The Puritans were expositors of the highest order, and their insights remain remarkably fresh.

John Owen's treatment of Romans 7:14-25 is among the most pastorally sensitive in all of Puritan literature. Owen was convinced that Paul is here describing the experience of a regenerate believer — not an unbeliever, not Paul before his conversion, but Paul the apostle writing about his present struggle with indwelling sin. Owen's reasoning was thorough: only a regenerate person could say 'I delight in the law of God after the inward man' (verse 22), and only a regenerate person would describe such intense grief over remaining sin.

Owen used this passage extensively in his 'Indwelling Sin in Believers' to demonstrate that remaining sin is not a sign of unregenerate condition but a mark of the ongoing battle that characterizes every true Christian. He argued that the believer has two principles at war within — the new nature created by the Spirit and the old nature of indwelling sin. These two are locked in perpetual conflict, and the believer experiences the tension described in verse 19: 'For the good that I would I do not: but the evil which I would not, that I do.'

Owen drew three critical pastoral lessons from this passage. First, no believer in this life is free from the struggle with indwelling sin. If Paul the apostle confessed, 'In me (that is, in my flesh,) dwelleth no good thing,' then no Christian should expect sinless perfection this side of glory. Second, the very fact that you grieve over sin is evidence of grace. The unregenerate do not cry, 'O wretched man that I am!' They are comfortable in their sin. The anguish of verse 24 is the mark of a soul that loves holiness and hates iniquity. Third, deliverance comes not through self-effort but through Jesus Christ our Lord. Owen insisted that the cry of verse 24 must always lead to the thanksgiving of verse 25. The answer to indwelling sin is not more trying but more trusting — casting yourself upon the grace of Christ and the power of the Spirit, which Paul will expound in chapter 8.

Romans 7:14-25 is one of the most intensely personal and deeply debated passages in the entire Bible. The central question is: who is the 'I' in this passage? Is Paul describing his pre-conversion experience as a Pharisee? Or is he describing his present experience as an apostle? The Reformed tradition, following Augustine, Calvin, Luther, and Owen, has largely held that Paul is describing the ongoing struggle of the regenerate believer with indwelling sin. Several features of the text support this reading.

First, the tenses shift. In verses 7-13, Paul uses the past tense (describing his experience when the law first convicted him). But from verse 14 onward, he switches to the present tense: 'I am carnal,' 'what I would, that do I not,' 'I delight in the law of God.' This suggests a present, ongoing experience.

Second, the 'I' of this passage has qualities that only a regenerate person possesses. He 'consents unto the law that it is good' (verse 16). He wills to do good (verse 18). He delights 'in the law of God after the inward man' (verse 22). An unregenerate person does none of these things. The natural man does not delight in God's law; he suppresses the truth in unrighteousness (Romans 1:18).

Third, the intensity of the struggle itself points to regeneration. The unregenerate are described elsewhere as 'dead in trespasses and sins' (Ephesians 2:1) — dead men do not struggle. It is the living soul that fights against sin. The very fact that Paul experiences anguish over his remaining sin is evidence of new life within him.

Verse 14 acknowledges the problem honestly: 'For we know that the law is spiritual: but I am carnal, sold under sin.' The law is from God and is therefore spiritual. But Paul, even as a believer, acknowledges a carnal element in himself — the remnants of the old nature that still cling to him. He feels 'sold under sin' — not in the sense of being a willing slave (that was his condition before Christ) but in the sense of being dragged by a power he hates but cannot fully escape.

Verses 15-20 describe the agonizing internal contradiction. 'For that which I do I allow not: for what I would, that do I not; but what I hate, that do I.' This is not hypocrisy — the hypocrite does not hate his sin. This is the tortured experience of a soul that loves righteousness but finds sin still operating within. Paul resolves the tension theologically in verse 17: 'Now then it is no more I that do it, but sin that dwelleth in me.' This is not a dodge of responsibility but a recognition that the believer has a new identity. The deepest 'I' — the regenerate self, created in Christ Jesus — does not want to sin. But indwelling sin, still resident in the flesh, continues to exert its influence.

Verse 18 contains one of the most brutally honest self-assessments in Scripture: 'For I know that in me (that is, in my flesh,) dwelleth no good thing: for to will is present with me; but how to perform that which is good I find not.' Paul is careful to specify 'in my flesh' — in his old nature, his unregenerate tendencies. He does not say there is nothing good in him at all (the Holy Spirit dwells in him), but in his flesh — his natural human capacities apart from grace — there is no good thing. He has the will but not the power. This is the universal experience of every sincere Christian who has ever tried to obey God in their own strength.

Verses 21-23 describe two laws at war within. 'I find then a law, that, when I would do good, evil is present with me. For I delight in the law of God after the inward man: But I see another law in my members, warring against the law of my mind.' There is a civil war in the soul of every believer. The inward man — the new creation — delights in God's law. But another principle — the law of sin in the members — wages guerrilla warfare against the mind. This is the reality of progressive sanctification: not sinless perfection but an ongoing battle in which the new nature is gradually gaining ground through the power of the Spirit.

Verse 24 is the great cry of the embattled saint: 'O wretched man that I am! who shall deliver me from the body of this death?' This is not the cry of a lost person but of a saved person who hates the sin that clings to them. The image may be drawn from an ancient punishment where a corpse was bound to a living person, who had to carry it until the decay consumed them both. Indwelling sin is like a dead body strapped to the living believer — repulsive, burdensome, and inescapable by human effort.

But the cry of despair immediately gives way to the shout of triumph: 'I thank God through Jesus Christ our Lord!' Deliverance comes not through self-effort, not through law-keeping, not through moral reformation, but through Jesus Christ. This verse points forward to chapter 8, where Paul will explain how the Spirit of life in Christ Jesus sets us free from the law of sin and death.

The final summary in verse 25b acknowledges the ongoing tension: 'So then with the mind I myself serve the law of God; but with the flesh the law of sin.' Both realities coexist in the believer until glorification. The Christian life is not either/or but both/and — simultaneously saint and sinner, as Luther said, justified yet still struggling with remaining corruption. But the trajectory is upward, and the final victory is certain.


The true test of understanding is application. If we have truly grasped tonight's passage, it will change the way we live tomorrow.

Romans 7:14-25 speaks directly to every Christian who has ever struggled with ongoing sin. Here are specific applications.

First, stop being surprised by the battle. If Paul the apostle experienced intense warfare with indwelling sin, so will you. The presence of struggle is not evidence that you are not truly saved; it is evidence that you are. Dead men do not fight. If you find yourself grieving over sin, desiring holiness, hating the evil that clings to you — rejoice. This is the mark of the regenerate heart.

Second, be honest about your struggle. Paul's transparency in this passage is extraordinary. He does not pretend to have arrived at sinless perfection. He openly confesses, 'In me dwelleth no good thing.' Many Christians live in shame because they think they are the only ones still struggling. You are not. Find a trusted brother or sister and practice the kind of radical honesty Paul models here. 'Confess your faults one to another, and pray one for another' (James 5:16).

Third, do not let the struggle lead to despair. The cry of verse 24 is not the last word. Verse 25 is: 'I thank God through Jesus Christ our Lord!' Your deliverance does not depend on your ability to conquer sin but on Christ's power to deliver you. When you fall, do not wallow in self-pity or self-condemnation. Get up, confess, look to Christ, and press on. The Spirit who raised Jesus from the dead dwells in you (Romans 8:11).

Fourth, long for glory. The ultimate answer to the struggle of Romans 7 is the glory of Romans 8. One day, this body of death will be transformed. One day, indwelling sin will be eradicated completely. One day, you will be like Christ, for you will see Him as He is (1 John 3:2). Let that hope sustain you in the battle. The war will end, and grace will triumph.

Write down one concrete action you will take this week in response to tonight's study. Do not let this truth remain theoretical. The Puritans measured the success of a sermon not by how much the hearer learned but by how much the hearer changed.


O God, we cry with Paul: wretched sinners that we are, who shall deliver us from this body of death? And with Paul we answer: we thank You through Jesus Christ our Lord! We confess that in our flesh dwells no good thing. We grieve over the sin that still clings to us, the evil we do not want to do, the good we fail to accomplish. Yet we thank You that the struggle itself is evidence of Your grace at work within us. Deliver us, Spirit of God, from the power of indwelling sin. Sustain us in the battle until the day when we see Christ face to face and are made like Him forever. Amen.

May the Lord bless your continued study of His Word. Until next time.
