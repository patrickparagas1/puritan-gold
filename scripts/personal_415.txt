PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Doctrine Deep-Dive, Day 15
"The Providence of God"
God's Sovereign Rule Over All Things

---

Good evening, student of the faith. Tonight we engage in one of the noblest pursuits of the Christian life: the study of doctrine. The Puritans did not shy away from deep theology. They embraced it, because they knew that right thinking about God leads to right living before God.

Tonight we draw from the work of Multiple (Flavel, Charnock, Watson), specifically from "Various works on divine providence." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.

--- Scripture ---

Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Romans 8:28:

"And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.

--- Doctrine Study ---

The doctrine of providence is the teaching that God not only created all things but sustains and governs all things according to His eternal purpose. Nothing happens by chance. Nothing falls outside His sovereign control. No sparrow falls to the ground apart from His will. This doctrine is one of the greatest comforts and most searching truths in all of theology.

John Flavel's 'The Mystery of Providence' is perhaps the finest Puritan treatment of this subject. Flavel defines providence as 'the care God takes of all creatures and all their actions.' He distinguishes between general providence (God's governance of the natural order) and special providence (God's particular care for His people). Both are real, but the latter is the special comfort of the believer.

Flavel argues that providence extends to every detail of life. Not only the great events — births, deaths, wars, and plagues — but the small ones: a chance meeting, a missed bus, a passing thought, a child's question at dinner. Flavel writes that 'there is no such thing as accident or chance in God's world.' What appears random to us is precisely ordered by the infinite wisdom of God.

Flavel provides a practical discipline: he urges believers to keep a journal of providences. Write down the ways God has ordered your circumstances, answered your prayers, preserved you from danger, and opened or closed doors. Over time, patterns emerge. The hand of God becomes visible in the tapestry of your life. Flavel writes that 'the Christian who records God's providences will never lack reasons for praise.'

Stephen Charnock, in 'The Existence and Attributes of God,' approaches providence from the perspective of God's character. Charnock argues that providence flows from God's wisdom (He knows the best ends and the best means to those ends), His power (He is able to accomplish whatever He purposes), His goodness (He intends the ultimate good of His people), and His faithfulness (He never abandons what He has begun). Providence is not blind fate or impersonal force; it is the personal, purposeful, loving governance of a God who knows each of His children by name.

Charnock addresses the great difficulty of providence: the problem of evil. How can a good and sovereign God permit suffering, injustice, and the apparent prosperity of the wicked? Charnock answers that God's purposes are not fully revealed in this life. What appears unjust in the immediate may be part of a larger design that will be vindicated in eternity. Charnock writes that 'God's providence is like a Hebrew word: it can only be read backwards.' We understand His purposes not as they unfold but as we look back.

Thomas Watson, in 'All Things for Good,' provides the most pastorally warm treatment. Watson takes Romans 8:28 and systematically shows how 'all things' includes even those things that seem to be against us. Watson argues that God works good through both the best and worst things in life: through afflictions (which purify), through temptations (which drive us to dependence), through the sins of others (which test and refine our faith), and even through our own sins (which humble us and increase our appreciation of grace).

Watson is careful to clarify: this does not mean that evil is good. It means that God is so sovereign and so wise that He can bring good out of evil. The cross itself is the supreme example: the worst act in human history — the murder of the Son of God — became the means of the world's redemption.

For the man growing in Reformed theology, the doctrine of providence is not merely an intellectual exercise; it is a pillow for the weary head. When circumstances are confusing, when suffering seems meaningless, when the wicked prosper and the righteous struggle, providence assures you that God is on His throne, that nothing is outside His control, and that all things — without exception — are working together for your good and His glory.

--- Reflection ---

Let us pause and reflect. Consider these questions in the quiet of your heart.

Question 1: Can I identify specific examples of God's particular providence in my life over the past year?

Question 2: How does the doctrine of providence change the way I respond to circumstances that seem negative or confusing?

Question 3: Am I living as though I truly believe that all things work together for good, or do I practically live as though some things are outside God's control?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.

--- Closing Prayer ---

Sovereign Lord, I confess that I often fail to see Your hand in the circumstances of my life. Teach me to trust Your providence when I cannot trace it. Help me to rest in Your wisdom when I cannot understand Your ways. All things are in Your hands. Amen.
