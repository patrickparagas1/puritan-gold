PURITAN GOLD — Episode 23
"Spurgeon on the Sovereignty of Grace"
Week 4, Day 2 (Tuesday) — Theological Reflection

---

Have you ever stood at the edge of the ocean... just stood there... and felt how small you are? The waves don't care about your schedule. The tide doesn't consult your preferences. The sheer immensity of it overwhelms you — and yet, somehow, it doesn't terrify you. It steadies you. Because you realize that something so much greater than you is at work in this world. And that realization, strangely enough, brings peace.

That's something like what it feels like to encounter the sovereignty of grace. And no preacher in the last three hundred years has proclaimed that sovereignty with more fire, more tenderness, and more joy than Charles Haddon Spurgeon — the "Prince of Preachers," as he's rightly been called.

Today on Puritan Gold, we step away from our verse-by-verse walk through Proverbs — just for a moment — to sit at the feet of Spurgeon and listen to how he understood and preached the doctrines of grace. Because these doctrines aren't abstract theology. They're the very heartbeat of the gospel. And they'll change how you read every chapter of Proverbs we've studied so far.

---

Now, before we dive in, let me ground us in the Proverbs we've been studying this week. Proverbs 16:9 says: "The heart of man plans his way, but the Lord establishes his steps." And Proverbs 19:21 tells us: "Many are the plans in the mind of a man, but it is the purpose of the Lord that will stand."

There it is. Right there in the wisdom literature. The sovereignty of God over human plans, human decisions, human destinies. Solomon understood it. The Puritans understood it. And Spurgeon preached it with every breath he had.

---

Charles Spurgeon was converted at the age of fifteen. It happened on a snowy January morning in 1850, in a small Primitive Methodist chapel in Colchester, England. The regular minister couldn't make it through the snow, so a layman — a simple, uneducated man — stood up and preached from Isaiah 45:22: "Look unto me, and be ye saved, all the ends of the earth."

And Spurgeon looked. And he was saved.

But here's what's remarkable: for the rest of his life, Spurgeon never attributed that moment to his own decision. He never said, "I chose to look." He said, "God made me look." He understood — from the very first moment of his Christian life — that salvation is of the Lord. All of it. From first to last.

In one of his most famous sermons, Spurgeon declared: "I believe the doctrine of election, because I am quite sure that if God had not chosen me, I should never have chosen Him; and I am sure He chose me before I was born, or else He never would have chosen me afterwards."

That's the kind of theology that either offends you or breaks you open with gratitude. And Spurgeon knew it. He didn't shy away from the offense. But he always — always — preached it as good news.

---

So let's unpack what Spurgeon meant when he preached the sovereignty of grace. And let me organize it around three great doctrines that he held dear: election, effectual calling, and the perseverance of the saints.

First — election. This is the doctrine that before the foundation of the world, God chose a people for Himself. Not because of anything they would do. Not because He foresaw their faith. But simply because it pleased Him to set His love upon them.

Now, Spurgeon was a Baptist, not a Presbyterian. He didn't hold to infant baptism. He had his own distinct ecclesiology. But on the doctrines of grace — on election, on the sovereignty of God in salvation — he was as Reformed as Calvin himself. And he was unapologetic about it.

He once said: "There is no attribute more comforting to His children than that of God's sovereignty. Under the most adverse circumstances, in the most severe trials, they believe that sovereignty has ordained their afflictions, that sovereignty overrules them, and that sovereignty will sanctify them all."

Do you hear that? Sovereignty isn't a cold, distant doctrine. It's the most comforting truth a suffering saint can cling to. Because if God is sovereign over all things — including my trials, my losses, my heartbreaks — then nothing is wasted. Nothing is random. Nothing falls outside His loving purpose.

And Proverbs confirms this. Remember 16:4 — "The Lord has made everything for its purpose, even the wicked for the day of trouble." Solomon wasn't embarrassed by God's sovereignty. He proclaimed it.

---

The second doctrine Spurgeon preached with great power was effectual calling. This is the idea that when God calls a sinner to Himself, that call actually accomplishes what it intends. It's not a mere invitation that can be ignored. It's a summons that awakens the dead.

Spurgeon illustrated this beautifully. He said that the gospel goes out to all — it's offered freely to everyone who hears. But there is a special, inward call of the Holy Spirit that reaches into the heart and makes the spiritually dead person alive. It opens blind eyes. It unstops deaf ears. It turns a heart of stone into a heart of flesh.

And this is what happened to Spurgeon himself in that little chapel. The outward call came through the words of a simple layman. But the inward call — the effectual call — came from God Himself. And Spurgeon could no more resist it than a dead man can resist being raised to life.

This is what theologians call "irresistible grace." And I know that phrase bothers some people. It sounds coercive. It sounds like God drags people into heaven kicking and screaming. But that's not what it means at all. What it means is that when God sets His love on a person, He changes their desires so that they come willingly. Joyfully. Eagerly. They don't come because they're forced — they come because, for the first time in their lives, they actually want to.

Spurgeon put it this way: "I am sure that the work of God's grace in the heart is not a thing which operates against our will, but it operates upon our will. It is not that the man is saved against his will, but his will is changed, and being changed, he then willingly and heartily accepts the Lord Jesus Christ."

That's the beauty of effectual calling. God doesn't override your will — He renews it. He gives you new eyes, new affections, a new heart. And with that new heart, you run to Christ.

---

The third doctrine — and this is one that Spurgeon preached with particular warmth — is the perseverance of the saints. The idea that those whom God has chosen and called, He will also keep. To the end. Without fail.

Spurgeon knew his flock. He knew that many of them were burdened with doubt, with fear, with the nagging sense that they might somehow fall away from grace. And he met that fear head-on — not with empty reassurance, but with the promises of God.

He once told his congregation: "If ever it should come to pass that the sheep of Christ should fall away and be lost, it would not be the sheep that would suffer most — it would be the Shepherd. It would not be the sinner that would most lament, but the a sin-bearer, who would then have borne His sufferings in vain."

Do you see what he's doing there? He's saying: the perseverance of the saints isn't ultimately about the strength of the saints. It's about the faithfulness of the Shepherd. Christ will not lose those whom the Father has given Him. John 6:39 — "And this is the will of him who sent me, that I should lose nothing of all that he has given me, but raise it up on the last day."

Your perseverance rests on His perseverance. Your holding on is grounded in His holding you. And that, friends, is a fortress that cannot be shaken.

---

Now here's what I find most remarkable about Spurgeon's preaching of these doctrines. He never used them as a club. He never wielded sovereignty as a weapon against the hurting. Instead, he used these truths to comfort the lowly, to encourage the struggling, and to humble the proud.

Because that's what sovereign grace does — it humbles and comforts simultaneously.

It humbles you because you realize you didn't save yourself. You didn't choose God before He chose you. You didn't generate your own faith. You were dead in trespasses and sins, and God — rich in mercy, great in love — made you alive. That leaves no room for boasting. None. As Paul says in Ephesians 2:8-9, "For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God, not a result of works, so that no one may boast."

And it comforts you because you realize that if God started this work, He'll finish it. If He chose you before you were born, He won't abandon you now. If He called you out of darkness, He won't let you wander back into it. Philippians 1:6 — "He who began a good work in you will bring it to completion at the day of Jesus Christ."

Spurgeon himself experienced severe bouts of depression throughout his life. He suffered physically. He was attacked in the press. He endured the Downgrade Controversy, which cost him friendships and left him feeling isolated. And yet, through it all, he clung to the sovereignty of grace. Not because it made life easy — but because it made life meaningful. It told him that even his suffering was held in the hands of a loving and sovereign God.

---

Let me bring this back to Proverbs for a moment. Because I don't want you to think these doctrines are disconnected from the practical wisdom we've been studying.

Proverbs 16:9 says, "The heart of man plans his way, but the Lord establishes his steps." This is sovereign grace applied to daily life. You make plans. You set goals. You chart a course. And that's good — Proverbs commends diligence and planning. But underneath all your planning, there is a deeper current flowing: the purpose of God. And His purpose will stand.

And Proverbs 19:21 echoes this: "Many are the plans in the mind of a man, but it is the purpose of the Lord that will stand."

This means you can hold your plans loosely. You can release your grip on the future. You can stop trying to control every outcome — because the Lord is directing your steps. Not randomly. Not carelessly. But wisely, lovingly, sovereignly.

That's what Spurgeon preached. That's what the Puritans believed. And that's what I want you to carry with you today.

---

I want to close with one final thought from Spurgeon. Late in his ministry, worn down by illness and controversy, he wrote these words in a letter to a friend: "I have learned to kiss the wave that throws me against the Rock of Ages."

That's sovereign grace in a single sentence. The wave is painful. The trial is real. But it throws you against Christ. And Christ is the Rock that will not move.

Whatever wave is crashing against you today... whatever trial, whatever sorrow, whatever confusion... let it throw you against the Rock. Let it drive you deeper into Christ. Because He chose you. He called you. And He will keep you — all the way home.

Let's pray.

O sovereign Lord, we bow before You in wonder and in gratitude. We did not choose You — You chose us. We did not find You — You found us. And we did not save ourselves — You saved us, by Your grace alone, through the finished work of Your Son. Humble us with this truth. Comfort us with this truth. And keep us, Lord, by the power of this truth, until the day we see You face to face. May we, like Spurgeon, learn to kiss the wave that throws us against You. For You are our Rock, our Refuge, and our everlasting Hope. In Jesus' name we pray. Amen.
