"The Doctrine of the Sabbath"
Rest as a Gift and a Command

---

Welcome. Doctrine is not a dry, academic exercise. It is the scaffolding on which the entire Christian life is built. Tonight we examine a foundational truth that, rightly understood, will transform the way you worship, pray, and live.

Tonight we draw from the work of Multiple (Owen, Watson, Greenham), specifically from "Various works on the Sabbath." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.

--- Scripture ---

Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Exodus 20:8-10:

"Remember the sabbath day, to keep it holy. Six days shalt thou labour, and do all thy work: But the seventh day is the sabbath of the LORD thy God: in it thou shalt not do any work, thou, nor thy son, nor thy daughter, thy manservant, nor thy maidservant, nor thy cattle, nor thy stranger that is within thy gates."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.

--- Doctrine Study ---

The Puritans held the Christian Sabbath in the highest regard. They saw the Lord's Day not as a legalistic burden but as one of God's greatest gifts to His people: a day of rest, worship, and spiritual renewal. In an age of relentless productivity, their theology of the Sabbath speaks with particular urgency.

The Westminster Confession of Faith (21.7-8) summarizes the Puritan position. It teaches that God has appointed one day in seven as a holy Sabbath, that under the New Testament this day is the first day of the week (the Lord's Day, in honor of Christ's resurrection), and that it is to be kept holy by resting from worldly employments and recreations, and spending the whole time in public and private exercises of worship and in works of necessity and mercy.

Thomas Watson, in 'The Ten Commandments,' provides the most thorough Puritan exposition of the fourth commandment. Watson argues that the Sabbath is rooted in creation (God rested on the seventh day), confirmed in the law (the fourth commandment), and transformed in the gospel (the resurrection of Christ on the first day). Watson writes that 'the Sabbath is as old as the world and as new as the resurrection.'

Watson identifies three purposes of the Sabbath. First, it is a day of rest. God designed human beings to need regular, rhythmic rest. The body that works seven days a week will eventually break down. The mind that never rests will grow dull. The soul that has no Sabbath will starve. Watson writes that 'God rested not because He was tired, but to teach us that we need rest.'

Second, it is a day of worship. The Sabbath is set apart for the public worship of God: hearing the Word preached, participating in the sacraments, joining in corporate prayer, and singing the praises of God. Watson writes that the Sabbath 'is the market day of the soul,' the day when the soul stocks up on spiritual provisions for the week ahead.

Third, it is a day of anticipation. The weekly Sabbath is a foretaste of the eternal Sabbath that awaits the people of God (Hebrews 4:9). Every Lord's Day is a rehearsal for the endless day when all work will cease, all tears will be wiped away, and the redeemed will rest in the presence of God forever. Watson writes that keeping the Sabbath is an act of faith: it is living today as though eternity were real.

John Owen, in his theological writings, addresses the relationship between the Old Testament Sabbath and the New Testament Lord's Day. Owen argues that the moral principle of the fourth commandment — one day in seven set apart for God — is perpetual and binding. What has changed is the specific day (from the seventh to the first) and the ceremonial details. The moral obligation to rest and worship remains.

Richard Greenham, an earlier Puritan pastor, provides practical counsel for Sabbath observance. Greenham advises preparation on Saturday evening: set aside work, prepare for worship, settle disputes, and compose the soul for a day of communion with God. On the Lord's Day itself, Greenham commends early rising, family worship, attendance at morning and evening services, personal Scripture reading, spiritual conversation, and works of mercy.

Greenham also addresses the spirit in which the Sabbath should be kept. It is not a day of grim abstinence but of joyful engagement with God. Isaiah prophesied: 'If thou turn away thy foot from the sabbath, from doing thy pleasure on my holy day; and call the sabbath a delight, the holy of the LORD, honourable... then shalt thou delight thyself in the LORD' (Isaiah 58:13-14). The Sabbath is a delight, not a duty only.

In our modern context, the Puritan vision of the Sabbath stands as a rebuke and a gift. A rebuke, because most Christian men treat Sunday as simply another day, perhaps with a brief nod to church attendance. A gift, because the God who made us knows that we need a day of rest, worship, and renewal. The man who reclaims the Lord's Day will find his entire week transformed: he will work better because he rests well, and he will rest well because he worships truly.

--- Reflection ---

Before we close, take a few moments with these reflection questions.

Question 1: How do I currently observe the Lord's Day, and does my practice reflect a belief that this day is holy and set apart?

Question 2: Have I been treating Sunday as a day of rest and worship, or as merely another day for errands, entertainment, and productivity?

Question 3: What practical changes could I make to reclaim the Lord's Day as a day of genuine spiritual renewal?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.

--- Closing Prayer ---

Lord, forgive me for treating Your day as my own. Teach me to call the Sabbath a delight and to honor it as holy. Help me to rest from my labors, to worship You with joy, and to anticipate the eternal rest that awaits all Your people. In Christ, Amen.
