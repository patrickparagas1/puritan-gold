PURITAN GOLD — Episode 16
"The Mortification of Sin"
Week 3, Day 2 (Tuesday) — John Owen, Proverbs 4:23 & 28:13

---

There is a sentence, written over three hundred years ago, that has haunted the consciences of Christians ever since. It was written by John Owen — the great Puritan pastor, theologian, and chaplain to Oliver Cromwell. A man of towering intellect and even deeper piety. And the sentence is this:

"Be killing sin, or sin will be killing you."

Let that sit with you for a moment. Be killing sin... or sin will be killing you.

There's no middle ground in that statement. No neutral zone. No comfortable truce where you and your sin can agree to live peacefully side by side. Owen is telling us — with the urgency of a field surgeon — that the Christian life is a war. And in this war, there is no ceasefire.

This is Puritan Gold, and today we're going to spend our time with one of the most important books ever written for the Christian soul — John Owen's "The Mortification of Sin." It's a small book. You could read it in an afternoon. But it will take you a lifetime to live it out.

Now, "mortification" is a word we don't use much anymore. It sounds archaic, maybe even a little frightening. But all it means is "putting to death." It comes from the Latin mors — death. And it echoes the language of the Apostle Paul in Romans 8:13: "For if ye live after the flesh, ye shall die: but if ye through the Spirit do mortify the deeds of the body, ye shall live."

Mortify. Put to death. Kill.

This is the language of Scripture itself. And Owen simply took that language and asked the most practical question imaginable: How? How do we actually do this? What does it look like, in the daily life of a believer, to put sin to death?

Before we get to Owen's answers, let's ground ourselves in the Scriptures. Because Owen always started there, and so should we.

Proverbs 4:23 says: "Keep thy heart with all diligence; for out of it are the issues of life."

Keep your heart. Guard it. Watch over it with all diligence — with every ounce of vigilance you can muster. Why? Because out of the heart flow the springs of life. Everything — your words, your actions, your desires, your relationships, your entire direction — flows from what's happening in your heart.

This is where Owen begins. He understood that sin is not primarily a behavior problem. It's a heart problem. You don't lie because you're a liar by profession. You lie because something in your heart — pride, fear, self-protection — has taken root and is producing its fruit. You don't lust because the world is full of temptations. You lust because there's a spring in your heart that those temptations tap into.

The heart. Always the heart.

And then there's Proverbs 28:13: "He that covereth his sins shall not prosper: but whoso confesseth and forsaketh them shall have mercy."

Two responses to sin. Covering and confessing. Hiding and forsaking. And only one of them leads to mercy.

Owen would say that one of the deadliest mistakes a Christian can make is to cover their sin — to minimize it, to rename it, to excuse it, to push it into a dark corner and pretend it isn't there. Because sin that is covered is sin that is growing. Sin that is hidden is sin that is strengthening. It's like an infection beneath the skin — just because you can't see it doesn't mean it isn't spreading.

So... how does indwelling sin actually work? Owen spends considerable time on this because he believed that if you don't understand your enemy, you can't fight your enemy.

He taught that sin remaining in the believer — what theologians call "indwelling sin" — operates like a law. Not a suggestion. Not an occasional visitor. A law. A principle of operation that is always at work within us. Even after conversion. Even after we've been born again by the Spirit of God. Even in our best moments.

Now, that might sound discouraging. But Owen isn't trying to discourage us. He's trying to wake us up. Because one of the greatest dangers in the Christian life is to think you've arrived. To think the battle is over. To let down your guard.

Owen says — and I'm paraphrasing his beautiful old English — that sin is always at work. When you're praying, sin is trying to distract your mind. When you're serving others, sin is trying to inject pride into your motives. When you're resting, sin is trying to breed laziness. When you're succeeding, sin is trying to cultivate self-sufficiency. There is no moment in your life when indwelling sin takes a vacation.

Does that sound exhausting? It should. And that's exactly why Owen's next point is so crucial.

Because here's what Owen does not say. He does not say, "Try harder." He does not say, "Just use more willpower." He does not say, "Make a list of rules and follow them perfectly."

Owen says that the mortification of sin is the work of the Holy Spirit.

Go back to Romans 8:13 — "if ye through the Spirit do mortify the deeds of the body, ye shall live." Through the Spirit. Not through sheer determination. Not through self-improvement programs. Not through accountability charts, as helpful as those might be. Through the Spirit.

This is the great difference between Christian mortification and mere moralism. The moralist says, "Stop doing bad things." The gospel says, "The Spirit of the living God dwells within you, and He is the one who gives you power to put sin to death."

Owen describes it like this — the Spirit weakens sin at its root. While we may hack at the branches through discipline and effort, the Spirit goes after the root system. The Spirit changes your desires. The Spirit opens your eyes to see the ugliness of what once looked attractive. The Spirit fills your heart with a love for Christ that makes the pleasures of sin grow pale by comparison.

Do you remember what we said yesterday about the two feasts in Proverbs 9? Folly's table looks sweet... until you've tasted wisdom's feast. That's the Spirit's work. He gives you a taste of something so much better that the old things lose their grip.

But — and this is important — Owen is careful to say that the Spirit's work does not make us passive. We are not simply to sit back and say, "Well, the Spirit will handle it." No. We are called to active engagement. We mortify through the Spirit. There is a human responsibility. There is effort. There is vigilance. There is the daily, sometimes hourly, discipline of saying no to sin and yes to Christ.

So what does Owen actually prescribe? Let me give you several of his key counsels, translated into language for our daily lives.

First, Owen says: get a clear sense of the guilt and danger of sin. Don't minimize it. Don't call it a "struggle" when it's a rebellion. Don't call it a "weakness" when it's a war. Look at your sin honestly and ask, "What is this doing to my soul? What is this doing to my communion with God? What would this look like if it were fully grown?"

James 1:15 tells us that sin, when it is full-grown, brings forth death. Owen says: trace the trajectory. If this sin in you continues unchecked, where does it lead? What does it become? Get a clear picture of that — and let it produce a holy alarm in your soul.

Second, Owen says: load your conscience with the guilt of sin. Now, he doesn't mean wallow in condemnation. He means feel the weight of it. Bring it before God. Don't treat it lightly. If you've been nursing a secret sin — if you've been entertaining a thought pattern, a habit, an indulgence that you know dishonors the Lord — don't shrug it off. Bring it into the light. Remember Proverbs 28:13 — confess it. Don't cover it.

Third, Owen says: consider what Christ endured to purchase your freedom from this very sin. This is where the gospel becomes the engine of mortification. Look at the cross. Look at what your sin cost the Son of God. Look at the blood that was shed, the body that was broken, the cry of dereliction — "My God, my God, why hast Thou forsaken me?" — and then ask yourself... shall I return to the very thing that nailed Him there?

This isn't guilt manipulation. This is love awakened. When you see what Christ suffered for your sin, something happens in your heart. A holy grief rises up. A tender affection for your Savior strengthens. And that affection becomes a weapon against temptation.

Fourth, Owen says: meditate on the fullness and sufficiency of Christ. Don't just think about what you're giving up — think about what you're gaining. Think about the surpassing worth of knowing Christ Jesus as Lord. Think about the peace that comes from a clean conscience. Think about the joy of unhindered fellowship with God. Fill your mind and heart with the beauty of Christ, and the beauty of sin will fade.

Fifth — and this is deeply practical — Owen says: act immediately against the first stirrings of sin. Don't wait. Don't negotiate. Don't say, "I'll deal with it later." The moment you feel temptation rising, the moment you sense the old pattern beginning to reassert itself — strike. Pray. Turn away. Pick up Scripture. Call a brother or sister. Do whatever you must, but do not let sin establish a beachhead.

Owen compared indwelling sin to a military enemy. If you let the enemy take even a small piece of ground, he will use it to advance further. But if you resist at the first approach — if you fight at the border — you have a much better chance of holding your ground.

Now, I want to speak tenderly here for a moment. Because I know... I know that some of you listening to this are weary. You've been fighting the same sin for years. Maybe decades. And you're tired. You feel like you keep losing the same battle. And Owen's words, as true as they are, might feel crushing rather than comforting.

Hear me, friend. Owen also knew that weariness. He pastored people who struggled. He was honest about his own struggles. And his ultimate counsel was never "try harder" — it was "look to Christ."

The mortification of sin is not ultimately about your strength. It's about His. The Holy Spirit, who raised Christ from the dead, dwells in you. That resurrection power — that same power that rolled away the stone and brought life out of death — is at work in your soul right now. Even when you can't feel it. Even when you've failed again. Even when you're on your knees in the dark, weeping over the same sin for the thousandth time.

God is not finished with you. The Spirit is still working. And the very fact that you grieve over your sin — that you hate it, that you long to be free of it — is itself evidence of the Spirit's presence. Dead men don't fight. The fact that you're fighting means you're alive.

Thomas Watson once said, "Where there is a sigh after sanctification, there is a seed of the Spirit." Even your sighing is evidence of grace.

So take heart. Be killing sin — yes. Be vigilant — yes. Guard your heart with all diligence — yes. But do it all in the strength of Christ, through the power of the Spirit, resting on the finished work of the cross.

Confess your sin. Forsake your sin. And receive mercy. That's the promise of Proverbs 28:13. Not condemnation. Mercy.

Let me close with Owen's own words — words that have strengthened the saints for centuries: "Set faith at work on Christ for the killing of thy sin. His blood is the great sovereign remedy for sin-sick souls. Live in this, and thou wilt die a conqueror."

Shall we pray?

O Lord, we confess that sin dwells in us still. It is deeper than we know, more subtle than we can trace, and stronger than our will to resist. But You are stronger still. Your Spirit is mightier than our flesh. Your grace is greater than our guilt. Help us, Lord, to mortify the deeds of the body — not in our own power, but through Your Holy Spirit. Show us the cross. Show us the beauty of Christ. Give us a holy hatred for sin and a burning love for holiness. And when we fall — for we will fall — lift us up again, wash us clean, and set our feet back on the path. In the precious name of Jesus, who died to set us free from sin's dominion. Amen.
