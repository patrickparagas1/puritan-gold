PURITAN GOLD -- Study Devotional, Day 25
Series: Romans
"Submit to the Higher Powers"
The Christian's relationship to civil government and earthly authority

---

Welcome to our study devotional. The Apostle Paul told Timothy to study to show himself approved unto God, a workman that needeth not to be ashamed. That is our aim tonight: to study with diligence and devotion.

We are studying the book of Romans together. Tonight's lesson is titled "Submit to the Higher Powers." Each session builds on what came before, so if you have been following along, you will see how tonight's passage connects to the larger argument of the book.

--- Scripture Reading ---

Let us read our passage for tonight. It comes from Romans 13:1-7:

"Let every soul be subject unto the higher powers. For there is no power but of God: the powers that be are ordained of God. Whosoever therefore resisteth the power, resisteth the ordinance of God: and they that resist shall receive to themselves damnation. For rulers are not a terror to good works, but to the evil. Wilt thou then not be afraid of the power? do that which is good, and thou shalt have praise of the same: For he is the minister of God to thee for good. But if thou do that which is evil, be afraid; for he beareth not the sword in vain: for he is the minister of God, a revenger to execute wrath upon him that doeth evil. Wherefore ye must needs be subject, not only for wrath, but also for conscience sake. For for this cause pay ye tribute also: for they are God's ministers, attending continually upon this very thing. Render therefore to all their dues: tribute to whom tribute is due; custom to whom custom; fear to whom fear; honour to whom honour."

Read that passage again to yourself. Mark any words or phrases that stand out. The Puritans read with a pen in hand, always ready to note what God was showing them.

--- Key Verse ---

Let us zoom in on a verse that is central to everything we will study tonight.

"Let every soul be subject unto the higher powers. For there is no power but of God: the powers that be are ordained of God." -- Romans 13:1

This verse is the lens through which we will examine the rest of tonight's passage. Keep it in the front of your mind as we proceed.

--- Exposition ---

Now we turn to the substance of tonight's study.

John Calvin offers valuable commentary on this passage. The Puritans were expositors of the highest order, and their insights remain remarkably fresh.

John Calvin, whose influence shaped the entire Puritan tradition, gave foundational treatment to Romans 13:1-7 in his commentaries and in his Institutes of the Christian Religion. Calvin argued that civil government is a divine institution, ordained by God for the restraint of evil and the promotion of justice. The magistrate is 'the minister of God' — not merely a human arrangement but a divinely appointed office. Calvin stressed that this applies to all legitimate governments, not merely to Christian or just ones. Even pagan rulers serve God's purposes providentially, as instruments of common grace.

However, Calvin was careful to establish limits on the obedience owed to civil authorities. He argued that while Christians must generally submit to government, they must disobey when government commands what God forbids or forbids what God commands. 'We must obey God rather than men' (Acts 5:29). Calvin pointed to the Hebrew midwives who refused Pharaoh's order to kill newborn boys (Exodus 1:17), to Daniel who prayed despite the king's decree (Daniel 6:10), and to the apostles who continued preaching when ordered to stop (Acts 4:19-20). In each case, obedience to God took priority over obedience to human authority.

Calvin also influenced the broader Puritan tradition's understanding of resistance. While private individuals should not rebel against lawful authority, Calvin argued that lower magistrates have a duty to resist tyrannical higher authorities on behalf of the people. This principle — known as the doctrine of the lesser magistrate — profoundly influenced Puritan political theology and played a significant role in shaping the concept of constitutional government in the English-speaking world. The Puritans who drew on Calvin's teaching saw civil government as accountable to God's law and saw lawful resistance to tyranny as not only permitted but required when rulers abandon their God-given mandate to serve justice.

Romans 13:1-7 addresses one of the most practically important questions facing Christians in every age: what is the believer's relationship to civil government? Paul wrote these words while Nero was emperor — a fact that should give us pause. The command to submit is not conditioned on having a righteous ruler.

Verse 1 states the principle clearly: 'Let every soul be subject unto the higher powers. For there is no power but of God: the powers that be are ordained of God.' Three truths are established. First, every person — including every Christian — is to be subject to governing authorities. Second, there is no authority except from God. All authority is ultimately derived from Him. Third, the specific governing authorities that exist have been established by God. This does not mean every decision of every ruler is God's will, but that the institution of government itself is God's provision for human society.

Verse 2 draws the inference: 'Whosoever therefore resisteth the power, resisteth the ordinance of God.' To resist the institution of government is to resist what God has ordained. This does not mean blind, unquestioning obedience (as we will see), but it does mean that resistance to government is a serious matter — not to be undertaken lightly or for trivial reasons.

Verses 3-4 explain the purpose of government: to restrain evil and promote good. 'For rulers are not a terror to good works, but to the evil.' This is the ideal function of government. When government works properly, the law-abiding citizen has nothing to fear. The ruler 'is the minister of God to thee for good.' The word 'minister' (diakonos) is the same word used for deacons in the church. The civil magistrate, in God's design, is a servant — a servant of God for the people's good.

'But if thou do that which is evil, be afraid; for he beareth not the sword in vain.' The 'sword' represents the government's God-given authority to use force — including capital punishment — to enforce justice. 'He is the minister of God, a revenger to execute wrath upon him that doeth evil.' Government is God's instrument of wrath against wrongdoers. When a criminal is punished by the state, God's justice is being partially administered through human agency.

Verse 5 provides two motivations for submission: 'Wherefore ye must needs be subject, not only for wrath, but also for conscience sake.' We submit not merely to avoid punishment (external motivation) but because it is right (internal motivation). Our conscience, informed by God's word, tells us that submission to governing authority is part of submission to God.

Verses 6-7 apply the principle to the practical matter of taxes: 'For for this cause pay ye tribute also: for they are God's ministers, attending continually upon this very thing.' Pay what you owe: taxes, revenue, respect, honor. Jesus taught the same: 'Render unto Caesar the things which are Caesar's; and unto God the things that are God's' (Matthew 22:21).

But this passage must be read alongside other Scriptures to get the full picture. The Bible also teaches that when government commands what God forbids, believers must obey God rather than men. Peter and John told the Sanhedrin, 'Whether it be right in the sight of God to hearken unto you more than unto God, judge ye' (Acts 4:19). The Hebrew midwives disobeyed Pharaoh's command to kill newborn boys and were commended by God (Exodus 1:17-21). Daniel prayed despite the king's decree and was vindicated (Daniel 6). Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego refused to bow to Nebuchadnezzar's image (Daniel 3).

The principle is clear: we submit to government as God's institution, but we obey God as the ultimate authority. When the two conflict, God wins. The Puritan tradition developed this into the doctrine of the lesser magistrate — the idea that when a higher authority becomes tyrannical, lower authorities have a duty to resist on behalf of the people they serve. This principle shaped constitutional government and the concept of the rule of law.

Paul's teaching on government also implies that rulers are accountable to God. The magistrate is God's minister — and ministers are accountable to the One who appointed them. A ruler who uses authority for personal gain rather than public good, who terrorizes the innocent rather than restraining the wicked, who acts as a tyrant rather than a servant — such a ruler has abandoned the divine mandate. Government is not self-justifying; it exists to serve God's purposes of justice and order.

For Christians living under unjust governments — as many do today — this passage provides both comfort and guidance. Government, even bad government, is under God's sovereign control. He raises up rulers and removes them according to His purposes (Daniel 2:21). Our primary citizenship is in heaven (Philippians 3:20), and our primary allegiance is to the King of kings. We submit to earthly authorities as long as they do not require us to disobey our heavenly authority.

--- Application ---

The Puritans asked three questions of every passage: What does it teach? What does it rebuke? What does it require? Let us ask those questions now.

Romans 13:1-7 has direct bearing on how you live as a citizen. Here are specific applications.

First, pray for those in authority. Paul commands submission to government, but elsewhere he commands prayer for rulers: 'I exhort therefore, that, first of all, supplications, prayers, intercessions, and giving of thanks, be made for all men; For kings, and for all that are in authority; that we may lead a quiet and peaceable life in all godliness and honesty' (1 Timothy 2:1-2). Whether you approve of your current leaders or not, pray for them by name. Pray for their wisdom, their justice, and their openness to God.

Second, pay your taxes honestly. Verse 7 commands: 'Render therefore to all their dues: tribute to whom tribute is due.' Christians must be scrupulously honest in financial matters, including taxes. Tax evasion is not a gray area; it is disobedience to this text. Let your financial dealings reflect the integrity of the gospel.

Third, be a model citizen. 'Do that which is good, and thou shalt have praise of the same.' As much as it depends on you, be the kind of citizen that rulers can commend. Obey the law. Serve your community. Vote wisely. Contribute to the common good. Let the world see that Christians are the best citizens, not because we worship the state, but because we serve the God who ordained it.

Fourth, know the limits of submission. When government requires you to sin — to deny Christ, to violate conscience, to participate in injustice — you must obey God rather than men. Pray for the discernment to know the difference between inconvenience (which requires patience) and injustice (which may require resistance), and the courage to act rightly in either case.

Write down one concrete action you will take this week in response to tonight's study. Do not let this truth remain theoretical. The Puritans measured the success of a sermon not by how much the hearer learned but by how much the hearer changed.

--- Closing ---

Sovereign God, You have established governing authorities for the good of humanity. We submit to them as Your ministers, not because they are perfect but because You have ordained them. Give wisdom to our rulers. Restrain them from injustice. Use them to promote good and punish evil. Make us faithful citizens — honest in our taxes, obedient to just laws, and prayerful for those in authority. But above all, keep us faithful to You. When human authority conflicts with Your commands, give us the courage to obey You rather than men. Our ultimate citizenship is in heaven, and our King is Jesus Christ. Amen.

Go in peace, student of the Word. God rewards those who diligently seek Him.
