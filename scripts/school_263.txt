"Of God and the Holy Trinity"
The Westminster Standards — Lesson 3

Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we ascend to the highest subject any mind can contemplate — the doctrine of God Himself. We will explore what the Westminster Standards teach in Chapter 2 of the Confession about who God is, what He is like, and how He exists eternally as three Persons in one divine essence. This is theology in its truest and most exalted sense — the study of God. Let us approach this lesson with reverence, humility, and a genuine desire to know the God who has made Himself known. Let us pray and begin.

Almighty God, we approach Thy majesty with reverence and awe. As we study Thy attributes today, expand our understanding and deepen our worship. Help us to know Thee truly, for this is eternal life — to know Thee, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom Thou hast sent. Grant that our knowledge may not remain in our heads but may descend into our hearts and transform our lives. In Christ's name, Amen.

--- Lesson ---

PART ONE: THE HIGHEST STUDY — KNOWING GOD

Before we open the Westminster Confession to Chapter 2, let us pause to consider the magnitude of what we are attempting. We are studying the doctrine of God — the infinite, eternal, and unchangeable Creator of all things. This is not merely one subject among many. It is the subject — the fountainhead from which all other truths flow. As the Puritan theologian Stephen Charnock wrote in the opening pages of his masterwork, The Existence and Attributes of God: "The knowledge of God is the most excellent of all sciences. It is the foundation of all religion, the spring of all worship, the root of all obedience, and the soul of all comfort."

The prophet Jeremiah, speaking the words of the Lord, expressed the supreme importance of knowing God: "Thus saith the LORD, Let not the wise man glory in his wisdom, neither let the mighty man glory in his might, let not the rich man glory in his riches: but let him that glorieth glory in this, that he understandeth and knoweth me, that I am the LORD which exercise lovingkindness, judgment, and righteousness, in the earth: for in these things I delight, saith the LORD" (Jeremiah 9:23-24, KJV). Notice what God says here. The thing most worth boasting about in all the world is not wisdom, or strength, or wealth, but the knowledge of God Himself.

And yet, this study must be approached with profound humility, for the God we are studying is infinitely beyond our ability to comprehend. The prophet Isaiah records God's own testimony to His transcendence: "For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways, saith the LORD. For as the heavens are higher than the earth, so are my ways higher than your ways, and my thoughts than your thoughts" (Isaiah 55:8-9, KJV). The Apostle Paul, after wrestling with the depths of God's wisdom in his letter to the Romans, burst into a doxology of astonishment: "O the depth of the riches both of the wisdom and knowledge of God! how unsearchable are his judgments, and his ways past finding out! For who hath known the mind of the Lord? or who hath been his counsellor?" (Romans 11:33-34, KJV).

We cannot fully comprehend God. But we can truly know Him — because He has chosen to reveal Himself to us in Scripture. And what He has revealed is glorious beyond description.

PART TWO: THE BEING OF GOD — CONFESSION, CHAPTER 2, SECTION 1

The Westminster Confession, Chapter 2, Section 1, provides one of the most comprehensive and carefully crafted statements about the being and attributes of God ever written. It reads:

"There is but one only living and true God, who is infinite in being and perfection, a most pure spirit, invisible, without body, parts, or passions, immutable, immense, eternal, incomprehensible, almighty, most wise, most holy, most free, most absolute, working all things according to the counsel of his own immutable and most righteous will, for his own glory, most loving, gracious, merciful, long-suffering, abundant in goodness and truth, forgiving iniquity, transgression, and sin; the rewarder of them that diligently seek him; and withal most just and terrible in his judgments; hating all sin; and who will by no means clear the guilty."

This is a magnificent statement — a single sentence that encompasses the vast ocean of God's perfections. Let us examine it section by section.

"There is but one only living and true God." The Confession begins with the fundamental assertion of monotheism — there is one God, not many. Moses declared this truth to Israel in the Shema, the great confession of the Hebrew faith: "Hear, O Israel: the LORD our God is one LORD" (Deuteronomy 6:4, KJV). The prophet Isaiah proclaimed God's exclusive deity: "I am the LORD, and there is none else, there is no God beside me" (Isaiah 45:5, KJV). And again: "Before me there was no God formed, neither shall there be after me. I, even I, am the LORD; and beside me there is no saviour" (Isaiah 43:10-11, KJV). The Apostle Paul affirmed this in his first letter to Timothy: "For there is one God, and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus" (1 Timothy 2:5, KJV).

Notice that the Confession says God is "living and true." He is living — not a dead idol, not an abstract principle, not a philosophical concept, but a personal, active, living Being who thinks, wills, speaks, acts, and relates to His creatures. And He is true — the genuine God, as opposed to the false gods that the nations worship. Every other "god" is a fiction, a fabrication, a projection of the human imagination. The living God alone is real.

"Infinite in being and perfection." God has no limits. He is not confined by space, bounded by time, or restricted in any way. His being — His very existence — is infinite. And His perfections are infinite — every attribute He possesses, He possesses to an infinite degree. He is not merely wise; He is infinitely wise. He is not merely powerful; He is infinitely powerful. He is not merely good; He is infinitely good. There is no ceiling to God's perfections, no limit to His excellencies, no boundary to His glory.

"A most pure spirit, invisible, without body, parts, or passions." God is spirit. He does not have a physical body. He is not visible to the naked eye. He does not have physical parts — hands, feet, eyes — in a literal sense, though the Bible often uses such language figuratively (what theologians call "anthropomorphisms") to help us understand God's actions. The Lord Jesus Himself declared, "God is a Spirit: and they that worship him must worship him in spirit and in truth" (John 4:24, KJV).

The phrase "without passions" requires careful explanation, for it can easily be misunderstood. The Confession does not mean that God is emotionless or indifferent. The Bible is full of language about God's love, joy, anger, and compassion. What the Confession means is that God is not subject to passions in the way that creatures are — that is, He is not overwhelmed by emotions, controlled by feelings, or subject to involuntary emotional changes. God's love is eternal and unchanging, not a response to external stimuli. God's wrath against sin is a settled, holy opposition to evil, not a fit of temper. God is not moody, capricious, or unstable in His emotional life. His affections are perfect, controlled, and consistent with His holy character.

The Puritan theologian Stephen Charnock devoted hundreds of pages to unpacking these attributes. On God's spirituality, Charnock wrote: "God being a Spirit, is not confined to any place, but fills all places. He doth not need the heavens for His dwelling, nor the earth for His footstool. He is above all, through all, and in all — sustaining all things by the word of His power."

PART THREE: THE SHORTER CATECHISM'S DEFINITION OF GOD

The Westminster Shorter Catechism addresses the doctrine of God with memorable precision. Question 4 asks, "What is God?" and answers: "God is a Spirit, infinite, eternal, and unchangeable, in his being, wisdom, power, holiness, justice, goodness, and truth."

This single sentence is one of the most profound and comprehensive statements about God ever composed. The great Puritan preacher Thomas Watson, in his exposition of the Shorter Catechism entitled A Body of Divinity, called it "the most complete definition of God that the wit of man could ever devise." Let us examine it carefully.

"God is a Spirit." As we have already discussed, God does not have a physical body. He transcends the limitations of material existence. He is everywhere present, perfectly aware, and actively engaged with His creation. When the Bible speaks of God's "eyes," it refers to His omniscience — His ability to see all things. When it speaks of His "hand," it refers to His power and action. When it speaks of His "heart," it refers to His affections and purposes.

"Infinite." God has no limits. His power is unlimited — He can do all that He wills. His knowledge is unlimited — He knows all things, past, present, and future, actual and possible. His presence is unlimited — there is no place in the universe where God is not. The Psalmist expressed this truth with poetic beauty: "Whither shall I go from thy spirit? or whither shall I flee from thy presence? If I ascend up into heaven, thou art there: if I make my bed in hell, behold, thou art there. If I take the wings of the morning, and dwell in the uttermost parts of the sea; even there shall thy hand lead me, and thy right hand shall hold me" (Psalm 139:7-10, KJV).

"Eternal." God has no beginning and no end. He did not come into existence at some point in the past, and He will never cease to exist. He is, as Moses declared, "from everlasting to everlasting" (Psalm 90:2, KJV). The full verse reads: "Before the mountains were brought forth, or ever thou hadst formed the earth and the world, even from everlasting to everlasting, thou art God." Time itself is a creation of God — He is not bound by it but stands above it and governs it. Past, present, and future are all equally present to God. He does not experience time as a sequence of moments, as we do. He sees all of history — from creation to consummation — in a single, eternal "now."

The early church father Augustine of Hippo grappled with the relationship between God and time in his Confessions, written around 400 AD. Augustine asked the famous question: "What was God doing before He created the world?" His answer was profound: there was no "before" creation, because time itself was created along with the world. God exists in eternity, which is not an infinitely long stretch of time but a mode of existence altogether different from temporal existence.

"Unchangeable." God does not change. His character is the same yesterday, today, and forever. He does not grow wiser, because He is already infinitely wise. He does not grow stronger, because He is already infinitely powerful. He does not change His mind in the way that humans do, because His decisions are based on perfect knowledge and perfect wisdom from eternity. The prophet Malachi declared, "For I am the LORD, I change not; therefore ye sons of Jacob are not consumed" (Malachi 3:6, KJV). The Apostle James wrote, "Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above, and cometh down from the Father of lights, with whom is no variableness, neither shadow of turning" (James 1:17, KJV).

The theological term for this attribute is "immutability," and it is one of the most comforting truths in all of theology. In a world where everything changes — where relationships shift, health fails, economies crash, and nations rise and fall — God remains the same. His love for His people does not waver. His promises do not expire. His purposes do not fail. He is the one fixed point in a universe of flux.

Thomas Watson elaborated on God's unchangeableness with pastoral warmth: "God is immutable in His essence. His nature admits no change. He cannot be any better, for He is the best; He cannot be any worse, for He is goodness itself. His power cannot increase, for He is almighty; His knowledge cannot increase, for He knows all things from eternity. The unchangeableness of God is a great comfort to the saints. His love doth not change; it is not as a blaze of straw, soon kindled and soon put out; but it is an infinite, eternal love."

These four qualities — Spirit, infinite, eternal, and unchangeable — apply to each of the seven attributes listed in the Catechism's answer. God is infinite, eternal, and unchangeable in His being — in His very existence. He is infinite, eternal, and unchangeable in His wisdom — He always knows the best end to pursue and the best means to achieve it, and His wisdom never fails. He is infinite, eternal, and unchangeable in His power — nothing is too hard for Him, and nothing can resist His sovereign will. The angel declared to Mary, "For with God nothing shall be impossible" (Luke 1:37, KJV), and the prophet Jeremiah affirmed, "Ah Lord GOD! behold, thou hast made the heaven and the earth by thy great power and stretched out arm, and there is nothing too hard for thee" (Jeremiah 32:17, KJV).

He is infinite, eternal, and unchangeable in His holiness — He is perfectly pure, perfectly righteous, completely set apart from all evil. Holiness is not merely one attribute among many; it is the attribute that qualifies all the others. His love is holy love. His justice is holy justice. His power is holy power. When the seraphim in Isaiah's vision cry out before the throne, they do not cry "Love, love, love" or "Power, power, power," though God is infinitely loving and infinitely powerful. They cry, "Holy, holy, holy, is the LORD of hosts: the whole earth is full of his glory" (Isaiah 6:3, KJV). Holiness is the crown jewel of the divine attributes, the attribute that most distinguishes God from all His creatures.

He is infinite, eternal, and unchangeable in His justice — He always does what is right. He rewards righteousness and punishes evil. He is never unfair, never arbitrary, never biased. The patriarch Abraham asked the question that echoes through Scripture: "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?" (Genesis 18:25, KJV). The answer, of course, is yes — always, inevitably, perfectly.

He is infinite, eternal, and unchangeable in His goodness — He is the source of all that is good, beautiful, and true. Every blessing, every kindness, every gift of grace flows from the inexhaustible fountain of divine goodness. The Psalmist testified, "The LORD is good to all: and his tender mercies are over all his works" (Psalm 145:9, KJV). And again, "O taste and see that the LORD is good: blessed is the man that trusteth in him" (Psalm 34:8, KJV).

He is infinite, eternal, and unchangeable in His truth — He cannot lie. His Word is absolutely reliable. His promises are certain. His character is transparent. There is no shadow of duplicity in God. The Apostle Paul declared that God cannot lie (Titus 1:2), and the book of Numbers affirms, "God is not a man, that he should lie; neither the son of man, that he should repent: hath he said, and shall he not do it? or hath he spoken, and shall he not make it good?" (Numbers 23:19, KJV).

PART FOUR: THE INCOMMUNICABLE AND COMMUNICABLE ATTRIBUTES

Theologians have traditionally divided God's attributes into two categories: incommunicable attributes and communicable attributes. Understanding this distinction will help us grasp the full scope of what the Westminster Standards teach about God.

The incommunicable attributes are those qualities that belong to God alone and have no analogy in the created world. These include His aseity (self-existence), His infinity, His eternity, His immutability, His omnipresence, and His simplicity (the truth that God is not composed of parts but is a perfectly unified being). No creature possesses any of these attributes, even in a limited degree. We are finite; God is infinite. We are temporal; God is eternal. We change; God is unchangeable. We exist in one place at a time; God is everywhere present simultaneously. These attributes mark the absolute boundary between the Creator and the creature.

The communicable attributes are those qualities that are reflected, in a finite and imperfect way, in the creatures God has made — especially in human beings, who are created in His image. These include wisdom, power, holiness, justice, goodness, and truth. Human beings can be wise, but only God is infinitely wise. Human beings can be powerful, but only God is omnipotent. Human beings can be good, but only God is the fountain of all goodness. The communicable attributes are the basis for the image of God in humanity — the reflection of God's character in His creatures.

The Puritan Edward Leigh, in his Treatise of Divinity, organized God's attributes with meticulous care and showed how each attribute relates to the others. Leigh taught that God's attributes are not separate components assembled together, like pieces of a machine, but are each identical with God's simple, undivided essence. God's wisdom is His power. His power is His goodness. His goodness is His holiness. Each attribute is the whole God considered under a different aspect. This is the doctrine of divine simplicity — a profound truth that the Westminster divines affirmed when they described God as "infinite in being and perfection."

The practical significance of this distinction between incommunicable and communicable attributes is this: the incommunicable attributes remind us of God's transcendence — that He is infinitely above us, utterly different from us, and beyond our full comprehension. The communicable attributes remind us of God's immanence — that He has made Himself knowable to us by creating us in His image, so that we can understand, however imperfectly, what He is like. We can never fully grasp God's infinity, but we can know His goodness. We cannot fathom His eternity, but we can experience His truth. The incommunicable attributes inspire awe; the communicable attributes invite relationship.

PART FIVE: THE DOCTRINE OF THE TRINITY

The Westminster Confession addresses the doctrine of the Trinity in Chapter 2, Sections 2 and 3. Section 3 states:

"In the unity of the Godhead there be three Persons of one substance, power, and eternity: God the Father, God the Son, and God the Holy Ghost. The Father is of none, neither begotten nor proceeding; the Son is eternally begotten of the Father; the Holy Ghost eternally proceeding from the Father and the Son."

The doctrine of the Trinity is the central mystery of the Christian faith. It teaches that there is one God who eternally exists as three distinct Persons — Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — each fully and equally God, yet there are not three Gods but one. This is not a contradiction, because we are not saying that God is one in the same way that He is three. God is one in essence (one divine nature) and three in Person (three distinct centers of consciousness, will, and relationship).

Let us trace the biblical foundations of this doctrine, for the Trinity is not a human invention but a divine revelation.

The unity of God is taught throughout the Old Testament, as we have already seen in the Shema: "Hear, O Israel: the LORD our God is one LORD" (Deuteronomy 6:4, KJV). But even within the Old Testament, there are hints of plurality within the Godhead. In the creation account, God says, "Let us make man in our image, after our likeness" (Genesis 1:26, KJV). The plural pronouns "us" and "our" suggest a plurality of Persons within the one God. In Isaiah's vision of the heavenly throne room, the Lord says, "Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?" (Isaiah 6:8, KJV) — again combining the singular "I" with the plural "us."

The New Testament reveals the Trinity explicitly. At the baptism of Jesus, all three Persons are present and active: "And Jesus, when he was baptized, went up straightway out of the water: and, lo, the heavens were opened unto him, and he saw the Spirit of God descending like a dove, and lighting upon him: and lo a voice from heaven, saying, This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased" (Matthew 3:16-17, KJV). The Son is baptized, the Spirit descends, and the Father speaks. Three distinct Persons, all present at the same moment.

The Great Commission is given in a Trinitarian formula: "Go ye therefore, and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost" (Matthew 28:19, KJV). Notice that Jesus says "in the name" (singular), not "in the names" (plural) — one name, yet three Persons. This points to the unity of the divine essence shared by the three Persons.

The Apostle Paul's benediction at the close of Second Corinthians is another explicitly Trinitarian text: "The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, and the love of God, and the communion of the Holy Ghost, be with you all" (2 Corinthians 13:14, KJV). Each Person of the Trinity contributes a distinct blessing: the grace of the Son, the love of the Father, and the fellowship of the Spirit.

The divinity of each Person is individually affirmed in Scripture. The Father is God — this is everywhere assumed and explicitly stated. The Son is God — the Apostle John opens his Gospel with the declaration, "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God" (John 1:1, KJV). Thomas, when confronted with the risen Christ, exclaimed, "My Lord and my God" (John 20:28, KJV). The Apostle Paul called Christ "God over all, blessed for ever" (Romans 9:5, KJV). The Holy Spirit is God — when Ananias lied to the Holy Spirit, Peter said he had "not lied unto men, but unto God" (Acts 5:4, KJV).

The early church spent several centuries working out the precise formulation of the doctrine of the Trinity, combating heresies that arose along the way. Arianism, which denied the full deity of the Son, was condemned at the Council of Nicaea in 325 AD. The Nicene Creed affirmed that the Son is "God of God, Light of Light, very God of very God, begotten, not made, being of one substance with the Father." The Council of Constantinople in 381 AD extended this affirmation to the Holy Spirit, declaring that He is "the Lord and Giver of Life, who proceeds from the Father, who together with the Father and the Son is worshipped and glorified."

Athanasius, Bishop of Alexandria, was the great champion of Trinitarian orthodoxy against the Arian heresy. He was exiled five times for his defense of the deity of Christ, yet he never wavered. His famous saying, "Athanasius contra mundum" — Athanasius against the world — captures his courageous stand for the truth. The Westminster divines revered Athanasius and his work, and the Athanasian Creed (whether or not Athanasius himself wrote it) was a major influence on their Trinitarian formulations.

The Puritan John Owen wrote extensively on the Trinity, particularly in his work Communion with God the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost. Owen taught that believers have distinct communion with each Person of the Trinity — they commune with the Father in His love, with the Son in His grace, and with the Spirit in His comfort and sanctification. This practical Trinitarianism was one of the hallmarks of Puritan devotion. The Trinity was not merely a doctrine to be believed but a reality to be experienced in daily worship and prayer.

Owen's approach to the Trinity was deeply pastoral. He argued that Christians often neglect communion with the Holy Spirit, focusing their devotion on the Father and the Son while forgetting the Person of the Spirit, who dwells within them. Owen taught that the Spirit's indwelling is a distinct source of comfort and strength that believers should consciously cultivate through prayer, meditation, and sensitivity to the Spirit's leading. Similarly, Owen argued that Christians sometimes neglect the distinct love of the Father, focusing so much on the work of Christ that they forget the paternal affection of the One who sent the Son into the world. "God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son" (John 3:16, KJV) — the love that motivated redemption was the Father's love.

The Confession's Trinitarian teaching also has implications for how we understand salvation. Salvation is a Trinitarian work. The Father planned it in eternity, choosing a people for Himself before the foundation of the world. The Son accomplished it in history, taking on human flesh, living a perfect life, dying an atoning death, and rising from the dead. The Spirit applies it in the hearts of believers, regenerating, converting, sanctifying, and preserving them unto glory. Each Person of the Trinity plays a distinct and essential role in our redemption. To understand this is to see the full richness of the gospel — that the triune God, in the unity of His eternal purpose and the distinction of His three Persons, has accomplished everything necessary for our salvation.

PART SIX: HERESIES CONCERNING THE TRINITY

The Westminster divines were aware that throughout church history, many false teachings about the Trinity had arisen, and they crafted their statements to exclude these errors. It is instructive for us to know what these heresies are, so that we can recognize and reject them.

Modalism (also called Sabellianism, after Sabellius, a third-century teacher) teaches that the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are not three distinct Persons but three different modes or manifestations of one Person. According to this view, God sometimes appears as the Father, sometimes as the Son, and sometimes as the Holy Spirit — but there is only one Person behind the three masks. This heresy denies the eternal, simultaneous existence of three Persons. It cannot account for the baptism of Jesus, where all three Persons are present at the same time, or for Jesus's prayers to the Father, which would be meaningless if the Father and the Son were the same Person.

Arianism (named after Arius of Alexandria, who taught in the early fourth century) teaches that the Son is not truly God but is a created being — the first and greatest of God's creatures, but a creature nonetheless. Arius's famous slogan was, "There was a time when the Son was not." This heresy strikes at the heart of the gospel, for if the Son is not truly God, then His death on the cross cannot have infinite value, and our salvation is insecure. The Council of Nicaea in 325 AD condemned Arianism and affirmed that the Son is "of one substance" (homoousios) with the Father — truly God, not a lesser being.

Tritheism is the error of treating the three Persons as three separate Gods. While this heresy is less common historically, it is a danger whenever the distinction between the Persons is overemphasized at the expense of the unity of the divine essence. The Westminster Confession guards against tritheism by affirming that the three Persons are "of one substance, power, and eternity" — they share one divine nature and are not three separate deities.

Subordinationism is the error of making the Son or the Spirit ontologically inferior to the Father — not merely functionally subordinate in their roles within the economy of redemption, but essentially lesser in their divine nature. The Confession rejects this by affirming that all three Persons share the same substance, power, and eternity.

The Puritan Thomas Manton preached powerfully against these heresies: "Take heed of the old errors concerning the blessed Trinity. Some would confound the Persons; others would divide the substance. But the Scripture teaches us to distinguish the Persons without dividing the substance, and to maintain the unity of the Godhead without confounding the Persons. This is the ancient faith, delivered to the saints, for which we must earnestly contend."

PART SEVEN: THE SELF-SUFFICIENCY OF GOD

The Confession, in Chapter 2, Section 2, makes a further statement about God's self-sufficiency:

"God hath all life, glory, goodness, blessedness, in and of himself; and is alone in and unto himself all-sufficient, not standing in need of any creatures which he hath made, nor deriving any glory from them, but only manifesting his own glory in, by, unto, and upon them; he is the alone fountain of all being, of whom, through whom, and to whom, are all things; and hath most sovereign dominion over them, to do by them, for them, or upon them, whatsoever himself pleaseth."

This teaching, known as the doctrine of divine aseity or self-sufficiency, is one of the most fundamental truths about God. God does not need anything. He did not create the world because He was lonely. He did not create humanity because He needed someone to love. He did not establish the church because He needed worshippers. God is perfectly complete, perfectly blessed, and perfectly fulfilled in Himself — in the eternal communion of love shared among the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit.

The Apostle Paul declared this truth to the Athenians on Mars Hill: "God that made the world and all things therein, seeing that he is Lord of heaven and earth, dwelleth not in temples made with hands; neither is worshipped with men's hands, as though he needed any thing, seeing he giveth to all life, and breath, and all things" (Acts 17:24-25, KJV). God is the giver, not the receiver. He is the fountain, not the cistern. All creatures depend on Him; He depends on no creature.

This truth is both humbling and comforting. It is humbling because it reminds us that God does not need us. We are not indispensable to God. He would be infinitely glorious, infinitely happy, and infinitely complete without us. But it is comforting because it means that God's love for us is entirely free and uncoerced. He loves us not because He needs us but because He freely chooses to love us. His love is pure grace — unmerited, unearned, and undeserved.

PART EIGHT: PRACTICAL APPLICATION — KNOWING GOD TRANSFORMS EVERYTHING

Understanding the doctrine of God is not merely an intellectual exercise. It is the foundation of worship, the spring of comfort, and the motive for obedience. Let us consider several practical implications of what we have learned.

First, the doctrine of God should lead us to worship. When we see who God truly is — infinite, eternal, unchangeable in all His perfections — we cannot help but fall on our faces in adoration. The angels around the throne do not tire of crying "Holy, holy, holy." The elders cast their crowns before the throne, saying, "Thou art worthy, O Lord, to receive glory and honour and power: for thou hast created all things, and for thy pleasure they are and were created" (Revelation 4:11, KJV). Our worship should be an echo of theirs.

Second, the doctrine of God should lead us to trust. If God is all-knowing, He understands your situation perfectly. If He is all-powerful, He is able to help you in any circumstance. If He is unchangeable, His love for you will never waver. If He is eternal, His purposes for you will never be thwarted by the passage of time. The Puritan pastor Thomas Brooks wrote: "He that hath God for his God hath all things for his treasure. The God of infinite wisdom knows what is best for thee. The God of infinite power is able to do what is best for thee. The God of infinite goodness will do what is best for thee."

Third, the doctrine of the Trinity should shape our prayer. We pray to the Father, through the Son, by the power of the Holy Spirit. Our access to the Father is secured by the mediatorial work of Christ. Our ability to pray is enabled by the indwelling Spirit, who "helpeth our infirmities: for we know not what we should pray for as we ought: but the Spirit itself maketh intercession for us with groanings which cannot be uttered" (Romans 8:26, KJV). Every genuine prayer is a Trinitarian act.

Fourth, the doctrine of God should produce humility. The more we know of God, the more we realize how little we know. The more we see of His greatness, the more we see our own smallness. Job, after God spoke to him from the whirlwind, responded with profound humility: "I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear: but now mine eye seeth thee. Wherefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes" (Job 42:5-6, KJV). Isaiah, when he saw the Lord high and lifted up, cried out, "Woe is me! for I am undone; because I am a man of unclean lips" (Isaiah 6:5, KJV). The knowledge of God, rightly grasped, always produces humility.

Fifth, the doctrine of God should inspire holy living. If God is infinitely holy, then we who are made in His image are called to be holy. "Be ye holy; for I am holy" (1 Peter 1:16, KJV). If God hates sin, then we should hate sin. If God loves righteousness, then we should love righteousness. The Shorter Catechism teaches that man's chief end is to glorify God and enjoy Him forever. We glorify God when our lives reflect His character — when we are truthful because He is true, just because He is just, loving because He is love, and holy because He is holy.

The Puritan Richard Sibbes, in his work The Bruised Reed, wrote: "Let us study the attributes of God. When we know what He is, we shall know what we ought to be. His holiness is the pattern of ours. His love is the spring of ours. His truth is the standard of ours. Study God, and you will learn how to live."

Sixth, the doctrine of God should give us courage in the face of suffering. When trials come — and they will — the believer can rest in the knowledge that an all-wise, all-powerful, all-good God is in control. The three friends of Daniel — Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego — faced the fiery furnace with a confidence rooted in their knowledge of God: "Our God whom we serve is able to deliver us from the burning fiery furnace, and he will deliver us out of thine hand, O king. But if not, be it known unto thee, O king, that we will not serve thy gods, nor worship the golden image which thou hast set up" (Daniel 3:17-18, KJV). Their theology sustained their courage. They knew that God was able to deliver them, and they trusted that even if He chose not to deliver them from the furnace, He would deliver them through it and beyond it into His eternal presence. A high view of God produces a strong heart in the hour of trial.

Seventh, the doctrine of God should deepen our evangelism. When we understand the infinite perfections of God, we realize that the message we carry to the world is not a mere religious opinion or a helpful philosophy. It is the truth about the living God — the God who made the heavens and the earth, who sustains all things by the word of His power, who sent His only Son to die for sinners, and who commands all people everywhere to repent. The Apostle Paul's evangelistic preaching was saturated with the doctrine of God. On Mars Hill, he proclaimed: "God that made the world and all things therein, seeing that he is Lord of heaven and earth, dwelleth not in temples made with hands; neither is worshipped with men's hands, as though he needed any thing, seeing he giveth to all life, and breath, and all things; and hath made of one blood all nations of men for to dwell on all the face of the earth, and hath determined the times before appointed, and the bounds of their habitation; that they should seek the Lord, if haply they might feel after him, and find him, though he be not far from every one of us: for in him we live, and move, and have our being" (Acts 17:24-28, KJV). Paul began his evangelism with the doctrine of God, and so should we.

Let us also consider the relationship between the doctrine of God and the comfort of believers in times of bereavement. When a loved one dies, the believer's comfort is not found in platitudes or sentimentality but in the character of God. God is sovereign — He has appointed the number of our days. God is good — He does not afflict willingly. God is eternal — death is not the end for those who are in Christ. God is faithful — His promises to raise the dead and reunite His people are as certain as His own unchangeable nature. The Puritan pastor John Flavel, who buried two wives and several children, found comfort in the attributes of God during his deepest sorrows. He wrote: "The same God who took my beloved from me is the God who gave her to me. And He who is infinite in wisdom knows what is best. And He who is infinite in love does what is best. I will trust Him, though He slay me."

The great hymn writer Isaac Watts, deeply influenced by the Westminster Standards, captured the majesty of God's attributes in his famous paraphrase of Psalm 90:

"Before the hills in order stood,
Or earth received her frame,
From everlasting thou art God,
To endless years the same."

These words echo the teaching of the Westminster Standards and remind us that the God we worship is the eternal, unchangeable Lord of all — the same God who was worshipped by Abraham, Moses, David, and the apostles, and who will be worshipped by the redeemed for all eternity.
