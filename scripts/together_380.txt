PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 19
"The Dance of Mahanaim"
Song of Solomon 6:11-13 — The Beloved's Return to the Garden


Welcome, beloved couple. Tonight we encounter one of the more mysterious passages in the Song — the bride's return to the garden, and a call from the community to behold her beauty. The imagery is rich and inviting.

Song of Solomon 6:11-13:

"I went down into the garden of nuts to see the fruits of the valley, and to see whether the vine flourished, and the pomegranates budded. Or ever I was aware, my soul made me like the chariots of Amminadib. Return, return, O Shulamite; return, return, that we may look upon thee. What will ye see in the Shulamite? As it were the company of two armies."


"I went down into the garden of nuts to see the fruits of the valley." The bride goes to the garden to inspect the growth. She wants to know whether the vine is flourishing, whether the pomegranates are budding. This is the posture of a gardener — not passive but actively checking on the health of what has been planted.

The Puritans applied this directly to marriage. William Gouge wrote that husbands and wives should regularly "inspect the garden" of their relationship — taking stock of what is growing, what is withering, what needs attention. This is not anxious inspection but loving cultivation. A good gardener does not yank up plants to check the roots. A good gardener looks at the leaves, checks the fruit, waters where it is dry, and prunes where it is overgrown.

How long has it been since you took stock of your marriage? Not in a crisis, not in a counselor's office, but in the quiet of an ordinary evening — simply asking each other: How are we doing? What is flourishing? What needs attention?

"Or ever I was aware, my soul made me like the chariots of Amminadib." This enigmatic verse has puzzled commentators for centuries. The bride says that before she knew what was happening, her soul carried her away — lifted her up with the speed and power of a chariot. The Puritans interpreted this as the overwhelming power of desire. Thomas Gataker wrote that when love is healthy, there is a spontaneity to it — a rushing toward the beloved that does not wait for calculation or planning. The soul moves faster than the mind.

In marriage, this spontaneity is precious and should be protected. The danger of long marriage is that everything becomes calculated, planned, and scheduled. There is wisdom in planning, of course. But there should also be room for the soul to leap — for an unexpected embrace, an unscheduled date, a sudden "I love you" that has no occasion other than the sheer impulse of affection. Richard Sibbes called these moments "the play of grace" — the spontaneous overflowing of a heart that is full.

"Return, return, O Shulamite; return, return, that we may look upon thee." The community calls to the bride. They want to see her. They want to behold her. The name "Shulamite" appears only here in the entire Bible. It is the feminine form of "Solomon" — she is his counterpart, his equal, his match. The Puritans treasured this detail. The bride is not merely the king's possession; she is his partner. They share a name. They are one.

"What will ye see in the Shulamite? As it were the company of two armies." The word "Mahanaim" means "two camps" or "two armies." It was the place where Jacob saw the angels of God and said, "This is God's host" (Genesis 32:2). The Shulamite is like two armies — not in the sense of conflict, but in the sense of doubled strength. She carries a power and a presence that commands attention. The Puritans saw in this the beauty of a woman fully alive — a wife who has received her husband's love, weathered the storms of distance and reconciliation, and emerged with the radiance of someone who knows she is beloved.

Jeremiah Burroughs wrote that the most beautiful thing a spouse can see in their partner is the evidence of God's work. When you look at your husband or wife and see someone who has grown — who has become kinder, stronger, wiser, more patient through the years of your marriage — that is the beauty of Mahanaim. That is the glory of two armies. That is the work of God on display in a human soul.

Tonight, look at your spouse and see the evidence of growth. See the fruit of the valley. See the budding pomegranates. And be thankful.


Turn to each other and consider these questions.

Question 1: When was the last time you "inspected the garden" of your marriage — took stock of what is growing and what needs attention? Take a few minutes to do that now.

Question 2: Is there room for spontaneity in your marriage — unplanned moments of affection, surprise gestures, the soul leaping like a chariot? How can you create more space for spontaneous love?

Question 3: What growth do you see in your spouse that was not there when you first married? Name it. Celebrate it. Thank God for it.


Tonight's prayer focus: Thank God for the growth He has produced in your spouse and in your marriage. Ask Him to continue the work — to make the vines flourish and the pomegranates bud. Pray for the grace of spontaneous, overflowing love that surprises both of you.


Return, return, O beloved. The garden is budding. The fruit is forming. The work of God in your marriage is not finished — it is only beginning. Goodnight.
