PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"Hear This, All Ye People"
June 25, 2026

---

"Hear this, all ye people; give ear, all ye inhabitants of the world: both low and high, rich and poor, together. My mouth shall speak of wisdom; and the meditation of my heart shall be of understanding. I will incline mine ear to a parable: I will open my dark saying upon the harp. Wherefore should I fear in the days of evil, when the iniquity of my heels shall compass me about?" -- Psalm 49:1-5

This psalm is a universal summons. It addresses not one nation but all nations, not one class but every class, not the rich alone or the poor alone but both together. And the wisdom it offers is not the wisdom of the marketplace or the academy but the wisdom of eternity -- the wisdom that confronts the one question which every human being must eventually face but which most spend their lives avoiding: what happens when we die, and what does death mean for all the things we have accumulated in this life?

The psalmist begins by establishing the universality of his audience and the gravity of his theme. "Hear this, all ye people; give ear, all ye inhabitants of the world." There is no exemption clause here. Kings must hear as well as beggars. The learned must listen as well as the ignorant. The wealthy must give ear as well as the destitute. For the truth that is about to be declared applies equally to every member of the human race, without distinction of rank, wealth, education, or station.

Spurgeon noted that few subjects are more unwelcome to the natural man than the subject of death and the limitations of wealth in the face of it. We live in a society that worships prosperity and dreads mortality, and the combination of these two idolatries produces a peculiar form of blindness. Men accumulate wealth as though it could save them from the grave. They build empires as though they would last forever. They surround themselves with luxury as though comfort could ward off death. And all the while the grave yawns before them, and their gold cannot purchase a single additional heartbeat when God calls their time.

"Wherefore should I fear in the days of evil, when the iniquity of my heels shall compass me about?" This enigmatic question has been variously interpreted, but the most satisfying reading is that the psalmist is asking why he should fear in times of adversity, when those who pursue him treacherously seem to have the upper hand. And the answer, which unfolds in the verses that follow, is that the wicked who trust in their wealth have built their confidence upon a foundation of sand. "They that trust in their wealth, and boast themselves in the multitude of their riches; none of them can by any means redeem his brother, nor give to God a ransom for him."

Thomas Watson preached with devastating clarity upon the impotence of riches in the hour of death. A man may buy the finest physicians in the world, but he cannot buy one hour of life beyond the time appointed by God. He may purchase the most splendid tomb ever carved from marble, but he cannot purchase a single ray of light in the darkness of the grave. He may leave behind a fortune that would make kings envious, but he cannot take a single penny with him into eternity. Death strips the rich man of everything that distinguished him from the beggar, and they lie down together in the same dust, equal at last in the only equality that matters.

John Owen meditated upon the phrase "nor give to God a ransom for him" and found in it a profound anticipation of the gospel. Man cannot ransom himself from death. No amount of wealth, no accumulation of good works, no human effort of any kind can pay the price that divine justice demands. The ransom is too great. The debt is too vast. And if man cannot ransom himself, then either he must perish forever, or someone else must pay the ransom for him. And this is precisely what Christ has done. He has given to God the ransom that no man could give. He has paid the price that no earthly fortune could cover. He has redeemed His people from the grave, not with corruptible things such as silver and gold, but with His own precious blood.

Jeremiah Burroughs urged his listeners to learn from this psalm the art of holding earthly possessions loosely. We are stewards, not owners. We are pilgrims, not settlers. Everything we possess in this world is on loan, and the day is coming when it will all be returned to the One from whom it came. The wise man, therefore, does not set his heart upon his wealth but upon his God. He does not trust in the multitude of his riches but in the grace of his Redeemer. He does not fear in the days of evil, because his hope is not in things that can be stolen or destroyed but in a treasure laid up in heaven, where neither moth nor rust doth corrupt and where thieves do not break through nor steal.

Let us hear this wisdom, all of us, rich and poor together. Let us lay down our confidence in the things that perish and take up our confidence in the God who endures forever. For no man can give to God a ransom for his brother, but Christ has given the ransom for us all. In Him alone is our hope, our redemption, and our everlasting life. Amen.
