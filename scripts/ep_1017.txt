PURITAN GOLD -- Study Topic: Female Piety, Part 7 of 10
"Female Influence on Society — How Godly Women Shape Culture and Community"
John Angell James — Female Piety

---

Welcome back, dear listener, to Puritan Gold. We are studying Female Piety by John Angell James, and today we broaden our vision beyond the walls of the home to consider the influence that godly women exert upon the whole of society. James was convinced that the piety of women is one of the most potent forces for good in any civilization, and that when this piety wanes, the entire moral fabric of a nation begins to unravel. Let us hear his argument.

Let us read from Proverbs 31:26 and 31, in the King James Version:

"She openeth her mouth with wisdom; and in her tongue is the law of kindness... Give her of the fruit of her hands; and let her own works praise her in the gates."

And from Judges 5:7:

"The inhabitants of the villages ceased, they ceased in Israel, until that I Deborah arose, that I arose a mother in Israel."

--- Teaching ---

John Angell James was a keen observer of the society around him. As pastor of one of the largest Nonconformist congregations in England, he had a front-row seat to the social, moral, and spiritual currents of early Victorian Britain. And what he saw convinced him that the influence of women upon society was both more pervasive and more powerful than most people realized.

James argues that women shape society through three principal channels: personal influence, charitable activity, and moral example. Let us examine each.

First, personal influence. James observes that women, by the very nature of their relationships, are constantly influencing those around them. The mother influences her children, who grow up to become citizens, leaders, and parents themselves. The wife influences her husband, whose decisions may affect businesses, churches, and communities. The friend influences her companions, the neighbor her neighborhood, the teacher her students. This web of personal influence is largely invisible to the public eye, but its cumulative effect is enormous.

James illustrates this point with examples from Scripture and from history. He calls to mind the influence of Sarah upon the household of Abraham, of Hannah upon the young Samuel, of the Shunammite woman upon the prophet Elisha, and of Deborah upon the nation of Israel. In each case, a woman's faith, wisdom, and courage had consequences that extended far beyond her immediate circle. Deborah's bold declaration — "I arose a mother in Israel" — captures the scope of what James has in mind. She was not merely a mother in the domestic sense; she was a mother to the whole nation, a woman whose piety and leadership shaped the course of Israelite history.

Among the Puritans, the influence of godly women was likewise widely recognized. Thomas Brooks, in his Precious Remedies Against Satan's Devices, acknowledged the spiritual strength of many women in his congregation whose prayers and faithfulness upheld the work of the ministry. Matthew Henry, the great commentator, famously credited his mother with the formation of his own character and piety. Susanna Wesley, though technically of a later era, stands as perhaps the supreme example of a woman whose domestic faithfulness produced world-changing fruit in the lives of her sons John and Charles.

James was also deeply aware of the role women played in the charitable and philanthropic movements of his day. The early nineteenth century in England saw an explosion of voluntary organizations dedicated to social reform, education, missions, and poor relief — and women were at the heart of many of them. They founded schools for the poor. They organized societies for the relief of widows and orphans. They supported foreign missions with their prayers and their purses. They visited prisons, hospitals, and workhouses, carrying the compassion of Christ into the darkest corners of society.

James commends this charitable activity warmly, but he insists that it must flow from genuine piety. Philanthropy without godliness, he warns, may accomplish outward good while leaving the deepest needs of the soul unaddressed. The Christian woman who serves the poor does so not merely out of humanitarian sentiment but out of love for Christ, who identified Himself with the least of His brethren. "Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me" (Matthew 25:40). This motive purifies the service, sustains the servant, and directs the work toward its true end — the glory of God and the salvation of souls.

Third, James speaks of the power of moral example. In every community, he observes, there are women whose lives serve as models for others. Their integrity, their compassion, their devotion to God, and their steadfastness in trial become a kind of living sermon that speaks more loudly than any words. When a woman of genuine piety walks through a crisis with grace and faith, the watching world takes notice. When she forgives an injury, serves a neighbor without thought of return, or maintains her integrity in the face of temptation, she demonstrates the reality and the power of the gospel in a way that arguments alone cannot.

James also issues a sober warning. If the influence of godly women is powerful for good, the influence of ungodly women is equally powerful for evil. When women abandon true religion, when they give themselves over to vanity, worldliness, and self-indulgence, the effects are catastrophic. James cites the prophets of the Old Testament, who repeatedly linked the spiritual condition of Israel's women to the moral health of the nation. Isaiah cried out against the vain daughters of Zion (Isaiah 3:16-26). Amos rebuked the women of Samaria who oppressed the poor and lived in luxury (Amos 4:1). In each case, the spiritual failure of women both reflected and accelerated the broader decline of the society.

This is why James regarded the cultivation of female piety as a matter of national importance. He was not merely concerned with the spiritual welfare of individual women, though he cared deeply about that. He was concerned with the health of the entire social order, which he believed was inextricably linked to the moral and spiritual condition of its women. A nation whose women fear God is a nation built on a firm foundation. A nation whose women have abandoned the faith is a nation in peril, regardless of its wealth, military power, or cultural sophistication.

James calls upon the women of his generation — and we may extend his call to our own — to recognize the weight of their influence and to wield it for the glory of God. He does not ask them to seek public platforms or political power. He asks them to be faithful where they are — in their homes, their churches, their friendships, and their communities — trusting that God will multiply the effects of their piety in ways that surpass all human calculation.

The Proverbs 31 woman is praised in the gates — that is, in the public square. But her praise is earned not by seeking public attention but by the quiet, faithful, Spirit-empowered conduct of her daily life. "Give her of the fruit of her hands; and let her own works praise her in the gates." The godly woman's influence speaks for itself. It needs no marketing campaign. It needs only faithfulness.

--- Application ---

1. Consider the spheres of influence God has given you — family, friends, neighbors, church, workplace, community. In which of these spheres are you most actively exercising a godly influence? In which are you most neglecting it? Ask the Lord to make you a faithful steward of every relationship He has entrusted to you.

2. Identify one area of charitable service where you could contribute your time, resources, or gifts. Whether it is serving in your church's mercy ministry, visiting the elderly, mentoring a younger woman, or supporting a mission — take one concrete step this week.

3. Reflect on the warning of the prophets regarding the spiritual decline of women. Are there areas of vanity, worldliness, or self-indulgence in your life that may be undermining your influence for good? Confess these to the Lord and ask for His help in putting them to death.

4. Pray for the women of your nation. Ask God to raise up a generation of women who fear Him, who love His Word, and who exercise their influence for the advancement of His kingdom.

--- Closing ---

Let us pray.

Almighty God, who hast given to women a sphere of influence that reaches from the cradle to the grave and from the home to the nation, we ask Thee to sanctify that influence for Thy glory. Raise up among us women of faith and courage, women of wisdom and compassion, women whose lives adorn the gospel and whose works praise Thee in the gates. Forgive us where we have wasted or misdirected the influence Thou hast given us. Help us to be faithful in small things and in great, trusting that Thou art able to do exceeding abundantly above all that we ask or think. Through Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.

Go in peace, dear listener, and remember that the influence of a godly life is never wasted. May God multiply the fruit of your faithfulness beyond all that you can imagine. Until next time.
