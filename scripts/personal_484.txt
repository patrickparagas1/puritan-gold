PURITAN GOLD -- My Growth, Day 23
"The Discipline of Suffering"
Pastoral Day — Finding God in the Furnace


Welcome. Today is a Pastoral day. Our subject is suffering — not its theological explanation (we covered that in providence) but its practical navigation. How do you walk through the fire and come out refined rather than destroyed?

Let us read 1 Peter 4:12-13 (KJV):

"Beloved, think it not strange concerning the fiery trial which is to try you, as though some strange thing happened unto you: But rejoice, inasmuch as ye are partakers of Christ's sufferings; that, when his glory shall be revealed, ye may be glad also with exceeding joy."


Peter says: do not think it strange. Suffering is not an anomaly in the Christian life. It is the norm. The Puritans understood this because they lived it. Many of them lost positions, properties, and liberty for the sake of conscience. Some, like Bunyan, spent years in prison. Others, like Baxter, were expelled from their pulpits. They wrote about suffering not from ivory towers but from the furnace itself.

The first counsel the Puritans offer is this: do not waste your suffering. Samuel Rutherford, writing from prison, said that afflictions are only lost when we refuse to learn from them. God sends suffering with a purpose — and the Christian who merely endures suffering without asking "What is God teaching me?" has missed the point. Every affliction is a classroom. What is the lesson?

Second, remember that suffering is temporary. Paul calls it "our light affliction, which is but for a moment." Rutherford wrote from his prison cell that the worst suffering in this life is merely the porch to glory. The porch is uncomfortable, but you do not live on the porch. You pass through it into the house. And the house — eternal glory — will make every moment of porch-standing worth it.

Third, cling to the promises of God. The Puritans were practical about this. They recommended having specific promises memorized for specific kinds of suffering. When you are afraid, recall "Fear thou not; for I am with thee" (Isaiah 41:10). When you are grieving, recall "Weeping may endure for a night, but joy cometh in the morning" (Psalm 30:5). When you feel abandoned, recall "I will never leave thee, nor forsake thee" (Hebrews 13:5). These promises are not magic words. They are anchors — truths that hold you steady when the waves are fierce.

Fourth, pour out your heart to God honestly. The Puritans did not counsel stoicism. They counseled honest, broken, raw prayer. The Psalms are full of complaint, and the Puritans took their cue from David. You do not have to pretend that everything is fine. You do not have to perform spiritual strength. You can weep. You can groan. You can ask "How long, O Lord?" These are not signs of weak faith. They are signs of real faith — faith that brings its pain to God rather than hiding it.

Fifth, seek the fellowship of other sufferers. The Puritans valued what they called the communion of suffering — the unique bond that forms between people who have endured hardship together. Richard Baxter wrote that the church is meant to be a hospital, not a museum. When you suffer, lean on your brothers and sisters. And when they suffer, be present for them. Shared suffering creates depths of friendship that prosperity can never produce.

Sixth, look for the fruit. James 1:2-4 says, "Count it all joy when ye fall into divers temptations; knowing this, that the trying of your faith worketh patience. But let patience have her perfect work, that ye may be perfect and entire, wanting nothing." The fruit of suffering includes patience, compassion, humility, dependence on God, detachment from the world, and a deeper capacity for joy. These fruits do not appear overnight. But they appear. And when they do, you will see that the furnace was not punishment but refining.

Seventh, keep your eyes on Christ. He suffered more than you ever will. He was despised, rejected, betrayed, mocked, beaten, and crucified. And He endured it all "for the joy that was set before him" (Hebrews 12:2). The Puritans looked to Christ as the supreme example of redemptive suffering — One who transformed the worst evil in history into the greatest good in eternity.


You are in the furnace. But you are not alone. The Fourth Man is in the fire with you. Lord, I am hurting. I will not pretend otherwise. But I trust that You are with me in the fire and that the flames are refining, not destroying. Give me patience to endure, wisdom to learn, and faith to believe that joy comes in the morning. Amen.
