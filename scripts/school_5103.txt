PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 3
"Of God and the Holy Trinity"
The Westminster Standards — Lesson 3

---

Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we ascend to the highest subject any mind can contemplate — the doctrine of God Himself. We will explore what the Westminster Standards teach about who God is, what He is like, and the glorious mystery of the Holy Trinity — one God in three Persons, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. This is not merely an intellectual exercise. The knowledge of God is the foundation of all true religion, all genuine worship, and all lasting happiness. As our Lord Jesus Christ declared in His great high priestly prayer: "And this is life eternal, that they might know thee the only true God, and Jesus Christ, whom thou hast sent" (John 17:3, KJV). To know God is the highest calling, the deepest joy, and the most urgent need of every human soul. Let us pray and begin.

Almighty God, we approach Thy majesty with reverence and awe. Thou art infinite, eternal, and unchangeable in all Thy perfections. As we study Thy attributes today, expand our understanding and deepen our worship. Help us to know Thee truly, for this is eternal life — to know Thee and Jesus Christ whom Thou hast sent. Grant that the truths we learn about Thee this day may not remain as mere words in our minds but may take root in our hearts and bear fruit in our lives. In Christ's name, Amen.

--- Lesson ---

Part One: The Highest Study

After establishing the authority of Scripture in Chapter 1, the Westminster Confession turns in Chapters 2 and 3 to the doctrine of God — His nature, His attributes, and the mystery of the Trinity. This is fitting, for the Bible exists to reveal God to us, and all other doctrines derive their meaning and importance from the doctrine of God. The great Puritan theologian Stephen Charnock, whose "Discourses upon the Existence and Attributes of God" stands as one of the monumental achievements of English theology, wrote: "The knowledge of the nature of God is the foundation of all religion and the ground of all worship." If we do not know who God is, we cannot worship Him rightly, trust Him fully, or serve Him faithfully.

The Shorter Catechism addresses the doctrine of God with memorable precision. Question 4 asks, "What is God?" and answers: "God is a Spirit, infinite, eternal, and unchangeable, in his being, wisdom, power, holiness, justice, goodness, and truth."

This single sentence — just twenty-two words in the original English — is one of the most profound and comprehensive statements about God ever composed by human hands. It has been memorized by millions of Christians over nearly four centuries, and its depth is inexhaustible. Let us examine it with the care and reverence it deserves, for we are handling the most sacred of all subjects.

Part Two: "God Is a Spirit"

The catechism begins with a foundational affirmation: "God is a Spirit." This teaching comes directly from the lips of our Lord Jesus Christ. In His conversation with the Samaritan woman at Jacob's well, Jesus declared: "God is a Spirit: and they that worship him must worship him in spirit and in truth" (John 4:24, KJV). This statement carries enormous implications.

To say that God is a Spirit means that God does not have a physical body. He is not composed of matter. He is not limited to one location. He cannot be perceived by the physical senses — seen with the eyes, heard with the ears, or touched with the hands — in His essential nature. Moses was told, "Thou canst not see my face: for there shall no man see me, and live" (Exodus 33:20, KJV). God transcends the material universe. He is not part of creation. He is the Creator of all that is, and He exists on a plane of being entirely different from anything He has made.

This does not mean that God is vague, impersonal, or distant. Quite the opposite. Because God is Spirit, He is not limited by the constraints of physical existence. He is everywhere present — "Do not I fill heaven and earth? saith the LORD" (Jeremiah 23:24, KJV). He is perfectly aware of all things — "The eyes of the LORD are in every place, beholding the evil and the good" (Proverbs 15:3, KJV). He is actively engaged with His creation at every moment — "in him we live, and move, and have our being" (Acts 17:28, KJV).

Stephen Charnock devoted extensive attention to God's spiritual nature, arguing that it is the foundation of all His other attributes. Because God is Spirit, He is not subject to decay, fatigue, limitation, or change. He is the most real, the most alive, the most active being in existence. The material world, by comparison, is dependent, derivative, and transient. God is the source and sustainer of all reality.

The practical implication is profound: we cannot worship God through physical images or material representations. The second commandment forbids the making of any graven image or likeness of God, precisely because any physical representation of the infinite, spiritual God would necessarily be a distortion. True worship is spiritual worship — the worship of the heart, mind, and soul, directed by truth, empowered by the Spirit, and offered through the mediation of Christ.

Part Three: "Infinite"

The next word in the catechism's answer is "infinite." God has no limits. There is no boundary to His being, no end to His power, no ceiling to His knowledge, no circumference to His presence. The concept of infinity is one that the human mind cannot fully comprehend, and that is precisely the point. God exceeds our capacity to understand Him. He is, as the theologians say, "incomprehensible" — not in the sense that we can know nothing about Him, but in the sense that we can never know everything about Him. Our knowledge of God is real but never exhaustive.

Consider the infinity of God's power. "Behold, I am the LORD, the God of all flesh: is there any thing too hard for me?" (Jeremiah 32:27, KJV). "With God all things are possible" (Matthew 19:26, KJV). God spoke the universe into existence by the word of His power. He sustains galaxies, governs atoms, directs the course of history, and holds the breath of every living creature in His hands — all simultaneously, all effortlessly, all without the slightest diminishment of His infinite resources.

Consider the infinity of God's knowledge. "Great is our Lord, and of great power: his understanding is infinite" (Psalm 147:5, KJV). God knows all things — past, present, and future; actual and possible; the thoughts of every heart, the words of every tongue, the deeds of every hand. Nothing is hidden from Him. Nothing surprises Him. He sees the end from the beginning and every detail in between. The Puritan Thomas Watson wrote: "God's knowledge extends to future contingencies. He knows what shall be to all eternity, and what would be if He did not prevent it."

Consider the infinity of God's presence. "Whither shall I go from thy spirit? or whither shall I flee from thy presence? If I ascend up into heaven, thou art there: if I make my bed in hell, behold, thou art there. If I take the wings of the morning, and dwell in the uttermost parts of the sea; even there shall thy hand lead me, and thy right hand shall hold me" (Psalm 139:7-10, KJV). There is no place in the universe — or beyond the universe — where God is not. His presence fills all things.

The infinity of God should produce in us not frustration but wonder. We worship a God who is greater than our minds can conceive, more powerful than our imaginations can picture, and more present than our senses can detect. As the prophet Isaiah recorded: "For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways, saith the LORD. For as the heavens are higher than the earth, so are my ways higher than your ways, and my thoughts than your thoughts" (Isaiah 55:8-9, KJV).

Part Four: "Eternal"

God has no beginning and no end. He did not come into existence at some point in the past, and He will never cease to exist. Before the mountains were formed, before the stars were kindled, before time itself began, God was. And when the present heavens and earth have passed away, God will still be. Moses, the man of God, declared: "Before the mountains were brought forth, or ever thou hadst formed the earth and the world, even from everlasting to everlasting, thou art God" (Psalm 90:2, KJV).

Time itself is a creation of God. He is not bound by it but stands above it and governs it. He does not experience time as we do — as a succession of moments, each replacing the last. All of reality is equally present to Him. He sees all of history — from creation to consummation — in a single, eternal act of knowledge. The apostle Peter reminds us: "But, beloved, be not ignorant of this one thing, that one day is with the Lord as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day" (2 Peter 3:8, KJV).

This has profound implications for our understanding of God's actions. When the Bible speaks of God planning, decreeing, creating, redeeming, and judging, it is using language adapted to our finite understanding. In reality, God's purposes are eternal — they do not unfold in sequence within His mind as they do in our experience of history. The Westminster Confession captures this when it speaks of God's "eternal decrees" — purposes established from everlasting that are executed in time according to His sovereign will.

Stephen Charnock wrote at great length on God's eternity, arguing that it is one of the most comforting of all divine attributes. Because God is eternal, His love is eternal — "I have loved thee with an everlasting love" (Jeremiah 31:3, KJV). His purposes are eternal — they cannot be thwarted or frustrated by the passage of time. His promises are eternal — they can never expire or become void. And the salvation He provides is eternal — "And I give unto them eternal life; and they shall never perish, neither shall any man pluck them out of my hand" (John 10:28, KJV).

Part Five: "Unchangeable"

God does not change. His character is the same yesterday, today, and forever. He does not grow wiser, because He is already infinitely wise. He does not grow stronger, because He is already infinitely powerful. He does not grow more loving, because His love is already perfect and complete. He does not change His mind in the way that humans do, because His decisions are based on perfect knowledge and perfect wisdom from all eternity. "For I am the LORD, I change not; therefore ye sons of Jacob are not consumed" (Malachi 3:6, KJV). "Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above, and cometh down from the Father of lights, with whom is no variableness, neither shadow of turning" (James 1:17, KJV).

The theological term for this is "immutability," and it is one of the most comforting truths in all of theology. In a world where everything changes — where governments rise and fall, where health deteriorates, where relationships fracture, where the ground itself shifts beneath our feet — God remains the same. His character does not fluctuate. His promises do not waver. His purposes do not shift. He is the one fixed point in a universe of change, the one absolutely reliable reality in a world of uncertainty.

But what about those passages in Scripture that speak of God "repenting" or "changing His mind" — as when it says that "it repented the LORD that he had made man on the earth" (Genesis 6:6, KJV)? The Westminster divines, following the best of Reformed theology, understood these passages as anthropomorphic language — human language used to describe God's actions in terms that we can understand. When the Bible says that God "repented," it does not mean that He experienced a change of mind or will in the way that we do. It means that His unchanging purposes resulted in a change of action that, from a human perspective, looks like a change of mind. God's will is eternally settled. What changes is not God but the circumstances to which His will is applied.

Thomas Watson, in his exposition of the Shorter Catechism, put it simply: "God is unchangeable in His essence, in His attributes, in His counsel, in His promises, in His threatenings. He is not like the chameleon, that changes color." This unchangeableness is the foundation of all our confidence in God. If God could change, we could never be sure of Him. But because He is the same from everlasting to everlasting, we can trust Him absolutely.

Part Six: The Seven Attributes

These four qualities — Spirit, infinite, eternal, unchangeable — apply to each of God's perfections. The catechism lists seven: being, wisdom, power, holiness, justice, goodness, and truth. God is infinite, eternal, and unchangeable in each of these.

His being — His very existence — is self-existent and self-sufficient. God depends on nothing outside Himself for His existence. He is, as He declared to Moses at the burning bush, "I AM THAT I AM" (Exodus 3:14, KJV). This divine name — the covenant name Yahweh, or Jehovah — expresses the absolute, self-existent being of God. He is the one who simply is. All other beings derive their existence from Him. He derives His existence from nothing and no one.

His wisdom is the perfection by which He always knows the best end to pursue and the best means to achieve it. God's wisdom is displayed in creation — "O LORD, how manifold are thy works! in wisdom hast thou made them all" (Psalm 104:24, KJV). It is displayed in providence — His governance of all events to accomplish His purposes. And it is supremely displayed in redemption — "the wisdom of God in a mystery, even the hidden wisdom, which God ordained before the world unto our glory" (1 Corinthians 2:7, KJV). The plan of salvation — rescuing guilty sinners through the death of His own Son — is the supreme masterpiece of divine wisdom.

His power is unlimited — nothing is too hard for Him, and nothing can resist His will. "The LORD God omnipotent reigneth" (Revelation 19:6, KJV). The Puritan John Flavel wrote: "The power of God is that ability and strength whereby He can bring to pass whatsoever He pleases, whatsoever His infinite wisdom may direct, and whatsoever the infinite purity of His will may resolve."

His holiness is His absolute moral purity and His complete separation from all evil. Holiness is not merely one attribute among many; it is the atmosphere in which all God's other attributes operate. His love is holy love. His justice is holy justice. His power is holy power. When the seraphim worship before God's throne, they do not cry "Love, love, love" or "Power, power, power" — though God is infinitely loving and powerful. They cry "Holy, holy, holy, is the LORD of hosts: the whole earth is full of his glory" (Isaiah 6:3, KJV). The threefold repetition expresses the superlative — God is not merely holy, not merely most holy, but holy beyond holiness, pure beyond purity. Charnock called holiness "the crown of all God's attributes."

His justice is the attribute by which He always does what is right. He rewards righteousness and punishes evil. He is never unfair, never arbitrary, never biased. "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?" (Genesis 18:25, KJV). His justice is the foundation of His throne — "Justice and judgment are the habitation of thy throne: mercy and truth shall go before thy face" (Psalm 89:14, KJV).

His goodness is the source of all that is good, beautiful, and true. Every blessing, every kindness, every gift of grace flows from the inexhaustible fountain of divine goodness. "The LORD is good to all: and his tender mercies are over all his works" (Psalm 145:9, KJV). Thomas Watson wrote: "God's goodness is the source and spring of all the good that comes to us. It is like a fountain that sends forth living water."

His truth is His absolute reliability and transparency. He cannot lie. His Word is certain. His promises are unbreakable. "God is not a man, that he should lie; neither the son of man, that he should repent: hath he said, and shall he not do it? or hath he spoken, and shall he not make it good?" (Numbers 23:19, KJV). There is no shadow of duplicity in God. What He says, He means. What He promises, He performs. What He threatens, He executes — unless repentance intervenes, in which case the relenting itself is part of His unchanging purpose.

The Puritan Thomas Manton, one of the members of the Westminster Assembly and later one of the great nonconformist preachers of Restoration England, preached an extended series of sermons on the attributes of God drawn from the Shorter Catechism. In these sermons, Manton demonstrated how each attribute has both a terrifying and a comforting aspect. God's holiness is terrifying to the sinner who stands exposed before His purity, but comforting to the saint who knows that the holy God has cleansed them through the blood of Christ. God's justice is terrifying to the unrepentant who face His righteous judgment, but comforting to the oppressed who know that the Judge of all the earth will set all things right. God's power is terrifying to His enemies who cannot resist His will, but comforting to His children who rest under the shadow of His almighty arm. Every attribute of God, rightly understood, drives us either to despair (if we remain in our sins) or to worship (if we have taken refuge in Christ).

These seven attributes, taken together, form a portrait of God that is majestic beyond description. He is the self-existent One who needs nothing, the all-wise One who is never confused, the almighty One who is never defeated, the holy One who is never tainted, the just One who is never unfair, the good One who is never cruel, and the true One who is never false. This is the God we worship. This is the God who has revealed Himself in His Word. This is the God who calls us into fellowship with Himself through His Son and by His Spirit.

Part Seven: The Doctrine of the Holy Trinity

The Westminster Confession addresses the doctrine of the Trinity in Chapter 2, Section 3: "In the unity of the Godhead there be three Persons of one substance, power, and eternity: God the Father, God the Son, and God the Holy Ghost. The Father is of none, neither begotten nor proceeding; the Son is eternally begotten of the Father; the Holy Ghost eternally proceeding from the Father and the Son."

This is the central mystery of the Christian faith — a truth revealed in Scripture and affirmed by the church throughout its history from the earliest centuries. The doctrine of the Trinity teaches that there is one God — not three gods — who eternally exists as three distinct Persons — Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — each fully and equally God, yet there are not three Gods but one.

Let us be clear about what the doctrine does and does not teach. It does not teach that God is one Person who appears in three different modes or roles — as if the Father, Son, and Spirit were merely different masks worn by a single divine actor. This heresy, known as Modalism or Sabellianism (after the third-century teacher Sabellius), was condemned by the early church. The Father, Son, and Spirit are distinct Persons who relate to one another — the Father loves the Son, the Son obeys the Father, the Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son. These are real relationships between real Persons, not different aspects of a single Person.

Nor does the doctrine teach that there are three separate Gods who happen to cooperate. This heresy, known as Tritheism, was also rejected by the church. There is one God — one divine essence, one divine nature, one divine being. The three Persons share this one nature completely and equally. There is no division of the divine essence among the three Persons. Each Person is fully God, possessing the whole of the divine nature.

The Confession's language is drawn from the great creeds and councils of the early church. The Council of Nicaea in 325 A.D. affirmed that the Son is "of one substance" (homoousios) with the Father — not a created being, not a lesser god, but truly and fully God. The Council of Constantinople in 381 A.D. extended this affirmation to the Holy Spirit, declaring Him to be "the Lord and Giver of life, who proceeds from the Father, who with the Father and the Son together is worshiped and glorified." The Athanasian Creed, which dates from approximately the fifth or sixth century, provides perhaps the most detailed statement of Trinitarian doctrine in the history of the church.

Part Eight: The Biblical Foundation of the Trinity

The doctrine of the Trinity is not a philosophical invention imposed on the Bible from outside. It is the necessary conclusion of what the Bible teaches. Consider the biblical evidence.

First, the Bible teaches that there is one God. "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God is one LORD" (Deuteronomy 6:4, KJV). "I am the LORD, and there is none else, there is no God beside me" (Isaiah 45:5, KJV). The monotheism of Scripture is absolute and uncompromising.

Second, the Bible teaches that the Father is God. This is assumed throughout Scripture and explicitly stated in passages like 1 Corinthians 8:6: "But to us there is but one God, the Father, of whom are all things, and we in him."

Third, the Bible teaches that the Son is God. "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God" (John 1:1, KJV). "And Thomas answered and said unto him, My Lord and my God" (John 20:28, KJV). "For in him dwelleth all the fulness of the Godhead bodily" (Colossians 2:9, KJV). Christ receives worship (Matthew 14:33, 28:9), forgives sins (Mark 2:5-7), and claims divine prerogatives ("Before Abraham was, I am" — John 8:58).

Fourth, the Bible teaches that the Holy Spirit is God. When Ananias lied to the Holy Spirit, Peter said, "Thou hast not lied unto men, but unto God" (Acts 5:4, KJV). The Spirit has divine attributes — He is omnipresent (Psalm 139:7-10), omniscient ("the Spirit searcheth all things, yea, the deep things of God" — 1 Corinthians 2:10), and omnipotent (generating the miraculous conception of Christ — Luke 1:35).

Fifth, the Bible distinguishes the three Persons from one another. At the baptism of Jesus, the Son stands in the water, the Father speaks from heaven, and the Spirit descends like a dove (Matthew 3:16-17). In the Great Commission, Jesus commands baptism "in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost" (Matthew 28:19) — one name, three Persons. Paul's apostolic benediction invokes all three: "The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, and the love of God, and the communion of the Holy Ghost, be with you all" (2 Corinthians 13:14, KJV).

The doctrine of the Trinity is the only way to hold all of these biblical truths together. One God. Three Persons. Each fully God. Each distinct from the others. This is the mystery that the church has confessed for two thousand years and that the Westminster Confession faithfully summarizes.

Part Nine: The Practical Importance of the Trinity

Understanding the doctrine of God is not merely an intellectual exercise. It is the foundation of worship, prayer, salvation, and the entire Christian life.

The Trinity is the foundation of worship. We worship one God in three Persons — praising the Father who planned our salvation, the Son who accomplished it, and the Spirit who applies it. True Christian worship is Trinitarian worship.

The Trinity is the foundation of prayer. We pray to the Father, in the name of the Son, by the power of the Spirit. Every prayer is a Trinitarian act, made possible by the distinct yet harmonious work of all three Persons of the Godhead.

The Trinity is the foundation of salvation. The Father elects, the Son redeems, and the Spirit regenerates. If any one of the three Persons were not truly God, our salvation would be in jeopardy. If the Son were a mere creature, His death could not atone for the sins of the world. If the Spirit were a mere force rather than a divine Person, He could not regenerate dead souls and unite them to Christ. The full deity of each Person is essential to the gospel.

The Trinity is the foundation of love. The Bible declares that "God is love" (1 John 4:8). But love requires an object. If God were a solitary, unipersonal being, He could not have been love from all eternity — for love requires another to love. But because God is Trinitarian — Father, Son, and Spirit in eternal fellowship — love has existed within the Godhead from before the creation of the world. The Father loves the Son. The Son loves the Father. The Spirit is the bond of love between them. This eternal, intratrinitarian love is the fountain from which God's love for His creatures flows.

The Puritan John Owen, in his magnificent treatise "Communion with God," explored at length how believers enjoy distinct communion with each of the three Persons of the Trinity. We commune with the Father in His love, with the Son in His grace, and with the Spirit in His comfort and sanctifying power. The Christian life is not a relationship with an abstract deity but a living, personal communion with the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

Part Ten: The Historical Development of Trinitarian Doctrine

It is valuable for homeschool students to understand how the doctrine of the Trinity was articulated and defended in the early centuries of church history, for this story reveals both the importance of the doctrine and the vigilance required to preserve it.

In the earliest centuries, the church simply worshiped the Father, Son, and Spirit as God, without needing to articulate the precise theological formulas that would later become necessary. Christians baptized in the threefold name. They prayed to the Father through the Son in the Spirit. They confessed Christ as Lord and worshiped Him alongside the Father. The Trinitarian pattern was embedded in their worship and their Scriptures long before it was articulated in creedal form.

The need for precise theological language arose when heretics began to deny or distort the biblical teaching. The most dangerous heresy was Arianism, taught by Arius, a priest of Alexandria in Egypt, beginning around 318 A.D. Arius taught that the Son of God was a created being — the first and greatest of God's creatures, but not truly God. "There was a time when the Son was not," Arius declared. This struck at the very heart of the Christian faith, for if the Son is a creature, His death cannot atone for sin, and we are still in our sins.

The great champion of orthodoxy was Athanasius, a young deacon of Alexandria who attended the Council of Nicaea in 325 A.D. and spent the rest of his life defending its declaration that the Son is "of one substance" (homoousios) with the Father — truly God, not a creature. Athanasius was exiled five times for his defense of the faith, spending a total of seventeen years in banishment. But he never wavered. His famous declaration — "Athanasius contra mundum" (Athanasius against the world) — became a byword for unwavering theological courage. He understood that the doctrine of the Trinity was not a speculative abstraction but the foundation of the gospel. If Christ is not truly God, He cannot save us. If the Spirit is not truly God, He cannot regenerate us. The full deity of all three Persons is essential to the Christian message.

The Council of Nicaea in 325 produced the Nicene Creed, which declared the Son to be "God of God, Light of Light, very God of very God, begotten, not made, being of one substance with the Father." The Council of Constantinople in 381 extended and completed the Creed, adding the crucial affirmation of the deity of the Holy Spirit. The Chalcedonian Definition of 451, while primarily concerned with the two natures of Christ, further clarified the Trinitarian framework within which Christology must be understood.

The Westminster divines stood in direct continuity with these ancient councils and creeds. When the Confession declares that "in the unity of the Godhead there be three Persons of one substance, power, and eternity," it is echoing the language of Nicaea and Constantinople. When it affirms that the Son is "eternally begotten of the Father" and the Spirit "eternally proceeding from the Father and the Son," it is drawing on centuries of careful theological reflection. The divines did not consider themselves innovators. They were faithful transmitters of the catholic (universal) Christian faith, articulating in the language of their own day the truths that the church had confessed since the apostolic era.

Part Eleven: Heresies Concerning the Trinity and How to Avoid Them

It is helpful for students to know the major Trinitarian heresies, not in order to be fascinated by error but in order to recognize and avoid it. Error often masquerades as a simpler or more reasonable version of the truth, and without knowledge of the historical heresies, well-meaning Christians can unwittingly fall into ancient traps.

Arianism, as we have seen, denies the full deity of the Son. Modern versions of Arianism include the Jehovah's Witnesses, who teach that Jesus is a created being — Michael the Archangel in human form. Any teaching that makes the Son less than fully God is a form of Arianism.

Modalism (also called Sabellianism or Patripassianism) denies the distinction of the three Persons. It teaches that God is one Person who appears in three different modes or roles — as Father in the Old Testament, as Son in the incarnation, and as Spirit after Pentecost. This heresy is common in popular-level Christianity. The analogy of water existing in three states — ice, liquid, and steam — is actually a modalist analogy, because it suggests that God switches between modes rather than existing eternally as three distinct Persons. A better analogy, though still imperfect, is the relationship of love within a family — for love requires distinct persons in relationship with one another.

Tritheism teaches that the Father, Son, and Spirit are three separate Gods who happen to cooperate. This heresy is avoided by insisting, as the Confession does, that there is one divine substance or essence shared equally by all three Persons. The three Persons are not three separate beings. They are one Being in three Persons.

Subordinationism teaches that the Son and Spirit are God but are somehow lesser or inferior to the Father. While there is an order within the Trinity — the Father sends the Son, the Father and Son send the Spirit — this order is one of relationship and function, not one of nature or dignity. All three Persons are equally God — equal in power, equal in glory, equal in eternity.

The Westminster Confession guards against all of these errors by its precise and balanced language. Each word was carefully chosen, each phrase deliberately crafted, to exclude heresy while affirming the full, biblical truth about the triune God.

Part Twelve: The Incomprehensibility of God

The Confession also teaches the incomprehensibility of God — not that we cannot know Him at all, but that we cannot know Him exhaustively. Chapter 2, Section 1 describes God as "a most pure spirit, invisible, without body, parts, or passions, immutable, immense, eternal, incomprehensible, almighty, most wise, most holy, most free, most absolute."

The word "incomprehensible" here does not mean "unknowable." It means that God is so infinitely great that no finite mind can ever fully grasp the totality of His being. We can know God truly — through His Word, by His Spirit — but we can never know Him exhaustively. There will always be more of God to discover, more depths to explore, more glories to behold. This is why the study of God never becomes tedious. It is an infinite subject for finite minds, and the exploration will continue for all eternity.

As Augustine of Hippo wrote in his reflections on the Trinity: "If you have been able to comprehend it, it is not God you have comprehended." And Jonathan Edwards, the great American theologian and pastor of Northampton, Massachusetts, who led the First Great Awakening in the 1730s and 1740s, declared that the saints in heaven will spend all of eternity growing in their knowledge and enjoyment of God — and will never exhaust the infinite riches of His being. This prospect is not discouraging but exhilarating. The inexhaustibility of God means that the worship and wonder of heaven will never grow stale. There will always be new depths to explore, new glories to discover, new reasons to praise the God who is infinite, eternal, and unchangeable in all His being, wisdom, power, holiness, justice, goodness, and truth.

Part Thirteen: The Doctrine of God and the Life of Faith

Let us draw out several more practical applications of the doctrine of God for the daily life of faith.

First, the doctrine of God's infinity should produce humility in our studies. We are finite creatures attempting to comprehend an infinite God. Our theology, however careful and biblical, is never exhaustive. We see "through a glass, darkly" (1 Corinthians 13:12, KJV). This should make us humble before God and charitable toward fellow believers who may understand certain aspects of His truth differently than we do — while never compromising on the clear teachings of Scripture.

Second, the doctrine of God's eternity should produce patience in our trials. The God who exists from everlasting to everlasting is never in a hurry. His timing is perfect because His knowledge is perfect. When we are tempted to think that God has forgotten us or that His promises have failed, we must remember that the eternal God operates on a timescale vastly different from our own. "But they that wait upon the LORD shall renew their strength; they shall mount up with wings as eagles; they shall run, and not be weary; and they shall walk, and not faint" (Isaiah 40:31, KJV).

Third, the doctrine of God's unchangeableness should produce confidence in our salvation. The God who loved us from eternity, who chose us in Christ before the foundation of the world, who called us by His Spirit and justified us by His grace — this God does not change. His love does not cool. His purpose does not shift. His commitment to His people does not waver. "The LORD thy God in the midst of thee is mighty; he will save, he will rejoice over thee with joy; he will rest in his love, he will joy over thee with singing" (Zephaniah 3:17, KJV).

Fourth, the doctrine of God's holiness should produce reverence in our worship. The casual, flippant approach to God that characterizes much of modern Christianity is utterly out of step with the biblical portrait of God's holiness. The God before whom the seraphim veil their faces and cry "Holy, holy, holy" is not a God to be approached carelessly. He is to be worshiped "with reverence and godly fear: for our God is a consuming fire" (Hebrews 12:28-29, KJV). This does not mean that our worship should be cold or distant — for the same God who is infinitely holy is also infinitely gracious, and He invites us to come boldly to His throne of grace. But our boldness should be the boldness of children who know their Father's love, not the presumption of sinners who have forgotten their Father's holiness.

Fifth, the doctrine of the Trinity should produce richness in our devotional life. Because God is triune, our relationship with Him is multifaceted. We enjoy the love of the Father, the grace of the Son, and the fellowship of the Spirit. Our prayers can be directed to the Father, offered through the Son, and empowered by the Spirit. Our worship can celebrate each Person of the Trinity in His distinct glory while adoring the one God who is all three.

When we see who God truly is — infinite, eternal, unchangeable in all His perfections — we cannot help but fall on our faces in adoration. The prophet Isaiah saw the Lord "high and lifted up" in the temple, and his immediate response was worship mingled with awe: "Woe is me! for I am undone; because I am a man of unclean lips" (Isaiah 6:5, KJV). And when we see that this same God — infinite, eternal, holy, incomprehensible — has drawn near to us in Christ, becoming man for our salvation, our worship is joined with an overwhelming, unspeakable gratitude.

The study of God's attributes is the highest calling of the human mind and the deepest joy of the human heart. We have contemplated today a God who is Spirit — transcending all physical limitation; infinite — surpassing all boundaries of power, knowledge, and presence; eternal — existing from everlasting to everlasting without beginning or end; and unchangeable — the same yesterday, today, and forever.
