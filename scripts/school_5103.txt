Westminster Standards — Lesson 3

Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we ascend to the highest subject any mind can contemplate — the doctrine of God Himself. We will explore what the Westminster Standards teach about who God is and what He is like. Let us pray and begin.

--- Lesson ---

After establishing the authority of Scripture in Chapter 1, the Westminster Confession turns in Chapters 2 and 3 to the doctrine of God — His nature, His attributes, and His eternal decrees. The Shorter Catechism addresses these truths with memorable precision. Question 4 asks, "What is God?" and answers: "God is a Spirit, infinite, eternal, and unchangeable, in his being, wisdom, power, holiness, justice, goodness, and truth."

This single sentence is one of the most profound and comprehensive statements about God ever composed. Let us examine it carefully.

"God is a Spirit." God does not have a physical body. He is not limited to one place or visible to the naked eye. As Jesus told the woman at the well, "God is a Spirit: and they that worship him must worship him in spirit and in truth" (John 4:24). This does not mean God is vague or impersonal. It means He transcends the limitations of physical existence. He is everywhere present, perfectly aware, and actively engaged with His creation.

"Infinite." God has no limits. His power is unlimited — He can do all that He wills. His knowledge is unlimited — He knows all things, past, present, and future. His presence is unlimited — there is no place in the universe where God is not. The concept of infinity is one that the human mind cannot fully grasp, and that is precisely the point. God exceeds our capacity to understand Him. This should produce in us not frustration but wonder.

"Eternal." God has no beginning and no end. He did not come into existence at some point in the past, and He will never cease to exist. He is, as Moses declared, "from everlasting to everlasting" (Psalm 90:2). Time itself is a creation of God — He is not bound by it but stands above it and governs it.

"Unchangeable." God does not change. His character is the same yesterday, today, and forever. He does not grow wiser, because He is already infinitely wise. He does not grow stronger, because He is already infinitely powerful. He does not change His mind in the way that humans do, because His decisions are based on perfect knowledge and perfect wisdom from eternity. The theological term for this is "immutability," and it is one of the most comforting truths in all of theology. In a world where everything changes, God remains the same.

These four qualities — infinite, eternal, unchangeable, and spiritual in nature — apply to each of God's attributes. God is infinite, eternal, and unchangeable in His being — in His very existence. He is infinite, eternal, and unchangeable in His wisdom — He always knows the best end to pursue and the best means to achieve it. He is infinite, eternal, and unchangeable in His power — nothing is too hard for Him, and nothing can resist His will.

He is infinite, eternal, and unchangeable in His holiness — He is perfectly pure, perfectly righteous, completely set apart from all evil. Holiness is not merely one attribute among many; it is the atmosphere in which all God's other attributes operate. His love is holy love. His justice is holy justice. His power is holy power.

He is infinite, eternal, and unchangeable in His justice — He always does what is right. He rewards righteousness and punishes evil. He is never unfair, never arbitrary, never biased. His justice is perfect.

He is infinite, eternal, and unchangeable in His goodness — He is the source of all that is good, beautiful, and true. Every blessing, every kindness, every gift of grace flows from the inexhaustible fountain of divine goodness.

He is infinite, eternal, and unchangeable in His truth — He cannot lie. His Word is absolutely reliable. His promises are certain. His character is transparent. There is no shadow of duplicity in God.

The Westminster Confession also addresses the doctrine of the Trinity in Chapter 2, Section 3: "In the unity of the Godhead there be three Persons of one substance, power, and eternity: God the Father, God the Son, and God the Holy Ghost." The doctrine of the Trinity teaches that there is one God who eternally exists as three distinct Persons — Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — each fully and equally God, yet there are not three Gods but one. This is the central mystery of the Christian faith, revealed in Scripture and affirmed by the church throughout its history.

Understanding the doctrine of God is not merely an intellectual exercise. It is the foundation of worship. When we see who God truly is — infinite, eternal, unchangeable in all His perfections — we cannot help but fall on our faces in adoration. And when we see that this same God has drawn near to us in Christ, our worship is joined with an overwhelming gratitude.

--- Review Questions ---

Review Question 1: Recite the Shorter Catechism's answer to "What is God?" and explain each of the four qualities: Spirit, infinite, eternal, and unchangeable.

Review Question 2: What are the seven attributes listed in the Shorter Catechism's answer, and what does each one mean?

Review Question 3: What does the Westminster Confession teach about the Trinity? How can there be three Persons and yet only one God?

--- Activity ---

Create a "Attributes of God" reference card. On one side, list each of the seven attributes from the Shorter Catechism answer. On the other side, for each attribute, write a definition in your own words and find one Bible verse that illustrates that attribute. Memorize the Shorter Catechism's answer to Question 4. Bonus challenge: find one hymn or psalm that praises God for each attribute.

--- Closing ---

The study of God's attributes is the highest calling of the human mind. As you continue to reflect on what you have learned today, let knowledge lead to worship and worship lead to wonder. Until next time, students.
