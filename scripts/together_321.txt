PURITAN GOLD -- Together: Couples Devotional, Day 21
"Building a Peaceful Home"
Proverbs 21:9, 19

---

Welcome, husband and wife. There is something powerful about two hearts turning toward God at the same time. When you both face the same direction, toward Christ, everything in your marriage finds its proper alignment. Let us do that now.

Every marriage needs a foundation that does not shift. Let us hear from that foundation now, from Proverbs 21:9, 19:

"It is better to dwell in a corner of the housetop, than with a brawling woman in a wide house. It is better to dwell in the wilderness, than with a contentious and an angry woman."

That is Proverbs 21:9, 19. Take a breath. Let those words settle between the two of you. God is speaking to your marriage through this passage.

--- Puritan Commentary ---

The Puritans treasured marriage as a covenant made before God. Matthew Henry wrote beautifully about how Scripture speaks to the hearts of married couples.

Solomon returns to this theme more than once in Proverbs, which itself indicates how destructive contentiousness is to the home. Matthew Henry writes: 'It is better to have but a corner of the housetop, which is exposed to wind and weather, than to have a whole house in common with a woman that is continually scolding and quarrelling.' The wide house, with all its comfort and space, becomes a prison when it is filled with strife.

Henry is pastorally careful to note that while Solomon speaks specifically of a contentious woman, the principle extends to any spouse who creates an atmosphere of perpetual conflict. A contentious husband makes the home equally miserable. The point is not to assign blame to one gender but to expose the devastating impact of an uncontrolled, combative spirit in the most intimate of human relationships.

Charles Bridges adds that contentiousness is a symptom of deeper spiritual problems: pride, selfishness, a lack of self-control, and a failure to trust God. The contentious spouse is one who must always be right, who cannot let an offense pass, who turns every disagreement into a battle. Bridges writes: 'The root of contention is the idol of self. Where self is dethroned and Christ is enthroned, peace fills the house.'

Henry concludes with a positive vision: if the absence of peace makes even a palace miserable, then the presence of peace can make even a humble dwelling a paradise. 'It is not the size of the house but the spirit of the home that matters,' Henry observes. A couple who pursues peace actively and consistently will find their home, however modest, to be a foretaste of heaven.

--- Marriage Application ---

This is a passage for honest self-examination, not finger-pointing. Before you think about how contentious your spouse may be, ask yourself: am I a peacemaker or a peace-breaker in this home?

Contentiousness often disguises itself as righteous concern. 'I am just being honest.' 'Someone needs to say it.' 'I would not have to nag if you would just listen.' But the fruit of contentiousness is always the same: an atmosphere of tension, walking on eggshells, a home where no one can relax.

Pursue peace actively. This means choosing your battles wisely. It means learning to let small offenses go. It means addressing real issues in a spirit of gentleness rather than accusation. It means creating a home where everyone — spouse, children, guests — feels safe, welcomed, and at rest. The peaceful home is one of the greatest gifts you can give your family.

The Puritans understood that marriage is not a destination but a journey. Every day presents new opportunities to love, to serve, to forgive, and to grow together. The truths we have studied tonight are not just for tonight. They are for tomorrow morning when the alarm goes off, for the difficult conversation that has been waiting, for the quiet moments when only God sees how you treat each other.

--- Reflection ---

Now turn to each other. Here are tonight's reflection questions. Answer from the heart, not from the head.

Question 1: Am I contributing to peace or contention in our home? What would my spouse honestly say?

Question 2: Are there patterns of nagging, complaining, or picking fights that I need to repent of?

Question 3: What would our home feel like if we both committed to active peacemaking this week?

Take a few minutes with each question. Do not rush. The best conversations in marriage happen when both people feel truly heard.

--- Prayer ---

The Puritans ended every evening with prayer. Let us follow their example and bring our marriage before the Lord.

Tonight's prayer focus: Ask God to reveal any contentiousness in your own heart and to replace it with a spirit of peace. Pray together for a home marked by tranquility, patience, and mutual respect. Ask for the humility to be peacemakers rather than peace-breakers.

If one of you finds it difficult to pray out loud, that is perfectly fine. Simply holding hands in silence before the Lord is itself a powerful act of worship. Let the Spirit intercede where words fail.

--- Blessing ---

May the God of peace Himself fill your home with His presence, casting out every spirit of contention and replacing it with the rest that only He can give. Amen.

May the words you have heard tonight bear fruit in your marriage for years to come. The Lord be with you both. Until next time.
