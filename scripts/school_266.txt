PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 6
"Of the Fall and Sin"
The Westminster Standards — Lesson 6

---

Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we come to one of the most solemn and vital subjects in all of Christian theology — the fall of man and the nature of sin. Without understanding the fall, we cannot understand why we need a Saviour. Without grasping the depth and extent of sin, we will never appreciate the height and breadth of grace. The Westminster Standards treat this doctrine with the gravity it deserves, and we shall do the same. Let us pray and begin.

Almighty and holy God, we come before Thee with reverent hearts. The subject we study today is painful, for it is the story of our rebellion against Thee. Yet we know that understanding our disease is the first step toward embracing the cure. Open our eyes to see the seriousness of sin, that we might flee to Christ with all the more urgency. Grant us humility, clarity, and hope as we study. In the name of Jesus Christ our Lord, Amen.

--- Lesson ---

Part One: The State of Innocence — What Was Lost

Before we can understand the fall, we must understand what man was before the fall. The Westminster Confession of Faith, Chapter 4, Section 2, teaches us that God created man "after all other creatures." He "created man, male and female, with reasonable and immortal souls, endued with knowledge, righteousness, and holiness, after his own image, having the law of God written in their hearts, and power to fulfil it; and yet under a possibility of transgressing, being left to the liberty of their own will, which was subject unto change."

This is a description of extraordinary dignity. Adam and Eve were not primitive creatures groping their way toward civilization. They were the crowning glory of God's creation — made in His very image, possessing true knowledge, genuine righteousness, and real holiness. The Shorter Catechism, Question 10, summarizes this beautifully: "God created man male and female, after his own image, in knowledge, righteousness, and holiness, with dominion over the creatures."

Let us consider what these gifts meant. True knowledge meant that Adam understood God, himself, and the world around him in a way that was accurate, clear, and uncorrupted by error. He did not need to grope for truth — truth was written on his heart. Righteousness meant that Adam's will was inclined toward what was good, just, and holy. He naturally desired to obey God and to do what was right. Holiness meant that Adam was set apart for God, pure in heart, free from every stain of moral corruption. Together, these constituted the image of God in man — not a physical likeness, but a moral and spiritual resemblance to the Creator Himself.

The great Puritan theologian Thomas Watson, in his Body of Divinity, published in 1692, wrote with great tenderness about this original state: "Man was the most exquisite piece in all the creation. He was the magnum miraculum, a great miracle." Watson observed that man was a kind of lesser world within the greater world — possessing a body formed from the dust of the earth and a soul breathed into him by the very breath of God. "His understanding was clear, his will was compliant with the divine will, his affections were orderly and regular."

The Westminster Larger Catechism, Question 17, adds further detail about man's original state. It teaches that after God had made all other creatures, He created man "male and female, formed the body of the man of the dust of the ground, and the woman of the rib of the man, endued them with living, reasonable, and immortal souls; made them after his own image, in knowledge, righteousness, and holiness; having the law of God written in their hearts, and power to fulfil it; and dominion over the creatures; yet subject to fall."

Notice that last phrase: "yet subject to fall." Adam's righteousness was real but mutable. He could change. He was not yet confirmed in holiness as the glorified saints in heaven are. He stood in a state of probation — genuinely upright, genuinely able to obey, but also genuinely able to disobey. This is a crucial point. God did not create a robot programmed to obey. He created a moral agent with genuine freedom and genuine responsibility.

The Genesis account provides the historical setting. In the garden of Eden — that paradise planted by God Himself in a region associated with the Tigris and Euphrates rivers in the ancient Near East — Adam and Eve lived in perfect communion with their Creator. Genesis 2:8-9 records: "And the LORD God planted a garden eastward in Eden; and there he put the man whom he had formed. And out of the ground made the LORD God to grow every tree that is pleasant to the sight, and good for food; the tree of life also in the midst of the garden, and the tree of knowledge of good and evil."

God gave Adam a specific command, recorded in Genesis 2:16-17: "And the LORD God commanded the man, saying, Of every tree of the garden thou mayest freely eat: But of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, thou shalt not eat of it: for in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die." This was the covenant of works in its simplest form — perfect obedience was required, and the penalty for disobedience was death. We shall explore the covenant of works more fully in our next lesson, but for now, note that the test was not arbitrary. It was a test of trust, submission, and love. Would Adam trust God's word? Would he submit to God's authority? Would he love God above all else — even above the fruit of a forbidden tree?

Part Two: The Fall — What Happened

The Westminster Confession, Chapter 6, Section 1, describes the fall with careful precision: "Our first parents, being seduced by the subtilty and temptation of Satan, sinned in eating the forbidden fruit. This their sin God was pleased, according to his wise and holy counsel, to permit, having purposed to order it to his own glory."

Let us trace the events as recorded in Genesis, chapter 3. The serpent — identified elsewhere in Scripture as Satan, the devil, that ancient adversary of God and man — approached Eve with a question designed to cast doubt upon God's word: "Yea, hath God said, Ye shall not eat of every tree of the garden?" (Genesis 3:1). Notice the subtlety. Satan did not begin with an outright denial of God's command. He began with a question — a seemingly innocent inquiry that was, in reality, a poisonous seed of doubt. "Did God really say that? Are you sure you understood Him correctly? Surely He did not mean to deprive you of something so delightful."

Eve responded by reciting God's command, but she made a significant addition. She said, "God hath said, Ye shall not eat of it, neither shall ye touch it, lest ye die" (Genesis 3:3). God had said nothing about touching the fruit. Eve was already beginning to distort God's word — making it seem more restrictive than it actually was.

Then Satan moved from doubt to denial: "Ye shall not surely die: For God doth know that in the day ye eat thereof, then your eyes shall be opened, and ye shall be as gods, knowing good and evil" (Genesis 3:4-5). Here was the great lie. Satan denied the penalty God had announced. He impugned God's character, suggesting that God was withholding something good from Adam and Eve out of jealousy or fear. And he dangled before them the most intoxicating temptation of all — the promise of being "as gods."

Genesis 3:6 records the tragic moment: "And when the woman saw that the tree was good for food, and that it was pleasant to the eyes, and a tree to be desired to make one wise, she took of the fruit thereof, and did eat, and gave also unto her husband with her; and he did eat." Notice the three-fold temptation: the lust of the flesh (good for food), the lust of the eyes (pleasant to the eyes), and the pride of life (desired to make one wise). The Apostle John would later use these same three categories to describe the world's temptations in 1 John 2:16: "For all that is in the world, the lust of the flesh, and the lust of the eyes, and the pride of life, is not of the Father, but is of the world."

The Puritan pastor and theologian Thomas Goodwin, who served as one of the Westminster Assembly's leading figures from 1643 onward, reflected deeply on the nature of this temptation. Goodwin observed that Satan attacked at the point of God's truthfulness. If God cannot be trusted to tell the truth about the consequences of sin, then He cannot be trusted at all. The fall, at its deepest level, was a failure of faith — a refusal to trust God's word.

Edward Reynolds, the Bishop of Norwich and another member of the Westminster Assembly, wrote in his remarkable treatise on the sinfulness of sin that the fall was an act of cosmic treason. Adam was not merely a private individual making a personal choice. He was the representative head of the entire human race, the federal head of all who would descend from him by ordinary generation. His act of disobedience was therefore an act that implicated every human being who would ever live.

Part Three: The Consequences of the Fall — Original Sin

The Westminster Confession, Chapter 6, Section 2, describes the immediate consequences of the fall: "By this sin they fell from their original righteousness, and communion with God, and so became dead in sin, and wholly defiled in all the faculties and parts of soul and body."

This is what theologians call "spiritual death." Adam and Eve did not immediately die physically on the day they ate the fruit — though physical death was now certain to come. But they died spiritually that very instant. Their communion with God was shattered. Their righteousness was lost. Their entire nature — mind, will, emotions, body — was corrupted by sin. Where once they had been inclined toward God, they now hid from Him. Where once they had been open and transparent, they now covered themselves with fig leaves and made excuses for their behavior.

But the consequences did not end with Adam and Eve. The Confession continues in Section 3: "They being the root of all mankind, the guilt of this sin was imputed, and the same death in sin and corrupted nature conveyed to all their posterity, descending from them by ordinary generation." This is the doctrine of original sin — one of the most important and most contested doctrines in the history of Christianity.

Original sin has two aspects. First, the guilt of Adam's first sin is imputed to all his descendants. We are not merely unfortunate inheritors of a bad nature. We are legally guilty in Adam. Just as a nation is bound by the actions of its duly appointed representative, so the human race is bound by the actions of its federal head. Romans 5:12 states this plainly: "Wherefore, as by one man sin entered into the world, and death by sin; and so death passed upon all men, for that all have sinned." And Romans 5:19 adds: "For as by one man's disobedience many were made sinners, so by the obedience of one shall many be made righteous."

Second, a corrupted nature is conveyed to all of Adam's descendants. We are not born morally neutral. We are born with a nature that is inclined toward sin and away from God. The Confession, Chapter 6, Section 4, describes this inherited corruption: "From this original corruption, whereby we are utterly indisposed, disabled, and made opposite to all good, and wholly inclined to all evil, do proceed all actual transgressions." Every sin we commit — every lie, every act of selfishness, every angry word, every impure thought — flows from this corrupted fountain within us.

The Shorter Catechism, Question 18, asks: "Wherein consists the sinfulness of that estate whereinto man fell?" and answers: "The sinfulness of that estate whereinto man fell consists in the guilt of Adam's first sin, the want of original righteousness, and the corruption of his whole nature, which is commonly called original sin; together with all actual transgressions which proceed from it."

The Puritan divine William Ames, whose Marrow of Theology published in 1627 profoundly influenced the Westminster divines, described original sin as a "habitual deviation of the whole nature of man from the law of God." It is not merely a weakness or a tendency. It is a fundamental disorder of the entire human person. Ames taught that this corruption affects every faculty: the understanding is darkened, the will is enslaved, the affections are disordered, and the conscience is defiled.

The great Geneva reformer John Calvin, writing nearly a century before the Westminster Assembly in his Institutes of the Christian Religion, first published in 1536 and expanded through multiple editions until 1559, described original sin as "a hereditary depravity and corruption of our nature, diffused into all parts of the soul, which first makes us liable to God's wrath, then also brings forth in us those works which Scripture calls works of the flesh." Calvin insisted that this depravity is not something added to human nature from the outside but is a corruption of the nature itself — like a disease that infects the very roots of a tree, causing every branch and every piece of fruit to be tainted.

Part Four: Total Depravity — The Extent of the Corruption

The Westminster Standards teach what Reformed theologians have traditionally called "total depravity." This does not mean that every human being is as wicked as possible, or that unregenerate people are incapable of any outward good. It means that every part of the human person is affected by sin — that there is no island of moral purity within us that remains untouched by the corruption of the fall.

The Confession, Chapter 6, Section 4, states that by original corruption we are "utterly indisposed, disabled, and made opposite to all good, and wholly inclined to all evil." This is strong language, and it is carefully chosen. We are not merely weakened in our ability to do good — we are "utterly indisposed" and "disabled." We are not merely tempted toward evil — we are "wholly inclined" toward it. Apart from the grace of God, we cannot do anything that is truly good in God's sight.

The Larger Catechism, Question 25, elaborates: "The sinfulness of that estate whereinto man fell, consisteth in the guilt of Adam's first sin, the want of that righteousness wherein he was created, and the corruption of his nature, whereby he is utterly indisposed, disabled, and made opposite unto all that is spiritually good, and wholly inclined to all evil, and that continually; which is commonly called original sin, and from which do proceed all actual transgressions."

Notice the word "continually." The corruption of our nature is not intermittent. It is constant. It affects us in every moment, in every thought, in every action. Even our best deeds are tainted by mixed motives, pride, and self-seeking. As the prophet Isaiah declared: "But we are all as an unclean thing, and all our righteousnesses are as filthy rags; and we all do fade as a leaf; and our iniquities, like the wind, have taken us away" (Isaiah 64:6).

The Puritan pastor Jeremiah Burroughs, who served as a preacher to two of the most influential congregations in London during the 1640s and was himself a member of the Westminster Assembly, wrote extensively about the evil of sin. In his work The Evil of Evils, Burroughs argued that sin is worse than the greatest affliction, worse than death itself, worse than hell — because sin is the cause of all affliction, all death, and all hell. "Sin," wrote Burroughs, "is the only thing that God is against. God is not against affliction; God is not against poverty; God is not against sickness — but God is against sin."

Stephen Charnock, the great Puritan divine who served as a chaplain to Henry Cromwell in Ireland during the 1650s and later ministered in London until his death in 1680, wrote in his monumental work The Existence and Attributes of God about the way sin distorts our knowledge of God Himself. Charnock argued that the fall so damaged the human intellect that we naturally suppress the knowledge of God and construct idols in His place. We do not merely fail to seek God — we actively flee from Him, replacing the true God with gods of our own making.

The Apostle Paul provides the most comprehensive biblical description of total depravity in Romans chapter 3. Let us hear these words from the King James Version: "As it is written, There is none righteous, no, not one: There is none that understandeth, there is none that seeketh after God. They are all gone out of the way, they are together become unprofitable; there is none that doeth good, no, not one. Their throat is an open sepulchre; with their tongues they have used deceit; the poison of asps is under their lips: Whose mouth is full of cursing and bitterness: Their feet are swift to shed blood: Destruction and misery are in their ways: And the way of peace have they not known: There is no fear of God before their eyes" (Romans 3:10-18).

This passage leaves no room for human boasting. No one is righteous. No one understands. No one seeks God. Every part of the human person — throat, tongue, lips, mouth, feet, eyes — is implicated in the indictment. Paul is quoting from several Old Testament passages, weaving them together into a devastating portrait of human sinfulness that applies to every person who has ever lived, apart from the Lord Jesus Christ.

Part Five: The Nature of Sin — What Sin Actually Is

The Westminster Standards provide a precise definition of sin. The Shorter Catechism, Question 14, asks: "What is sin?" and answers: "Sin is any want of conformity unto, or transgression of, the law of God." This brief definition contains two critical ideas.

First, sin is "any want of conformity unto the law of God." This means that sin is not merely doing what God forbids. It is also failing to do what God commands. Sins of omission — the failure to love, the failure to pray, the failure to show mercy, the failure to worship — are just as real and just as serious as sins of commission. The Apostle James makes this point: "Therefore to him that knoweth to do good, and doeth it not, to him it is sin" (James 4:17).

Second, sin is "transgression of the law of God." This is the more familiar idea — sin is crossing the line that God has drawn, violating His commandments, breaking His law. As the Apostle John writes: "Whosoever committeth sin transgresseth also the law: for sin is the transgression of the law" (1 John 3:4).

The Larger Catechism, Question 24, expands on this definition: "Sin is any want of conformity unto, or transgression of, any law of God, given as a rule to the reasonable creature." This addition is important. The law of God is not an arbitrary set of rules imposed by a capricious deity. It is a rule given to "the reasonable creature" — that is, to beings capable of understanding, choosing, and acting morally. The law reflects the character of God Himself and is therefore the standard of all genuine goodness.

Thomas Watson, in his exposition of the Shorter Catechism, offered a memorable description of the nature of sin. He identified several features that make sin so dreadful. Sin is a violation of God's righteous law. Sin is a form of spiritual rebellion — high treason against the King of heaven. Sin is an act of ingratitude — turning the very gifts God has given us into weapons against Him. Sin is a form of spiritual insanity — choosing what harms us and rejecting what blesses us. Sin is, in Watson's vivid phrase, "the dare of God's justice, the rape of his mercy, the jeer of his patience, the slight of his power, and the contempt of his love."

The Westminster Standards also distinguish between different degrees and kinds of sin. The Larger Catechism, Question 150, teaches that "all transgressions of the law of God are not equally heinous; but some sins in themselves, and by reason of several aggravations, are more heinous in the sight of God than others." A sin committed against greater light, against clearer knowledge, against stronger warnings, against more solemn vows, or against more abundant mercies is more heinous than a sin committed in ignorance. A sin committed by a leader, a parent, or a teacher carries greater weight than the same sin committed by someone with less influence. Not all sins are equal in their gravity, though all sins are equal in that they all deserve God's wrath and curse.

Part Six: The Penalty for Sin — Death and the Curse

The Shorter Catechism, Question 19, asks: "What is the misery of that estate whereinto man fell?" and answers: "All mankind, by their fall, lost communion with God, are under his wrath and curse, and so made liable to all miseries in this life, to death itself, and to the pains of hell for ever."

This is a comprehensive statement of the penalty for sin. Let us examine its components.

First, we have "lost communion with God." This is the fundamental tragedy of sin. We were made for fellowship with God — to know Him, to love Him, to walk with Him. Sin severed that relationship. We became alienated from our Creator, strangers to the One who formed us and breathed life into us. As Isaiah 59:2 declares: "But your iniquities have separated between you and your God, and your sins have hid his face from you, that he will not hear."

Second, we are "under his wrath and curse." God's wrath is not a petty, vindictive anger. It is the settled, righteous indignation of a holy God against all that violates His character. The curse is the judicial sentence pronounced upon sin — the comprehensive ruin that sin brings into every dimension of human life. God's curse upon sin is declared in Genesis 3:14-19, where God pronounces judgment upon the serpent, upon the woman, and upon the man. Pain in childbirth, thorns and thistles in the ground, sweat and toil in labor, and the return of the body to dust — all of these are manifestations of the curse that sin has brought upon the human race and upon the created order itself.

Third, we are "made liable to all miseries in this life." Every sickness, every sorrow, every hardship, every disappointment, every broken relationship, every natural disaster, every war, every famine — all of these are consequences of sin's entrance into the world. This does not mean that every specific suffering is a punishment for a specific sin. But it does mean that the entire framework of human misery exists because of the fall. Romans 8:20-22 describes the groaning of creation under the weight of the curse: "For the creature was made subject to vanity, not willingly, but by reason of him who hath subjected the same in hope, Because the creature itself also shall be delivered from the bondage of corruption into the glorious liberty of the children of God. For we know that the whole creation groaneth and travaileth in pain together until now."

Fourth, we are liable "to death itself." Physical death — the separation of the soul from the body — was not part of God's original design for humanity. It entered the world through sin. "For the wages of sin is death" (Romans 6:23). Death is not natural. It is not a friend. It is an enemy — the last enemy that shall be destroyed (1 Corinthians 15:26). Every funeral, every grave, every obituary is a reminder that sin has consequences.

Fifth, we are liable "to the pains of hell for ever." This is the most fearful consequence of sin. Hell is not a medieval invention or a tool of religious manipulation. It is a reality taught by the Lord Jesus Christ Himself with more clarity and frequency than any other figure in Scripture. Jesus spoke of "everlasting fire, prepared for the devil and his angels" (Matthew 25:41). He warned of "outer darkness" where "there shall be weeping and gnashing of teeth" (Matthew 25:30). He described the fate of the wicked in terms of unending judgment: "And these shall go away into everlasting punishment: but the righteous into life eternal" (Matthew 25:46).

The Puritan Richard Baxter, whose pastoral ministry in Kidderminster from 1647 to 1661 transformed an entire town, wrote in his Saints' Everlasting Rest that the contemplation of hell should drive sinners to flee to Christ. Baxter urged his readers to consider the eternity of hell's punishment — that it never ends, that there is no parole, no second chance, no eventual release. "O sinner," Baxter wrote with pastoral urgency, "stop and think what it is that thou art running into."

Part Seven: The Imputation of Adam's Sin — A Deeper Look

The doctrine of the imputation of Adam's sin requires careful theological reflection. The Westminster Larger Catechism, Question 22, asks: "Did all mankind fall in that first transgression?" and answers: "The covenant being made with Adam as a public person, not for himself only, but for his posterity, all mankind descending from him by ordinary generation, sinned in him, and fell with him in that first transgression."

Adam was a "public person" — that is, a representative. He acted not just for himself but for all who would descend from him. This concept is known as "federal headship." The word "federal" comes from the Latin foedus, meaning "covenant." Adam was the covenant head of the human race. His obedience would have secured blessing for all; his disobedience secured condemnation for all.

This principle of representation is not foreign to human experience. When a president signs a treaty, the entire nation is bound by it. When a general surrenders, the entire army lays down its arms. Adam stood in a representative capacity before God, and when he fell, the entire human race fell with him.

The Apostle Paul develops this parallel extensively in Romans 5:12-21, drawing a comparison between Adam and Christ. Just as Adam's one act of disobedience brought condemnation to all, so Christ's one act of obedience brings justification to all who believe: "For as by one man's disobedience many were made sinners, so by the obedience of one shall many be made righteous" (Romans 5:19). And again: "Therefore as by the offence of one judgment came upon all men to condemnation; even so by the righteousness of one the free gift came upon all men unto justification of life" (Romans 5:18).

The great Dutch Reformed theologian Herman Witsius, whose Economy of the Covenants Between God and Man was published in 1677 and widely read among English Puritans and their successors, explained the imputation of Adam's sin with a helpful illustration. He compared Adam to the root of a great tree. When the root is poisoned, every branch, every leaf, every fruit is poisoned as well. The corruption does not begin in the branches and work its way down to the root. It begins in the root and flows outward to every part of the tree. So it is with Adam. His sin corrupted the root of human nature, and from that corrupted root, all of his descendants receive a corrupted nature.

Part Eight: The Bondage of the Will

One of the most important consequences of the fall is what the Confession calls the bondage of the will. Chapter 9, Section 3 states: "Man, by his fall into a state of sin, hath wholly lost all ability of will to any spiritual good accompanying salvation; so as a natural man, being altogether averse from that good, and dead in sin, is not able, by his own strength, to convert himself, or to prepare himself thereunto."

This is a remarkable and humbling statement. Fallen man has not merely weakened his will — he has "wholly lost all ability of will to any spiritual good." He cannot save himself. He cannot even prepare himself for salvation. He is "dead in sin" — not sick, not weak, not injured, but dead. A dead man cannot choose to live. He must be made alive by a power outside himself.

The great Augustine of Hippo, writing in the early fifth century in his controversies with the Pelagian heresy, insisted upon this truth with all his strength. Pelagius taught that human beings retain the ability to choose God apart from grace. Augustine, drawing upon passages such as Ephesians 2:1-5, argued that fallen man is spiritually dead and can be made alive only by the sovereign grace of God. "For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: Not of works, lest any man should boast" (Ephesians 2:8-9).

Martin Luther, in his famous work The Bondage of the Will, published in 1525 in response to the great humanist scholar Erasmus, defended this same truth with characteristic force. Luther argued that the will of fallen man is like a beast of burden — it goes where its rider directs it. If God rides it, it goes where God wills. If Satan rides it, it goes where Satan wills. But the beast itself cannot choose its rider. Apart from grace, the will is bound in sin and can move only in the direction of sin.

John Owen, perhaps the greatest of all the Puritan theologians, who served as the Vice-Chancellor of the University of Oxford during the 1650s and wrote prolifically until his death in 1683, devoted extensive attention to the bondage of the will in his works on sin and temptation. Owen taught that the understanding is darkened so that it cannot perceive spiritual truth, the will is enslaved so that it cannot choose spiritual good, and the affections are disordered so that they cling to created things rather than to the Creator. The entire soul is in bondage, and only the almighty power of God can set it free.

This is precisely what the gospel offers. Jesus Christ came to set captives free. He said in John 8:34-36: "Verily, verily, I say unto you, Whosoever committeth sin is the servant of sin. And the servant abideth not in the house for ever: but the Son abideth ever. If the Son therefore shall make you free, ye shall be free indeed."

Part Nine: Sin and Its Effects on the World

The fall did not merely corrupt individual human beings. It brought ruin upon the entire created order. Romans 8:20-22, which we quoted earlier, teaches that the creation itself was "made subject to vanity" because of sin. The ground was cursed for Adam's sake (Genesis 3:17). Thorns and thistles sprang up where once only beauty and fruitfulness had reigned. Animals that had lived in harmony with man and with one another became predators and prey. The entire natural world was thrown into disorder by the fall.

The Puritans were keenly aware of this cosmic dimension of sin. They saw in every earthquake, every plague, every drought, every storm a reminder that the world is not as it should be — that creation groans under the weight of the curse and longs for the day of restoration. Yet they also saw in the beauty that remains — in every sunset, every flower, every newborn child — evidence of God's common grace, His ongoing kindness to a world that has rebelled against Him.

Jonathan Edwards, the great American Puritan theologian who served as pastor in Northampton, Massachusetts, from 1729 to 1750 and later as the president of the College of New Jersey (now Princeton University), wrote in his masterwork on Original Sin, published posthumously in 1758, that the evidence for the fall is visible everywhere. The universal experience of death, the universal prevalence of sin, the universal tendency of human beings toward selfishness, cruelty, and idolatry — all of these point unmistakably to the reality of the fall. Edwards argued that no other explanation adequately accounts for the human condition. Philosophy, psychology, and sociology may describe the symptoms, but only the biblical doctrine of original sin explains the cause.

Part Ten: The Testimony of History — Sin's Universal Reach

The doctrine of the fall and original sin is not merely a theological abstraction. It is confirmed by the universal testimony of human history. Every civilization that has ever existed has been marked by war, injustice, cruelty, and moral failure. The ancient empires of Babylon, Persia, Greece, and Rome — for all their achievements in art, law, and governance — were built upon slavery, conquest, and the exploitation of the weak by the strong. The medieval period, for all its devotion to the church, was scarred by corruption, violence, and superstition. The modern era, for all its advances in science and technology, has produced horrors unprecedented in human history — the slave trade, two world wars, genocides, and weapons capable of destroying the earth itself.

The Puritans looked upon human history and saw in it a vast and unmistakable confirmation of the biblical doctrine of the fall. William Perkins, the great Cambridge Puritan who taught and preached from 1585 until his death in 1602 and who profoundly influenced the generation that would produce the Westminster Assembly, argued that the universality of sin is itself a powerful proof of original sin. If sin were merely a matter of bad choices made by individuals, we would expect at least some people, in some place, at some time, to have lived without sin. But the fact that sin is universal — that every culture, every age, every individual is marked by moral failure — points to a common root: the corruption of human nature inherited from Adam.

David, the king of Israel and a man after God's own heart, confessed this truth in Psalm 51:5: "Behold, I was shapen in iniquity; and in sin did my mother conceive me." David's sin with Bathsheba, recorded in 2 Samuel chapter 11, was not merely an isolated lapse in an otherwise perfect life. It was the eruption of a corrupted nature that had been present from the moment of his conception. And if David — the anointed king, the psalmist, the man whom God chose — could fall into adultery and murder, then no human being is safe from the power of indwelling sin.

Part Eleven: The Seriousness of Sin and the Need for Grace

Why does all of this matter? Why do the Westminster Standards devote such careful attention to the doctrine of sin? Because without understanding sin, we cannot understand grace. The deeper our knowledge of the disease, the more we will value the cure. The more clearly we see our bondage, the more gratefully we will embrace our Deliverer.

As Jesus Himself said, "They that be whole need not a physician, but they that are sick" (Matthew 9:12). And again, "The Son of man is come to seek and to save that which was lost" (Luke 19:10). If we are not lost, we do not need to be found. If we are not sick, we do not need a doctor. If we are not dead in sin, we do not need to be made alive. But the Westminster Standards, faithful to the teaching of Scripture, insist that we are indeed lost, sick, and dead — and therefore in desperate need of the grace of God in Jesus Christ.

The Puritan pastor and hymn writer John Newton, who lived from 1725 to 1807 and who is best remembered for the lines of his most famous hymn, understood this truth from personal experience. Newton had been a slave trader, a blasphemer, and a profligate sinner. But when grace found him, he was overwhelmed by the contrast between what he had been and what God had made him. His whole theology was shaped by this contrast — the depth of his sin and the height of God's grace.

The Shorter Catechism, Question 20, asks: "Did God leave all mankind to perish in the estate of sin and misery?" and answers: "God, having out of his mere good pleasure, from all eternity, elected some to everlasting life, did enter into a covenant of grace to deliver them out of the estate of sin and misery, and to bring them into an estate of salvation by a Redeemer." Here is the gospel in miniature. God did not abandon the human race to the consequences of its own rebellion. Out of His mere good pleasure — not because we deserved it, not because we earned it, not because we asked for it — God chose a people for Himself and entered into a covenant of grace to save them through a Redeemer.

This is where the doctrine of sin leads us — not to despair but to Christ. The fall is not the end of the story. It is the occasion for the most glorious display of God's love, mercy, and power that the universe has ever seen. And that display is found in the person and work of Jesus Christ, whom we shall study in the lessons to come.

Let us close this section with a passage that summarizes the entire movement from sin to grace. Ephesians 2:1-7 declares: "And you hath he quickened, who were dead in trespasses and sins; Wherein in time past ye walked according to the course of this world, according to the prince of the power of the air, the spirit that now worketh in the children of disobedience: Among whom also we all had our conversation in times past in the lusts of our flesh, fulfilling the desires of the flesh and of the mind; and were by nature the children of wrath, even as others. But God, who is rich in mercy, for his great love wherewith he loved us, Even when we were dead in sins, hath quickened us together with Christ, (by grace ye are saved;) And hath raised us up together, and made us sit together in heavenly places in Christ Jesus: That in the ages to come he might shew the exceeding riches of his grace in his kindness toward us through Christ Jesus."

Dead in sins — but made alive in Christ. Children of wrath — but objects of mercy. Enslaved to the flesh — but seated in heavenly places. This is the miracle of grace, and it shines all the brighter against the dark backdrop of the fall.

--- Closing ---

The fall of man is the darkest chapter in the story of creation — but it is not the final chapter. The God who permitted the fall also purposed to redeem. The same Bible that tells us we are dead in sin also tells us that God is rich in mercy. Know the depth of the disease, that you might prize the greatness of the cure. And let the doctrine of sin drive you not to despair but to the open arms of Christ, who came to seek and to save that which was lost. Until next time, students.
