PURITAN GOLD -- My Growth, Day 29
"The Discipline of Self-Examination"
Pastoral Day — Testing the Health of the Soul


Welcome. Today is a Pastoral day. Our subject is self-examination — the regular, honest assessment of the soul's condition that the Puritans practiced as faithfully as a doctor checks a patient's vital signs.

Let us read 2 Corinthians 13:5 (KJV):

"Examine yourselves, whether ye be in the faith; prove your own selves. Know ye not your own selves, how that Jesus Christ is in you, except ye be reprobates?"


The Puritans took self-examination seriously because they understood that the human heart is desperately wicked and deceitful above all things. Without regular, honest assessment, the believer can drift into spiritual decline without even noticing. Symptoms go undetected. Sin goes unconfessed. Growth stalls. And what passes for vibrant faith is actually spiritual numbness.

Richard Baxter recommended daily self-examination as a non-negotiable discipline. He suggested doing it in the evening, as the day's events are still fresh. He offered a series of questions that believers should ask themselves regularly.

First, the question of faith. Do I still believe? Not perfectly, but genuinely? Is my trust in Christ growing or diminishing? Am I more or less confident in the Gospel than I was a month ago? The purpose of this question is not to create anxiety but to identify drift. Many Christians slide into practical atheism — living as if God were absent — without any dramatic loss of faith. The decline is subtle, gradual, and invisible without examination.

Second, the question of love. Do I love God? Not merely acknowledge Him, not merely serve Him, but love Him — with genuine, heartfelt affection? Do I delight in His presence, treasure His Word, long for communion with Him? And do I love my neighbor — not merely tolerate them but genuinely seek their good? The Puritans measured spiritual health primarily by the thermometer of love. Where love grows cold, the soul is in danger.

Third, the question of obedience. Am I obeying God's commands — not perfectly, but directionally? Is there a pattern of obedience in my life, or a pattern of compromise? Is there a specific command that I am deliberately ignoring? A sin that I am knowingly committing? A duty that I am consciously neglecting? Thomas Watson wrote that the Christian who examines his obedience regularly will catch sin early, before it becomes a deeply rooted habit.

Fourth, the question of growth. Am I growing? This is not a question about perfection but about trajectory. Am I more patient than I was a year ago? More generous? More humble? More disciplined in prayer? More sensitive to the Holy Spirit? If not — if I am stagnant or declining — then something is wrong, and it must be identified and addressed.

Fifth, the question of fruit. What fruit is my life producing? Jesus said, "By their fruits ye shall know them." The fruit of the Spirit — love, joy, peace, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, faith, meekness, temperance — should be increasingly evident in the life of a growing believer. The Puritans also looked for the fruit of good works — service, generosity, witness, hospitality, mercy.

Sixth, the question of idolatry. Is anything taking God's place in my heart? The Puritans defined idolatry broadly — not merely the worship of statues but the elevation of any created thing above the Creator. Money, success, reputation, comfort, relationships, entertainment — any of these can become functional idols that displace God from the throne of the heart.

The Puritans warned against two errors in self-examination. The first is neglect — never examining yourself at all, assuming that all is well simply because no crisis has occurred. This is spiritual negligence. The second is morbid introspection — examining yourself so obsessively that you are paralyzed by guilt and unable to function. This is spiritual hypochondria. The healthy middle ground is regular, honest, faith-filled examination — checking the vital signs of the soul and bringing what you find to God in confession and trust.

Jonathan Edwards composed his famous Resolutions as a form of ongoing self-examination. He resolved to review them weekly, measuring his life against his commitments, and confessing wherever he fell short. This practice sustained one of the most extraordinary spiritual lives in Christian history.


Examine yourself tonight. Not with morbid anxiety, but with honest, hopeful, Spirit-led assessment. Lord, search me and know me. Show me where I am growing and where I am stagnant. Reveal the idols I have not noticed and the sins I have not confessed. And when the examination is done, cover me with Your grace and send me forward in Your strength. Amen.
