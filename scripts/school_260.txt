"Truth-Telling and Contentment"
Christian Ethics & Moral Philosophy — Lesson 20

---

Welcome back, students, to the final lesson in our series on the Ten Commandments within the Puritan Gold curriculum. Today we bring our extended study of God's moral law to its conclusion by examining the ninth and tenth commandments together — the commandment that protects truth and the commandment that governs the desires of the human heart. These two commandments form a fitting conclusion to the Decalogue, for they address the two most interior dimensions of the moral life: the words we speak and the desires we harbor. "Thou shalt not bear false witness against thy neighbour" (Exodus 20:16). "Thou shalt not covet thy neighbour's house, thou shalt not covet thy neighbour's wife, nor his manservant, nor his maidservant, nor his ox, nor his ass, nor any thing that is thy neighbour's" (Exodus 20:17). Together, these commandments reveal that God's moral law does not merely regulate outward behavior — it penetrates to the deepest chambers of the human soul.

--- Lesson ---

PART ONE: THE NINTH COMMANDMENT — THE ORIGINAL CONTEXT

The ninth commandment — "Thou shalt not bear false witness against thy neighbour" — was originally situated in the context of legal proceedings. In ancient Israel, the administration of justice depended almost entirely upon the testimony of witnesses. There were no forensic laboratories, no DNA testing, no surveillance cameras, no recording devices. When a dispute arose or a crime was alleged, the elders of the community would gather at the city gate, and witnesses would be called to testify. The fate of the accused — his life, his liberty, his property, his reputation — hung upon the truthfulness of those who testified.

The stakes could not have been higher. Under the Mosaic law, the testimony of two or three witnesses was required for a conviction: "At the mouth of two witnesses, or three witnesses, shall he that is worthy of death be put to death; but at the mouth of one witness he shall not be put to death" (Deuteronomy 17:6). The law also imposed a severe penalty upon false witnesses: "If a false witness rise up against any man to testify against him that which is wrong; Then shall both the men, between whom the controversy is, stand before the LORD, before the priests and the judges, which shall be in those days; And the judges shall make diligent inquisition: and, behold, if the witness be a false witness, and hath testified falsely against his brother; Then shall ye do unto him, as he had thought to have done unto his brother: so shalt thou put the evil away from among you" (Deuteronomy 19:16-19). The false witness was to receive the same penalty that would have been imposed upon the person he accused. If he had accused someone of a capital crime, the false witness himself could be put to death. This severe penalty reveals how seriously God regards the sin of false testimony and how essential truth-telling is to the fabric of a just society.

The case of Naboth's vineyard, recorded in 1 Kings 21, provides one of the most dramatic and disturbing examples of false witness in all of Scripture. King Ahab coveted the vineyard of Naboth the Jezreelite and offered to buy it, but Naboth refused because the vineyard was his ancestral inheritance, protected by the laws of God. Ahab's wife, the wicked Jezebel, then orchestrated a plot to destroy Naboth through false testimony. She arranged for two "sons of Belial" — worthless men — to testify falsely that Naboth had blasphemed God and the king. On the strength of their false testimony, Naboth was convicted and stoned to death, and Ahab seized his vineyard. The prophet Elijah then confronted Ahab with a devastating pronouncement of divine judgment: "Hast thou killed, and also taken possession? In the place where dogs licked the blood of Naboth shall dogs lick thy blood, even thine" (1 Kings 21:19).

The Naboth narrative illustrates the interconnection of the commandments that the Puritans frequently emphasized. The violation of the ninth commandment (false witness) was inseparably connected to violations of the tenth commandment (coveting Naboth's vineyard), the eighth commandment (stealing the vineyard), the sixth commandment (murdering Naboth), and the first commandment (the worship of false gods by Ahab and Jezebel). When one commandment falls, the others follow. The moral law is a unified fabric, and a tear in one place quickly spreads to the rest.

PART TWO: THE BROADER SCOPE OF THE NINTH COMMANDMENT

While the ninth commandment was originally directed at courtroom testimony, the Puritans, following the Reformed tradition, understood it as encompassing the entire domain of truth-telling. The Westminster Larger Catechism, in Questions 143 and 144, provides an exhaustive treatment of the duties required and the sins forbidden by the ninth commandment.

The sins forbidden are numerous and varied. The most obvious is outright lying — stating what is false with the intention of deceiving. Thomas Watson, in his exposition of the ninth commandment, identified lying as one of the most common and most destructive of all sins. Watson wrote that "a lie is an abomination to God, for God is truth." He cited Proverbs 6:16-19, which lists "a lying tongue" and "a false witness that speaketh lies" among the seven things that the Lord hates.

But the Puritans identified many forms of falsehood beyond outright lying. Flattery — praising someone insincerely for selfish purposes — is a form of deception. The flatterer uses the appearance of admiration as a tool for manipulation, telling people what they want to hear rather than what they need to hear. Proverbs 29:5 warns: "A man that flattereth his neighbour spreadeth a net for his feet." The Puritans despised flattery as a particularly insidious form of lying, because it corrupts not only the flatterer but the person being flattered, filling him with a false sense of his own virtue or importance.

Exaggeration — inflating the truth beyond what the facts warrant — is another form of falsehood. The person who exaggerates is not inventing entirely from nothing, but he is distorting reality to create a false impression. Whether the exaggeration is in the direction of making something seem better than it is (to boast) or worse than it is (to elicit sympathy or to condemn), it violates the ninth commandment's requirement of truthfulness.

Equivocation — using ambiguous language to create a false impression while maintaining technical accuracy — is perhaps the most sophisticated form of lying. The equivocator says something that is literally true but deliberately misleading. He exploits the ambiguity of language to deceive while preserving the appearance of honesty. The Puritans condemned equivocation with particular severity, viewing it as a form of hypocrisy — the sin of appearing righteous while practicing deception.

Silence in the face of falsehood is also condemned by the ninth commandment. When the truth is being distorted and we have the ability and responsibility to correct it, our failure to speak is a form of complicity in the lie. The Puritans insisted that truth-telling is not merely passive — we are not only required to refrain from lying but actively required to defend and promote the truth whenever it is under attack.

PART THREE: THE SINS OF THE TONGUE AGAINST OUR NEIGHBOR'S REPUTATION

The ninth commandment also addresses what the Puritans called "the sins of the tongue" that damage the reputation of our neighbor. The Puritans identified several distinct sins in this category, each with its own particular character and destructive effect.

Slander is the spreading of false information about another person with the intent to damage their reputation. Slander is a form of murder — it kills not the body but the good name, which, as Solomon observed, "is rather to be chosen than great riches, and loving favour rather than silver and gold" (Proverbs 22:1). A person's reputation is among the most valuable things he possesses, and to destroy it through lies is to inflict a wound that may never fully heal. The slanderer commits a double sin: he lies, and he harms his neighbor — violating both the spirit of the ninth commandment and the spirit of the sixth.

Gossip is the spreading of true but private information for the purpose of entertainment, curiosity, or malice. The distinction between slander and gossip is important: the slanderer spreads falsehood, while the gossip spreads truth — but truth that was not his to share. Gossip violates the ninth commandment because it damages the reputation of our neighbor by exposing private information that should have remained confidential. "A talebearer revealeth secrets: but he that is of a faithful spirit concealeth the matter" (Proverbs 11:13).

Backbiting is speaking ill of someone behind their back — saying things about a person in their absence that we would not say to their face. The backbiter is a coward who hides behind the absent person's inability to defend himself. Psalm 15:1-3 includes the refusal to backbite among the marks of the righteous person: "LORD, who shall abide in thy tabernacle? who shall dwell in thy holy hill? He that walketh uprightly, and worketh righteousness, and speaketh the truth in his heart. He that backbiteth not with his tongue, nor doeth evil to his neighbour, nor taketh up a reproach against his neighbour."

Talebearing is the practice of carrying reports from one person to another in a way designed to create strife and division. The talebearer may not add any falsehood to what he reports, but he selects, frames, and delivers information in a way calculated to turn people against each other. "The words of a talebearer are as wounds, and they go down into the innermost parts of the belly" (Proverbs 18:8). The talebearer violates the ninth commandment because he uses truth as a weapon rather than as a tool of love.

Richard Baxter, in his "Christian Directory," devoted an extensive section to the government of the tongue, counseling believers to ask three questions before speaking about another person: Is it true? Is it necessary? Is it kind? If the answer to any of these questions is no, Baxter advised, we should hold our tongues. He observed that "more mischief is done in the world by the unruly tongue than by the unruly sword," and he urged Christians to cultivate a discipline of speech that reflects the character of God, who is truth, love, and wisdom.

PART FOUR: THE POSITIVE REQUIREMENTS OF THE NINTH COMMANDMENT

The ninth commandment does not merely forbid falsehood — it requires the active pursuit and defense of truth. The Westminster Larger Catechism lists among the positive duties required by the ninth commandment: "the preserving and promoting of truth between man and man, and the good name of our neighbour, as well as our own; appearing and standing for the truth; and from the heart, sincerely, freely, clearly, and fully, speaking the truth, and only the truth, in matters of judgment and justice, and in all other things whatsoever; a charitable esteem of our neighbours; loving, desiring, and rejoicing in their good name; sorrowing for and covering of their infirmities; freely acknowledging of their gifts and graces, defending their innocency; a ready receiving of a good report, and unwillingness to admit of an evil report, concerning them; discouraging talebearers, flatterers, and slanderers."

Notice the extraordinary breadth and depth of these requirements. The ninth commandment calls us to be people who love truth and speak truth — but also people who love our neighbors and actively seek to protect their good name. We are called to interpret the actions of others charitably, to rejoice in their successes, to grieve over their failings, to defend them against unjust accusation, and to refuse to listen to gossip and slander.

The Puritans called this disposition "Christian candor" — a generous habit of interpretation that gives others the benefit of the doubt rather than assuming the worst about their motives and actions. Matthew Henry, in his commentary, observed that "love thinketh no evil" (1 Corinthians 13:5) — that genuine Christian love inclines us to put the best possible construction on the words and actions of others, rather than rushing to judgment or eagerly seizing upon every negative report.

PART FIVE: TRUTH-TELLING IN PUBLIC LIFE

The Puritans understood that the ninth commandment has profound implications for public life — for the conduct of government, the administration of justice, the practice of journalism, the work of education, and the discourse of civil society.

They insisted that rulers must tell the truth to their subjects. A government built on lies will inevitably betray the trust of its people and undermine the foundations of social order. Samuel Rutherford, in his landmark work "Lex, Rex" published in 1644, argued that the covenant between a ruler and his people depends upon mutual trust and truthfulness. When a ruler deceives his people, he violates not only the ninth commandment but the very basis of his authority.

They insisted that ministers must preach the truth from their pulpits. The pastor who twists Scripture to suit his audience, who softens unpopular truths to avoid controversy, or who proclaims what he knows to be false in order to maintain his popularity is guilty of bearing false witness in the most sacred context possible — the proclamation of God's Word. Paul charged Timothy with solemn urgency: "I charge thee therefore before God, and the Lord Jesus Christ, who shall judge the quick and the dead at his appearing and his kingdom; Preach the word; be instant in season, out of season; reprove, rebuke, exhort with all longsuffering and doctrine. For the time will come when they will not endure sound doctrine; but after their own lusts shall they heap to themselves teachers, having itching ears; And they shall turn away their ears from the truth, and shall be turned unto fables" (2 Timothy 4:1-4).

They insisted that teachers must teach the truth to their students. The educator who misleads his students — whether through deliberate falsehood, careless inaccuracy, or the suppression of inconvenient facts — violates the ninth commandment and betrays the sacred trust that parents and society have placed in him. Education, for the Puritans, was fundamentally an enterprise of truth-seeking and truth-sharing, and the integrity of the teacher was essential to the integrity of the educational process.

And they insisted that merchants must deal honestly with their customers. As we discussed in our lesson on the eighth commandment, the marketplace is not a moral-free zone — it is a sphere of human activity governed by God's law, including the ninth commandment's requirement of truthfulness. Honest advertising, accurate descriptions of goods, the fulfillment of promises, and the transparent conduct of business are all expressions of the ninth commandment applied to commercial life.

PART SIX: THE NINTH COMMANDMENT AND THE GOD OF TRUTH

At its deepest level, the ninth commandment reflects the very character of God. God is truth. Jesus declared: "I am the way, the truth, and the life" (John 14:6). The Holy Spirit is called "the Spirit of truth" (John 16:13). God's Word is truth: "Sanctify them through thy truth: thy word is truth" (John 17:17). Every act of God is truthful; every word of God is reliable; every promise of God is certain. Truth is not merely an attribute of God — it is essential to His nature. He cannot lie: "God is not a man, that he should lie; neither the son of man, that he should repent: hath he said, and shall he not do it? or hath he spoken, and shall he not make it good?" (Numbers 23:19). "In hope of eternal life, which God, that cannot lie, promised before the world began" (Titus 1:2).

Because God is truth, every lie is a contradiction of His character. And because truth is rooted in the nature of God, it is not relative or subjective — it is absolute and objective. The Puritans would have no patience with the modern notion that truth is merely a social construct, a matter of perspective, or a tool of power. Truth, they insisted, is grounded in the eternal, unchanging nature of God Himself. To speak the truth is to align ourselves with reality as God has made it. To lie is to construct a false reality — to rebel against the God who is truth by substituting our own fabrications for His reality.

Jesus identified the devil as "the father of lies" and declared that "there is no truth in him. When he speaketh a lie, he speaketh of his own: for he is a liar, and the father of it" (John 8:44). This is a startling statement. Every lie, however small or seemingly insignificant, aligns us with the character and purposes of Satan rather than with the character and purposes of God. When we speak truth, we reflect the image of the God of truth. When we lie, we speak the language of the one who has been a liar from the beginning.

PART SEVEN: TRANSITION TO THE TENTH COMMANDMENT — THE HEART OF THE MATTER

We turn now to the tenth and final commandment: "Thou shalt not covet thy neighbour's house, thou shalt not covet thy neighbour's wife, nor his manservant, nor his maidservant, nor his ox, nor his ass, nor any thing that is thy neighbour's" (Exodus 20:17).

The tenth commandment is unique among the Ten Commandments because it does not address an outward action. You cannot observe covetousness. You cannot photograph it, record it, or prove it in a court of law. Covetousness is entirely an affair of the heart — an inward disposition, a desire, a craving for what belongs to someone else. And yet God places it among the Ten Commandments, giving it the same weight and authority as the prohibitions against murder, adultery, theft, and false testimony.

This is a profoundly important fact. It tells us that God's moral law does not stop at the surface of human behavior. It penetrates to the deepest level of human motivation. God does not merely require right actions — He requires right desires. He is not satisfied with a person who refrains from stealing but burns with envy for what his neighbor possesses. He demands not merely clean hands but a clean heart.

PART EIGHT: THE APOSTLE PAUL AND THE TENTH COMMANDMENT

The apostle Paul identified the tenth commandment as the instrument that exposed the true condition of his heart and shattered his self-righteousness. In Romans 7:7-11, Paul provides one of the most revealing autobiographical passages in all of his epistles: "What shall we say then? Is the law sin? God forbid. Nay, I had not known sin, but by the law: for I had not known lust, except the law had said, Thou shalt not covet. But sin, taking occasion by the commandment, wrought in me all manner of concupiscence. For without the law sin was dead. For I was alive without the law once: but when the commandment came, sin revived, and I died. And the commandment, which was ordained to life, I found to be unto death. For sin, taking occasion by the commandment, deceived me, and by it slew me."

Paul was a Pharisee — a man who was "touching the righteousness which is in the law, blameless" (Philippians 3:6). He did not murder, did not commit adultery, did not steal, did not bear false witness. He kept the external requirements of the law with meticulous care. But when he confronted the tenth commandment — when the law said "Thou shalt not covet" — he discovered that the law reached beyond his behavior to his desires. And there, in the hidden chambers of his heart, Paul found himself guilty. He coveted. He desired what was not his. He burned with ambitions and yearnings that no amount of external obedience could conceal or satisfy.

The tenth commandment was the instrument that broke Paul's pride, exposed his need, and drove him to Christ. It is the commandment that reveals the ultimate purpose of the entire law — not to make us righteous through our own effort, but to show us our sinfulness and point us to the Savior who alone can cleanse our hearts and satisfy our deepest desires. The Puritans understood this perfectly. They saw the tenth commandment as the final, decisive blow that the law strikes against human self-righteousness. A man may convince himself that he has kept all the other commandments — that he has not worshipped idols, has not murdered, has not committed adultery, has not stolen, has not borne false witness. But when the law says "Thou shalt not covet," it reaches into the most hidden recesses of the heart and exposes the sin that lurks beneath every surface appearance of righteousness. No one can honestly claim to have never coveted. The tenth commandment, therefore, closes every mouth and brings the whole world guilty before God (Romans 3:19).

PART NINE: THOMAS WATSON ON THE SPECIES OF COVETOUSNESS

Thomas Watson offers one of the most penetrating analyses of covetousness in all of Puritan literature. He defines covetousness as "an insatiable desire of getting the world." It is not merely wanting things — desire itself is natural and not inherently sinful. Covetousness is wanting what belongs to someone else, wanting more than God has allotted, and wanting with an intensity that displaces God from the center of the heart.

Watson identifies several species of covetousness. The first is discontent — the restless dissatisfaction with what God has provided. The discontented person never has enough. His portion is always insufficient. Others always seem to have received more. He looks at his neighbor's prosperity with resentment and at his own circumstances with bitterness. Discontent is covetousness in its most basic form — the refusal to accept God's provision with gratitude and trust.

The second is envy — the specific resentment we feel when someone else prospers. Envy does not merely want what the neighbor has — it resents that the neighbor has it. It is pained by another's joy, diminished by another's success, and secretly pleased by another's misfortune. Envy is one of the most corrosive and antisocial of all sins, because it turns the blessings of others into sources of personal torment. Watson observed that "envy is like a worm that eats the fruit from within" — it does not merely want more for itself but actively resents the good fortune of others.

The Puritan preacher Jeremiah Burroughs, in his "Rare Jewel of Christian Contentment" published in 1648, described envy as "a sin against charity, a sin against the glory of God who distributes His gifts as He pleases, and a sin against our own peace." Burroughs argued that the envious person is essentially quarreling with God's providence — accusing God of unfairness in the distribution of His blessings and demanding a different allotment than the one God has wisely appointed.

The third species is greed — the insatiable appetite for accumulation. Greed transforms the pursuit of provision into the worship of possession. It turns money from a tool into a master, from a means into an end. Paul warned Timothy with great solemnity: "But they that will be rich fall into temptation and a snare, and into many foolish and hurtful lusts, which drown men in destruction and perdition. For the love of money is the root of all evil: which while some coveted after, they have erred from the faith, and pierced themselves through with many sorrows" (1 Timothy 6:9-10).

Notice Paul's precision. He does not say that money is the root of all evil — he says that the love of money is the root of all evil. Money itself is morally neutral — it can be used for great good or great harm. The sin is not in possessing money but in loving money — in making the accumulation of wealth the central pursuit of one's life, displacing God from the throne of the heart and putting Mammon in His place. Jesus Himself declared: "No man can serve two masters: for either he will hate the one, and love the other; or else he will hold to the one, and despise the other. Ye cannot serve God and mammon" (Matthew 6:24).

PART TEN: THE REMEDY FOR COVETOUSNESS — CONTENTMENT

The remedy for covetousness, the Puritans insisted with one voice, is contentment — and contentment is not a natural human quality but a supernatural grace, cultivated by faith and nourished by the promises of God.

Paul wrote from prison — from chains, deprivation, and uncertainty — one of the most remarkable statements in all of Scripture: "Not that I speak in respect of want: for I have learned, in whatsoever state I am, therewith to be content. I know both how to be abased, and I know how to abound: every where and in all things I am instructed both to be full and to be hungry, both to abound and to suffer need. I can do all things through Christ which strengtheneth me" (Philippians 4:11-13).

Note the word "learned." Contentment does not come naturally. It is a discipline, a habit, a virtue developed over time through the practice of gratitude, the meditation on God's faithfulness, and the deliberate redirection of desire from earthly things to heavenly ones. Paul did not begin his Christian life as a contented man — he learned contentment through years of experience, through trials and triumphs, through abundance and deprivation, through the school of suffering in which God gradually taught him to find his sufficiency in Christ alone.

The writer to the Hebrews provides what may be the single most powerful antidote to covetousness in all of Scripture: "Let your conversation be without covetousness; and be content with such things as ye have: for he hath said, I will never leave thee, nor forsake thee" (Hebrews 13:5). The cure for covetousness is the presence of God. If God is with us — if the infinite, eternal, all-sufficient God has promised never to leave us or forsake us — then we have everything we need. No amount of earthly wealth can add to the sufficiency of God's presence. No earthly deprivation can diminish it.

Jeremiah Burroughs devoted his entire treatise to the theme of contentment, and his exposition remains one of the masterpieces of Puritan devotional literature. Burroughs defined contentment as "that sweet, inward, quiet, gracious frame of spirit, which freely submits to and delights in God's wise and fatherly disposal in every condition." He identified several marks of the contented soul. The contented person sees God's hand in every circumstance — both pleasant and painful. He trusts that God's purposes are wise, even when they are mysterious. He finds his joy not in his circumstances but in his relationship with God. He is grateful for what he has rather than resentful about what he lacks. And he rests in the assurance that God, who has given him Christ, will also give him everything else he truly needs: "He that spared not his own Son, but delivered him up for us all, how shall he not with him also freely give us all things?" (Romans 8:32).

PART ELEVEN: THE TENTH COMMANDMENT AND THE FIRST COMMANDMENT — COMING FULL CIRCLE

The tenth commandment brings our study of the moral law full circle. The first commandment says: "Thou shalt have no other gods before me." The tenth commandment says: "Thou shalt not covet." Together, they make the same essential demand: let God be supreme in your heart. Let nothing and no one displace Him. Let your deepest desires be oriented not toward the things of this world but toward the God who created this world and who offers Himself to you as your eternal portion and everlasting joy.

Paul made this connection explicit in his letter to the Colossians: "Mortify therefore your members which are upon the earth; fornication, uncleanness, inordinate affection, evil concupiscence, and covetousness, which is idolatry" (Colossians 3:5). Covetousness is idolatry. When we give to any created thing the devotion that belongs to God alone — when we look to money, possessions, status, or pleasure for the satisfaction that only God can provide — we are guilty of breaking the first commandment as well as the tenth. The Decalogue ends where it begins: with the absolute claim of God upon the human heart.

PART TWELVE: THE NINTH AND TENTH COMMANDMENTS IN PURITAN PRACTICE

The Puritans did not merely theorize about truth-telling and contentment — they sought to practice these virtues with rigorous consistency in their daily lives. Their communities were structured to promote honesty, accountability, and mutual care in ways that reflected the ninth and tenth commandments.

In Puritan New England, the practice of church discipline was closely connected to the ninth commandment. Members of Puritan congregations were expected to speak truthfully about themselves and about others. Gossip, slander, and false accusation were treated as serious offenses, subject to correction by the church elders. When disputes arose between members, the church followed the procedure outlined by Jesus in Matthew 18:15-17: first, the offended party was to speak privately with the offender; if that failed, he was to bring one or two witnesses; and only if both private approaches failed was the matter to be brought before the whole church. This process was designed to ensure that truth prevailed while protecting the reputations of all parties involved.

The Puritans also cultivated contentment through their disciplines of prayer, family worship, and Sabbath observance. The daily rhythm of family devotions — including the reading of Scripture, prayer, and the singing of psalms — was intended to fix the minds and hearts of family members upon God and His promises, counteracting the natural human tendency toward covetousness and discontent. The weekly Sabbath, with its emphasis on worship, rest, and spiritual reflection, provided a regular opportunity to step back from the pursuit of earthly goods and to reorient the heart toward heavenly things.

Cotton Mather, in his diary, provides a vivid example of the Puritan practice of self-examination in relation to the tenth commandment. Mather frequently recorded his struggles with envy and ambition, confessing to God the moments when he coveted the success, the recognition, or the possessions of others. He combated these temptations through deliberate acts of gratitude — listing the blessings God had given him and meditating on the sufficiency of God's provision. Mather also practiced what the Puritans called "heavenly-mindedness" — the deliberate cultivation of desire for eternal things rather than temporal things. "Set your affection on things above, not on things on the earth," Paul had commanded (Colossians 3:2), and Mather took this instruction with utmost seriousness.

Jonathan Edwards, the great theologian and preacher of Northampton, Massachusetts, wrote his famous "Resolutions" as a young man in his early twenties, and several of these resolutions directly address the ninth and tenth commandments. Resolution 34 states: "Resolved, In narrations, never to speak anything but the pure and simple truth." Resolution 44 states: "Resolved, That no other end but religion, shall have any influence at all on any of my actions; and that no action shall be, in the least circumstance, any otherwise than the religious end will carry it." Edwards understood that truth-telling and contentment are not isolated virtues — they are expressions of a heart wholly devoted to God, a heart that finds its satisfaction in the glory of God rather than in the approval of men or the acquisition of things.

The Puritan practice of journaling and self-examination served as a powerful tool for cultivating both truthfulness and contentment. By regularly examining their own hearts in the light of God's Word, the Puritans sought to identify and root out the subtle deceptions of self-interest and the hidden cravings of covetousness. This practice required a painful honesty with oneself — a willingness to see one's own faults clearly and to confess them to God without excuse or evasion. The ninth commandment begins at home: before we can speak truth to others, we must be truthful with ourselves, and before we can be truthful with ourselves, we must invite the searchlight of God's Word to illuminate the darkest corners of our hearts. David prayed: "Search me, O God, and know my heart: try me, and know my thoughts: And see if there be any wicked way in me, and lead me in the way everlasting" (Psalm 139:23-24). This was the prayer of every Puritan who took the ninth and tenth commandments seriously.

PART THIRTEEN: THE NINTH AND TENTH COMMANDMENTS AND THE GOSPEL

As with every commandment we have studied, the ninth and tenth commandments ultimately point us to Christ.

The ninth commandment reveals our need for a Savior who is truth incarnate. We are a race of liars — not merely occasional liars but habitual, instinctive, reflexive liars. We lie to protect ourselves, to promote ourselves, to manipulate others, and to avoid uncomfortable realities. The ninth commandment exposes this tendency with ruthless clarity and drives us to the One who is "the way, the truth, and the life."

The tenth commandment reveals our need for a Savior who can satisfy the restless desires of the human heart. We are a race of coveters — always wanting more, never satisfied, always comparing, always grasping. The tenth commandment exposes this tendency and points us to the One who said: "If any man thirst, let him come unto me, and drink. He that believeth on me, as the scripture hath said, out of his belly shall flow rivers of living water" (John 7:37-38).

Christ is the fulfillment of both commandments. He is the truth that the ninth commandment requires, and He is the satisfaction that the tenth commandment reveals we need. In Him, our tongues are freed from the slavery of falsehood and trained in the ways of truth. In Him, our hearts are freed from the tyranny of covetousness and filled with the peace of contentment. Christ is the end of the law for all who believe — not in the sense that He abolishes the law, but in the sense that He fulfills its every demand and provides us with the grace to pursue its standards with renewed hearts and empowered wills.

PART THIRTEEN: CONCLUDING REFLECTIONS ON THE TEN COMMANDMENTS

As we conclude our study of the Ten Commandments, let us step back and consider the great arc of the moral law as a whole. We began with the first commandment — the foundational claim of God upon the human heart: "Thou shalt have no other gods before me." We end with the tenth commandment — the final demand of the law: "Thou shalt not covet." Between these two bookends, the moral law has addressed every dimension of human life: our worship, our speech, our use of time, our relationships with authority, our treatment of human life, our sexual conduct, our economic behavior, our truthfulness, and our desires.

The Puritans saw in the Ten Commandments not a cold list of prohibitions but a comprehensive portrait of the good life — the life that God designed human beings to live, the life that flourishes under His blessing, the life that reflects His character in every dimension. When we love God supremely (commandments 1-4), love our neighbor genuinely (commandments 5-9), and desire rightly (commandment 10), we are living the life for which we were created — the life of true freedom, true joy, and true peace.

At the same time, the Puritans recognized that the law, however beautiful and good, cannot save us. The law reveals God's standard, but it does not provide the power to meet that standard. The law diagnoses the disease, but it does not supply the cure. The law shows us our sin, but it cannot take away our sin. For that, we need the gospel — the good news that Jesus Christ, the Son of God, lived the perfect life that we could never live, died the death that our sins deserved, and rose again in triumph over sin and death, offering free and full forgiveness to all who trust in Him.

The relationship between law and gospel was one of the central themes of Puritan theology. The Puritans insisted that the law and the gospel are not enemies but allies — that the law drives us to Christ by showing us our need, and Christ fulfills the law by providing both the righteousness that the law demands and the power to pursue the holiness that the law describes. As Paul wrote: "Do we then make void the law through faith? God forbid: yea, we establish the law" (Romans 3:31).

May the study of God's moral law that we have undertaken together in these lessons bear fruit in your lives — not merely as head knowledge but as heart transformation. May the Ten Commandments serve as a mirror to reveal your need for Christ, as a guide to direct your steps in the path of righteousness, and as a window through which you glimpse the beauty and holiness of the God who gave them. And may the gospel of grace empower you to live according to His law — not in your own strength, but in the strength of the Holy Spirit, who writes God's law upon the tablets of our hearts and enables us to walk in His ways with joy and thanksgiving.

--- Closing ---

The Ten Commandments are not the enemy of happiness — they are its roadmap. The soul that loves God supremely, loves neighbor genuinely, speaks truth faithfully, and desires rightly is the soul that has found the path to true and lasting joy. The ninth commandment calls us to be people of truth — reflecting the character of the God who cannot lie. The tenth commandment calls us to be people of contentment — finding our deepest satisfaction not in the things of this world but in the God who made this world and who offers Himself to us as our eternal portion. May God write His law not merely on tablets of stone but on the tablets of our hearts, and may the gospel of His grace empower us to walk in His ways all the days of our lives.
