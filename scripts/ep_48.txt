PURITAN GOLD -- Study Devotional, Day 17
Series: Romans
"The Potter's Right"
God's sovereign choice and the mystery of election

---

Good evening. There is no shortcut to spiritual depth. It comes through sustained, careful engagement with God's Word. That is what we are doing together in this study series, and tonight we take the next step.

We are studying the book of Romans together. Tonight's lesson is titled "The Potter's Right." Each session builds on what came before, so if you have been following along, you will see how tonight's passage connects to the larger argument of the book.

--- Scripture Reading ---

Let us read our passage for tonight. It comes from Romans 9:1-18:

"I say the truth in Christ, I lie not, my conscience also bearing me witness in the Holy Ghost, That I have great heaviness and continual sorrow in my heart. For I could wish that myself were accursed from Christ for my brethren, my kinsmen according to the flesh... For the children being not yet born, neither having done any good or evil, that the purpose of God according to election might stand, not of works, but of him that calleth; It was said unto her, The elder shall serve the younger. As it is written, Jacob have I loved, but Esau have I hated... For he saith to Moses, I will have mercy on whom I will have mercy, and I will have compassion on whom I will have compassion. So then it is not of him that willeth, nor of him that runneth, but of God that sheweth mercy... Therefore hath he mercy on whom he will have mercy, and whom he will he hardeneth."

Read that passage again to yourself. Mark any words or phrases that stand out. The Puritans read with a pen in hand, always ready to note what God was showing them.

--- Key Verse ---

The Puritans often identified a single verse as the hinge of a passage. Tonight, that verse is this:

"So then it is not of him that willeth, nor of him that runneth, but of God that sheweth mercy." -- Romans 9:16

This verse is the lens through which we will examine the rest of tonight's passage. Keep it in the front of your mind as we proceed.

--- Exposition ---

Let us unfold the meaning of this passage together.

A.W. Pink offers valuable commentary on this passage. The Puritans were expositors of the highest order, and their insights remain remarkably fresh.

A.W. Pink, in his 'The Sovereignty of God,' gave extended treatment to Romans 9, which he considered the most explicit statement of divine sovereignty in all of Scripture. Pink argued that the passage must be taken at face value: God's choice of Jacob over Esau was made before either child was born, before either had done good or evil, for the express purpose of demonstrating that election rests 'not of works, but of him that calleth.' Pink insisted that any attempt to soften this into a mere foreknowledge of human choices does violence to the text, which explicitly states that the choice was made before any choices were made.

Pink was especially forceful on verse 16: 'So then it is not of him that willeth, nor of him that runneth, but of God that sheweth mercy.' He argued that this verse demolishes every system of theology that makes human will the decisive factor in salvation. Salvation does not originate in the human will ('him that willeth') or in human effort ('him that runneth') but in God who shows mercy. Pink acknowledged that human beings do will and do run — but their willing and running are the effects of grace, not the cause of it.

Pink also addressed the hardening of Pharaoh in verse 18, arguing that God's hardening is a judicial act upon those who are already sinful. God does not create sin or force innocent people into rebellion. Rather, He withdraws His restraining grace from those who have already chosen rebellion, allowing them to follow their own sinful inclinations to their appointed end. Pink saw in this the perfect consistency of divine sovereignty and human responsibility: God ordains the end, man acts according to his own nature, and God's purposes are accomplished through (not against) human choices. Pink urged believers not to shy away from this doctrine but to bow before it in worship, recognizing that if God's mercy depended on human merit, no one would ever be saved.

Romans 9 marks a major transition in the epistle. Having established the great doctrines of justification, sanctification, and glorification in chapters 1-8, Paul now addresses a burning question: if the gospel is true, what about Israel? God made irrevocable promises to Abraham's descendants. Yet the majority of Israel has rejected Christ. Has God's word failed?

Paul begins not with abstract theology but with agonized love. Verses 1-3 reveal a heart broken for his kinsmen: 'I have great heaviness and continual sorrow in my heart. For I could wish that myself were accursed from Christ for my brethren, my kinsmen according to the flesh.' This is no cold, detached theologian. Paul would exchange his own salvation for theirs if it were possible. This echoes Moses' prayer after the golden calf: 'Blot me, I pray thee, out of thy book' (Exodus 32:32). The doctrine of election, properly understood, does not produce hard-hearted indifference; it produces compassion.

Verses 4-5 catalog Israel's privileges: the adoption, the glory, the covenants, the giving of the law, the service of God, the promises, the patriarchs, and supremely, 'of whom as concerning the flesh Christ came, who is over all, God blessed for ever. Amen.' Notice the explicit affirmation of Christ's deity — He is 'over all, God blessed for ever.'

Verse 6 states the thesis: 'Not as though the word of God hath taken none effect.' God's promises have not failed. But the promises were never intended for every physical descendant of Abraham. 'For they are not all Israel, which are of Israel.' There is an Israel within Israel — a spiritual remnant chosen by grace. This is not a new idea; it has been true from the beginning.

Verses 7-9 prove this from Abraham's own family. Not all of Abraham's children were children of promise. Ishmael was Abraham's son, but Isaac was the child of promise. The line of descent was not merely physical but elective — God chose Isaac, not Ishmael.

Verses 10-13 take the argument further with Jacob and Esau. These were not merely half-brothers (as Isaac and Ishmael) but twins, born of the same mother at the same time. Yet 'the children being not yet born, neither having done any good or evil, that the purpose of God according to election might stand, not of works, but of him that calleth; It was said unto her, The elder shall serve the younger.' The choice was made before birth, before any deeds, for the explicit purpose of showing that election depends on God's call, not human works. 'As it is written, Jacob have I loved, but Esau have I hated' (Malachi 1:2-3).

The word 'hated' has generated much discussion. Some soften it to 'loved less,' which is linguistically possible but does not escape the fundamental point: God chose Jacob and did not choose Esau, before either had done anything to merit favor or disfavor. This is the doctrine of unconditional election — God's choice is based on His own sovereign purpose, not on foreseen human merit.

Verse 14 raises the inevitable objection: 'Is there unrighteousness with God? God forbid.' God is not unjust in exercising sovereign choice because no one deserves mercy. If God gave everyone what they deserve, all would be condemned. The question is not 'why does God choose some?' but 'why does God choose any?'

Verses 15-16 answer with God's own declaration to Moses: 'I will have mercy on whom I will have mercy, and I will have compassion on whom I will have compassion.' Mercy, by definition, is undeserved. If it were deserved, it would be justice, not mercy. God is free to bestow mercy on whomever He wills. 'So then it is not of him that willeth, nor of him that runneth, but of God that sheweth mercy.' The decisive factor in salvation is not human will or human effort but divine mercy.

Verses 17-18 present the case of Pharaoh: 'For the scripture saith unto Pharaoh, Even for this same purpose have I raised thee up, that I might shew my power in thee, and that my name might be declared throughout all the earth.' God raised Pharaoh to a position of power for the express purpose of demonstrating His own power through the plagues and the Exodus. Pharaoh hardened his own heart (Exodus 8:15, 32; 9:34), and God hardened Pharaoh's heart (Exodus 9:12; 10:1, 20, 27). Both are true. Human responsibility and divine sovereignty are not contradictory but complementary truths.

Verse 18 states the comprehensive conclusion: 'Therefore hath he mercy on whom he will have mercy, and whom he will he hardeneth.' This is not arbitrary caprice but sovereign wisdom. God's decisions are never random; they are always purposeful, always just, always directed toward His glory and the good of His people.

This passage is difficult for many, and it should be approached with humility. We are not called to explain God's sovereignty but to bow before it. As Paul will say in verse 20, 'who art thou that repliest against God?' The proper response to election is not philosophical debate but humble worship — marveling that God, who owed mercy to no one, chose to show mercy to us.

--- Application ---

Study without application is like a seed without soil. Let us now plant what we have learned in the ground of daily life.

The doctrine of God's sovereignty in Romans 9:1-18 should shape your life in specific ways.

First, let election produce humility, not pride. If your salvation depends on God's sovereign mercy rather than your own merit, you have nothing to boast about — absolutely nothing. You did not choose God first; He chose you (John 15:16). You did not seek Him first; He sought you. Every shred of spiritual pride is demolished by the doctrine of election. Let this produce profound humility in all your dealings.

Second, let sovereignty produce compassion. Paul, the great teacher of election, began this chapter with tears for the lost. The more deeply you understand that salvation is entirely God's work, the more freely you should weep over those who are perishing and the more boldly you should proclaim the gospel to all. Election does not undermine evangelism; it empowers it. We preach to all because we do not know whom God has chosen, and we trust that God will use our proclamation to call His elect to faith.

Third, let God's freedom in mercy produce worship. 'I will have mercy on whom I will have mercy.' This is the declaration of a sovereign King who does as He pleases. And what pleased Him was to show mercy to you. You could have been Esau. You could have been Pharaoh. But God, in His inscrutable wisdom and unfathomable love, chose to set His love upon you. This should produce not theological argument but doxology.

Fourth, rest in God's unshakeable purpose. If your salvation depends on God's purpose in election, it cannot fail. Human purposes fail; God's purposes never do. 'The purpose of God according to election' stands firm regardless of circumstances. Your security is anchored not in your faith but in God's faithfulness, not in your grip on Him but in His grip on you.

Write down one concrete action you will take this week in response to tonight's study. Do not let this truth remain theoretical. The Puritans measured the success of a sermon not by how much the hearer learned but by how much the hearer changed.

--- Closing ---

Sovereign Lord, we bow before the mystery of Your electing love. We cannot fully comprehend why You chose to have mercy on us, but we worship You for it. Forgive us for presuming to question Your justice or demanding that You explain Yourself. You are the potter; we are the clay. Have mercy on us according to Your will. Give us hearts like Paul — hearts that weep for the lost even as they rest in Your sovereignty. Let the doctrine of election produce in us not pride but humility, not indifference but passion, not argument but worship. In Christ Jesus our Lord. Amen.

Continue to meditate on tonight's passage. The more you return to it, the more it will reveal.
