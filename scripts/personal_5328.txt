PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Theological Reflection, Day 28
"Walking in Integrity"
Psalm 15 and Upright Living — The Character God Honors

---

Good evening, friend. Tonight we slow down and reflect on a short but searching psalm — Psalm 15. David asks a simple question: who may dwell in God's presence? And the answer he receives is a portrait of integrity — a life that is consistent, honest, and true in every dimension. The Puritans took this portrait seriously, and so must we.

--- Scripture ---

Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Psalm 15:

"LORD, who shall abide in thy tabernacle? who shall dwell in thy holy hill? He that walketh uprightly, and worketh righteousness, and speaketh the truth in his heart. He that backbiteth not with his tongue, nor doeth evil to his neighbour, nor taketh up a reproach against his neighbour. In whose eyes a vile person is contemned; but he honoureth them that fear the LORD. He that sweareth to his own hurt, and changeth not. He that putteth not out his money to usury, nor taketh reward against the innocent. He that doeth these things shall never be moved."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.

--- Theological Reflection ---

Psalm 15 begins with a question of staggering importance: who may dwell in the presence of God? In the Old Testament context, this referred to approaching the tabernacle, the place where God's glory resided among His people. But the question reaches far beyond the physical temple. It asks, in essence: what kind of person can have fellowship with the holy God? And David's answer paints a portrait not of perfection but of integrity — a life in which the inner man and the outer man are the same.

The Puritans were deeply drawn to this psalm because they understood that Christianity is not merely a set of beliefs but a way of life. Thomas Watson wrote that a man's religion is not measured by his creed but by his character. A man may profess all the right doctrines and practice all the right rituals, but if his life does not match his confession, his religion is vain. Psalm 15 describes the man whose life matches his lips.

Consider the virtues David lists. The first is uprightness — walking with integrity, without duplicity or hidden agendas. The upright man is the same in private as he is in public. He does not wear masks. He does not play roles. He is what he appears to be. John Owen called this "sincerity of heart" and considered it one of the most essential marks of true conversion. The hypocrite performs for an audience; the sincere man lives before the face of God.

The second cluster of virtues concerns speech. The man of integrity speaks the truth in his heart — that is, he is honest not only with others but with himself. He does not backbite, which is to speak against someone behind their back. He does not take up a reproach against his neighbor, which means he does not listen eagerly to gossip or spread rumors. The Puritans took the sins of the tongue with deadly seriousness. Thomas Brooks warned that more harm is done in the church by the tongue than by any other instrument of evil. A single careless word can destroy a reputation, divide a fellowship, and wound a soul beyond easy repair.

The third virtue is moral discernment. In the eyes of the man of integrity, a vile person is held in contempt — not with personal malice but with moral clarity. He does not admire wickedness, no matter how successful or powerful the wicked person may be. And he honors those who fear the Lord, no matter how humble or obscure they may be. This is a radical reversal of the world's values. The world honors the rich, the famous, the powerful. The man of Psalm 15 honors the godly. Stephen Charnock wrote that true wisdom consists in esteeming things according to their true value, and nothing is more valuable than the fear of the Lord.

The fourth virtue is faithfulness in commitments. The man of integrity keeps his word even when it costs him. He swears to his own hurt and does not change. The Puritans considered promise-keeping a sacred duty. Richard Baxter taught that a man's word should be as binding as a legal contract, not because the law requires it but because God requires it. In an age of broken promises and convenient lies, the man who keeps his word stands as a living testimony to the faithfulness of the God he serves.

The psalm concludes with a promise: he who does these things shall never be moved. This does not mean he will never face trials. It means his foundation is secure. He is built upon the rock. When the storms come — and they will come — he will stand, because his life is grounded not in circumstances but in character, and not in his own strength but in the God whose character he reflects.

--- Application ---

First, conduct an honest inventory of your integrity. Is there a gap between your public profession and your private behavior? Ask the Spirit to reveal any area of duplicity and give you the courage to close the gap.

Second, guard your tongue this week with special care. Before you speak about another person, ask yourself: is this true? Is it kind? Is it necessary? The Puritans practiced this discipline with rigor, and it transformed their relationships.

Third, examine your commitments. Have you made promises you have not kept? Have you given your word and then looked for a way out when the cost became clear? Repent of any broken promises and resolve, by God's grace, to be a person whose word can be trusted.

--- Closing Prayer ---

O Lord, search us and know our hearts. Try us and know our thoughts. Reveal every way in us that is not upright, and lead us in the way everlasting. Make us people of integrity — the same in the dark as in the light, the same in private as in public. May our lives adorn the gospel we profess. Through Christ, who is Himself the Truth, Amen.

Walk with God tomorrow, friend, and let your life speak what your lips confess.
