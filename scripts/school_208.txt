Lesson 3: "Vanity Fair — Living in the World"
The Pilgrim's Journey


Good morning, students. Welcome to the Puritan Academy. Let us begin this day as the Puritans did, by acknowledging God as the source of all knowledge and wisdom. Before we open our textbooks, let us open our hearts.

Almighty God, the fountain of all wisdom, we ask You to bless our studies today. Open our minds to understand, our hearts to receive, and our wills to obey the truth. Grant us diligence, humility, and a spirit of inquiry that honors You. Through Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.

Let us meditate on a portion of Scripture before we begin our main lesson.

Our Scripture for today is 1 John 2:15-17:

"Love not the world, neither the things that are in the world. If any man love the world, the love of the Father is not in him. For all that is in the world, the lust of the flesh, and the lust of the eyes, and the pride of life, is not of the Father, but is of the world. And the world passeth away, and the lust thereof: but he that doeth the will of God abideth for ever."

As you step into this day, remember that you are a pilgrim passing through Vanity Fair. The world will display its wares before you — pleasure, comfort, recognition, wealth — and it will invite you to buy. But you carry a different currency. Your treasure is in heaven, where neither moth nor rust corrupts and where thieves do not break through and steal. Do not set your heart on what is passing away. Set it on what endures forever. Love not the world, for the world is fading. But the one who does the will of God abides forever. Walk through the fair with your eyes fixed on the Celestial City, and do not stop to purchase what you cannot keep.

As we prepare for today's lesson, remember that everything we study flows from this one fountain: the Word of God. Our lesson today is part of The Pilgrim's Journey, and it is titled: Vanity Fair — Living in the World. Let us proceed with minds engaged and hearts open.


Now let us turn to our main lesson for today.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes in 1 John 2:15-17.

The relationship between the Christian and the world is one of the most important and most challenging topics in all of Scripture. First John 2:15-17 issues a command that is as countercultural today as it was in the first century: "Love not the world, neither the things that are in the world. If any man love the world, the love of the Father is not in him." This is not a suggestion or a recommendation but an absolute prohibition. The believer is forbidden to set his affections upon the world system — its values, its priorities, its pleasures, and its pursuits.

But what does "the world" mean in this context? John is not referring to the physical creation, which God made and declared "very good" (Genesis 1:31). Nor is he referring to the people of the world, whom God loves and for whom Christ died (John 3:16). Rather, "the world" in 1 John 2:15 refers to the organized system of values, beliefs, and practices that stands in opposition to God — what we might call the spirit of the age, the culture of rebellion against divine authority. It is the world system that Satan administers as "the god of this world" (2 Corinthians 4:4) and "the prince of this world" (John 12:31).

John identifies three categories that encompass everything the world offers: "the lust of the flesh, and the lust of the eyes, and the pride of life." The lust of the flesh refers to the sinful desires of our fallen nature — the craving for physical pleasure apart from God's design. The lust of the eyes refers to the covetous desire for what we see — the craving for material possessions, beauty, and spectacle. The pride of life refers to the arrogant ambition for status, recognition, and self-glorification. These three categories are not exhaustive descriptions of specific sins but comprehensive headings under which all worldly temptations fall.

It is striking that these same three categories appear in the first temptation in human history. When Eve saw that the tree was "good for food" (lust of the flesh), "pleasant to the eyes" (lust of the eyes), and "to be desired to make one wise" (pride of life), she took the fruit and ate (Genesis 3:6). The same three categories appear in Satan's temptation of Christ. He tempted Jesus to turn stones to bread (lust of the flesh), showed Him all the kingdoms of the world (lust of the eyes), and challenged Him to throw Himself from the temple pinnacle (pride of life) — Matthew 4:1-11. The world's temptations are remarkably consistent: they appeal to the same desires in every generation.

Romans 12:1-2 provides the positive counterpart to John's prohibition: "I beseech you therefore, brethren, by the mercies of God, that ye present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable unto God, which is your reasonable service. And be not conformed to this world: but be ye transformed by the renewing of your mind, that ye may prove what is that good, and acceptable, and perfect, will of God." Paul uses two contrasting images: conformity to the world versus transformation by the renewing of the mind. The Greek word for "conformed" (suschematizo) suggests an outward, superficial molding to an external pattern. The Greek word for "transformed" (metamorphoo) suggests a deep, inward change of nature — the same word used for Christ's transfiguration. We are not to let the world press us into its mold; we are to be radically changed from within by the truth of God.

In John 17:14-16, Jesus prays for His disciples: "I have given them thy word; and the world hath hated them, because they are not of the world, even as I am not of the world. I pray not that thou shouldest take them out of the world, but that thou shouldest keep them from the evil. They are not of the world, even as I am not of the world." This prayer reveals the Christian's paradoxical relationship with the world: we are in the world but not of the world. Jesus does not pray for our removal from the world but for our protection in the world. We are not called to withdraw into monasteries or communes but to live in the midst of a fallen world while maintaining our distinctiveness as citizens of heaven.

James 4:4 speaks with bracing directness: "Ye adulterers and adulteresses, know ye not that the friendship of the world is enmity with God? whosoever therefore will be a friend of the world is the enemy of God." James uses the language of marital unfaithfulness — "adulterers and adulteresses" — to describe believers who become intimate with the world. Just as a married person who takes a lover betrays the marriage covenant, a Christian who embraces the world's values betrays the covenant with God. There is no middle ground. Friendship with the world is enmity with God. One cannot serve two masters (Matthew 6:24).

Yet the Bible does not call believers to hate the world's people or to disengage from society. We are called to be "the salt of the earth" and "the light of the world" (Matthew 5:13-14) — preserving influences and illuminating witnesses. We are called to "do good unto all men" (Galatians 6:10) and to "let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven" (Matthew 5:16). The distinction is between loving the world system and loving the world's people. We reject the former; we embrace the latter.

The ultimate motivation for not loving the world is found in 1 John 2:17: "And the world passeth away, and the lust thereof: but he that doeth the will of God abideth for ever." The world is temporary; God's kingdom is eternal. Everything the world offers — pleasure, wealth, fame, power — is passing away. It is fading even now. But the one who does God's will has an inheritance that is incorruptible, undefiled, and that fadeth not away (1 Peter 1:4). This is not escapism but realism — the most clear-eyed assessment of reality available.

This biblical foundation is essential. Without it, everything else we study today would be like a house built on sand. But with it, we stand on solid rock.


Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. John Bunyan wrote extensively on this subject in "The Pilgrim's Progress." The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life.

John Bunyan's depiction of Vanity Fair in "The Pilgrim's Progress" is one of the most memorable and theologically significant episodes in all of English literature. When Christian and his companion Faithful arrive at the town of Vanity, they find a great fair — a perpetual marketplace — that has been operating since ancient times. Bunyan tells us that the fair was established by Beelzebub, Apollyon, and Legion, and that it offers for sale every conceivable worldly commodity: houses, lands, trades, places, honors, preferments, titles, countries, kingdoms, lusts, pleasures, delights, and "bodies and souls of men."

This last item — "bodies and souls of men" — is Bunyan's most devastating critique of the world system. The world does not merely offer harmless diversions; it trades in human souls. Every pleasure, every ambition, every pursuit that the world offers apart from God is ultimately a trap that enslaves the soul and leads to destruction. Bunyan drew this image from Revelation 18:13, where the merchants of Babylon are said to traffic in "slaves, and souls of men." The world system is not neutral territory; it is enemy territory, governed by the prince of darkness for the purpose of capturing and destroying human souls.

When Christian and Faithful refuse to buy anything at the fair, a great commotion arises. The merchants are offended by their indifference. They are mocked, beaten, placed in a cage for public ridicule, and eventually brought to trial before a court composed of some of the fair's most prominent citizens. The names of the jurymen are devastatingly satirical: Mr. Blind-man, Mr. No-good, Mr. Malice, Mr. Love-lust, Mr. Live-loose, Mr. Heady, Mr. High-mind, Mr. Enmity, Mr. Liar, Mr. Cruelty, Mr. Hate-light, and Mr. Implacable. These names represent the character of the world that sits in judgment upon the people of God.

Faithful is condemned to death and executed with great cruelty. Bunyan describes his death in terms that echo the martyrdom of the early Christians and the Puritan saints of his own generation. But Faithful does not truly die. Bunyan tells us that behind the crowd, a chariot and horses stand waiting, and that Faithful is carried through the clouds to the Celestial Gate with the sound of trumpets. His earthly suffering leads to eternal glory. This is Bunyan's supreme illustration of the truth that the world can kill the body but not the soul (Matthew 10:28).

Christian, meanwhile, escapes from Vanity Fair and continues his journey. Bunyan thus shows that God may allow one believer to be martyred while preserving another — both outcomes are equally in accord with divine sovereignty and equally for the glory of God. The important thing is not whether we escape the world's persecution but whether we remain faithful to Christ in the face of it.

Bunyan's treatment of Vanity Fair reflects the broader Puritan understanding of the believer's relationship to the world. The Puritans were not world-denying ascetics who rejected all earthly enjoyment. They believed that God's creation is good and that its gifts — food, drink, marriage, work, beauty, friendship — are to be received with thanksgiving (1 Timothy 4:4). What the Puritans rejected was not creation but the idolatry of creation — the elevation of any created thing to the place that belongs to God alone. Watson wrote that "the world is a beautiful book, but woe to the man who reads only the book and never looks up to see the Author." The gifts are good; the Giver is better. The world becomes dangerous only when it becomes an end in itself rather than a means to knowing and glorifying God.

The Puritans were particularly concerned about the subtle ways in which worldliness creeps into the church. They observed that the world's influence is not always obvious — it does not always come in the form of gross sin or blatant idolatry. Sometimes it comes in the form of spiritual complacency, materialism disguised as responsible stewardship, ambition disguised as ministry, and entertainment disguised as worship. Bunyan warned through his allegory that the most dangerous aspects of Vanity Fair are not the obviously sinful offerings but the respectable, socially acceptable ones that quietly replace devotion to God with devotion to comfort, security, and reputation.

The Puritans offered practical wisdom for living in the world without being consumed by it. First, they urged regular self-examination. Believers should frequently ask themselves: Am I loving the world? Are my affections set on things above or on things on the earth (Colossians 3:1-2)? Second, they urged moderation in the use of worldly things. Paul's counsel was their guide: "And they that use this world, as not abusing it: for the fashion of this world passeth away" (1 Corinthians 7:31). Third, they urged active engagement in godly fellowship. The believer who isolates himself from other Christians is far more vulnerable to worldly influence than the one who is surrounded by a community of faith. Fourth, they urged constant meditation on eternity. Watson wrote: "He that has his eye fixed upon the world to come will hold the things of this world with a loose hand."

Bunyan himself modeled this balanced approach. He was no recluse. He was a father, a husband, a pastor, and an active member of his community. He worked with his hands as a tinker and cared for his family's practical needs. But he held all these earthly things loosely, knowing that his true citizenship was in heaven. When forced to choose between conformity to the world and faithfulness to Christ, he chose faithfulness — and paid for it with twelve years of his freedom. His example and his writing continue to challenge believers to live as pilgrims and strangers in a world that is passing away, with their hearts set on a city whose builder and maker is God.

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.


Now let us pause and consider what we have learned. Discussion is where understanding deepens.

Discussion Question 1: John identifies 'the lust of the flesh, the lust of the eyes, and the pride of life' as the categories of worldly temptation. Can you give modern examples of each? How do these temptations manifest in your daily life?

Discussion Question 2: Jesus prayed that His disciples would be kept 'from the evil' while remaining 'in the world' (John 17:15-16). What does this balanced approach look like practically?

Discussion Question 3: In Bunyan's Vanity Fair, the most dangerous offerings are not the obviously sinful ones but the respectable, socially acceptable ones. Can you identify worldly values that have subtly influenced the church today?

Discussion Question 4: Faithful was martyred at Vanity Fair while Christian escaped. What does this teach us about God's sovereignty over suffering and the different paths believers may walk?

Discussion Question 5: The Puritans warned against worldliness but were not world-denying ascetics. How do we enjoy God's good gifts without making idols of them?

Take time with each question. The Puritans valued what they called "holy conference," the practice of discussing spiritual truths together. Do not settle for quick, surface answers. Push deeper. Ask follow-up questions. Listen carefully to one another.

Application is where truth becomes life. It is not enough to know what 1 John 2:15-17 says. We must ask: how does this change the way I live today? How does this shape my thoughts, my words, my actions? The Puritans insisted that a truth that does not transform is a truth that has not been truly understood.


Let us now delve deeper into the biblical teaching on worldliness and separation by examining additional Scripture passages and the insights of other Puritan divines.

Consider the sobering example of Demas, mentioned by Paul in 2 Timothy 4:10: "For Demas hath forsaken me, having loved this present world, and is departed unto Thessalonica." Demas had once been a fellow laborer with Paul (Colossians 4:14; Philemon 24). He had worked alongside the great apostle in the ministry of the gospel. But when the cost of discipleship became too high, Demas abandoned the faith because he "loved this present world." The Greek word for "loved" is agapao — the same word used for the highest form of love. Demas did not merely dabble in worldliness; he gave the full devotion of his heart to the present world. His story is a permanent warning to every believer: worldliness can creep into the heart so gradually that even someone who once served faithfully alongside the apostle Paul can be drawn away from the faith.

Colossians 3:1-4 provides the positive counterpart to the command not to love the world: "If ye then be risen with Christ, seek those things which are above, where Christ sitteth on the right hand of God. Set your affection on things above, not on things on the earth. For ye are dead, and your life is hid with Christ in God. When Christ, who is our life, shall appear, then shall ye also appear with him in glory." The remedy for worldliness is not mere self-denial but the positive cultivation of heavenly-mindedness. Paul does not simply say "Stop loving the world." He says "Seek the things above. Set your affection on things above." The heart that is full of heavenly things has no room for worldly things. The believer who is captivated by the beauty of Christ and the glory of His kingdom will find the trinkets of Vanity Fair increasingly unattractive.

Matthew 13:22 records Christ's warning about the deceitfulness of worldly concerns in the Parable of the Sower: "He also that received seed among the thorns is he that heareth the word; and the care of this world, and the deceitfulness of riches, choke the word, and he becometh unfruitful." Notice the word "deceitfulness." Riches and worldly cares are deceptive — they promise satisfaction but deliver emptiness. They promise security but deliver anxiety. They promise freedom but deliver slavery. The thorns do not kill the plant outright; they choke it gradually, slowly strangling its growth until it produces no fruit. This is precisely how worldliness works in the believer's life — not through a sudden, dramatic departure from the faith, but through a slow, gradual suffocation of spiritual vitality.

The Puritan pastor Jeremiah Burroughs (c. 1600-1646) addressed worldliness in his treatise "The Evil of Evils," in which he argued that the sinfulness of sin — including the sin of worldliness — is a greater evil than any outward affliction. Burroughs taught that many believers fear suffering more than they fear sin, but this is a tragic inversion of priorities. Suffering is temporary and may produce great spiritual good; sin is the real enemy that separates the soul from God and brings lasting damage. Burroughs wrote: "One drop of sin is worse than a sea of affliction." This perspective helps explain why Bunyan's pilgrims refused to buy anything at Vanity Fair even when it meant persecution and death. They understood that the loss of worldly comfort was a small price to pay for the preservation of spiritual integrity.

The great Puritan pastor Richard Baxter (1615-1691) addressed the challenge of living in the world without being consumed by it in his monumental work "The Saints' Everlasting Rest." Baxter argued that the primary remedy for worldliness is the regular, disciplined meditation on heaven. He urged believers to spend at least half an hour each day in deliberate contemplation of the glories that await them in the Celestial City. Baxter reasoned that if our hearts are filled with the vision of heaven, the pleasures of the world will lose their power over us. He wrote: "A heavenly mind is the nearest and truest way to a life of comfort. A man who has his heart in heaven can enjoy more sweetness in one hour of meditation on eternal glory than all the pleasures of the world can provide in a lifetime."

Baxter also taught that heavenly-mindedness does not make believers useless in the world but actually makes them more useful. The person whose heart is set on heaven is free from the anxious grasping, the jealous competition, and the fearful hoarding that characterize the worldly mind. Instead, the heavenly-minded believer can engage with the world generously, fearlessly, and joyfully, knowing that his true treasure is secure in heaven. Baxter himself exemplified this balance — he was a tireless pastor, a prolific writer, a faithful husband, and an active citizen who engaged fully with the world while maintaining his heart's allegiance to the Celestial City.

Consider the instruction of Galatians 6:14, where Paul declares: "But God forbid that I should glory, save in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ, by whom the world is crucified unto me, and I unto the world." Paul uses the language of crucifixion to describe the believer's relationship to the world. The world has been crucified to Paul — it is dead to him, stripped of its power to attract and enslave. And Paul has been crucified to the world — in the world's estimation, he is dead, a fool, a nobody. This mutual crucifixion is the result of beholding the cross of Christ. When you truly see what Christ suffered to save you from the world, the world's offerings become contemptible by comparison. The cross is the great disenchanter — it breaks the spell that the world casts over the human heart and sets the believer free to live for something infinitely greater.

The Puritan divine John Owen (1616-1683) wrote about the danger of worldliness with characteristic precision in his work "The Grace and Duty of Being Spiritually Minded." Owen taught that spiritual-mindedness — the habitual inclination of the heart toward God and eternal things — is not merely a desirable quality in the Christian life but an essential mark of genuine conversion. He grounded this teaching in Romans 8:6: "For to be carnally minded is death; but to be spiritually minded is life and peace." Owen argued that the carnal mind — the mind set on worldly things — is not merely sick or weak; it is death. Spiritual-mindedness is not a luxury for advanced Christians; it is life itself. The person who habitually thinks about, desires, and pursues the things of God is alive; the person who habitually thinks about, desires, and pursues the things of the world is, spiritually speaking, dead.

Owen identified several practical tests of spiritual-mindedness. First, what do you think about when your mind is not occupied with duties? The direction of our spontaneous thoughts reveals the true inclination of our hearts. If our minds naturally gravitate toward worldly things — entertainment, possessions, social status, physical pleasures — this suggests that our hearts are set on the world. If our minds naturally turn to God, His Word, His promises, and His glory, this suggests that our hearts are set on things above. Second, what gives you the most joy? If worldly success brings you more joy than spiritual growth, your heart is worldly. If communion with God brings you more joy than any earthly pleasure, your heart is heavenly. Third, what causes you the most grief? If the loss of worldly things grieves you more than the loss of spiritual vitality, your priorities need realignment.


Before we move to the afternoon activity, let us consider specific ways to live as pilgrims in the midst of Vanity Fair.

First, practice daily detachment from the world's values. This does not mean becoming a hermit or rejecting all earthly enjoyment. It means holding everything in this world with an open hand, recognizing that everything you have is temporary and everything God promises is eternal. The Puritans spoke of "weaned affections" — the heart that has been gently detached from earthly things and reattached to heavenly things. Ask God each morning to wean your heart from the world and to fix it upon Christ.

Second, guard your mind against the world's propaganda. We live in an age of unprecedented media saturation. Every day, through screens, advertisements, music, and social media, the world is preaching its message: Buy this. Desire this. Envy this. Pursue this. You deserve this. The believer who is not actively guarding his mind against this barrage will be gradually shaped by it. Romans 12:2 commands, "Be not conformed to this world: but be ye transformed by the renewing of your mind." The renewal of the mind requires intentional effort — daily Bible reading, prayer, meditation on Scripture, and the deliberate replacement of worldly thoughts with godly ones.

Third, cultivate friendships with other pilgrims. In Bunyan's allegory, Christian does not walk through Vanity Fair alone — he has Faithful at his side. One of the most important safeguards against worldliness is the fellowship of like-minded believers who share your values and hold you accountable. Proverbs 13:20 warns, "He that walketh with wise men shall be wise: but a companion of fools shall be destroyed." Surround yourself with friends who love Christ, who pursue holiness, and who will lovingly challenge you when they see you drifting toward worldliness.

Fourth, practice regular fasting from worldly pleasures. The Puritans practiced spiritual disciplines that kept their hearts free from worldly attachment. Periodic fasting from food, entertainment, social media, or other pleasures can serve as a powerful reminder that your ultimate satisfaction is found not in created things but in the Creator Himself. When you fast from something you enjoy, you create space in your heart for God to fill. And you train yourself to say "no" to legitimate desires, which strengthens your ability to resist illegitimate temptations.

Fifth, keep the brevity of life constantly before your eyes. Psalm 90:12 prays, "So teach us to number our days, that we may apply our hearts unto wisdom." James 4:14 asks, "What is your life? It is even a vapour, that appeareth for a little time, and then vanisheth away." The world invests everything in the present moment because the present moment is all it has. But the believer knows that this life is a vapor — a brief passage between birth and eternity. Every day spent pursuing worldly vanity is a day wasted. Every day spent pursuing God's kingdom is a day invested in eternity. Number your days, and spend them wisely.


Now we move to our afternoon activity, where we put truth into practice.

Students will create a modern-day 'Vanity Fair Marketplace.' On a large poster or whiteboard, students will design a market layout with booths representing the things the modern world offers as substitutes for God: entertainment, social media fame, material wealth, romantic relationships apart from God's design, academic prestige, physical appearance, and others. For each booth, students should write: (1) what the world promises this will provide, (2) what it actually delivers in the long run, and (3) what biblical truth exposes the emptiness of this false promise. At the center of the marketplace, students should place a sign pointing to the narrow gate that leads out of Vanity Fair, with a relevant Scripture verse. This exercise helps students critically evaluate the worldly values that surround them daily.

Materials needed: Large poster paper or whiteboard, markers, sticky notes, Bibles, magazines or printed images for cutting out (optional, for visual representation of worldly offerings)

As you complete this activity, think about how it connects to what you studied in today's lesson. The goal is not just to do something, but to reinforce the truths you have been learning. The Puritans believed that education should engage the whole person: mind, heart, and hands.

Take your time with this activity. Quality matters more than speed. When you are finished, reflect on what you learned through the process of doing. Often we understand something far better after we have tried to put it into practice.


One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

The Puritan movement represented one of the most sustained attempts in Western history to live according to biblical principles while remaining engaged with society. Study how the Puritans in both England and New England sought to create a godly society — what John Winthrop called 'a city upon a hill.' Discuss both their successes (education, work ethic, family life, democratic governance) and their failures (legalism, persecution of dissenters). How does their experience illustrate the challenges of living in the world without being of the world?

Literature Connection:

Vanity Fair has become one of the most enduring images in English literature. William Makepeace Thackeray titled his 1848 novel 'Vanity Fair' as a direct reference to Bunyan's allegory. Compare how Bunyan and Thackeray use the image of a worldly marketplace to critique their respective societies. Discuss how allegory and satire function differently as literary tools for social and moral criticism. Consider other literary works that address the tension between worldly values and spiritual truth.

Science Connection:

The psychology of desire and advertising provides a scientific perspective on the 'lust of the eyes' that John warns against. Study how modern advertising uses color, imagery, music, and emotional appeals to create desire for products and experiences. Research the neuroscience of desire — how dopamine pathways in the brain are activated by the anticipation of pleasure. Discuss how understanding these biological mechanisms can help believers recognize and resist the world's attempts to capture their affections.

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.


Let us now explore the deeper theological foundations of the pilgrim's relationship to the world, examining additional Scripture passages and Puritan insights that illuminate what it means to be a citizen of heaven while living as a stranger on earth.

The apostle Peter addresses believers with a title that defines their entire relationship to the world: "Dearly beloved, I beseech you as strangers and pilgrims, abstain from fleshly lusts, which war against the soul" (1 Peter 2:11). The word "strangers" (parepidemos) refers to someone living in a foreign country — a resident alien who lives among a people but does not belong to them. The word "pilgrims" (paroikos) refers to a sojourner, a traveler passing through on the way to his true home. These two words together capture the dual reality of the Christian's existence: we are in this world but we are not of this world. We live among the citizens of Vanity Fair, but we hold passports from the Celestial City.

Hebrews 11:13-16 expands upon this pilgrim identity with profound theological depth: "These all died in faith, not having received the promises, but having seen them afar off, and were persuaded of them, and embraced them, and confessed that they were strangers and pilgrims on the earth. For they that say such things declare plainly that they seek a country. And truly, if they had been mindful of that country from whence they came out, they might have had opportunity to have returned. But now they desire a better country, that is, an heavenly: wherefore God is not ashamed to be called their God: for he hath prepared for them a city." The heroes of faith — Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and all the rest — lived as strangers and pilgrims because they were seeking a better country. They did not look backward to the land they had left; they looked forward to the heavenly city that God had prepared. And because of this forward-looking faith, "God is not ashamed to be called their God." This is one of the most remarkable statements in Scripture. The infinite, almighty Creator is not ashamed to be associated with wandering pilgrims who have nothing in this world — because they have everything in the world to come.

Philippians 3:20-21 makes the believer's heavenly citizenship explicit: "For our conversation is in heaven; from whence also we look for the Saviour, the Lord Jesus Christ: Who shall change our vile body, that it may be fashioned like unto his glorious body, according to the working whereby he is able even to subdue all things unto himself." The word "conversation" (politeuma) refers to citizenship — our commonwealth, our state, our true homeland is in heaven. This does not mean that earthly citizenship is meaningless or that Christians should disengage from civic life. But it does mean that our primary allegiance, our deepest loyalty, and our ultimate hope are directed toward heaven, not toward any earthly nation or institution.

The Puritan divine Richard Sibbes (1577-1635) addressed the pilgrim's relationship to the world with characteristic gentleness and wisdom in his work "The Soul's Conflict with Itself." Sibbes taught that the Christian's inner conflict between love for the world and love for Christ is not a sign of spiritual failure but a normal part of the pilgrim experience. He wrote that "grace does not take away nature but sanctifies it." The Christian still has natural desires for comfort, security, pleasure, and recognition — these desires are not sinful in themselves. What is sinful is allowing these desires to eclipse the love of Christ and become the ruling passions of the heart. Sibbes counseled believers to bring their worldly desires to Christ rather than trying to suppress them by sheer willpower. He wrote that "the heart is cured of its worldliness not by emptying it of desire but by filling it with a greater desire — the desire for Christ and His glory."

Consider the searching question posed by the apostle Paul in Galatians 1:10: "For do I now persuade men, or God? or do I seek to please men? for if I yet pleased men, I should not be the servant of Christ." The pilgrim must choose whom he will serve — the world or Christ. The desire to please men is one of the most subtle and powerful forms of worldliness. It does not look like obvious sin; it looks like politeness, accommodation, and social sensitivity. But when the desire to be accepted by the world leads a Christian to compromise his convictions, silence his testimony, or conform to ungodly standards, it becomes a betrayal of his pilgrim identity. The Puritans faced this temptation constantly — they were pressured by the established church, by the government, and by society to conform to practices they believed were unbiblical. Their refusal to conform cost them their livelihoods, their freedom, and sometimes their lives. But they chose to please God rather than men, and history has vindicated their faithfulness.

The prophet Elijah confronted this choice with dramatic directness in 1 Kings 18:21: "And Elijah came unto all the people, and said, How long halt ye between two opinions? if the LORD be God, follow him: but if Baal, then follow him." There is no middle ground between serving God and serving the world. The attempt to serve both — to maintain a Christian profession while pursuing worldly values — is what Elijah called "halting between two opinions." The word "halt" in Hebrew means to limp, to stagger, to waver. The double-minded person is spiritually crippled, unable to walk steadily in either direction. The remedy is a decisive commitment: if the LORD be God, follow Him with your whole heart, your whole soul, and your whole strength.

The Puritan pastor Thomas Adams (1583-1652), known as "the Shakespeare of Puritan theologians" for his vivid and eloquent prose, wrote powerfully about the deceptive nature of worldly pleasures. Adams compared the world to a painted sepulchre — beautiful on the outside but full of corruption within. He wrote that "the world promises more than it performs, and performs less than it promises." Every worldly pleasure carries within it the seed of disappointment. The excitement fades. The novelty wears off. The satisfaction proves temporary. And the soul that has fed on worldly pleasures finds itself hungrier than before — because only God can satisfy the deepest longings of the human heart. As Augustine famously prayed, "Thou hast made us for Thyself, and our hearts are restless until they find their rest in Thee."

Adams also addressed the specific danger of covetousness — the desire for more than God has appointed. He pointed to 1 Timothy 6:6-10: "But godliness with contentment is great gain. For we brought nothing into this world, and it is certain we can carry nothing out. And having food and raiment let us be therewith content. But they that will be rich fall into temptation and a snare, and into many foolish and hurtful lusts, which drown men in destruction and perdition. For the love of money is the root of all evil: which while some coveted after, they have erred from the faith, and pierced themselves through with many sorrows." The love of money is called "the root of all evil" — not because money itself is evil but because the love of money displaces the love of God and becomes the controlling passion of the soul. Those who covet riches "pierce themselves through with many sorrows." The very thing they seek for happiness becomes the instrument of their misery.

Second Corinthians 6:14-17 establishes the principle of separation from the world's spiritual values with unmistakable clarity: "Be ye not unequally yoked together with unbelievers: for what fellowship hath righteousness with unrighteousness? and what communion hath light with darkness? And what concord hath Christ with Belial? or what part hath he that believeth with an infidel? And what agreement hath the temple of God with idols? for ye are the temple of the living God; as God hath said, I will dwell in them, and walk in them; and I will be their God, and they shall be my people. Wherefore come out from among them, and be ye separate, saith the Lord, and touch not the unclean thing; and I will receive you." Paul asks five rhetorical questions, each expecting the answer "none." There is no fellowship between righteousness and unrighteousness, no communion between light and darkness, no concord between Christ and Satan, no partnership between a believer and an unbeliever, no agreement between God's temple and idols. The conclusion is inescapable: "Come out from among them, and be ye separate."

Practical Application — Walking as Pilgrims Through Vanity Fair:

1. Remind yourself daily that this world is not your home. You are a stranger and a pilgrim, passing through on the way to the Celestial City. Do not put down roots too deeply in a place you are only passing through. Hold earthly possessions, relationships, and comforts with an open hand, knowing that your true treasure is stored in heaven where it cannot be lost, stolen, or corrupted (Matthew 6:19-20).

2. Develop a sensitivity to the world's subtle influences on your thinking. The world's values are communicated not only through obvious temptations but through the assumptions, priorities, and narratives that pervade culture. Ask yourself regularly: Am I thinking like a citizen of heaven or a citizen of the world? Am I evaluating success, significance, and security by God's standards or by the world's standards?

3. Practice the discipline of simplicity. The Puritans lived simply not because they were joyless but because they were free. A life cluttered with possessions, commitments, and entertainments leaves little room for God. Simplify your life so that you have more time for prayer, more resources for generosity, and more energy for the things that matter eternally.

4. Be willing to be misunderstood by the world. When Christian and Faithful refused to buy anything at Vanity Fair, the merchants were bewildered and offended. Pilgrims will always seem strange to the citizens of Vanity Fair. Your refusal to pursue the world's priorities, your contentment with less, your commitment to moral standards that the world rejects — all of these will provoke confusion, mockery, and sometimes hostility. Accept this as part of the pilgrim's calling and rejoice that you are counted worthy to suffer reproach for the name of Christ (Acts 5:41).

5. Keep the Celestial City always in view. The pilgrim's power to resist the temptations of Vanity Fair comes not from moral willpower but from a superior vision. When you can see the Celestial City, the wares of Vanity Fair lose their luster. When you are captivated by the beauty of Christ, the baubles of the world seem cheap and tawdry. Fix your eyes on Jesus, the author and finisher of your faith, and the world will grow strangely dim in the light of His glory and grace.
