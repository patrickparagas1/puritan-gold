PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"Great Is the Lord, and Greatly to Be Praised"
June 24, 2026

---

"Great is the Lord, and greatly to be praised in the city of our God, in the mountain of his holiness. Beautiful for situation, the joy of the whole earth, is mount Zion, on the sides of the north, the city of the great King. God is known in her palaces for a refuge. For, lo, the kings were assembled, they passed by together. They saw it, and so they marvelled; they were troubled, and hasted away." -- Psalm 48:1-5

Mount Zion was never much to look at by the standards of the world's great cities. It had neither the architectural splendour of Babylon nor the military might of Nineveh nor the commercial wealth of Tyre. By any earthly measure, it was a modest hill in a small country on the eastern shore of the Mediterranean Sea. And yet the psalmist calls it "the joy of the whole earth," and he calls it this not because of anything that belonged to Zion itself, but because of the God who dwelt there. The greatness of the city was the greatness of its God, and the beauty of the place was the beauty of His presence.

Spurgeon observed that what Zion was to ancient Israel, the church of Jesus Christ is to the world today. The church may appear small and insignificant in the eyes of the mighty. The nations may despise it. The philosophers may mock it. The powerful may ignore it. But the church is the city of the great King, the dwelling place of the Almighty, the one institution on earth that has the promise of the gates of hell not prevailing against it. And its greatness, like Zion's, consists not in its outward appearance but in the presence of the God who inhabits it.

"God is known in her palaces for a refuge." This is the testimony of experience, not merely of theology. The people of Zion did not merely believe in theory that God was their refuge; they had proved it in practice. When enemies assembled against them, when armies surrounded them, when destruction seemed certain and inevitable, God had made Himself known as a very present help in trouble. And this experiential knowledge of God as refuge is worth infinitely more than any amount of theoretical belief. The man who has actually fled to God in the hour of danger and has found there a shelter that no storm can breach knows something about God that no book can teach him.

Thomas Goodwin wrote with great depth upon the experimental knowledge of God, distinguishing it from the merely notional knowledge that satisfies the intellect without transforming the heart. A man may have a perfectly orthodox theology, may affirm every article of the creed, may pass every doctrinal examination, and yet be utterly ignorant of God in the way that matters most. For to know God as a refuge is to have trusted Him in the crisis, to have cast oneself upon Him when every other support has failed, to have proved by personal experience that He is faithful and true and sufficient for every extremity.

"For, lo, the kings were assembled, they passed by together. They saw it, and so they marvelled; they were troubled, and hasted away." Here is a dramatic picture of the enemies of God confronting the reality of His presence and being utterly confounded. They came with confidence, these assembled kings. They marched with banners flying and trumpets sounding, expecting an easy conquest. But when they saw Zion -- not just the walls and the towers, but the invisible presence of God that defended them -- they were seized with astonishment and terror. Their confidence evaporated in an instant. Their armies melted away. They turned and fled like men who have seen a ghost, "troubled and hasting away" from the city they had come to destroy.

Richard Sibbes drew from this picture a powerful lesson about the ultimate futility of all opposition to the purposes of God. Let the kings assemble. Let the nations rage. Let the powers of darkness muster their forces and march against the city of God with all the weapons in their arsenal. They shall see, and they shall marvel, and they shall be troubled, and they shall haste away. For greater is He that is in us than he that is in the world, and the gates of hell shall not prevail against the church of the living God.

John Owen applied this psalm to the internal enemies of the soul as well as to external adversaries. The sins that assemble against us, the temptations that besiege us, the doubts that trouble us, the fears that threaten to overwhelm us -- these are the kings that come against Zion. But when God makes Himself known in the palaces of the soul as a refuge, when the believer turns to Christ and finds in Him an impregnable fortress, these enemies see, and marvel, and are troubled, and haste away. Sin cannot stand before the presence of the living God. Temptation cannot maintain its siege when Christ is in the citadel. The enemies of the soul, like the enemies of Zion, are scattered by the sheer majesty of the Divine Presence.

Let us therefore glory not in ourselves but in the God who dwells among us. Great is the Lord, and greatly to be praised. He is our refuge, proved in a thousand trials. He is our defence, vindicated in a thousand battles. And when the kings assemble against us, we shall not fear, for God is in the midst of us, and we shall not be moved. Amen.
