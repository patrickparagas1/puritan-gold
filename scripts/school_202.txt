Knowing God — His Attributes


Welcome to a new day of learning at the Puritan Academy. The Puritans believed that all truth is God's truth, and that the pursuit of knowledge is a form of worship. Let us worship Him through our studies today.

Heavenly Father, we dedicate this day of learning to You. We confess that apart from You we can understand nothing rightly. Illuminate our minds with the light of Your truth. Make us faithful students who study to show ourselves approved. In Christ's name. Amen.

Before we open our lesson books, let us sit quietly with a passage from God's Word.

Our Scripture for today is Psalm 103:19:

"The LORD hath prepared his throne in the heavens; and his kingdom ruleth over all."

Begin this day by resting in the absolute sovereignty of your God. Before you open your eyes to face the uncertainties of the day, remember that nothing will come to you today that has not first passed through the loving, wise, and sovereign hand of your heavenly Father. He has prepared His throne in the heavens, and His kingdom rules over all — including the details of your life today. Whatever challenges arise, whatever unexpected turns the day may take, God is not surprised, and God is not worried. His counsel shall stand. He will do all His pleasure. Let this truth settle deep into your soul and produce a calm, confident trust that cannot be shaken.

As we prepare for today's lesson, remember that everything we study flows from this one fountain: the Word of God. Our lesson today is part of Knowing God — His Attributes, and it is titled: God's Sovereignty & Our Trust. Let us proceed with minds engaged and hearts open.


With our hearts prepared, let us move into the substance of today's lesson.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes in Psalm 103:19.

The sovereignty of God is one of the most comforting and most challenging doctrines in all of Scripture. Psalm 103:19 declares with majestic simplicity, "The LORD hath prepared his throne in the heavens; and his kingdom ruleth over all." Notice the completeness of this statement: God's kingdom does not rule over some things, or most things, but over all things without exception. There is no corner of the universe, no event in history, no molecule in motion, and no thought in any creature's mind that falls outside the governing authority of Almighty God. His throne is "prepared" — established, fixed, immovable — in the heavens, from where He exercises absolute dominion over all that He has made.

Daniel 4:35 provides one of the most sweeping declarations of divine sovereignty in all of Scripture. This testimony comes not from a Hebrew prophet but from Nebuchadnezzar, the mighty king of Babylon, who had been humbled by God and driven to madness for seven years before his reason was restored. Having experienced firsthand the irresistible power of God, Nebuchadnezzar declared: "And all the inhabitants of the earth are reputed as nothing: and he doeth according to his will in the army of heaven, and among the inhabitants of the earth: and none can stay his hand, or say unto him, What doest thou?" This is a comprehensive statement of sovereignty. God acts according to His own will — not according to the will of creatures, not according to the demands of circumstances, but according to His own eternal, wise, and holy purpose. He does what He pleases, when He pleases, where He pleases, and with whom He pleases, and no creature in heaven or on earth can hinder Him or call Him to account.

The apostle Paul takes up this theme in Romans 9:19-21 in the context of God's sovereign election: "Thou wilt say then unto me, Why doth he yet find fault? For who hath resisted his will? Nay but, O man, who art thou that repliest against God? Shall the thing formed say to him that formed it, Why hast thou made me thus? Hath not the potter power over the clay, of the same lump to make one vessel unto honour, and another unto dishonour?" Paul does not shrink from the implications of God's sovereignty. He does not try to soften the doctrine or explain it away. Instead, he confronts the human objection head-on: Who are you, O creature, to question the Creator? The potter has absolute authority over the clay. He may shape it as He pleases, for purposes that He alone determines. This does not make God unjust — for as Paul has already established in Romans 9:14, "There is no unrighteousness with God." Rather, it reveals the vast distance between the infinite wisdom of God and the finite understanding of man.

Isaiah 46:9-10 takes us even deeper into the sovereignty of God by connecting it to His foreknowledge and His eternal purposes: "Remember the former things of old: for I am God, and there is none else; I am God, and there is none like me, Declaring the end from the beginning, and from ancient times the things that are not yet done, saying, My counsel shall stand, and I will do all my pleasure." God does not merely foresee the future — He determines it. His "counsel" — His deliberate, purposeful plan — shall stand. Not might stand, not probably will stand, but shall stand with absolute certainty. And He will do "all" His pleasure — not part of it, not most of it, but all of it, without any failure or frustration.

This sovereignty extends over what we call "natural" events. Psalm 135:6-7 declares, "Whatsoever the LORD pleased, that did he in heaven, and in earth, in the seas, and all deep places. He causeth the vapours to ascend from the ends of the earth; he maketh lightnings for the rain; he bringeth the wind out of his treasuries." God controls the weather. He directs the path of every storm, determines the fall of every raindrop, and sends the wind from His storehouses. Jesus affirmed this when He said that the Father "maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good, and sendeth rain on the just and on the unjust" (Matthew 5:45). There are no accidents in God's universe.

God's sovereignty also extends over the seemingly random events of life. Proverbs 16:33 teaches, "The lot is cast into the lap; but the whole disposing thereof is of the LORD." Even what appears to be pure chance — the casting of lots — is governed by the sovereign hand of God. This is why Joseph could say to his brothers who had sold him into slavery, "But as for you, ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save much people alive" (Genesis 50:20). The same event — Joseph's sale into Egypt — had two authors: the brothers who meant it for evil, and God who meant it for good. God's sovereignty does not eliminate human responsibility, but it does ensure that even the sinful actions of men serve His ultimate purposes.

The sovereignty of God over suffering is perhaps the most difficult aspect of this doctrine for believers to embrace. Yet Scripture is clear. God said to Moses, "Who hath made man's mouth? or who maketh the dumb, or deaf, or the seeing, or the blind? have not I the LORD?" (Exodus 4:11). The prophet Amos asked, "Shall there be evil in a city, and the LORD hath not done it?" (Amos 3:6). The word "evil" here refers not to moral evil but to calamity and disaster. God is sovereign even over the painful events of life. This does not make Him the author of sin — Scripture is equally clear that "God cannot be tempted with evil, neither tempteth he any man" (James 1:13). But it does mean that nothing happens outside His sovereign permission and overruling purpose.

The proper response to God's sovereignty is not fatalism but trust. Because God is sovereign, we know that nothing can happen to us that has not first passed through His wise and loving hands. Because God is sovereign, we know that His purposes cannot be thwarted, His promises cannot fail, and His people cannot be lost. Because God is sovereign, we can rest in the assurance that "all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose" (Romans 8:28). Sovereignty, rightly understood, is the pillow upon which the believer lays his weary head and finds perfect peace.

This biblical foundation is essential. Without it, everything else we study today would be like a house built on sand. But with it, we stand on solid rock.


Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. Thomas Watson wrote extensively on this subject in "A Body of Divinity." The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life.

Thomas Watson (c. 1620-1686) was one of the most gifted Puritan preachers and writers, known for his clarity, warmth, and practical application of deep theological truths. In his masterwork "A Body of Divinity," Watson devoted careful attention to the sovereignty of God, treating it not as a dry philosophical concept but as a living truth that should transform the believer's daily experience. Watson defined God's sovereignty as "His absolute right to do all things according to the good pleasure of His will." He emphasized that sovereignty is not merely power — for power without wisdom and goodness would be terrifying — but the exercise of infinite power directed by infinite wisdom and infinite love.

Watson was particularly skillful in showing how the sovereignty of God should be a source of comfort rather than anxiety for the believer. He observed that many Christians struggle with the doctrine of sovereignty because they imagine God as an arbitrary ruler who acts without reason or concern for His creatures. Watson corrected this misunderstanding by insisting that God's sovereignty is always exercised in harmony with His other attributes. God is sovereign, but He is also wise, just, good, and faithful. His sovereignty means that He always has the power to do what is best — and His wisdom, goodness, and faithfulness guarantee that He always will do what is best. Watson wrote: "God's sovereignty is not a blind, unreasoning power, but a wise and holy government. He rules all things, not as a tyrant, but as a Father."

Watson applied the sovereignty of God to the problem of suffering with remarkable pastoral sensitivity. He acknowledged that believers often struggle to understand why God permits affliction, pain, and loss. But he urged his hearers to trust the sovereign wisdom of God even when they could not trace it. Watson used the analogy of a physician who sometimes prescribes bitter medicine because he knows it will bring healing. The patient may not understand why the medicine must be bitter, but he trusts the physician's skill and goodness. In the same way, the believer may not understand why God permits suffering, but he trusts the sovereign wisdom and unfailing love of his heavenly Father. Watson wrote: "Where reason cannot wade, faith may swim. God's sovereignty should silence all our quarrels and composeth us into a sweet serenity of spirit."

Watson also addressed the relationship between God's sovereignty and human responsibility with characteristic balance. He rejected the idea that sovereignty makes human effort meaningless. On the contrary, he argued that because God is sovereign, our efforts are meaningful — for God has ordained not only the ends but also the means. God has decreed that His people shall be saved — but He has also decreed that they shall be saved through faith, repentance, and the hearing of the Word. God has decreed that crops shall grow — but He has also decreed that they shall grow through plowing, planting, and watering. Watson illustrated this with memorable directness: "God commands us to use means, and then He blesses those means. The God who ordained that we shall eat has also ordained that we shall work."

Stephen Charnock, whose work complemented Watson's in many ways, treated the sovereignty of God in connection with God's eternal decrees. Charnock taught that before the foundation of the world, God decreed everything that would come to pass — not in a general or vague way, but with infinite specificity and detail. Every event, every creature, every moment of history was foreordained by the sovereign will of God. Charnock was careful to distinguish between God's decretive will (what God has determined shall happen) and God's preceptive will (what God has commanded His creatures to do). These two aspects of God's will can appear to conflict — for example, God decreed that Christ should be crucified, but the men who crucified Him sinned in doing so (Acts 2:23). Yet Charnock insisted that there is no real conflict, for God's sovereign decree encompasses the free choices of His creatures without coercing or violating them.

Watson addressed a common objection to sovereignty — that it makes prayer unnecessary. If God has already determined what will happen, why pray? Watson's answer was both logical and pastoral. He explained that God has ordained prayer as one of the means through which He accomplishes His sovereign purposes. When we pray, we are not trying to change God's mind — we are aligning ourselves with God's will and participating in His sovereign plan. Prayer, Watson taught, is the means by which God brings His decree to pass. He ordained that Hezekiah should live fifteen years longer — but He also ordained that Isaiah should pray for his recovery (2 Kings 20). He ordained that Israel should be restored from exile — but He also ordained that Daniel should pray for that restoration (Daniel 9). Watson wrote: "Prayer is the key that opens God's treasures. God has already determined to give, but He has determined to give in answer to prayer."

Watson concluded his treatment of God's sovereignty with practical applications that remain relevant today. First, he urged believers to worship God with awe and reverence. A sovereign God is not to be treated casually or approached flippantly. Second, he urged submission to God's providential dealings. If God is sovereign over all events, then every circumstance of life — whether pleasant or painful — comes from His hand and serves His purpose. Third, he urged confidence in prayer. Because God is sovereign, He is able to answer every prayer and to overrule every obstacle. Fourth, he urged evangelistic boldness. Because God is sovereign over salvation, the success of the gospel does not depend on human eloquence or persuasion but on the sovereign power of the Holy Spirit. Watson wrote that the sovereignty of God should make us "bold as lions" in proclaiming the truth, knowing that God will use His Word to accomplish His purposes.

The Puritan understanding of sovereignty, as expressed by Watson and Charnock, was not cold, abstract theology but warm, living faith. These men saw the sovereignty of God as the wellspring of Christian confidence. Because God is in control, the believer need not fear the future. Because God's counsel shall stand, the believer can trust His promises without reservation. Because no creature can stay His hand, the believer can rest secure in His love. This is the sovereignty that undergirds all true Christian experience — a sovereignty that is not opposed to love but is the very foundation upon which divine love operates with unfailing power and purpose.

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.

Discussion Question 1: How does Nebuchadnezzar's testimony in Daniel 4:35 illustrate the absolute nature of God's sovereignty? Why is it significant that this testimony comes from a pagan king?

Discussion Question 2: Paul's analogy of the potter and the clay in Romans 9 can seem harsh. How does understanding God's other attributes (wisdom, love, goodness) help us receive this teaching rightly?

Discussion Question 3: Watson said that 'where reason cannot wade, faith may swim.' What does this mean practically when we face situations we cannot understand?

Discussion Question 4: How does the sovereignty of God relate to prayer? If God has already determined everything, why should we pray?

Discussion Question 5: How should the doctrine of God's sovereignty affect our response to suffering, disappointment, and unexpected changes in our lives?

Take time with each question. The Puritans valued what they called "holy conference," the practice of discussing spiritual truths together. Do not settle for quick, surface answers. Push deeper. Ask follow-up questions. Listen carefully to one another.

Application is where truth becomes life. It is not enough to know what Psalm 103:19 says. We must ask: how does this change the way I live today? How does this shape my thoughts, my words, my actions? The Puritans insisted that a truth that does not transform is a truth that has not been truly understood.


Let us press deeper into the doctrine of God's sovereignty by examining additional Scripture passages and the insights of other Puritan divines who devoted their lives to understanding this great truth.

Consider the remarkable declaration of Ephesians 1:11, where Paul describes believers as those "who have been predestinated according to the purpose of him who worketh all things after the counsel of his own will." The phrase "worketh all things" is breathtaking in its scope. God does not merely observe all things, or permit all things, or react to all things — He worketh all things. He is the active agent behind all that happens in His universe. And He does so "after the counsel of his own will" — not randomly, not impulsively, not reactively, but according to a deliberate, eternal, and perfectly wise plan. This is the sovereignty that the Puritans worshipped and the sovereignty that sustained them through the fiercest trials.

Psalm 115:3 states with magnificent simplicity: "But our God is in the heavens: he hath done whatsoever he hath pleased." This verse contrasts the living God with the dead idols described in the preceding verses, which "have mouths, but they speak not: eyes have they, but they see not" (Psalm 115:5). The idols of the nations are impotent — they can do nothing. But our God is in the heavens, elevated above all creation, and He does whatever He pleases. There is no obstacle that can hinder Him, no power that can resist Him, and no counsel that can thwart Him. His pleasure is the ultimate explanation for everything that exists and everything that occurs.

The sovereignty of God over the hearts of men is taught in Proverbs 21:1: "The king's heart is in the hand of the LORD, as the rivers of water: he turneth it whithersoever he will." If even the most powerful human rulers — kings who command armies and govern nations — have their hearts directed by God, then no creature operates independently of His sovereign government. God turned the heart of Cyrus to release the Jewish captives from Babylon (Ezra 1:1). God hardened the heart of Pharaoh to display His power in the Exodus (Romans 9:17-18). God moved the heart of Artaxerxes to grant Nehemiah permission to rebuild Jerusalem's walls (Nehemiah 2:8). In every case, human decisions that shaped the course of history were under the sovereign direction of Almighty God.

The Puritan theologian Jeremiah Burroughs (c. 1600-1646) wrote a masterful treatise called "The Rare Jewel of Christian Contentment" in which he applied the doctrine of God's sovereignty to the believer's daily experience. Burroughs defined contentment as "that sweet, inward, quiet, gracious frame of spirit, which freely submits to and delights in God's wise and fatherly disposal in every condition." For Burroughs, contentment was not passive resignation but active delight in the sovereign will of God. He taught that the contented Christian is the one who has so thoroughly embraced the sovereignty of God that he can say with Paul, "I have learned, in whatsoever state I am, therewith to be content" (Philippians 4:11).

Burroughs identified several marks of true Christian contentment. First, the contented soul does not merely endure God's providence but delights in it, recognizing that a sovereign and loving God has arranged every circumstance for the believer's good. Second, the contented soul does not need external comforts to maintain its peace, because its peace comes from within — from the indwelling Spirit and the promises of God. Third, the contented soul does not murmur or complain against God's dealings, because it trusts that God's wisdom infinitely surpasses its own understanding. Burroughs wrote that murmuring against God's providence is, in effect, claiming to be wiser than God — a form of blasphemy that the contented soul abhors.

John Flavel (c. 1627-1691), another Puritan pastor of remarkable warmth and insight, wrote "The Mystery of Providence," a work devoted entirely to tracing the sovereign hand of God in the daily experiences of the believer. Flavel urged Christians to keep a journal of God's providential dealings — recording the ways in which God answered prayer, guided decisions, protected from danger, and brought good out of seeming evil. Flavel taught that the more carefully a believer observes God's providence, the more clearly he sees the sovereign hand at work, and the more deeply his trust is established. Flavel wrote: "Some providences, like Hebrew letters, must be read backwards. What seems at the time to be a disaster may prove in retrospect to be one of God's greatest mercies."

Flavel also addressed the relationship between God's sovereignty and the problem of evil with notable sensitivity. He acknowledged that many of God's providential dealings are mysterious and that believers are not always given to understand the reasons behind their sufferings. But Flavel counseled patience and faith, pointing to Romans 11:33: "O the depth of the riches both of the wisdom and knowledge of God! how unsearchable are his judgments, and his ways past finding out!" Flavel taught that the inability to understand God's ways is not a deficiency in God but a limitation in man. The finite mind cannot comprehend the infinite wisdom. But what we cannot understand, we can trust — because the God whose ways are past finding out is the same God who loved us enough to give His Son for our redemption.

Consider also the testimony of the prophet Daniel, who declared in Daniel 2:20-21: "Blessed be the name of God for ever and ever: for wisdom and power are his: And he changeth the times and the seasons: he removeth kings, and setteth up kings: he giveth wisdom unto the wise, and knowledge to them that know understanding." Daniel saw God's sovereignty operating in the rise and fall of empires. Babylon, Persia, Greece, and Rome — the great kingdoms of the ancient world — rose and fell according to the sovereign decree of God. No political revolution, no military conquest, no economic upheaval happens apart from the governing hand of the Almighty. This truth gave Daniel courage to stand before pagan kings and to maintain his faithfulness even under threat of death. And it should give us courage to face whatever circumstances our sovereign God has appointed for us.

The sovereignty of God extends even to the timing of events. Ecclesiastes 3:1 declares, "To every thing there is a season, and a time to every purpose under the heaven." God not only determines what happens but when it happens. The incarnation of Christ occurred at exactly the right moment in history: "But when the fulness of the time was come, God sent forth his Son, made of a woman, made under the law" (Galatians 4:4). God waited until the Roman roads provided ease of travel, until the Greek language provided a universal medium of communication, until the Pax Romana provided relative peace across the empire. Every detail was sovereignly orchestrated so that the gospel could spread rapidly across the known world. God's timing is always perfect because God's sovereignty is always complete.


Before we transition to the afternoon activity, let us consider specific ways to apply the doctrine of God's sovereignty to daily life.

First, let the sovereignty of God calm your anxious heart. Jesus said in Matthew 6:25-26, "Therefore I say unto you, Take no thought for your life, what ye shall eat, or what ye shall drink; nor yet for your body, what ye shall put on. Is not the life more than meat, and the body than raiment? Behold the fowls of the air: for they sow not, neither do they reap, nor gather into barns; yet your heavenly Father feedeth them. Are ye not much better than they?" If God sovereignly feeds the sparrows and clothes the lilies of the field, will He not care for His own children? Anxiety is, at its root, a failure to trust the sovereignty of God. When worry knocks at the door of your heart, answer it with the truth that your heavenly Father knows what you need and has the sovereign power to provide it.

Second, let the sovereignty of God strengthen your prayers. Since God is sovereign, He is able to answer every prayer according to His perfect will. You never pray to a God who is unable to help. You never bring a request that is too difficult for Him. Jeremiah 32:17 declares, "Ah Lord GOD! behold, thou hast made the heaven and the earth by thy great power and stretched out arm, and there is nothing too hard for thee." Pray with confidence, knowing that the sovereign God who controls the universe is listening to your every word.

Third, let the sovereignty of God inform your response to disappointment. When plans fail, when expectations are not met, when doors close that you expected to be open, remember that God is working all things according to the counsel of His own will. What seems like a setback may be a sovereign redirection. What feels like a loss may be a divine protection from something worse. Watson counseled believers to "kiss the rod" of God's providence — meaning to receive even painful circumstances with submission and trust, knowing that a loving Father is behind every event.

Fourth, let the sovereignty of God fuel your evangelism. Some believers mistakenly think that God's sovereignty makes evangelism unnecessary. The opposite is true. Because God is sovereign, we can share the gospel with confidence, knowing that He has His elect among every nation and that His Word will not return void (Isaiah 55:11). Paul's reasoning in Romans 10:14-15 is instructive: "How then shall they call on him in whom they have not believed? and how shall they believe in him of whom they have not heard? and how shall they hear without a preacher?" God has sovereignly chosen to save sinners through the preaching of the gospel. Our task is to proclaim; His task is to convert. This frees us from the burden of thinking that the success of the gospel depends on our eloquence or persuasive ability. We sow the seed; God gives the increase (1 Corinthians 3:6).

Fifth, let the sovereignty of God anchor your hope in eternity. Because God is sovereign, His promises about the future are absolutely certain. Heaven is real. Christ is coming again. The dead will be raised. Justice will be done. Every tear will be wiped away. These are not mere hopes or wishes — they are sovereign decrees that will be fulfilled as surely as every other promise God has ever made. Let this certainty give you unshakeable hope in a world of uncertainty.


Learning by doing was central to Puritan education. Here is today's hands-on activity.

Students will create a 'Sovereignty Timeline' tracing how God's sovereign hand directed events in the life of Joseph (Genesis 37-50). On a large sheet of poster paper, students will draw a horizontal timeline and mark each major event: Joseph's dreams, his brothers' jealousy, being sold into slavery, Potiphar's house, false accusation, prison, interpreting dreams, rise to power, famine, brothers' arrival in Egypt, and the family reunion. For each event, students must write two entries: (1) what it looked like from a human perspective (often painful or unjust), and (2) what God was accomplishing through it from a sovereign perspective. The timeline should visually show how God wove even sinful human actions into His perfect plan, culminating in Joseph's declaration in Genesis 50:20.

Materials needed: Large poster paper or butcher paper (at least 3 feet long), colored markers, ruler, Bible for reference, sticky notes for adding observations

As you complete this activity, think about how it connects to what you studied in today's lesson. The goal is not just to do something, but to reinforce the truths you have been learning. The Puritans believed that education should engage the whole person: mind, heart, and hands.

Take your time with this activity. Quality matters more than speed. When you are finished, reflect on what you learned through the process of doing. Often we understand something far better after we have tried to put it into practice.


One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

Throughout history, powerful rulers have claimed absolute sovereignty over their domains. Study how the concept of 'divine right of kings' developed in European history and how rulers like Nebuchadnezzar, Alexander the Great, and various Roman emperors asserted their authority. Compare their temporary and limited sovereignty with the absolute and eternal sovereignty of God. Discuss how the Reformation's emphasis on God's sovereignty challenged political tyranny and contributed to the development of constitutional government.

Literature Connection:

The theme of sovereignty — who is truly in control — runs through much of the world's great literature. In Shakespeare's plays, characters often struggle against fate and providence. In John Milton's 'Paradise Lost,' the sovereignty of God over even the rebellion of Satan is a central theme. Examine how the literary tension between human free will and divine sovereignty creates compelling narrative and reflects deep theological truth.

Science Connection:

The natural world displays remarkable order and design that points to a sovereign Creator. The laws of physics — gravity, thermodynamics, electromagnetism — operate with perfect consistency across the universe. The precise values of fundamental constants (the gravitational constant, the speed of light, Planck's constant) are finely tuned to permit the existence of life. Study how these 'anthropic coincidences' reflect the sovereignty of a God who 'doeth according to his will' in every realm of creation.

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.


Let us now examine a dimension of divine sovereignty that demands careful attention: the relationship between God's sovereign rule and the believer's life of prayer, worship, and daily obedience. The Puritans devoted enormous intellectual and pastoral energy to this subject, and their insights remain indispensable for every serious student of Scripture.

The sovereignty of God over the spiritual life of the believer is powerfully taught in Philippians 2:12-13: "Wherefore, my beloved, as ye have always obeyed, not as in my presence only, but now much more in my absence, work out your own salvation with fear and trembling. For it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do of his good pleasure." This passage holds together two truths that seem, on the surface, to be in tension. On the one hand, believers are commanded to "work out" their salvation — to actively pursue holiness, obedience, and spiritual growth. On the other hand, it is God who is working in them "both to will and to do" — He supplies not only the power to obey but even the desire to obey. The Puritans resolved this apparent tension by teaching that God's sovereignty and human responsibility are not contradictory but complementary. God works in us so that we may work out what He has worked in. His sovereign grace does not eliminate our effort; it energizes and directs it.

The Puritan divine William Bridge (1600-1671) explored this dynamic in his pastoral work "A Lifting Up for the Downcast." Bridge taught that one of the primary ways God exercises His sovereignty in the believer's daily experience is through the means of grace — those appointed channels through which God communicates His blessings to His people. These means include the reading and preaching of Scripture, prayer, the sacraments of baptism and the Lord's Supper, and the fellowship of the saints. Bridge emphasized that God is not bound by these means — He can work without them if He chooses — but He has ordinarily appointed them as the instruments through which His sovereign purposes are accomplished in the lives of believers.

Bridge was particularly effective in addressing the pastoral problem of believers who neglected the means of grace because they misunderstood God's sovereignty. Some argued, "If God is sovereign, He will save me regardless of what I do. Why should I pray, read my Bible, or attend worship?" Bridge responded with characteristic directness: "God has decreed not only the end but also the means. He has decreed that His people shall grow in grace — but He has also decreed that they shall grow through the faithful use of the means of grace. To neglect the means while presuming upon the end is not faith but presumption."

Consider the remarkable prayer of King Solomon at the dedication of the temple in 1 Kings 8:27-30: "But will God indeed dwell on the earth? behold, the heaven and heaven of heavens cannot contain thee; how much less this house that I have builded? Yet have respect unto the prayer of thy servant, and to his supplication, O LORD my God, to hearken unto the cry and to the prayer, which thy servant prayeth before thee to day: That thine eyes may be open toward this house night and day, even toward the place of which thou hast said, My name shall be there: that thou mayest hearken unto the prayer which thy servant shall make toward this place. And hearken thou to the supplication of thy servant, and of thy people Israel, when they shall pray toward this place: and hear thou in heaven thy dwelling place: and when thou hearest, forgive." Solomon understood that the sovereign God who fills heaven and earth condescends to hear the prayers of His people. The God who needs nothing from us nevertheless invites us to bring everything to Him. Prayer does not change God's eternal plan, but it is the means through which God brings His eternal plan to fruition in the lives of His people.

The Puritan theologian John Howe (1630-1705), chaplain to Oliver Cromwell and later a leading Nonconformist minister, wrote "The Blessedness of the Righteous," in which he explored the sovereignty of God in relation to the believer's eternal happiness. Howe taught that the sovereign God has not only decreed the salvation of His people but has also decreed their progressive happiness — their growing enjoyment of His presence and His goodness throughout their earthly lives and into eternity. Howe wrote that "the blessedness of the righteous consists in the vision and fruition of God" — in knowing God and enjoying Him, which is precisely what the Westminster Shorter Catechism identifies as man's chief end.

Howe argued that the sovereignty of God should produce in believers a profound and settled peace. He pointed to Isaiah 26:3: "Thou wilt keep him in perfect peace, whose mind is stayed on thee: because he trusteth in thee." The Hebrew literally reads "peace, peace" — a doubling that indicates completeness and intensity. Perfect peace belongs to those whose minds are "stayed" — fixed, anchored, established — upon God. And the basis of this peace is trust in God's sovereign character. When we truly believe that God is in control, that His purposes cannot be thwarted, and that He is working all things for our good, the anxieties that plague the human heart are replaced by a deep, abiding tranquility.

Psalm 46:10 speaks directly to this truth: "Be still, and know that I am God: I will be exalted among the heathen, I will be exalted in the earth." The command to "be still" is not merely a call to physical quietness but a call to spiritual rest — to cease our frantic striving and anxious worrying and to rest in the knowledge that God is sovereign over all. The Puritans understood this "stillness" not as passivity but as active trust — the deliberate choice to lay down our burdens and entrust them to the sovereign care of our heavenly Father. As Watson wrote, "The sovereignty of God is the pillow upon which the Christian rests his head."

Consider also the sovereignty of God over the nations as declared in Psalm 22:28: "For the kingdom is the LORD's: and he is the governor among the nations." This truth sustained the Puritans through some of the most turbulent political events in English history — civil war, regicide, restoration, persecution. They believed that behind every political upheaval, every change of government, and every act of persecution stood the sovereign hand of God directing the course of history toward His appointed end. This belief did not make them passive in the face of political injustice — many Puritans were active in political life. But it gave them a perspective that transcended the anxieties of the moment. They knew that the kingdoms of this world would ultimately become the kingdoms of their Lord and of His Christ (Revelation 11:15).

The Puritan pastor Samuel Rutherford (c. 1600-1661), writing from his exile in Aberdeen, expressed the comfort of God's sovereignty with characteristic beauty. In his famous letters, Rutherford wrote that "the Lord's people have a back door out of every trouble" — meaning that God's sovereignty always provides a way through suffering and trial, even when no human escape seems possible. Rutherford endured banishment, the loss of his wife and children, and constant opposition for his faith. Yet he wrote from exile: "My Lord hath given me new lungs since I came to Aberdeen. Preaching Christ is the sweetest work on this side of heaven." Rutherford's experience demonstrates that the sovereignty of God is not merely a doctrine to be debated but a reality to be experienced — a living truth that sustains the soul through the deepest valleys of affliction.

Practical Application — Living Under God's Sovereign Rule:

1. Begin each morning by consciously submitting every aspect of your day to God's sovereign will. Pray as the Lord Jesus taught: "Thy will be done in earth, as it is in heaven" (Matthew 6:10). Surrender your plans, your desires, your fears, and your expectations to the One who "worketh all things after the counsel of his own will" (Ephesians 1:11). This daily act of surrender is the foundation of a peaceful, purposeful life.

2. When unexpected events disrupt your plans, resist the temptation to panic or complain. Instead, remind yourself that "the steps of a good man are ordered by the LORD: and he delighteth in his way" (Psalm 37:23). Every interruption, every closed door, every surprising turn of events is under the sovereign direction of your heavenly Father. Train yourself to respond to unexpected events with the question, "Lord, what are You doing in this?" rather than "Why is this happening to me?"

3. Practice the discipline of thanksgiving in all circumstances. First Thessalonians 5:18 commands, "In every thing give thanks: for this is the will of God in Christ Jesus concerning you." Thanksgiving is the natural response to sovereignty — when we truly believe that God is directing all things for our good, we can give thanks even in painful circumstances, not because the pain is pleasant, but because we trust the purposes of the One who permits it.

4. Refuse to worry about the future. Jesus Himself commanded, "Take therefore no thought for the morrow: for the morrow shall take thought for the things of itself" (Matthew 6:34). Worry is, at its root, a denial of God's sovereignty. It says, "I am not sure God can handle this." But the sovereign God who has controlled every detail of the past is equally in control of every detail of the future. Cast your cares upon Him, for He careth for you (1 Peter 5:7).

5. Let the sovereignty of God produce in you a spirit of bold, confident prayer. The Puritans prayed with extraordinary boldness precisely because they believed in a sovereign God who could do anything. Approach the throne of grace with confidence (Hebrews 4:16), knowing that the God who hears your prayers is the same God who governs the universe. No request is too great for His power, no situation too complex for His wisdom, and no need too small for His concern.
