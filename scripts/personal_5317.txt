PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Doctrine Deep-Dive, Day 17
"The Doctrine of Sanctification"
Progressive Holiness

---

Good evening, friend. Tonight we explore one of the most practical doctrines of the Christian faith: the doctrine of sanctification. If justification answers the question "How am I made right with God?" then sanctification answers the question "How do I grow in holiness?" The Puritans wrote more about sanctification than perhaps any other subject, because they believed that the whole purpose of salvation is to make people holy.

--- Scripture ---

Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is 1 Thessalonians 4:3 and 2 Corinthians 3:18:

"For this is the will of God, even your sanctification."

"But we all, with open face beholding as in a glass the glory of the Lord, are changed into the same image from glory to glory, even as by the Spirit of the Lord."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.

--- Teaching ---

The word "sanctification" means to be made holy, to be set apart for God. In systematic theology, it is carefully distinguished from justification. Justification is a legal declaration: God pronounces the sinner righteous on the basis of Christ's imputed righteousness. It happens once, completely, and irreversibly at the moment of faith. Sanctification, by contrast, is a transforming process: God makes the justified sinner progressively more righteous by the work of the Holy Spirit within him. It begins at regeneration and continues until the believer is glorified in the presence of Christ. Justification changes our standing before God. Sanctification changes our condition.

The Westminster Shorter Catechism defines sanctification as "the work of God's free grace, whereby we are renewed in the whole man after the image of God, and are enabled more and more to die unto sin, and live unto righteousness." Notice several things about this careful definition. First, sanctification is a work of God's free grace. It is not a human achievement. The Puritans were emphatic on this point. Just as the sinner cannot justify himself, he cannot sanctify himself. Holiness is not produced by human effort alone but by divine grace working through human effort. John Owen explains that the Holy Spirit is the principal agent of sanctification. He works in the believer's mind, enlightening it to understand the will of God. He works in the will, inclining it to obey. He works in the affections, directing them toward God and away from sin.

Second, sanctification involves the renewal of the whole man. Sin has corrupted every part of human nature: the mind, the will, the affections, the body, the conscience. Sanctification addresses every part. It is not merely the reformation of outward behavior but the transformation of inward nature. The mind is renewed to think God's thoughts. The will is renewed to choose God's ways. The affections are renewed to love what God loves and to hate what God hates.

Third, sanctification is progressive. The phrase "more and more" is crucial. The Puritans rejected the notion that a single experience of grace could produce instant and complete holiness. Sanctification is a lifelong process of dying to sin and living to righteousness. There are seasons of rapid growth and seasons of apparent stagnation. There are victories and painful defeats. But the overall trajectory of the true believer's life bends upward — toward greater conformity to the image of Christ.

Thomas Watson provides a helpful analysis of the means by which sanctification progresses. The first means is the Word of God. Jesus prayed, "Sanctify them through thy truth: thy word is truth" (John 17:17). The Scriptures are the instrument the Spirit uses to renew the mind and conform the heart to the will of God. The second means is prayer. Through prayer, the believer lays hold of the sanctifying grace of God, confessing his sins, exposing his weaknesses, and asking for the specific grace needed for specific battles. The third means is the sacraments, particularly the Lord's Supper, through which the believer feeds upon Christ by faith and receives fresh grace for the ongoing battle against sin. The fourth means is affliction. The Puritans recognized that God uses suffering as a refining fire. "He doth not afflict willingly" (Lamentations 3:33), but He afflicts purposefully, burning away the dross of worldliness and pride.

Walter Marshall, in his important work "The Gospel Mystery of Sanctification," makes a critical point the Puritans considered essential. Marshall argues that sanctification flows from union with Christ. The believer grows in holiness not by trying harder in his own strength but by abiding more deeply in Christ. "As the branch cannot bear fruit of itself, except it abide in the vine; no more can ye, except ye abide in me" (John 15:4). The fruit of holiness is produced not by the branch straining but by the branch remaining connected to the vine.

--- Application ---

First, examine whether you are using the means of grace God has appointed for your sanctification: Scripture, prayer, the Lord's Table, fellowship, and even the afflictions He sends. These are not optional extras; they are the channels through which the Spirit does His transforming work.

Second, remember that sanctification is God's work in you, not your self-improvement project. Fight sin with all your might — but fight in the power of the Spirit, not in the strength of your own resolve. Abide in Christ, and the fruit will come.

Third, be patient with yourself. Growth in holiness is real, but it is often slow. The oak tree does not reach its full height in a single season. Neither does the Christian reach full maturity overnight. Press on, and trust the Gardener.

--- Closing ---

Holy Spirit, we invite Thee to continue Thy transforming work in us. Renew our minds, redirect our affections, and strengthen our wills for obedience. We cannot make ourselves holy, but Thou canst. Work in us both to will and to do of Thy good pleasure. Through Christ, in whose image we are being formed, Amen.

Rest in His grace tonight, friend. The God who began the work will finish it.
