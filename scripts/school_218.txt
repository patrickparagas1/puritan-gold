Lesson 3: "The Shield of Faith — Standing Firm"
Spiritual Armor


Welcome, young scholars. Today we gather in the tradition of the great Puritan academies, where every subject was studied under the lordship of Christ. No knowledge is secular when it is received from the hand of God.

Our Father, the Puritans prayed before every lesson, and so do we. We ask for Your blessing on today's studies. Let the truths we learn take root in our hearts and produce fruit in our lives. We study not for our own glory, but for Yours. Amen.

Let us still our hearts with the Word of God before we proceed.

Our Scripture for today is Ephesians 6:16:

"Above all, taking the shield of faith, wherewith ye shall be able to quench all the fiery darts of the wicked."

Take up the shield of faith this morning. The enemy's fiery darts are already in flight — temptations, doubts, fears, accusations — and only faith can quench them. But your faith does not rest on your own strength; it rests on the promises of Almighty God, who cannot lie and who will not fail. Whatever dart comes your way today, there is a promise in God's Word that can extinguish it. Fear? 'I will never leave thee.' Guilt? 'The blood of Jesus cleanseth from all sin.' Doubt? 'I give unto them eternal life; they shall never perish.' Lift up the shield. Trust the Word. And watch the darts fall harmless at your feet.

As we prepare for today's lesson, remember that everything we study flows from this one fountain: the Word of God. Our lesson today is part of Spiritual Armor, and it is titled: The Shield of Faith — Standing Firm. Let us proceed with minds engaged and hearts open.


Having laid the foundation in prayer and Scripture, let us build upon it.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes in Ephesians 6:16.

The shield of faith occupies a position of special importance in Paul's description of the spiritual armor. The words "above all" can be translated "in addition to all" or "over all" — indicating that the shield is taken up together with all the other pieces of armor and provides an additional, comprehensive layer of protection. The Roman soldier's shield (the "thureos" or door-shaped shield) was a large, rectangular shield approximately four feet tall and two and a half feet wide, made of wood covered with leather. Before battle, the leather was soaked in water so that it could extinguish the flaming arrows that were one of the most feared weapons in ancient warfare. Paul's analogy is precise and vivid: the shield of faith quenches "all the fiery darts of the wicked."

The "fiery darts of the wicked" represent the attacks of Satan and his forces against the believer. These attacks take many forms: temptations to sin, blasphemous thoughts injected into the mind, feelings of despair and hopelessness, accusations of guilt and unworthiness, doubts about God's love and faithfulness, fears about the future, and any other assault designed to wound the soul and undermine the believer's spiritual vitality. These are called "fiery" because, like flaming arrows, they are designed not only to pierce but to burn — to inflict lasting damage that spreads beyond the initial point of impact.

Faith is the shield that quenches these darts. But what does it mean to take up the shield of faith? Hebrews 11:1 defines faith: "Now faith is the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen." Faith is not mere wishful thinking or positive feelings. It is "substance" — something real, solid, and reliable. It is "evidence" — the conviction based on the testimony of God's Word. Faith looks beyond what the eyes can see and grasps what God has promised. Faith trusts the character of God when circumstances seem to contradict His goodness. Faith holds firm to the promises of Scripture when feelings suggest they are not true.

Hebrews 11:1-6 provides a gallery of faith's heroes who wielded the shield successfully. Abel offered a sacrifice by faith. Enoch walked with God by faith. Noah built the ark by faith. Abraham left his homeland by faith. Sarah conceived a child by faith. Each of these men and women faced circumstances that made faith difficult — they could not see what they were trusting, they could not predict how God would fulfill His promises, and they often had to wait many years before the promises were realized. But their faith was rewarded, and their names stand in Scripture as witnesses to the power and reliability of trusting God.

First John 5:4 declares, "For whatsoever is born of God overcometh the world: and this is the victory that overcometh the world, even our faith." Faith is not merely a defensive weapon; it is the means of victory. The believer who fights with the shield of faith does not merely survive the enemy's attacks — he overcomes them. He comes through the battle not merely intact but triumphant. This is because faith connects the believer to the infinite resources of Almighty God. When we believe God's promises, we access God's power. When we trust God's character, we tap into God's strength. Faith is the hand that lays hold of omnipotence.

Romans 10:17 reveals how faith is obtained and strengthened: "So then faith cometh by hearing, and hearing by the word of God." Faith is not a human achievement but a divine gift, produced by the Word of God applied to the heart by the Holy Spirit. We do not generate faith by an act of will; we receive faith by hearing and believing God's Word. This means that the primary way to strengthen our shield of faith is to immerse ourselves in the Scriptures — reading them, studying them, meditating upon them, memorizing them, and listening to them faithfully preached. The believer who neglects the Word of God is a soldier who lets his shield rot and weaken.

The shield of faith is also effective because of its comprehensiveness. Paul says it can quench "all" the fiery darts — not some, not most, but all. There is no temptation, no accusation, no fear, no doubt, no attack of any kind that the shield of faith cannot handle. This is because faith connects us to an infinite God whose resources are inexhaustible and whose power is unlimited. No dart forged in hell is a match for the shield of a faith that rests on the promises of Almighty God. As Paul declared in Romans 8:37, "Nay, in all these things we are more than conquerors through him that loved us."

The practical exercise of the shield of faith involves a deliberate, conscious decision to trust God's Word over every contrary experience, feeling, or suggestion. When Satan whispers, "God has abandoned you," faith responds with Hebrews 13:5: "He hath said, I will never leave thee, nor forsake thee." When Satan says, "Your sins are too great to be forgiven," faith responds with 1 John 1:7: "The blood of Jesus Christ his Son cleanseth us from all sin." When fear says, "The future is hopeless," faith responds with Jeremiah 29:11: "For I know the thoughts that I think toward you, saith the LORD, thoughts of peace, and not of evil, to give you an expected end." This is the active, conscious, Word-based trust that constitutes the shield of faith in daily spiritual warfare.

This biblical foundation is essential. Without it, everything else we study today would be like a house built on sand. But with it, we stand on solid rock.


Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. William Gurnall wrote extensively on this subject in "The Christian in Complete Armour." The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life.

William Gurnall devoted more pages to the shield of faith than to any other single piece of armor in "The Christian in Complete Armour," reflecting his conviction that faith is the most important and most comprehensive weapon in the believer's arsenal. Gurnall wrote that the shield of faith is "the grace of graces" — the virtue that activates all other virtues and without which no other piece of armor can function effectively. The belt of truth requires faith to believe the truth. The breastplate of righteousness requires faith to receive imputed righteousness. The sword of the Spirit requires faith to wield the Word effectively. Faith is the master key that unlocks all the resources of God's grace.

Gurnall began by carefully analyzing the nature of the fiery darts that the shield must quench. He catalogued the different types of satanic attacks with the precision of a military strategist. There are darts of temptation — sudden, intense urges to sin that seem to come from nowhere. There are darts of accusation — overwhelming feelings of guilt and condemnation that paralyze the believer and make him feel that God could never accept or love him. There are darts of doubt — questions about the truth of Scripture, the reality of God, and the authenticity of one's own conversion. There are darts of fear — terror of the future, dread of death, anxiety about circumstances beyond our control. There are darts of discouragement — the sense that the Christian life is too hard, that the battle is too long, and that the effort is not worth the struggle.

Gurnall observed that these darts are "fiery" for a reason. Fire spreads. A single fiery dart, if not quickly quenched, can set the whole soul ablaze with sin, doubt, or despair. A small temptation, if entertained, grows into a consuming passion. A slight doubt, if nurtured, grows into a full-blown crisis of faith. A momentary fear, if indulged, grows into paralyzing anxiety. This is why the shield must be raised immediately, at the first sign of attack. Delay is dangerous. The believer who hesitates to exercise faith when the dart arrives is like a soldier who waits until the arrow is already embedded in his chest before raising his shield.

Gurnall devoted extensive attention to the specific promises of God that serve as the "water" on the shield — the truth that quenches the fire of Satan's attacks. Against temptation to sin, faith holds up the promise of 1 Corinthians 10:13: "God is faithful, who will not suffer you to be tempted above that ye are able; but will with the temptation also make a way to escape." Against accusations of guilt, faith holds up the promise of Romans 8:1: "There is therefore now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus." Against doubt, faith holds up the promise of John 10:28: "I give unto them eternal life; and they shall never perish." Against fear, faith holds up the promise of Isaiah 41:10: "Fear thou not; for I am with thee: be not dismayed; for I am thy God."

Gurnall was particularly effective in addressing the practical challenges of exercising faith during intense spiritual warfare. He acknowledged that there are times when faith feels weak, when the shield seems too heavy to lift, when the darts are coming too fast and too thick. In these moments, Gurnall counseled believers not to rely on the strength of their faith but on the object of their faith. "It is not the strength of your grip that saves you," he wrote, "but the strength of the rope you are holding. A weak faith in a strong Christ is infinitely more valuable than a strong faith in a weak christ." This pastoral insight has comforted countless believers who feared that their faith was too small to withstand the enemy's attacks.

Gurnall also addressed the corporate dimension of the shield of faith. In the Roman army, soldiers were trained to fight side by side, overlapping their shields to form an impenetrable wall. When one soldier's shield was weakened or when a gap appeared, his companions would close ranks to protect him. Gurnall applied this image to the church. Believers are not meant to fight alone. When one member's faith is weak, others should come alongside with encouragement, prayer, and fellowship. The church is a community of faith, and the shield of faith is strongest when believers stand together.

Gurnall concluded his treatment of the shield of faith with an appeal to believers to exercise faith actively and deliberately every day. He warned against the complacency that comes from prosperity and ease. When times are good, the temptation is to set the shield aside and rely on our own resources. But this is precisely when the enemy is most likely to attack, for he knows that an unshielded believer is an easy target. Gurnall urged constant vigilance, daily Scripture reading, fervent prayer, and regular fellowship as the means by which faith is kept strong and the shield remains in place. He wrote: "Faith is a grace that must be exercised daily, or it will grow weak. The shield that is left unused will rust and decay. Take it up every morning. Raise it against every dart. Trust the promises of God in every circumstance. And you will find that no weapon formed against you shall prosper.'

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.


True learning requires reflection. Let us discuss today's key ideas.

Discussion Question 1: Paul says the shield of faith can quench 'all' the fiery darts of the wicked. What gives faith this comprehensive power?

Discussion Question 2: Gurnall catalogued several types of 'fiery darts': temptation, accusation, doubt, fear, and discouragement. Which of these do you find most challenging to defend against?

Discussion Question 3: Gurnall said 'It is not the strength of your grip that saves you but the strength of the rope you are holding.' How does this distinction comfort believers whose faith feels weak?

Discussion Question 4: Romans 10:17 says faith comes by hearing the Word of God. What practical steps can you take to strengthen your shield of faith through greater engagement with Scripture?

Discussion Question 5: How does the corporate dimension of faith — believers standing together with overlapping shields — apply to the church today?

Take time with each question. The Puritans valued what they called "holy conference," the practice of discussing spiritual truths together. Do not settle for quick, surface answers. Push deeper. Ask follow-up questions. Listen carefully to one another.

Application is where truth becomes life. It is not enough to know what Ephesians 6:16 says. We must ask: how does this change the way I live today? How does this shape my thoughts, my words, my actions? The Puritans insisted that a truth that does not transform is a truth that has not been truly understood.


The afternoon brings us to our activity time. Let us engage our hands as well as our minds.

Students will create a 'Shield of Faith' study card system. Using index cards, students will write one biblical promise on each card and organize them by category: promises for temptation, promises for accusation/guilt, promises for doubt, promises for fear, and promises for discouragement. Each card should have the full KJV text of the promise on one side and a brief explanation of how to apply it during spiritual attack on the other. Students should create at least fifteen cards (three per category) and bind them together with a ring or keep them in a small pouch. This portable 'shield' can be carried and consulted whenever a specific spiritual attack occurs, providing immediate, Scripture-based defense.

Materials needed: Index cards (at least 15 per student), fine-tipped markers or pens, small ring binder or pouch for cards, Bibles, concordance for finding promises

As you complete this activity, think about how it connects to what you studied in today's lesson. The goal is not just to do something, but to reinforce the truths you have been learning. The Puritans believed that education should engage the whole person: mind, heart, and hands.

Take your time with this activity. Quality matters more than speed. When you are finished, reflect on what you learned through the process of doing. Often we understand something far better after we have tried to put it into practice.


One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

The development of defensive armor and shields throughout military history reflects humanity's ongoing efforts to protect against new threats. Study the evolution from the bronze shields of ancient Greece to the chain mail of medieval knights to the body armor of modern soldiers. Discuss how each development was a response to new weapons and tactics. Connect this to the spiritual warfare principle that the believer must continually update and strengthen his faith to meet new and evolving spiritual threats.

Literature Connection:

The shield is one of the most universal symbols in world literature and mythology. From Achilles' shield in Homer's Iliad (which depicted the entire cosmos) to Captain America's shield (which represents ideals of justice and sacrifice) to the Shield of Narnia (which bears Aslan's image), shields in literature often represent more than mere physical protection — they represent the values, beliefs, and commitments that define and protect a character's identity. Discuss how these literary shields illuminate the biblical concept of faith as spiritual protection.

Science Connection:

The science of materials and protective equipment provides practical analogies for understanding the shield of faith. Modern ballistic shields are tested under extreme conditions — they must withstand multiple impacts, extreme temperatures, and various types of projectiles. The testing process ensures that the shield will perform reliably when needed most. Similarly, biblical faith is tested through trials and difficulties (1 Peter 1:7), and these tests strengthen and prove its genuineness. Discuss how the scientific process of testing and validation parallels the spiritual process of faith development through trial.

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.


Let us now deepen our study of the shield of faith with additional Scripture and Puritan teaching that will strengthen our understanding and our confidence in spiritual warfare.

The nature of saving faith is one of the most important subjects in all of theology, and the Puritans explored it with extraordinary depth and precision. The Westminster Shorter Catechism, Question 86, asks: "What is faith in Jesus Christ?" The answer declares: "Faith in Jesus Christ is a saving grace, whereby we receive and rest upon him alone for salvation, as he is offered to us in the gospel." Notice the two verbs: receive and rest. Faith receives Christ — it actively embraces Him, welcomes Him, takes hold of Him as the only Savior. And faith rests upon Christ — it trusts Him, relies upon Him, leans the full weight of the soul upon His promises and His finished work. The shield of faith is this receiving and resting applied to every spiritual attack. When a fiery dart comes, faith receives the promise of God that addresses that specific attack, and faith rests upon that promise with the full confidence that God will fulfill it.

Thomas Watson explored the nature of faith with his characteristic blend of theological precision and pastoral warmth. In "A Body of Divinity," Watson identified three essential components of saving faith. First, there is knowledge — faith is not blind; it is based upon the truth of God's Word. You cannot believe what you do not know. Second, there is assent — faith agrees that what God says is true. The mind acknowledges the reality of what Scripture teaches. Third, there is trust — faith personally relies upon Christ and His promises for salvation and daily sustenance. Watson wrote: "Knowledge without assent is dead speculation. Assent without trust is mere opinion. But knowledge, assent, and trust together constitute the living faith that takes up the shield and quenches the fiery darts of the enemy." Watson was particularly insistent that faith is not merely intellectual — it is personal and relational. It is not enough to know about Christ; we must know Christ. It is not enough to believe that Christ can save; we must trust that Christ will save us.

John Owen addressed the relationship between faith and spiritual warfare with his characteristic thoroughness in "The Grace and Duty of Being Spiritually Minded." Owen taught that the great enemy of faith is not external opposition but internal distraction. Satan's most effective strategy is often not a frontal assault but a slow, steady erosion of the believer's spiritual focus. He fills the mind with worldly concerns, earthly anxieties, trivial entertainments, and passing pleasures until the things of God are crowded to the margins of consciousness. Owen called this condition "spiritual deadness" — a state in which the believer is technically alive in Christ but functionally asleep, unable to raise the shield of faith because his hands are full of lesser things. Owen wrote: "The great contest between the flesh and the spirit is fought on the battlefield of the mind. The flesh labors to fill the mind with earthly things. The Spirit labors to fill the mind with heavenly things. And whichever prevails determines whether the shield of faith is raised or abandoned." Owen's remedy was the deliberate, disciplined cultivation of heavenly-mindedness — the practice of intentionally directing the mind toward God, Christ, heaven, and eternal realities throughout the day.

Consider the remarkable example of Abraham, whom the apostle Paul holds up as the supreme model of faith in Romans 4:18-21: "Who against hope believed in hope, that he might become the father of many nations, according to that which was spoken, So shall thy seed be. And being not weak in faith, he considered not his own body now dead, when he was about an hundred years old, neither yet the deadness of Sarah's womb: He staggered not at the promise of God through unbelief; but was strong in faith, giving glory to God; And being fully persuaded that, what he had promised, he was able also to perform." Abraham's faith was tested to the uttermost. Every natural circumstance argued against the fulfillment of God's promise. His body was old. Sarah's womb was dead. The years had passed without fulfillment. Yet Abraham "staggered not" — he did not waver, did not vacillate, did not go back and forth between belief and doubt. He was "fully persuaded" that God would do what He had promised. This is the shield of faith at its strongest — a settled, unwavering, unshakeable confidence in the character and promises of God, maintained in the face of every contrary circumstance.

Richard Sibbes, in "The Soul's Conflict," explored the experience of believers who struggle with weak faith. Sibbes was one of the most compassionate pastors among the Puritans, and he wrote with special tenderness for those whose faith was small, fragile, and frequently overwhelmed by doubt. Sibbes taught that even the weakest faith, if it is genuine, is sufficient to quench the fiery darts of the enemy. He used the analogy of a hand grasping a rope: "It is not the strength of your hand that holds you from falling, but the strength of the rope. A trembling hand that clings to a strong rope is safer than a confident hand that clings to a rotten one. And your faith, however weak and trembling, clings to the strongest rope in the universe — the promises of the eternal, unchanging, Almighty God." Sibbes also encouraged weak believers by reminding them that Christ does not break the bruised reed or quench the smoking flax (Isaiah 42:3). The smallest spark of genuine faith is precious to Christ, and He will tenderly fan it into a flame rather than snuff it out.

Sibbes distinguished between the strength of faith and the genuineness of faith. A believer may have strong faith — bold, confident, unwavering — or weak faith — timid, trembling, frequently assaulted by doubt. But the question that matters is not how strong the faith is but whether the faith is real. Does it look to Christ? Does it rest on His promises? Does it, however feebly, cling to the Savior? If so, it is saving faith, and it will quench every fiery dart, because its power lies not in itself but in the Christ it clings to. Sibbes wrote: "A weak hand can receive a rich gift. A weak faith can receive a great Savior. Do not despise the day of small things. Do not cast away your confidence because it feels small. Small faith in a great God is infinitely more powerful than great confidence in a small god."

Thomas Brooks, in "Precious Remedies against Satan's Devices," catalogued the specific lies that Satan uses to undermine faith and provided specific scriptural antidotes for each one. When Satan says, "God does not hear your prayers," faith responds with Psalm 34:17: "The righteous cry, and the LORD heareth, and delivereth them out of all their troubles." When Satan says, "Your suffering proves that God does not love you," faith responds with Hebrews 12:6: "For whom the Lord loveth he chasteneth, and scourgeth every son whom he receiveth." When Satan says, "You will never overcome this sin," faith responds with Romans 6:14: "For sin shall not have dominion over you: for ye are not under the law, but under grace." When Satan says, "You are alone in your struggle," faith responds with Hebrews 13:5: "I will never leave thee, nor forsake thee." Brooks' method demonstrates that the shield of faith is not a vague, formless trust in God's goodness but a specific, targeted, Scripture-armed response to specific attacks. Each dart has a corresponding promise that quenches it. The believer who knows these promises and uses them deliberately is wielding the shield with maximum effectiveness.

The apostle Peter provides crucial instruction on the relationship between faith and trials in 1 Peter 1:6-7: "Wherein ye greatly rejoice, though now for a season, if need be, ye are in heaviness through manifold temptations: That the trial of your faith, being much more precious than of gold that perisheth, though it be tried with fire, might be found unto praise and honour and glory at the appearing of Jesus Christ." Peter teaches that trials are not obstacles to faith but instruments for refining faith. Just as fire purifies gold by burning away impurities, trials purify faith by burning away its impurities — self-reliance, presumption, spiritual complacency, superficial trust. The faith that emerges from the furnace of trial is stronger, purer, and more precious than the faith that entered it. The Puritans took great comfort from this truth. They understood that the fiery darts of the enemy, far from destroying their faith, actually served to strengthen it. Every attack that was met with a promise of God and survived by the grace of God made the shield stronger and the believer more confident.

Jonathan Edwards preached on the nature of true faith in his sermon "A Divine and Supernatural Light." Edwards taught that genuine faith is not merely a human decision to believe but a divine work of the Holy Spirit in the heart that opens the eyes to see the beauty, the excellency, and the truth of the gospel. Edwards wrote: "There is a difference between having a rational judgment that honey is sweet, and having a sense of its sweetness. The one is a matter of the head; the other is a matter of the heart. True faith is not merely a rational conviction that the gospel is true; it is a spiritual sense of the sweetness, the beauty, the excellency of the gospel — a sense that is produced by the Holy Spirit and that transforms the entire orientation of the soul." This spiritual sense is what makes the shield of faith effective. It is not merely an intellectual decision to believe God's promises; it is a heart-deep, Spirit-wrought conviction that God's promises are beautiful, excellent, and worthy of absolute trust.

Consider also the words of the Lord Jesus to His disciples during the storm on the Sea of Galilee. The disciples were terrified as the waves crashed over the boat and the wind howled around them. Jesus was asleep in the stern. They woke Him in a panic: "Master, carest thou not that we perish?" (Mark 4:38). Jesus arose, rebuked the wind, and spoke to the sea: "Peace, be still" (Mark 4:39). And then He turned to His disciples with a question that cuts to the heart of the shield of faith: "Why are ye so fearful? how is it that ye have no faith?" (Mark 4:40). The disciples had faith enough to call upon Jesus in their distress. But they did not have faith enough to rest in His presence before the crisis was resolved. They needed to learn that the presence of Christ in the boat was sufficient protection, regardless of the violence of the storm. The shield of faith is the settled conviction that Christ is in the boat — that He is present, that He is powerful, and that no storm, however fierce, can prevail against a soul that is sheltered by His sovereign care.

Samuel Rutherford, writing from his exile in Aberdeen, expressed the triumph of faith over circumstances with incomparable beauty. Rutherford had lost everything — his family, his pastorate, his freedom, his health. Yet he wrote: "I have tasted more of Christ in my exile than I ever tasted in my prosperity. The fiery darts flew thick, but the shield held firm. And every dart that was quenched became a fresh occasion for praise, for it proved once more that the promises of my God are true and His faithfulness endures forever." Rutherford's testimony reminds us that the shield of faith is not tested in the classroom but in the crucible. It is not proven by theory but by experience. And every believer who has passed through the fire with the shield of faith in hand can testify that God is faithful, that His promises are true, and that no fiery dart — not one — can ultimately harm the soul that trusts in Christ.

William Bridge, the Puritan pastor and member of the Westminster Assembly, wrote a valuable work entitled "A Lifting Up for the Downcast" that directly addresses the relationship between faith and discouragement. Bridge observed that discouragement is one of Satan's most effective fiery darts because it attacks the believer's motivation to continue fighting. The discouraged believer is tempted to put down the shield, abandon the battle, and simply give up. Bridge counseled believers to remember that discouragement is a temptation, not a reality. The feelings of hopelessness and futility are lies of the enemy, not reflections of the truth. Bridge wrote: "When you are downcast and your faith seems unable to rise, remember that your condition is better than your feelings suggest. You are a child of God, purchased by the blood of Christ, indwelt by the Holy Spirit, and destined for glory. These realities do not change because your feelings change. Lift up the shield. Recite the promises. And in due time, the discouragement will pass, and your faith will shine brighter for having been tested."

Jeremiah Burroughs contributed to our understanding of the shield of faith through his teaching on Christian contentment. In "The Rare Jewel of Christian Contentment," Burroughs argued that faith produces contentment because faith trusts in the sovereign goodness of God in every circumstance. The believer who truly believes that God is sovereign over all things and that He works all things together for good (Romans 8:28) can face any situation with settled peace. Burroughs wrote: "Christian contentment is the direct fruit of faith. The contented soul believes that God has ordered every circumstance of life with infinite wisdom and infinite love. It does not murmur against God's providences or wish for a different lot. It takes up the shield of faith and says, 'The LORD gave, and the LORD hath taken away; blessed be the name of the LORD' (Job 1:21)."

Let us also consider the practical discipline of strengthening the shield of faith through what the Puritans called "the means of grace." The means of grace are the channels through which God strengthens and sustains the faith of His people. They include the reading and preaching of Scripture, prayer both private and corporate, the sacraments of baptism and the Lord's Supper, Christian fellowship, and the singing of psalms and hymns. The Puritans were insistent that faith is not strengthened by mystical experiences or extraordinary revelations but by the faithful, consistent, disciplined use of these ordinary means. Owen wrote: "He that would have a strong faith must be a diligent user of the means of grace. There is no shortcut to spiritual strength. The believer who neglects the Word, prayer, and fellowship will find his faith growing weak, his shield becoming heavy, and his arms unable to lift it when the darts begin to fly." This is profoundly practical counsel for every believer. If you want a strong shield, use the means. Read the Word daily. Pray without ceasing. Gather regularly with the people of God. Come to the Lord's Table with grateful hearts. And you will find that your faith grows stronger with every passing day, and the shield becomes easier to wield with every battle.


Let us now draw out the practical implications of the shield of faith for our daily lives.

First, the shield of faith must be taken up deliberately each morning. The Puritans began every day by consciously committing themselves to the care and keeping of God. They reviewed His promises, reminded themselves of His faithfulness, and asked for the strength of faith to face whatever the day might bring. This deliberate, morning exercise of faith is not automatic — it requires intentional effort and discipline. Just as a soldier does not accidentally find himself armored for battle, the believer does not accidentally find himself girded with faith. He must take up the shield on purpose, every single day.

Second, the shield of faith must be exercised in specific situations. When a specific temptation arises, a specific promise must be applied. When a specific fear threatens, a specific assurance must be claimed. The believer who has memorized the promises of God for various situations is like a soldier who has practiced with his shield against various types of attack — he knows instinctively how to position his defense against each specific threat. This is why Scripture memorization is so vital. The promises you have stored in your heart are the ammunition of the shield of faith, ready for immediate deployment in the moment of attack.

Third, the shield of faith must be maintained through regular fellowship. No soldier fights alone. The Roman army's greatest strength was its discipline and teamwork — soldiers fighting side by side, shields overlapping, each man protecting his neighbor as well as himself. The church is God's army, and the shield of faith is strongest when believers stand together. When your faith is weak, let others strengthen you with their faith. When another's faith is wavering, come alongside with encouragement, prayer, and the promises of God. This mutual strengthening is one of the primary purposes of Christian fellowship, and the believer who isolates himself from the body of Christ is a soldier who has stepped out of formation and left himself dangerously exposed.

Memory Verse:

"Above all, taking the shield of faith, wherewith ye shall be able to quench all the fiery darts of the wicked." -- Ephesians 6:16

Read this verse aloud five times. Then close your eyes and try to recite it from memory. Write it out by hand on a card or in your journal. The Puritans memorized vast portions of Scripture, and it was one of the great sources of their spiritual strength. Even one verse, truly hidden in the heart, can sustain you in times of trial.

Extended Reflection Questions:

Extended Question 1: Abraham "staggered not at the promise of God through unbelief" (Romans 4:20), even when every natural circumstance contradicted the promise. Describe a situation in your own life — or in the life of a biblical character — where faith had to be maintained against seemingly impossible odds. What sustained that faith through the trial?

Extended Question 2: Richard Sibbes taught that "a weak hand can receive a rich gift" and that weak faith in a great God is more powerful than strong confidence in a small god. How does this truth comfort you when your faith feels inadequate? How does it change the way you think about the strength of your own faith?

Extended Question 3: Jonathan Edwards described true faith as "a spiritual sense of the sweetness, the beauty, the excellency of the gospel." How is this different from merely believing that the gospel is factually true? What role does the Holy Spirit play in producing this kind of faith?

Extended Question 4: The disciples on the Sea of Galilee had faith enough to call upon Jesus but not enough to rest in His presence during the storm. In what ways do you call upon Christ in crisis but fail to rest in His presence before the crisis is resolved? What would it look like to develop a more settled, restful faith?

Extended Question 5: Samuel Rutherford testified that he "tasted more of Christ in exile than in prosperity." How can suffering and trial actually deepen our faith and make the shield stronger? Why do Christians who have endured the most severe trials often display the strongest faith?

Additional Memory Verse:

"For whatsoever is born of God overcometh the world: and this is the victory that overcometh the world, even our faith." -- 1 John 5:4

Closing Reflection:

As we conclude today's study, let us hear once more the words of our Lord: "Have faith in God" (Mark 11:22). This is both a command and an invitation. It is a command because faith is not optional for the Christian — it is the essential weapon without which no other piece of armor can function. And it is an invitation because the God in whom we are called to trust is infinitely worthy of our trust — He is sovereign over all things, He is faithful to all His promises, He is powerful to accomplish all His purposes, and He is loving toward all His children. The shield of faith rests not on the strength of the one who holds it but on the strength of the One it trusts. And that One is the Almighty God, the Maker of heaven and earth, the Redeemer of His people, and the Conqueror of every foe. Take up the shield, student of God's truth. Raise it high against every fiery dart. And know that in the strength of your God, not one dart will reach your heart.

That brings us to the end of today's study. The Puritans would close their school day with a prayer of thanksgiving. Let us do the same. Thank You, Lord, for the gift of learning. May we be faithful stewards of what we have received. Amen.
