PURITAN GOLD — Episode 1
"The Beginning of Wisdom"
Week 1, Day 1 (Monday) — Proverbs 1

---

"The fear of the Lord is the beginning of knowledge: but fools despise wisdom and instruction."

That's Proverbs chapter one, verse seven. And friend... if there is one verse in all of Scripture that could serve as a doorway into the book of Proverbs, it's that one. It's the thesis statement. It's the foundation stone. Everything else that Solomon writes in these thirty-one chapters flows from that single, magnificent truth.

So let's begin there. Let's begin at the beginning.

This is Puritan Gold, and over these next seven days, we're going to walk slowly and carefully through the opening chapters of Proverbs — chapters one through four — and we're going to sit alongside one of the great preachers of the Christian faith, Charles Haddon Spurgeon, and let his wisdom illuminate these ancient words for us.

But today... today is about beginnings. And what a place to begin.

Let me read to you the opening of Proverbs chapter one, verses one through seven:

"The proverbs of Solomon the son of David, king of Israel; To know wisdom and instruction; to perceive the words of understanding; To receive the instruction of wisdom, justice, and judgment, and equity; To give subtilty to the simple, to the young man knowledge and discretion. A wise man will hear, and will increase learning; and a man of understanding shall attain unto wise counsels: To understand a proverb, and the interpretation; the words of the wise, and their dark sayings. The fear of the Lord is the beginning of knowledge: but fools despise wisdom and instruction."

Now... I want you to notice something. Solomon doesn't begin with rules. He doesn't begin with a list of dos and don'ts. He begins with a purpose statement. He tells us why he's writing. And his purpose is beautifully layered — wisdom, instruction, understanding, justice, judgment, equity, discretion. He's painting a picture of the whole person. Not just the mind... but the character. Not just what you know... but who you are becoming.

And then he gives us the key that unlocks it all: "The fear of the Lord is the beginning of knowledge."

Now let's slow down here, because this phrase — "the fear of the Lord" — is one of the most misunderstood concepts in all of Scripture. And if we get this wrong at the start, everything else will be built on a crooked foundation.

What does it mean to fear the Lord?

Some people hear that word "fear" and they think of terror. They think of cowering. They think of a child hiding from an angry parent. And friend, that is not what Solomon means. Not at all.

The Hebrew word here is "yirah," and it carries the idea of reverential awe. It's the kind of fear you might feel standing at the edge of the Grand Canyon — not terror that the canyon will hurt you, but an overwhelming awareness of something so vast, so magnificent, so far beyond your comprehension... that you can only stand in wonder.

Charles Spurgeon put it beautifully. In his writings, Spurgeon often returned to this theme of reverential awe before God. He understood that true fear of the Lord is not about running away from God... but about being drawn toward Him in humble amazement. Spurgeon taught that when a soul truly perceives the holiness, the majesty, the infinite grandeur of the Almighty... there is a trembling — yes — but it is a trembling mixed with love. It's the trembling of a bride, not the trembling of a prisoner.

Think about that for a moment.

The fear of the Lord is the response of a created being who suddenly realizes — truly realizes — who God is. It's what Isaiah felt when he saw the Lord high and lifted up and cried out, "Woe is me! for I am undone." It's what Peter felt when, after the miraculous catch of fish, he fell at Jesus' knees and said, "Depart from me; for I am a sinful man, O Lord."

It's not that they wanted to get away from God. It's that the presence of God revealed to them something about themselves — their smallness, their sinfulness, their utter dependence — and the only appropriate response was awe.

And Solomon says... that's where knowledge begins.

Not with textbooks. Not with degrees. Not with intellectual achievement. Knowledge — true knowledge, the kind that shapes a life and builds a character and leads a person safely through this dangerous world — that knowledge begins on your knees.

Let me read on. Proverbs 1, verses 8 and 9:

"My son, hear the instruction of thy father, and forsake not the law of thy mother: For they shall be an ornament of grace unto thy head, and chains about thy neck."

Solomon shifts here to the language of family. And this is important, because the Proverbs are written as a father's instruction to his son. It's intimate. It's personal. It's not a lecture delivered to a crowd... it's counsel whispered across a dinner table.

And I want you to hear that tone as we walk through these chapters together. This is not God wagging His finger at you from a distance. This is your Father leaning in close and saying, "My child... listen. I have something to tell you that will save your life."

That's the heart of Proverbs. That's the heart of God.

Now, as we continue through chapter one, Solomon introduces something sobering. He warns his son about the enticement of sinners. Let me read verses 10 through 15:

"My son, if sinners entice thee, consent thou not. If they say, Come with us, let us lay wait for blood, let us lurk privily for the innocent without cause: Let us swallow them up alive as the grave; and whole, as those that go down into the pit: We shall find all precious substance, we shall fill our houses with spoil: Cast in thy lot among us; let us all have one purse: My son, walk not thou in the way with them; refrain thy foot from their path."

Now, this might sound dramatic to modern ears. Laying wait for blood? Lurking for the innocent? But Solomon is giving us the end of the road, not just the first step. He's showing us where the path of folly ultimately leads. And the truth is... the enticement of sin rarely begins with something obviously evil. It begins with something that sounds reasonable. Even attractive.

"Cast in thy lot among us. Let us all have one purse." In other words... join us. Be part of the group. Share in what we have. It sounds like community. It sounds like belonging. And that's exactly what makes it so dangerous.

Spurgeon, in his Lectures to My Students, warned young ministers about the subtle drift that can happen when we seek the approval of the world more than the approval of God. He understood that the pull toward compromise rarely feels like compromise in the moment. It feels like being practical. It feels like being relevant. It feels like wisdom.

But true wisdom — the wisdom that begins with the fear of the Lord — has the courage to say no. Even when it costs you something. Even when it costs you everything.

And then Solomon gives us one of the most striking images in all of Proverbs. Beginning in verse 20:

"Wisdom crieth without; she uttereth her voice in the streets: She crieth in the chief place of concourse, in the openings of the gates: in the city she uttereth her words, saying, How long, ye simple ones, will ye love simplicity? and the scorners delight in their scorning, and fools hate knowledge?"

Do you see what's happening here? Wisdom is personified as a woman — and she's not hiding. She's not locked away in some ivory tower, available only to the elite and the educated. She's in the streets. She's at the gates. She's crying out to everyone who will listen.

And her cry is urgent: "How long?"

How long will you love simplicity? How long will you prefer the easy path over the right path? How long will you reject the instruction that could save you?

There's a heartbreak in Wisdom's voice here. It's the heartbreak of a parent watching a child walk toward a cliff. The warning has been given. The truth has been spoken. And still... the child walks on.

Verses 24 through 28 are among the most sobering in all of Scripture:

"Because I have called, and ye refused; I have stretched out my hand, and no man regarded; But ye have set at nought all my counsel, and would none of my reproof: I also will laugh at your calamity; I will mock when your fear cometh; When your fear cometh as desolation, and your destruction cometh as a whirlwind; when distress and anguish cometh upon you. Then shall they call upon me, but I will not answer; they shall seek me early, but they shall not find me."

Now... these are hard words. And I don't want to soften them, because Solomon didn't intend for them to be soft. He intended for them to stop you in your tracks. He intended for them to make you say, "I will listen. I will pay attention. I will not ignore the voice of wisdom when she calls."

But here's the grace in it, friend. Here's the mercy. Wisdom is still calling. Right now. Today. In this very moment, as you listen to these words... Wisdom — God's wisdom — is reaching out to you and saying, "Turn. Come. Listen. It's not too late."

Spurgeon often preached with that same urgency. He would plead with his congregation — sometimes with tears — to not delay, to not put off responding to the call of God. He knew, as Solomon knew, that there is a window of opportunity... and the wise person is the one who responds while that window is open.

And then... the chapter closes with this promise. Verse 33:

"But whoso hearkeneth unto me shall dwell safely, and shall be quiet from fear of evil."

What a contrast. On one side, the fool who ignores wisdom and reaps the whirlwind. On the other side, the wise person who listens... and dwells safely. Quietly. Without the fear of evil.

Not without trouble — let's be clear about that. The book of Proverbs doesn't promise us a life without difficulty. But it promises us something better than ease. It promises us security. The security that comes from being rooted in the fear of the Lord, from having your life built on the only foundation that cannot be shaken.

So where does that leave us today?

Let me suggest three things to carry with you from Proverbs chapter one.

First... begin with reverence. Whatever else you do today, take a moment — even just a moment — to be still before God. Not to ask for things. Not to rush through a prayer list. Just... be still. Let the reality of who God is settle over your soul. Let the fear of the Lord — that reverential awe, that holy wonder — be the starting point of your day.

Second... listen for Wisdom's voice. She is calling. In Scripture. In the counsel of godly friends. In the quiet promptings of the Holy Spirit. In the circumstances God arranges around you. Wisdom is not silent. The question is whether we have ears to hear.

And third... don't delay. Whatever God is calling you to do today — whatever step of obedience, whatever act of repentance, whatever decision you've been putting off — don't put it off another day. The time to respond to wisdom is now. Always now.

Let me close with a prayer.

Father in heaven... we come before You at the beginning of this journey through Your Word, and we confess that so often we have been the simple ones who love simplicity. We have ignored Your voice. We have preferred our own way. Forgive us, Lord. And grant us, by Your Spirit, the fear of the Lord — that holy, reverent, trembling awe that is the beginning of all true knowledge. Teach us wisdom, Father. Not the wisdom of the world, but the wisdom that comes down from above — pure, peaceable, gentle, easy to be entreated, full of mercy and good fruits. We ask this in the name of Jesus Christ, our Lord and our Wisdom. Amen.

Until tomorrow, friend. Walk in wisdom today.

[END OF EPISODE 1]
