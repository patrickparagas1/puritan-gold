SEGMENT 1: MORNING DEVOTION
----------------------------------------

Welcome, young scholars. Today we gather in the tradition of the great Puritan academies, where every subject was studied under the lordship of Christ. No knowledge is secular when it is received from the hand of God.

Our Father, the Puritans prayed before every lesson, and so do we. We ask for Your blessing on today's studies. Let the truths we learn take root in our hearts and produce fruit in our lives. We study not for our own glory, but for Yours. Amen.

Let us still our hearts with the Word of God before we proceed.

Our Scripture for today is Romans 8:13; Colossians 3:5-10; Galatians 5:16-17, 24; Romans 13:14:

"For if ye live after the flesh, ye shall die: but if ye through the Spirit do mortify the deeds of the body, ye shall live. Mortify therefore your members which are upon the earth; fornication, uncleanness, inordinate affection, evil concupiscence, and covetousness, which is idolatry: For which things' sake the wrath of God cometh on the children of disobedience: In the which ye also walked some time, when ye lived in them. But now ye also put off all these; anger, wrath, malice, blasphemy, filthy communication out of your mouth. Lie not one to another, seeing that ye have put off the old man with his deeds; And have put on the new man, which is renewed in knowledge after the image of him that created him. This I say then, Walk in the Spirit, and ye shall not fulfil the lust of the flesh. For the flesh lusteth against the Spirit, and the Spirit against the flesh: and these are contrary the one to the other: so that ye cannot do the things that ye would. And they that are Christ's have crucified the flesh with the affections and lusts. But put ye on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make not provision for the flesh, to fulfil the lusts thereof."

What sin has you in its grip? Not the general, comfortable admission that you are a sinner — every Christian can manage that — but the specific, uncomfortable acknowledgment of that particular sin that keeps returning, keeps defeating you, keeps grieving the Spirit and robbing you of joy. You know what it is. God knows what it is. And He is calling you today to bring it to the cross and leave it there. Not to merely feel sorry about it, not to merely resolve to do better, but to mortify it — to put it to death by the power of the Holy Spirit. Owen says you must be killing sin, or it will be killing you. There is no truce, no armistice, no peaceful coexistence. But take heart: you do not fight alone. The Spirit who raised Jesus from the dead lives in you, and He is more than sufficient for this battle. Look to the cross. See there the price your Savior paid for this very sin. Let that sight break its power over you. And then rise and fight, knowing that the victory has already been won.

As we prepare for today's lesson, remember that everything we study flows from this one fountain: the Word of God. Our lesson today is part of Growing in Christ — Sanctification, and it is titled: Mortification — Putting Sin to Death. Let us proceed with minds engaged and hearts open.

SEGMENT 2: MAIN LESSON PART 1 -- BIBLICAL FOUNDATION
----------------------------------------

Having laid the foundation in prayer and Scripture, let us build upon it.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes in Romans 8:13; Colossians 3:5-10; Galatians 5:16-17, 24; Romans 13:14.

The apostle Paul issues one of the most urgent commands in all of Scripture in Romans 8:13: "For if ye live after the flesh, ye shall die: but if ye through the Spirit do mortify the deeds of the body, ye shall live." The word "mortify" comes from the Latin mortificare, meaning to put to death. Paul is saying that the Christian life involves a daily, deliberate, Spirit-empowered putting to death of sinful desires and practices. This is not a suggestion or a helpful tip for advanced Christians — it is a matter of spiritual life and death. Those who refuse to fight against sin will perish; those who, by the Spirit's power, wage war against sin will live.

Several crucial truths emerge from this verse. First, mortification is the duty of every believer. Paul is writing to the church at Rome — to people who are already justified, already adopted into God's family, already indwelt by the Holy Spirit. Yet even these believers must mortify sin. Conversion does not eliminate the need for mortification; it creates the possibility of mortification. Before conversion, we were slaves to sin and had no power to resist it. After conversion, we have been freed from sin's dominion and empowered by the Spirit to fight against it. But fight we must.

Second, mortification is accomplished "through the Spirit." This is not a human achievement but a divine enablement. We do not put sin to death by sheer willpower, moral effort, or behavioral modification techniques. We do it through the power of the Holy Spirit working in us. The Spirit provides the strength, the conviction, the illumination, and the transforming grace that make mortification possible. Without the Spirit, our best efforts at self-improvement are like a man trying to lift himself off the ground by pulling on his own bootstraps — futile and absurd.

Third, mortification is active and ongoing. The Greek present tense indicates continuous action — keep on putting to death, day after day, the deeds of the body. Mortification is not a one-time event but a lifelong discipline. The moment we stop fighting, sin begins to regain ground. The moment we grow complacent, temptation finds an opening. The Christian life is warfare from conversion to glorification, and the enemy never takes a day off.

In Colossians 3:5-10, Paul provides a more detailed account of what mortification looks like in practice. He commands, "Mortify therefore your members which are upon the earth; fornication, uncleanness, inordinate affection, evil concupiscence, and covetousness, which is idolatry." Paul lists specific sins that must be put to death — sexual immorality, impurity, uncontrolled passion, evil desire, and covetousness. He does not speak in vague generalities but names particular sins that must be identified, confronted, and killed. This is important because sin thrives in vagueness. As long as we deal with sin in general terms — "I know I'm not perfect" — it retains its power. It is when we identify and name specific sins — "I am guilty of covetousness; I harbor bitterness toward this person; I indulge in this particular temptation" — that we begin to gain the upper hand.

Paul adds a second list of sins that must be "put off" like soiled clothing: "anger, wrath, malice, blasphemy, filthy communication out of your mouth" (Colossians 3:8). The imagery of changing clothes is vivid and practical. Just as you would strip off filthy garments and put on clean ones, so you must strip off the sinful habits of the old nature and put on the virtuous habits of the new nature. But the old clothes do not come off by themselves — you must deliberately remove them. And the new clothes do not appear by magic — you must deliberately put them on.

Paul then describes the positive counterpart to mortification: "And have put on the new man, which is renewed in knowledge after the image of him that created him" (Colossians 3:10). Mortification is never merely negative — it is never simply about stopping bad behavior. It is about replacing bad behavior with good behavior, sinful habits with holy habits, the works of the flesh with the fruit of the Spirit. The garden analogy is helpful: you do not simply pull up weeds and leave the ground bare. You pull up weeds and plant flowers, vegetables, and fruit-bearing trees. If you only pull weeds without planting anything, the weeds will return. But if you fill the garden with productive growth, the weeds are crowded out.

In Galatians 5:16-17, Paul reveals the inner conflict that makes mortification necessary: "Walk in the Spirit, and ye shall not fulfil the lust of the flesh. For the flesh lusteth against the Spirit, and the Spirit against the flesh: and these are contrary the one to the other: so that ye cannot do the things that ye would." Every believer experiences this civil war within. The new nature, created by the Spirit, desires holiness. The old nature, inherited from Adam, desires sin. These two are locked in perpetual combat, and the battle is fought every day in the decisions, desires, and thoughts of the believer. The solution is not to pretend the battle does not exist but to fight it with the right weapons — walking in the Spirit, depending on His power, following His leading.

Paul provides the decisive statement in Galatians 5:24: "And they that are Christ's have crucified the flesh with the affections and lusts." In our union with Christ, the flesh has been crucified — nailed to the cross, sentenced to death. But a crucified man does not die instantly; he dies slowly, painfully, over a period of time. So it is with the old nature. It has been fatally wounded by the cross of Christ, but it continues to struggle, to kick, to resist. Mortification is the process by which we carry out the execution that was decreed at Calvary — applying the death of Christ to the specific sins that still cling to us.

Finally, Paul gives the most practical of all his commands regarding mortification in Romans 13:14: "But put ye on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make not provision for the flesh, to fulfil the lusts thereof." The phrase "make not provision for the flesh" means do not supply your sinful nature with the fuel it needs to burn. Do not put yourself in situations where temptation is maximized. Do not feed your eyes, ears, and mind with material that inflames sinful desire. Cut off the supply lines to the enemy. Starve the old nature while feeding the new nature. This is the wisdom of mortification — not merely resisting temptation when it comes but arranging your life so that temptation has as little opportunity as possible to gain a foothold.

This biblical foundation is essential. Without it, everything else we study today would be like a house built on sand. But with it, we stand on solid rock.

SEGMENT 3: MAIN LESSON PART 2 -- PURITAN TEACHING
----------------------------------------

Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. John Owen wrote extensively on this subject in "Of the Mortification of Sin in Believers." The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life.

John Owen's treatise "Of the Mortification of Sin in Believers," first published in 1656, is universally regarded as the most important and influential work ever written on the subject of putting sin to death in the Christian life. It has been read, studied, and applied by believers for nearly four centuries, and its wisdom remains as timely and urgent today as when Owen first put pen to paper. The treatise is based on Romans 8:13 and is structured as a practical guide for believers engaged in the daily struggle against remaining sin.

Owen begins with a foundational principle that governs everything that follows: "Be killing sin, or it will be killing you." This memorable declaration captures the urgency and the stakes of the battle. Mortification is not optional. It is not a hobby for spiritually advanced Christians. It is a matter of life and death for every believer. Sin that is left unmortified does not remain static — it grows, spreads, strengthens, and eventually dominates. Like a cancer that is ignored, it may seem dormant for a season, but it is always at work, and if left untreated, it will destroy.

Owen identifies several characteristics of unmortified sin. First, it is restless — it never takes a break but is always pressing for expression, always seeking an opening, always working to reassert its dominion. Second, it is deceitful — it disguises itself, makes excuses, provides rationalizations, and presents itself as less dangerous than it really is. Owen writes that sin will tell you it is just a small indulgence, just a momentary weakness, just a harmless thought — but behind every small indulgence is a desire for total control. Third, unmortified sin hardens the conscience. Every time we yield to a temptation without repentance, our sensitivity to that sin diminishes. What once troubled us deeply becomes merely uncomfortable, then tolerable, then normal. This progressive desensitization is one of sin's deadliest weapons.

Owen then lays out his method for mortification, organized into several clear principles. The first principle is that only believers can truly mortify sin. Unbelievers may reform their behavior externally, but they cannot strike at the root of sin in their hearts because they lack the indwelling Holy Spirit. All genuine mortification is the work of the Spirit through the believer. This does not mean the believer is passive — we are called to active engagement — but it means that our activity must be Spirit-dependent, not self-reliant.

The second principle is that universal obedience is required. Owen writes that a person who fights against one particular sin while tolerating others is not truly engaged in mortification but merely in preference. You cannot negotiate with sin, keeping some and discarding others. You must be at war with all sin — not only the sins that embarrass you publicly but also the secret sins of the heart that no one else sees. Owen observes that many believers are strict about outward sins while harboring pride, envy, bitterness, or lust in their hearts. This selective morality is not mortification but hypocrisy.

The third principle is that the believer must know the specific sin that most easily besets him and give special attention to mortifying it. Owen calls this the "besetting sin" — the particular temptation to which a person is most vulnerable, the weak point in their armor, the gate through which the enemy most often attacks. Every believer has at least one besetting sin, and often several. Identifying this sin requires honest self-examination, openness to the correction of others, and sensitivity to the convictions of the Holy Spirit. Once the besetting sin is identified, it must become the primary target of the believer's mortification efforts.

Owen provides several practical directives for mortifying specific sins. First, load your conscience with the guilt and danger of the sin. Do not treat it lightly. Meditate on what Scripture says about it. Consider the dishonor it brings to God, the grief it causes the Holy Spirit, the damage it does to your soul, and the consequences it brings upon your life and testimony. Many believers fail in mortification because they have never been truly convinced of the seriousness of their sin. They know it is wrong in theory, but they have never felt its wrongness in their hearts.

Second, Owen counsels believers to get a clear sense of the guilt of the sin before God, as opposed to merely fearing its consequences. There is a difference between being sorry that sin brings trouble and being sorry that sin offends God. The first kind of sorrow produces only temporary behavioral change; the second produces genuine repentance and lasting transformation.

Third, Owen urges believers to meditate regularly on the sufferings of Christ and to consider how their sin contributed to His agony on the cross. When we see our particular sin in the light of Calvary — when we realize that this specific temptation, this cherished indulgence, is part of what nailed the Son of God to the cross — our affection for it withers and our hatred of it deepens. The cross is the most powerful weapon against sin because it simultaneously reveals the horror of sin and the love of God, breaking the power of temptation by replacing our love for sin with a greater love for Christ.

Fourth, Owen emphasizes the importance of filling the mind and heart with thoughts that are contrary to the sin being mortified. If the besetting sin is lust, fill your mind with thoughts of purity, with meditation on the beauty of holiness, with the contemplation of the glory of God. If the besetting sin is pride, meditate on the humility of Christ, the unworthiness of your own nature, and the unmerited character of every blessing you possess. If the besetting sin is anger, fill your heart with thoughts of patience, forgiveness, and the longsuffering of God toward you. Sin cannot thrive where its opposite is cultivated.

Owen concludes with a warning against discouragement. He acknowledges that the battle against sin is long, difficult, and often discouraging. There will be setbacks, failures, and seasons of apparent defeat. But the believer must never give up. The one who persists in mortification, who gets up after every fall, who refuses to make peace with sin — that person will see progress. It may be slow, but it is real. And the day is coming when the last remnant of sin will be utterly destroyed, when we shall see Christ and be made perfectly like Him. Until that day, we fight — not in our own strength, but in the power of the Spirit, with the weapons of faith, and with the certainty that sin's doom has already been sealed at Calvary.

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.

SEGMENT 4: DISCUSSION AND APPLICATION
----------------------------------------

True learning requires reflection. Let us discuss today's key ideas.

Discussion Question 1: Owen says, 'Be killing sin, or it will be killing you.' What does he mean by this? Why is there no neutral ground in the battle against sin?

Discussion Question 2: Paul commands believers to 'mortify' specific sins rather than speaking in vague generalities. Why is it important to identify and name our specific sins? What makes this difficult?

Discussion Question 3: Owen teaches that mortification requires 'universal obedience' — fighting against all sin, not just selected ones. How does selective morality undermine genuine spiritual growth?

Discussion Question 4: How does meditating on the sufferings of Christ help us in the battle against specific temptations? What is the connection between the cross and mortification?

Discussion Question 5: Paul says to 'make not provision for the flesh.' What are practical examples of 'making provision for the flesh,' and how can we cut off the supply lines to our besetting sins?

Take time with each question. The Puritans valued what they called "holy conference," the practice of discussing spiritual truths together. Do not settle for quick, surface answers. Push deeper. Ask follow-up questions. Listen carefully to one another.

Application is where truth becomes life. It is not enough to know what Romans 8:13; Colossians 3:5-10; Galatians 5:16-17, 24; Romans 13:14 says. We must ask: how does this change the way I live today? How does this shape my thoughts, my words, my actions? The Puritans insisted that a truth that does not transform is a truth that has not been truly understood.

SEGMENT 5: AFTERNOON ACTIVITY
----------------------------------------

The afternoon brings us to our activity time. Let us engage our hands as well as our minds.

Students will conduct a detailed study of Owen's method of mortification by creating a 'Battle Plan Against Sin' workbook. The workbook will include: (1) A self-examination section based on Owen's guidance for identifying besetting sins — students will reflect on recurring patterns of temptation and areas of spiritual weakness; (2) A 'Know Your Enemy' section where students study what Scripture says about their identified area of struggle, including its nature, consequences, and God's promises of deliverance; (3) A 'Weapons of Warfare' section listing specific spiritual resources for fighting the identified sin — relevant Scripture passages, prayer strategies, accountability measures, and practical steps for 'cutting off provision'; (4) A 'Cross-Centered Meditation' section where students write a reflection on how Christ's suffering on the cross addresses their specific area of temptation. All personal reflection sections are private and confidential.

Materials needed: Printed 'Battle Plan' workbook template, concordance for finding relevant Scripture passages, copies of Owen's key principles summarized in student-accessible language, pens, private folders for completed workbooks to ensure confidentiality

As you complete this activity, think about how it connects to what you studied in today's lesson. The goal is not just to do something, but to reinforce the truths you have been learning. The Puritans believed that education should engage the whole person: mind, heart, and hands.

Take your time with this activity. Quality matters more than speed. When you are finished, reflect on what you learned through the process of doing. Often we understand something far better after we have tried to put it into practice.

SEGMENT 6: CROSS-SUBJECT CONNECTIONS
----------------------------------------

One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

Owen wrote 'Of the Mortification of Sin' during the English Interregnum, a period when Puritans held political power and faced the temptation of becoming worldly and complacent rather than maintaining spiritual vigilance. Study the paradox that often confronts religious movements: how gaining power and influence can weaken the very spiritual disciplines that produced the movement's strength. How did the Puritans' political success during the Cromwellian era affect their spiritual vitality? What lessons does this teach about the relationship between external success and internal holiness?

Literature Connection:

Owen's 'Mortification of Sin' is a masterpiece of persuasive prose. Analyze his rhetorical strategy: how does he establish urgency (the life-or-death stakes), build his argument logically (from principle to application), use vivid imagery and analogy (sin as disease, as enemy, as deceiver), and motivate action (pointing to the cross and the Spirit's power)? Compare Owen's approach to persuasive writing with a modern self-help book on breaking bad habits. What are the fundamental differences in their assumptions about human nature, the source of the problem, and the means of change?

Science Connection:

Modern neuroscience has discovered that habitual behaviors create neural pathways in the brain — physical connections that make repeated behaviors easier and more automatic over time. This principle of neuroplasticity provides a scientific parallel to Owen's observation that unmortified sin becomes increasingly powerful and ingrained. Study the neuroscience of habit formation and habit breaking. How do neural pathways form, and how can they be replaced with new ones? How does this scientific understanding complement the biblical-Puritan teaching on mortification and the development of new patterns of holy living?

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.
