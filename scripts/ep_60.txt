PURITAN GOLD -- Study Devotional, Day 29
Series: Romans
"Christ Received Us"
Bearing with the weak, the unity of Jew and Gentile, and the God of hope

---

Good evening, and welcome to our study devotional from Puritan Gold. We are continuing our journey through one of the most important books in all of Scripture. The Puritans treasured careful, verse-by-verse study, and tonight we follow in their footsteps.

We are studying the book of Romans together. Tonight's lesson is titled "Christ Received Us." Each session builds on what came before, so if you have been following along, you will see how tonight's passage connects to the larger argument of the book.

--- Scripture Reading ---

Let us read our passage for tonight. It comes from Romans 15:1-13:

"We then that are strong ought to bear the infirmities of the weak, and not to please ourselves. Let every one of us please his neighbour for his good to edification. For even Christ pleased not himself; but, as it is written, The reproaches of them that reproached thee fell on me... Now the God of patience and consolation grant you to be likeminded one toward another according to Christ Jesus: That ye may with one mind and one mouth glorify God, even the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ. Wherefore receive ye one another, as Christ also received us to the glory of God... Now I say that Jesus Christ was a minister of the circumcision for the truth of God, to confirm the promises made unto the fathers: And that the Gentiles might glorify God for his mercy... Now the God of hope fill you with all joy and peace in believing, that ye may abound in hope, through the power of the Holy Ghost."

Read that passage again to yourself. Mark any words or phrases that stand out. The Puritans read with a pen in hand, always ready to note what God was showing them.

--- Key Verse ---

Let us focus on one verse that captures the heart of tonight's passage.

"Now the God of hope fill you with all joy and peace in believing, that ye may abound in hope, through the power of the Holy Ghost." -- Romans 15:13

This verse is the lens through which we will examine the rest of tonight's passage. Keep it in the front of your mind as we proceed.

--- Exposition ---

Now let us move into the exposition of this passage.

Martyn Lloyd-Jones offers valuable commentary on this passage. The Puritans were expositors of the highest order, and their insights remain remarkably fresh.

Martyn Lloyd-Jones regarded Romans 15:1-13 as one of the most beautiful passages on Christian unity in all of Scripture. He stressed that Paul's argument for unity is not sociological or pragmatic but Christological. The reason we should bear with one another is that Christ bore with us. The reason we should receive one another is that Christ received us. Every command in this passage is grounded in the example and work of Christ.

Lloyd-Jones gave particular attention to verse 3: 'For even Christ pleased not himself.' He argued that this is the most counter-cultural statement imaginable. The entire modern world is built on self-fulfillment, self-expression, and self-actualization. But the Son of God lived an entire life of not pleasing Himself. He pleased the Father. He served others. He bore reproaches that were aimed at God. Lloyd-Jones saw in this the pattern for all Christian relationships: not self-assertion but self-sacrifice, not demanding our rights but bearing one another's burdens.

Lloyd-Jones was especially moved by the prayer of verse 13: 'Now the God of hope fill you with all joy and peace in believing, that ye may abound in hope, through the power of the Holy Ghost.' He called this the most comprehensive benediction in the New Testament, encompassing the whole Trinity (the God of hope, the object of believing, and the power of the Holy Ghost) and the whole Christian experience (joy, peace, and abounding hope). Lloyd-Jones argued that this verse describes the normal Christian life — not the extraordinary or the exceptional, but what every believer should experience through the power of the Spirit. If you are not filled with joy and peace and abounding in hope, something is wrong, and the solution is believing — taking God at His word and trusting the Holy Spirit to produce what you cannot produce yourself.

Romans 15:1-13 brings to a magnificent conclusion the practical section that began in chapter 14 about the relationship between strong and weak believers. But it does far more than resolve a pastoral problem; it unveils the glory of God's purpose in bringing Jew and Gentile together into one worshiping body, and it culminates in one of the most beautiful prayers in the Bible.

Verses 1-2 state the obligation of the strong: 'We then that are strong ought to bear the infirmities of the weak, and not to please ourselves. Let every one of us please his neighbour for his good to edification.' The word 'bear' (bastazo) means to carry, to hold up, to support — the same word used of carrying a heavy load. The strong do not merely tolerate the weak; they actively carry their burdens. And the motivation is not self-pleasure but neighbor-edification. Every action should be aimed at building up the brother, not satisfying self.

Verse 3 grounds this in the example of Christ: 'For even Christ pleased not himself; but, as it is written, The reproaches of them that reproached thee fell on me.' Paul quotes Psalm 69:9, applying it to Christ. Jesus lived His entire life for others, not for Himself. The insults directed at God fell on Him. The reproaches we deserved He bore in our place. If the Son of God did not live to please Himself, how can His followers claim the right to do so?

Verse 4 pauses to affirm the value of the Old Testament: 'For whatsoever things were written aforetime were written for our learning, that we through patience and comfort of the scriptures might have hope.' The Old Testament is not obsolete; it is written for our instruction. It produces patience (endurance in affliction), comfort (encouragement in sorrow), and hope (confident expectation of future glory). The Scriptures are the fuel of Christian perseverance.

Verses 5-6 contain Paul's first prayer in this section: 'Now the God of patience and consolation grant you to be likeminded one toward another according to Christ Jesus: That ye may with one mind and one mouth glorify God, even the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ.' Unity of mind leads to unity of worship. When strong and weak believers receive one another in love, they can worship together with one voice — and this unified worship brings glory to God. Division in the church hinders the worship of God; unity enhances it.

Verse 7 is the practical command: 'Wherefore receive ye one another, as Christ also received us to the glory of God.' The standard of our acceptance of one another is Christ's acceptance of us. How did Christ receive us? While we were yet sinners. While we were enemies. While we were weak and ungodly. He did not wait for us to clean up our act. He received us in our mess and then transformed us. We must do the same with our brothers and sisters.

Verses 8-12 demonstrate from Scripture that God always planned to unite Jew and Gentile in one worshiping community. Verse 8: 'Jesus Christ was a minister of the circumcision for the truth of God, to confirm the promises made unto the fathers.' Christ came first to Israel, fulfilling the covenant promises made to the patriarchs. Verse 9: 'And that the Gentiles might glorify God for his mercy.' Christ also came for the Gentiles — not on the basis of covenant promise but on the basis of sheer mercy.

Paul then strings together four Old Testament quotations proving that Gentile worship was always part of God's plan. Psalm 18:49 (verse 9): 'I will confess to thee among the Gentiles.' Deuteronomy 32:43 (verse 10): 'Rejoice, ye Gentiles, with his people.' Psalm 117:1 (verse 11): 'Praise the Lord, all ye Gentiles.' Isaiah 11:10 (verse 12): 'There shall be a root of Jesse, and he that shall rise to reign over the Gentiles; in him shall the Gentiles trust.' From the law, the psalms, and the prophets — the entire Old Testament bears witness that the Gentiles were always included in God's redemptive purpose.

Verse 13 is the crown of the passage and one of the most glorious benedictions in Scripture: 'Now the God of hope fill you with all joy and peace in believing, that ye may abound in hope, through the power of the Holy Ghost.' Notice the Trinitarian structure: the God of hope is the source; believing (in Christ) is the means; the Holy Spirit is the power. And the result is comprehensive: all joy, all peace, abounding hope.

God is the 'God of hope' — He is the origin and object of all genuine hope. The filling Paul prays for includes 'all joy' — not partial, not occasional, but full, overflowing, complete joy. And 'peace' — the deep, settled tranquility of a soul that knows it is right with God and resting in His care. And this joy and peace come 'in believing' — not in circumstances, not in human achievements, not in successful outcomes, but in the act of trusting God.

The result of being filled with joy and peace is that we 'abound in hope.' Hope is not static; it overflows. It grows. It multiplies. And it does so 'through the power of the Holy Ghost.' This is not something we manufacture by positive thinking or self-effort. It is produced in us by the Spirit of God. Our part is to believe; the Spirit's part is to fill. When we bring faith, the Spirit brings power, and the result is joy, peace, and overflowing hope.

--- Application ---

Now let us consider: how does this truth apply to our lives? The Puritans never separated doctrine from practice. Every truth they studied was immediately applied.

Romans 15:1-13 calls us to Christ-like service, deep unity, and Spirit-filled hope.

First, bear the burdens of weaker believers. This week, identify someone in your church who is struggling — whether with doubts, with a tender conscience, or with practical needs. Do not merely feel sympathy; take action. Carry their burden. Offer practical help. Spend time listening. 'Bear ye one another's burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ' (Galatians 6:2).

Second, pursue unity in worship. 'That ye may with one mind and one mouth glorify God.' Unity is not uniformity — it does not require everyone to agree on every minor issue. But it does require that we value the glory of God above our personal preferences. In your church, are there divisions over secondary matters? Are there people you avoid because they differ from you on non-essential issues? Take a step toward reconciliation this week. Receive them as Christ received you.

Third, study the Old Testament with fresh eyes. Verse 4 tells us that 'whatsoever things were written aforetime were written for our learning.' The Old Testament is not a dusty relic; it is a living source of patience, comfort, and hope. This week, read a chapter from the Psalms or the Prophets with this in mind: what does this teach me about God's faithfulness? How does this give me hope?

Fourth, pray Romans 15:13 for yourself and others. Make this your daily prayer: 'God of hope, fill me with all joy and peace in believing, that I may abound in hope, through the power of the Holy Ghost.' Pray it for your family, your church, your friends. This is not merely a nice sentiment; it is an apostolic prayer with the full weight of Scripture behind it. Expect God to answer it.

Write down one concrete action you will take this week in response to tonight's study. Do not let this truth remain theoretical. The Puritans measured the success of a sermon not by how much the hearer learned but by how much the hearer changed.

--- Closing ---

God of hope, fill us with all joy and peace in believing, that we may abound in hope through the power of the Holy Ghost. Teach us to bear one another's burdens as Christ bore ours. Give us one mind and one mouth to glorify You. Help us to receive one another as Christ received us — with grace, with patience, with unconditional love. May the Scriptures fill us with patience, comfort, and hope. And may the unity of Jew and Gentile in Your church bring glory to Your name in all the earth. Through Jesus Christ, the Root of Jesse, in whom the nations trust. Amen.

Until next time, keep studying. The Word of God will never run dry.
