PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 11
"Come with Me from Lebanon"
Song of Solomon 4:8-11 — The Invitation to Leave and Cleave


Welcome, dear couple. Tonight the bridegroom issues a powerful invitation — come away from the old and into the new. This passage speaks to one of the most fundamental principles of marriage: leaving in order to cleave. Listen together.

Song of Solomon 4:8-11:

"Come with me from Lebanon, my spouse, with me from Lebanon: look from the top of Amana, from the top of Shenir and Hermon, from the lions' dens, from the mountains of the leopards. Thou hast ravished my heart, my sister, my spouse; thou hast ravished my heart with one of thine eyes, with one chain of thy neck. How fair is thy love, my sister, my spouse! how much better is thy love than wine! and the smell of thine ointments than all spices! Thy lips, O my spouse, drop as the honeycomb: honey and milk are under thy tongue; and the smell of thy garments is like the smell of Lebanon."


"Come with me from Lebanon, my spouse." Lebanon was the northern boundary of Israel — wild, mountainous, beautiful but dangerous. The bridegroom calls his bride to leave Lebanon behind and come with him. He is asking her to leave behind the familiar, the old attachments, the places where lions and leopards roam, and to join him in a new life.

Genesis 2:24 establishes the principle: "Therefore shall a man leave his father and his mother, and shall cleave unto his wife: and they shall be one flesh." This leaving is not merely geographical. It is emotional, relational, and spiritual. The Puritans were emphatic about this. William Gouge wrote extensively about the danger of allowing parents, siblings, or old loyalties to intrude upon the sacred space of marriage. While honor toward parents is commanded, the marriage bond must take precedence.

Many couples struggle with this. Perhaps one spouse has never fully left the orbit of their family of origin. Perhaps old friendships, old habits, or old patterns of living compete with the marriage for first place. The bridegroom's call is clear: Come away. Leave the mountains where the predators roam. Come with me into the safety and intimacy of our covenant.

But notice the tenderness of the call. He does not command her to abandon her past. He invites her to come with him. "Come with me" — it is an invitation to shared life, shared adventure, shared destiny. He is not taking her from something so much as he is inviting her into something better. This is the posture that makes leaving possible: when the new is more compelling than the old, leaving becomes a joy rather than a sacrifice.

"Thou hast ravished my heart, my sister, my spouse." The word "ravished" is extraordinary. It means to be captured, seized, overwhelmed. The bride has captured the bridegroom's heart. And notice the double address: "my sister, my spouse." She is both family and lover. She is his closest companion and his most intimate partner. The Puritans treasured this dual reality. Thomas Gataker called marriage "the highest friendship clothed in the deepest affection" — a relationship that combines the loyalty of kinship with the passion of romantic love.

"How fair is thy love, my sister, my spouse! how much better is thy love than wine!" Earlier, the bride said this about the bridegroom. Now he echoes it back to her. In healthy marriages, admiration flows in both directions. The husband delights in his wife's love, and the wife delights in his. Neither one is merely the recipient; both are givers and receivers.

Richard Sibbes noted that this mutual delight is not automatic — it must be cultivated. He compared it to a garden that requires daily watering. "Love in marriage," Sibbes wrote, "must be continually fed, or it will starve." How are you feeding your love? Through attention? Through words of affirmation? Through physical touch? Through acts of service? Through shared time? The ways of feeding love are many, but neglect produces the same result regardless: a starving relationship.

"Thy lips, O my spouse, drop as the honeycomb: honey and milk are under thy tongue." The bridegroom delights in his wife's words — they are sweet like honey and nourishing like milk. The Puritans saw this as a picture of the ideal marriage conversation: sweet, nourishing, life-giving. Proverbs 16:24 says, "Pleasant words are as an honeycomb, sweet to the soul, and health to the bones." In marriage, your words are either honey or vinegar. They either nourish or corrode. Every day, you choose which they will be.

"And the smell of thy garments is like the smell of Lebanon." Lebanon was famous for its cedar forests — the most fragrant, most prized wood in the ancient world. The bride carries the fragrance of Lebanon with her. Even though she has left Lebanon, its beauty clings to her. The Puritans applied this to the spiritual life of the believer: when you leave the world and come into the covenant of marriage, you do not lose your identity. You bring the best of who you are — your gifts, your personality, your unique beauty — into the new relationship. Marriage does not erase you; it enhances you.

Tonight, consider what you have left behind in order to build your marriage, and what you have brought with you. Celebrate both.


Turn to each other and consider these questions.

Question 1: Is there anything from your past — family patterns, old loyalties, former habits — that still competes with your marriage for first place? How can you more fully "come away" into your covenant with each other?

Question 2: The bridegroom says his bride's love is better than wine. In what specific ways does your spouse's love nourish and delight you more than any other earthly pleasure?

Question 3: Are your words to each other more like honey or vinegar? What would need to change for your daily conversation to be consistently sweet and life-giving?


Tonight's prayer focus: Pray for the grace to leave fully and cleave completely. Ask God to loosen any lingering ties that compete with your marriage and to deepen the bond that holds you together. Thank Him for the unique beauty your spouse brings to your life.


Come away, beloved. Leave behind the old and step together into the new. Your spouse is calling. Your covenant is waiting. Go in peace and sweetness tonight. Goodnight.
