PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"As the Hart Panteth after the Water Brooks"
June 20, 2026

---

"As the hart panteth after the water brooks, so panteth my soul after thee, O God. My soul thirsteth for God, for the living God: when shall I come and appear before God? My tears have been my meat day and night, while they continually say unto me, Where is thy God? Why art thou cast down, O my soul? and why art thou disquieted in me? hope thou in God: for I shall yet praise him for the help of his countenance." -- Psalm 43:1-2, 5 [combining Psalm 42:1-3 and 43:5]

The sons of Korah, who penned this psalm, have given to the church one of the most exquisite descriptions of spiritual longing that human language can express. The image of the hart -- the deer -- panting after the water brooks is a picture drawn from the wilderness of Judea, where the scorching sun beats down upon the barren hills and the streams dry up in the summer heat, and the wild creatures are driven half mad with thirst. The deer does not merely desire water; it pants for it. Every fibre of its being is consumed with a single, overwhelming, desperate need. And so it is with the soul that has tasted the sweetness of God's presence and has been cut off from it by the circumstances of life.

"My soul thirsteth for God, for the living God." Not for religion in the abstract. Not for theological knowledge. Not for the comfort of religious observances or the satisfaction of spiritual disciplines. But for God Himself -- the living God, the personal God, the God who is not a concept but a being, not a force but a Father, not an idea but the great I AM who spoke to Moses from the burning bush and who calls every one of His children by name. This is the distinguishing mark of true spirituality. The formal religionist may be satisfied with rituals and ceremonies. The intellectual may be satisfied with doctrines and systems. But the true child of God will never be satisfied with anything less than God Himself.

Spurgeon preached upon this psalm with tears in his eyes, confessing that he knew from personal experience the agony of spiritual thirst. There were seasons in his ministry when the felt presence of God was withdrawn, when the heavens seemed as brass, when the soul cried out for the living God and received no answer. And in those seasons, the most elaborate worship service was as tasteless as ashes, the most stirring sermon was as dry as dust, and the most beautiful hymn was as hollow as an empty shell. For the soul that has known God cannot be satisfied with anything less than God.

"My tears have been my meat day and night, while they continually say unto me, Where is thy God?" Here is the double anguish of the sufferer: the pain of God's absence compounded by the mockery of the ungodly. The psalmist weeps, and his tears become his only nourishment. Day after day, night after night, the sorrow continues without relief. And to make the pain even sharper, his enemies taunt him with the question that strikes at the very heart of his faith: "Where is thy God?" If God is real, if God cares, if God is truly the refuge and fortress you claim Him to be, then where is He now? Why does He not come to your aid? Why does He leave you to weep alone while we stand by and laugh?

Thomas Goodwin addressed this question with great theological depth, explaining that the apparent absence of God is not evidence of His indifference but of His pedagogy. God is teaching. He is training the soul. He is deepening the channels of desire so that when His presence returns, it will be received with a gratitude and a joy that would have been impossible without the preceding season of drought. The deer that has never known thirst does not appreciate the water brook. The soul that has never known the hiding of God's face does not fully appreciate the light of His countenance.

"Why art thou cast down, O my soul? and why art thou disquieted in me? Hope thou in God: for I shall yet praise him for the help of his countenance." Here the psalmist turns from his sorrow to preach to his own soul, and this is one of the most important exercises in the Christian life. Instead of listening to his feelings, he talks to them. Instead of allowing his emotions to dictate his theology, he brings his theology to bear upon his emotions. He reminds himself of what he knows to be true, even though his feelings contradict it. God has not changed. God has not abandoned him. God will yet deliver him. He shall yet praise. The sorrow is real, but it is temporary. The promise of God is more real still, and it is eternal.

William Bridge wrote a wonderful treatise entitled "A Lifting Up for the Downcast," in which he addressed this very exercise of preaching to one's own soul. He taught that the downcast believer must not sit passively under his sorrow, waiting for the cloud to lift of its own accord. He must take up the weapons of faith and fight. He must open the Scriptures and read the promises. He must recall the mercies of the past. He must reason with his own heart, saying, "Why art thou cast down? Is God less faithful than He was yesterday? Is Christ less powerful? Is the Holy Spirit less present? Has a single promise been revoked? Then what cause have you, O my soul, for this disquiet?"

Let us therefore learn from this psalm both the honesty of godly sorrow and the remedy for it. Let us bring our tears to God and not be ashamed of them. But let us also preach to our own souls the unchanging truth of God's faithfulness, and let us hope in God, for we shall yet praise Him for the help of His countenance. Amen.
