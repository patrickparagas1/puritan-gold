PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"As the Hart Panteth"
May 8, 2026

---

"As the hart panteth after the water brooks, so panteth my soul after thee, O God. My soul thirsteth for God, for the living God: when shall I come and appear before God? My tears have been my meat day and night, while they continually say unto me, Where is thy God? Why art thou cast down, O my soul? and why art thou disquieted in me? hope thou in God: for I shall yet praise him for the help of his countenance." -- Psalm 42:1-3, 5

There is a thirst that no earthly fountain can satisfy, a longing that no created thing can fill, a hunger that grows more intense the more a man tries to appease it with the bread of this world. It is the thirst of the soul for the living God, and it is this thirst that the psalmist describes with such vivid and piercing beauty in the opening verse of this psalm. "As the hart panteth after the water brooks, so panteth my soul after thee, O God."

Picture the scene. A deer, driven by hunters through the scorching wilderness, its tongue parched, its strength failing, its whole being consumed with a single overwhelming desire for water. It has been chased from every stream. The dogs are upon its heels. Its sides heave with exhaustion. And yet it presses on, driven by an instinct more powerful than its pain, more urgent than its weariness, toward the water brooks where alone its thirst can be quenched. This is the image the Holy Spirit sets before us, and it is a picture not of mere religious sentiment, but of the deepest and most vital experience of the regenerate soul.

The psalmist, who was one of the sons of Korah, appears to have been separated from the house of God, perhaps by exile or persecution, and his separation from the place of worship was to him like the separation of the deer from the water. "My soul thirsteth for God, for the living God: when shall I come and appear before God?" Notice that he does not thirst merely for the services of the temple, or for the fellowship of the saints, or for the beauty of the ceremonies, though all these were dear to him. He thirsts for God Himself. For the living God. Not for a concept, not for a theological system, not for a religious experience, but for the person of the Almighty, the living, breathing, speaking, acting God who made him and who alone can satisfy the deepest cravings of his nature.

Augustine of Hippo expressed this same truth in those famous words that have echoed through the centuries: "Thou hast made us for thyself, O Lord, and our hearts are restless until they find their rest in thee." The human soul was created for communion with God, and until it finds that communion, it is like a fish out of water, like a bird without air, like a deer without water. It may try a thousand substitutes. It may drink from the broken cisterns of wealth, of pleasure, of fame, of human love. But none of these can quench the thirst that God Himself has placed within the soul as a homing signal, calling it back to its Creator.

"My tears have been my meat day and night, while they continually say unto me, Where is thy God?" Here is a grief that the world cannot understand. The ungodly man weeps when he loses his money, his health, or his reputation. But the godly man weeps when he loses the felt sense of God's presence. And the taunt of the wicked, "Where is thy God?" is like a knife twisted in an open wound. For the believer knows that God has not ceased to exist, that He has not withdrawn His love, that He has not broken His covenant. And yet the felt enjoyment of God's presence is gone, and in its absence the soul is plunged into a darkness that is more painful than any physical suffering.

The great Puritan William Bridge, in his treatise on spiritual depression, counselled believers in such seasons to distinguish carefully between the presence of God and the sense of God's presence. God is always present with His people. He has promised never to leave them nor forsake them. But the conscious enjoyment of His presence may be withdrawn for a season, as a wise father may hide his face from his child in order to teach the child how precious that face truly is. The sun does not cease to exist when clouds cover it. And God does not cease to love His children when He hides His countenance from them.

And so the psalmist preaches to his own soul. "Why art thou cast down, O my soul? and why art thou disquieted in me? hope thou in God: for I shall yet praise him for the help of his countenance." This is one of the most remarkable features of this psalm. The psalmist does not wait for his feelings to change before he speaks words of hope. He takes his feelings in hand and addresses them with the truth of God. He reasons with his own despondent heart. He commands his downcast soul to hope. He declares, in the teeth of his present experience, that the day of praise will come again. "I shall yet praise him." Not "I may praise him," or "I hope to praise him," but "I shall yet praise him." This is the language of faith, not of feeling. It is the declaration of a man who knows that his God is faithful, even when his own heart is faithless.

Richard Sibbes, that tender-hearted Puritan, often spoke of the soul's dealings with itself as one of the most important aspects of the spiritual life. The believer, he said, must learn to talk to his heart more than he listens to it. The heart will tell him that God has forgotten him, that his prayers are unheard, that his sins are unforgivable, that his case is hopeless. But faith replies with the Word of God: "He hath said, I will never leave thee, nor forsake thee. He is faithful that promised. The Lord's mercies are not consumed, because his compassions fail not. They are new every morning."

Let every soul that thirsts for God today take comfort from this psalm. Your thirst is itself a proof that you belong to Him. The dead do not thirst. The unregenerate do not pant after the living God. It is the Spirit of God within you that creates this longing, and He who created it will surely satisfy it. The water brooks are not dried up. The fountain of living waters is as full today as it was when first it burst from the throne of God. And the day is coming, beloved, when every thirst shall be satisfied, every tear shall be wiped away, and the soul that panted after God in the wilderness shall drink its fill in the paradise of His eternal presence. Hope thou in God. Amen.
