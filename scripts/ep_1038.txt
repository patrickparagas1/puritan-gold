PURITAN GOLD -- Melchizedek: A Complete Biblical Study, Part 8 of 8
Melchizedek in Puritan and Reformed Theology

---

Welcome back to Puritan Gold for the final episode of our complete biblical study of Melchizedek. Over the past seven episodes, we have read every major passage of Scripture concerning this mysterious priest-king — Genesis 14, Psalm 110, Hebrews 5 through 7, and the broader Old Testament types and shadows. Now we turn to the Puritan and Reformed theologians themselves and ask: how did these great men of God understand and apply the Melchizedek doctrine? What did they see in these texts that speaks to the church in every age?

The Puritans were not casual readers of Scripture. They were men who spent hours each day in study, who wrote commentaries of thousands of pages, who preached sermons that could run two hours or more, and who believed that every line of God's Word was worthy of the deepest possible reflection. When they came to the Melchizedek passages, they brought the full weight of their learning, their piety, and their pastoral concern. Let us hear from them.

We begin with John Owen — the greatest of the Puritan theologians, and the man who wrote what is still regarded as the most exhaustive commentary on the epistle to the Hebrews ever produced. Owen's exposition of Hebrews chapters 5 through 7 fills hundreds of pages. His treatment of Melchizedek is the most detailed in the history of Protestant theology.

Owen's first great concern was to establish the identity of Melchizedek. In his day, as in ours, there were three main views. Some said Melchizedek was Shem, the son of Noah, who would have been alive in Abraham's time according to the biblical chronology. Others said he was an angel or a theophany — a pre-incarnate appearance of Christ Himself. Owen rejected both of these views. He argued that Melchizedek was a real, historical, human king-priest — a Canaanite ruler of the city of Salem whom God had raised up and appointed to serve as priest of the most high God in the midst of a pagan world.

Owen's reasoning was careful. If Melchizedek were Shem, he would have had a genealogy — and the entire argument of Hebrews 7 depends on his having no recorded genealogy. If he were Christ Himself, then the statement that he was "made like unto the Son of God" would be nonsensical — you cannot be made like yourself. No, Owen concluded, Melchizedek was a real man, appointed by God to a real priesthood, whose historical existence and whose deliberately obscured origins in the biblical record together created the perfect type of the eternal Son.

Owen then expounded at great length on what he called the excellencies of Christ's Melchizedekian priesthood. He identified several that run through his commentary like recurring themes.

The first excellency is the union of king and priest in one person. Owen writes that this union, which was impossible under the Mosaic system and which brought judgment upon every Israelite king who attempted it, is perfectly realized in Christ. As King, Christ has sovereign authority to rule, to command, to execute judgment. As Priest, He has the compassion and the mediatorial standing to intercede, to atone, to reconcile. These two offices, which in fallen men would create a dangerous concentration of power, are in the sinless Son of God perfectly harmonized. His kingship serves His priesthood, and His priesthood enriches His kingship. He rules in order to save, and He saves in order to rule.

The second excellency is the perpetuity of the priesthood. The Levitical priests served for a season and died. Each death was a reminder that the priestly work was incomplete. If the sacrifice had truly been sufficient, why would another priest need to arise and offer it again? But Christ, because He lives forever, holds His priesthood forever. Owen writes that the perpetuity of Christ's priesthood is the foundation of the doctrine of the perseverance of the saints. If Christ's intercession could be interrupted — by death, by weariness, by any cause whatsoever — then the salvation of believers could be interrupted. But because His priesthood is unchangeable, our salvation is unchangeable.

The third excellency is the oath. Owen returns to this point again and again. The Levitical priests were appointed by law; Christ was appointed by oath. A law can be superseded by a later law. But an oath of God stands forever. When God swore, "Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek," He placed His own unchangeable nature behind the appointment. Owen writes that this oath is the deepest possible assurance God could give to sinners. It is as though God said: I have nothing greater than Myself by which to swear, so I swear by Myself — your Priest shall never fail.

Now let us hear from Thomas Goodwin, Owen's contemporary and fellow Puritan divine. Goodwin was particularly concerned with the internal, experiential dimensions of doctrine. He asked not only what is true about Christ but how does this truth affect the believer's heart?

Goodwin's treatment of Melchizedek focuses especially on the comfort that Christ's priesthood brings to the afflicted conscience. He writes that when a believer is tormented by guilt, when Satan accuses, when the memory of past sins rises up to condemn, the remedy is not to look within but to look upward — to the throne of grace, where Christ sits as a priest forever. Goodwin argues that the phrase "after the order of Melchizedek" should function in the believer's mind as a kind of spiritual anchor. When doubt assails, the believer should reason thus: Christ is my priest. His priesthood is not of the failing, dying, temporary Levitical kind. It is of the Melchizedekian kind — eternal, established by oath, unchangeable. Therefore His intercession for me cannot fail. Therefore my sins, though they are great, are answered by a greater sacrifice. Therefore I may come boldly to the throne of grace.

Goodwin also wrote extensively on the bread and wine that Melchizedek brought forth. He saw in this a picture of the spiritual provision that Christ makes for His people in every season of their lives. After battle comes refreshment. After the weariness of spiritual warfare comes the communion table, where Christ feeds His people with Himself. Goodwin notes that Melchizedek did not wait for Abraham to come seeking refreshment. He came out to meet him. So Christ does not wait for His weary soldiers to find their own way to comfort. He comes to them — in the preaching of the Word, in the sacraments, in the quiet whispers of the Spirit — bringing bread and wine for the journey.

Matthew Henry, writing a generation after Owen and Goodwin, brought a more pastoral and accessible style to the same material. Henry's commentary on Hebrews 7 is perhaps the most widely read Puritan treatment of Melchizedek, and his practical applications are characteristically warm and searching.

Henry emphasizes that Christ as our Melchizedekian priest is everything that the Levitical priests were not. They were sinful; He is holy. They were mortal; He lives forever. They offered the blood of animals; He offered His own blood. They served in a tent made with hands; He serves in the true tabernacle in heaven. They entered the Holy of Holies once a year; He abides there perpetually. Every point of comparison reveals the infinite superiority of Christ's ministry.

Henry also draws practical lessons from the tithe that Abraham gave to Melchizedek. He writes that the act of tithing was an acknowledgment of God's sovereignty and Melchizedek's priestly authority. In the same way, the believer's giving — of money, of time, of service — is an acknowledgment that all we have belongs to God and that Christ our High Priest has authority over every aspect of our lives. Giving is not a burden but a privilege — the joyful response of a heart that recognizes its Priest-King.

Stephen Charnock, in his great work on the attributes of God, touches on the Melchizedek theme when discussing divine immutability. He argues that the unchangeableness of God's nature is reflected in the unchangeableness of Christ's priesthood. Because God does not change, His oath does not change. Because His oath does not change, Christ's appointment does not change. Because Christ's appointment does not change, His intercession does not change. Because His intercession does not change, the salvation of His people does not change. Charnock builds a golden chain of immutability from the nature of God to the security of the believer, and Melchizedek stands at the center of that chain.

Richard Sibbes, the gentle Puritan known for his tender pastoral writings, also reflected on Melchizedek in his treatment of Christ as Mediator. Sibbes writes that the combination of king and priest in one person means that Christ is both mighty enough and merciful enough to save His people. A king without priestly compassion might crush sinners under the weight of justice. A priest without kingly power might pity sinners but lack the authority to deliver them. But Christ is both — mighty to save and moved with compassion to save. He has the power of a king and the tenderness of a priest. He wields the scepter with one hand and extends the censer with the other.

Finally, let us consider how the Puritan tradition understood the phrase "without father, without mother, without descent" in Hebrews 7:3. The Reformed consensus, articulated most carefully by Owen, is that this describes Melchizedek as he appears in the biblical record, not as he existed in historical fact. The Holy Spirit deliberately omitted Melchizedek's genealogy from Genesis so that the silence of Scripture might itself become a teaching tool. The absence of recorded origin and ending made Melchizedek the perfect type of the One who truly has no beginning and no end — the eternal Son of God.

Owen writes that this teaches us a vital hermeneutical principle: the silences of Scripture are as inspired as its speeches. What God chose not to record about Melchizedek was as much a work of the Holy Spirit as what He did record. And both the record and the silence serve the same purpose — to point the reader to Christ.

Beloved, this completes our eight-part study of Melchizedek. We have read every relevant passage. We have heard from the greatest Puritan expositors. And the conclusion is this: Melchizedek was given to the church as a window into the glories of Christ. His priesthood — royal, eternal, established by oath, combining righteousness and peace, offering bread and wine, receiving the tithes of the patriarch, blessing the father of the faithful — is a shadow of the priesthood that Christ now exercises at the right hand of God.

Let us close with the words of Hebrews 7:25: "Wherefore he is able also to save them to the uttermost that come unto God by him, seeing he ever liveth to make intercession for them."

Come to God by Him. He is able. He ever lives. He saves to the uttermost. Amen.
