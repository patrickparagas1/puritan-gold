The Work of Christ


Good morning. Every day is a gift from God, and every lesson is an opportunity to see His glory more clearly. Welcome to the Puritan Academy. Let us open this day in prayer.

Father, we thank You for the privilege of education. Not everyone has the opportunity we have today. Help us to steward it well, to study with excellence, and to use what we learn in service of Your kingdom. In Jesus' name. Amen.

Let us begin with a time of prayerful reading, in the Puritan tradition.

Our Scripture for today is John 15:1-7:

"I am the true vine, and my Father is the husbandman. Every branch in me that beareth not fruit he taketh away: and every branch that beareth fruit, he purgeth it, that it may bring forth more fruit. Now ye are clean through the word which I have spoken unto you. Abide in me, and I in you. As the branch cannot bear fruit of itself, except it abide in the vine; no more can ye, except ye abide in me. I am the vine, ye are the branches: He that abideth in me, and I in him, the same bringeth forth much fruit: for without me ye can do nothing. If a man abide not in me, he is cast forth as a branch, and is withered; and men gather them, and cast them into the fire, and they are burned. If ye abide in me, and my words abide in you, ye shall ask what ye will, and it shall be done unto you."

As you begin this day, remember this truth: you are in Christ, and Christ is in you. This is not a distant hope or a future promise — it is your present reality. The same life that raised Jesus from the dead now flows through you. The same Spirit that empowered His ministry now indwells you. You are a branch grafted into the true vine, and as long as you abide in Him, you will bear fruit. Do not try to live this day in your own strength. Draw your strength from Christ. Do not try to produce fruit by your own effort. Let the life of Christ flow through you. Abide in Him, and He will abide in you. Apart from Him, you can do nothing. In Him, you can do all things.

As we prepare for today's lesson, remember that everything we study flows from this one fountain: the Word of God. Our lesson today is part of The Work of Christ, and it is titled: Union with Christ — Living in Him. Let us proceed with minds engaged and hearts open.


It is time for our main lesson. Give this your full attention.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes in John 15:1-7.

Union with Christ is, in many ways, the central truth of the Christian life. It is the doctrine that ties together justification, sanctification, and glorification into a single, organic reality. All the blessings of salvation flow from our union with Christ, and apart from this union, there is no spiritual life, no spiritual fruit, and no spiritual hope. In John 15:1-7, Jesus Himself provides the most vivid and comprehensive illustration of this union: the vine and the branches.

"I am the true vine, and my Father is the husbandman." Jesus identifies Himself as the "true" vine — in contrast to Israel, which was often described as God's vine in the Old Testament (Isaiah 5:1-7, Psalm 80:8-16) but which proved faithless and unfruitful. Christ is the true vine — the one who perfectly fulfills what Israel failed to be. And the Father is the "husbandman" — the vinedresser, the gardener — who tenderly cultivates, prunes, and cares for the vine and its branches.

The central command is in verse 4: "Abide in me, and I in you." The word "abide" is the Greek "meno," which means to remain, to dwell, to stay, to continue. It speaks not of a momentary visit but of a permanent residence. The Christian is not someone who occasionally visits Christ but someone who lives in Christ — who draws life, sustenance, and strength from Christ continuously, just as a branch draws sap from the vine.

The necessity of this union is stated with absolute clarity: "As the branch cannot bear fruit of itself, except it abide in the vine; no more can ye, except ye abide in me." A branch severed from the vine is dead. It may still look like a branch for a time, but it has no life, no growth, and no fruit. In the same way, a person severed from Christ has no spiritual life, regardless of outward religious activity. And then comes the sweeping declaration: "Without me ye can do nothing." Not "without me you can do very little" or "without me you can do most things but not everything." Without Christ, we can do nothing of any spiritual value whatsoever.

Galatians 2:20 expresses the personal dimension of union with Christ: "I am crucified with Christ: nevertheless I live; yet not I, but Christ liveth in me: and the life which I now live in the flesh I live by the faith of the Son of God, who loved me, and gave himself for me." Paul describes a profound exchange: his old self has been crucified — put to death — with Christ. But he is still alive — a new life, animated by a new power. Christ Himself lives in Paul. This is not a metaphor for moral improvement but a description of a mystical, spiritual reality. The risen Christ, by the Holy Spirit, actually dwells within the believer, becoming the source and power of the new life.

Ephesians 2:4-6 reveals the cosmic scope of union with Christ: "But God, who is rich in mercy, for his great love wherewith he loved us, Even when we were dead in sins, hath quickened us together with Christ, (by grace ye are saved;) And hath raised us up together, and made us sit together in heavenly places in Christ Jesus." Because of our union with Christ, everything that happened to Christ has happened to us. He died — we died with Him. He was raised — we were raised with Him. He ascended to heaven — we are seated with Him in heavenly places. This is not something we will experience in the future; it is our present spiritual reality, accomplished by grace and received by faith.

Romans 6:3-5 applies this union specifically to Christ's death and resurrection: "Know ye not, that so many of us as were baptized into Jesus Christ were baptized into his death? Therefore we are buried with him by baptism into death: that like as Christ was raised up from the dead by the glory of the Father, even so we also should walk in newness of life. For if we have been planted together in the likeness of his death, we shall be also in the likeness of his resurrection." Union with Christ means death to sin and life to God. The old life of rebellion and self-will has been crucified. The new life of obedience and holiness has been raised. We are to "walk in newness of life" — our daily conduct should reflect the reality of our spiritual union with the risen Christ.

The fruit of this union is described in John 15:5: "He that abideth in me, and I in him, the same bringeth forth much fruit." Spiritual fruit — love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control (Galatians 5:22-23) — is not produced by human effort alone but by the life of Christ flowing through the believer. Just as a branch produces grapes not by straining but by remaining connected to the vine, so the Christian produces spiritual fruit not by trying harder but by abiding more deeply in Christ.

The promise attached to this union is extraordinary: "If ye abide in me, and my words abide in you, ye shall ask what ye will, and it shall be done unto you" (v. 7). This is not a blank check for selfish desires but a promise for those who are so united with Christ that His words shape their desires. When Christ's words abide in us, our prayers align with His will, and prayers aligned with God's will are always answered.

This biblical foundation is essential. Without it, everything else we study today would be like a house built on sand. But with it, we stand on solid rock.


Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. John Flavel wrote extensively on this subject in "The Fountain of Life." The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life.

John Flavel considered the doctrine of union with Christ to be one of the most important and most neglected truths in all of Christian theology. In "The Fountain of Life" and in his treatise "The Method of Grace," Flavel devoted extensive attention to explaining what it means to be united to Christ and what practical difference this union should make in the daily life of the believer.

Flavel began by asserting that union with Christ is the foundation of every other spiritual blessing. Justification, sanctification, adoption, perseverance, and glorification all flow from union with Christ. A person who is not united to Christ has none of these blessings, regardless of their religious activity. A person who is united to Christ possesses all of them, regardless of their feelings or circumstances. Flavel wrote: "Union with Christ is the first and fundamental blessing, from which all other blessings of the covenant of grace flow to us."

Flavel used multiple analogies to illustrate the nature of this union. He compared it to the union of a head and body — Christ is the head, and believers are the members of His body, receiving direction, coordination, and life from Him (Ephesians 1:22-23). He compared it to the union of a foundation and a building — Christ is the foundation, and believers are the living stones built upon Him (1 Peter 2:4-5). He compared it to the union of husband and wife — Christ is the bridegroom, and the church is His bride, joined to Him in a covenant of love (Ephesians 5:25-32). Each analogy highlights a different aspect of the union: the head and body emphasize vital connection and shared life; the foundation and building emphasize stability and security; the husband and wife emphasize love, intimacy, and covenant commitment.

Flavel was particularly insightful in explaining how union with Christ is effected. He taught that the Holy Spirit is the agent of union. It is the Spirit who regenerates the sinner, creating new spiritual life and joining the believer to Christ. And faith is the instrument of union — the means by which the sinner grasps Christ and is united to Him. Flavel was careful to insist that faith does not create the union; it receives the union that the Spirit creates. Faith is the hand that receives the gift, not the power that produces it.

The practical implications of union with Christ, as Flavel developed them, are far-reaching. First, union with Christ means that all of Christ's riches belong to the believer. Just as a wife shares in her husband's estate, so the believer shares in Christ's spiritual wealth — His righteousness, His holiness, His peace, His joy, His power. Flavel wrote with characteristic warmth: "When you were united to Christ, all that is His became yours. His righteousness is your covering. His blood is your cleansing. His Spirit is your strength. His Father is your Father. His heaven is your home."

Second, union with Christ means that all of the believer's burdens are borne by Christ. Just as a vine bears the weight of its branches, so Christ bears the weight of His people's sins, sorrows, and weaknesses. This does not mean that believers never experience difficulty, but it means they never experience it alone. Christ shares in every burden and sustains His people through every trial.

Third, union with Christ means that the believer's identity is fundamentally changed. The believer is no longer defined by his past, his sin, his failures, or his weaknesses. He is defined by his union with Christ. Paul captured this new identity in Galatians 2:20: the old Paul — the Pharisee, the persecutor, the self-righteous legalist — has been crucified. The new Paul — the apostle, the servant, the beloved child of God — lives by the faith of the Son of God. This transformation of identity is the deepest and most liberating aspect of union with Christ.

The Puritans as a whole placed enormous emphasis on union with Christ. They saw it not as an abstract doctrine but as the most practical truth in the Christian life. When a believer struggles with sin, the answer is not to try harder in his own strength but to abide more deeply in Christ, who is his sanctification. When a believer struggles with guilt, the answer is not to earn forgiveness through good works but to rest in his union with Christ, who is his justification. When a believer struggles with fear, the answer is not to summon more courage but to draw near to Christ, who is his security and peace.

Flavel concluded his treatment of union with Christ with an earnest appeal: "Come to Christ. Abide in Christ. Find in Christ everything your soul needs — pardon for guilt, strength for weakness, comfort for sorrow, wisdom for confusion, and hope for despair. He is the vine; you are the branch. Apart from Him, you can do nothing. In Him, you can do all things. Let this truth be the anchor of your soul and the joy of your heart. You are in Christ, and Christ is in you. What more do you need?

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.


Let us now deepen our understanding of union with Christ by exploring additional Scripture and the insights of other Puritan theologians.

The Westminster Shorter Catechism, Question 30, asks: "How doth the Spirit apply to us the redemption purchased by Christ?" The answer is: "The Spirit applieth to us the redemption purchased by Christ, by working faith in us, and thereby uniting us to Christ in our effectual calling." Notice the precise order: the Spirit works faith in us, and faith unites us to Christ. Union with Christ is not achieved by human effort or by religious activity but by the sovereign work of the Holy Spirit creating faith in the human heart. This is why the doctrine of union with Christ is ultimately a doctrine of grace — it is God's doing from beginning to end.

John Calvin, the great Reformer who profoundly influenced Puritan theology, made union with Christ the organizing center of his entire theology of salvation. In the Institutes of the Christian Religion, Calvin taught that all the benefits of Christ's work — justification, sanctification, adoption, perseverance — are received through union with Christ. Calvin wrote that as long as Christ remains outside of us, His work on the cross profits us nothing. It is only when we are united to Him by faith, through the work of the Holy Spirit, that His righteousness becomes our righteousness, His death becomes our death, and His resurrection becomes our resurrection. The Puritans inherited this Calvinist understanding and developed it with characteristic thoroughness and pastoral application.

Robert Letham, a modern Reformed theologian who has written extensively on the Puritan understanding of this doctrine, has observed that the Puritans saw union with Christ as encompassing four distinct but inseparable aspects. First, there is the federal or covenantal union — the reality that Christ stood as the representative head of His people in the covenant of grace, just as Adam stood as the representative head of the human race in the covenant of works. This union existed in the eternal decree of God before the foundation of the world. Ephesians 1:4 declares that God "hath chosen us in him before the foundation of the world, that we should be holy and without blame before him in love." Before we were born, before the world was created, God chose us in Christ.

Second, there is the incarnational union — the reality that in the incarnation, the eternal Son of God united Himself to human nature. By taking on flesh, Christ joined Himself to the human race and became our kinsman-redeemer. Hebrews 2:14 explains: "Forasmuch then as the children are partakers of flesh and blood, he also himself likewise took part of the same; that through death he might destroy him that had the power of death, that is, the devil." Christ became what we are so that we might become what He is.

Third, there is the spiritual or mystical union — the reality that by the Holy Spirit, through faith, the believer is joined to the living Christ in a bond that is real, personal, and unbreakable. This is the union that Paul describes in Galatians 2:20 and that Jesus illustrates in John 15. It is not merely a legal arrangement or a metaphorical way of speaking. It is a vital, living, spiritual reality — as real as the connection between a vine and its branches, as intimate as the relationship between a husband and wife.

Fourth, there is the eschatological or glorified union — the reality that in the resurrection and the life to come, the believer will be fully and perfectly united to Christ in body and soul forever. First John 3:2 promises: "Beloved, now are we the sons of God, and it doth not yet appear what we shall be: but we know that, when he shall appear, we shall be like him; for we shall see him as he is." The union that begins in this life will be perfected in the next. We will see Him face to face. We will be like Him. And we will dwell with Him forever.

Richard Sibbes, one of the most beloved of all Puritan pastors, who lived from 1577 to 1635 and served as preacher at Gray's Inn, London, and later as Master of Catherine Hall, Cambridge, wrote with extraordinary tenderness about union with Christ. Sibbes was known as "the sweet dropper" because of the honey-like quality of his preaching, and his works — particularly "The Bruised Reed" and "The Soul's Conflict" — have comforted countless struggling believers over the centuries. Sibbes taught that union with Christ is not merely a theological concept but the most intimate personal relationship that a human being can experience. He compared it to a marriage in which the bridegroom shares everything He has with His bride — His name, His home, His wealth, His honor — and in return, the bride shares everything she has with Him, including her debts and her shame. Sibbes wrote: "In the spiritual marriage between Christ and the soul, there is a glorious exchange. Christ takes upon Himself all our sins, all our debts, all our misery. And He gives to us all His righteousness, all His riches, all His glory. We bring nothing to this marriage but our need, and He supplies all."

Consider also the profound passage in Colossians 3:1-4: "If ye then be risen with Christ, seek those things which are above, where Christ sitteth on the right hand of God. Set your affection on things above, not on things on the earth. For ye are dead, and your life is hid with Christ in God. When Christ, who is our life, shall appear, then shall ye also appear with him in glory." Paul describes the believer's life as "hid with Christ in God" — a breathtaking image of security. The believer's life is not exposed and vulnerable on the surface of this world; it is hidden, concealed, protected in the safest place in the universe — in Christ, who is in God. No enemy can reach it. No circumstance can destroy it. No power in heaven or earth can separate the believer from the life that is hidden with Christ in God.


How does union with Christ apply to the everyday realities of our lives? The Puritans were insistent that this doctrine is not merely for theologians but for every believer in every circumstance.

First, union with Christ transforms how we face temptation. When temptation comes — and it will come — the believer does not fight alone. He fights as one who is united to Christ, who has already conquered sin. Romans 6:6-7 teaches: "Knowing this, that our old man is crucified with him, that the body of sin might be destroyed, that henceforth we should not serve sin. For he that is dead is freed from sin." Because we died with Christ, sin no longer has dominion over us. We are free to say no. We are empowered to resist. The temptation may be fierce, but we are not alone — Christ is in us, and He is stronger than any temptation.

Second, union with Christ transforms how we face suffering. When trials come, the believer does not suffer alone. He suffers as one who is united to Christ, who suffered before him and who suffers with him. Philippians 3:10 speaks of "the fellowship of his sufferings, being made conformable unto his death." There is a fellowship in suffering — a shared experience between Christ and the believer that deepens intimacy and produces perseverance. The Puritans who suffered persecution, imprisonment, and loss found extraordinary comfort in the knowledge that they were sharing in the sufferings of their Savior and that their sufferings were not wasted but were being used by God to conform them to the image of Christ.

Third, union with Christ transforms how we approach daily work. Whether we are studying, working, serving, or resting, we do it as those who are in Christ. Colossians 3:17 instructs: "And whatsoever ye do in word or deed, do all in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to God and the Father by him." Every activity of the day — from the most exalted act of worship to the most mundane household chore — becomes an act of worship when done in union with Christ. The student who studies in Christ's name sanctifies his learning. The worker who labors in Christ's name sanctifies his toil. Nothing is secular for the believer who is united to Christ, for Christ transforms everything He touches.

Fourth, union with Christ transforms how we relate to other believers. If every Christian is united to Christ, then every Christian is also united to every other Christian through Christ. We are members of the same body, branches of the same vine, stones in the same building. This is the basis of Christian community and fellowship. When one member suffers, all suffer together. When one member rejoices, all rejoice together (1 Corinthians 12:26). The Puritans placed enormous emphasis on the church as a community of believers united to Christ and to one another. They practiced mutual accountability, mutual encouragement, and mutual care because they understood that their union with Christ made them inseparably connected to one another.

Fifth, union with Christ transforms how we face death. For the believer, death is not the end but a transition — from union with Christ by faith to union with Christ by sight. Paul could say, "For to me to live is Christ, and to die is gain" (Philippians 1:21) because he knew that death would bring him into the immediate, unmediated, face-to-face presence of the Christ to whom he was already united by the Spirit. The Puritans faced death with remarkable composure and even joy because they understood that dying meant going home to be with the One they loved most.

The Puritans insisted that a truth that does not transform is a truth that has not been truly understood.


The best learning happens when we do something with what we have heard. Here is your activity.

Students will create a visual diagram illustrating the doctrine of union with Christ. Using a large vine and branches drawing as the central image, students will show how each spiritual blessing flows from union with Christ. The vine trunk represents Christ; the branches represent individual believers; the roots represent the Holy Spirit who unites us to Christ; and the fruit represents the spiritual benefits that flow from this union. Students should label specific fruits with their corresponding blessings: justification, sanctification, adoption, peace with God, access to prayer, spiritual power, hope of glory, and others. Around the margin, students should write key Scripture references for each blessing. This visual theology exercise helps students see the centrality and comprehensiveness of union with Christ.

Materials needed: Large poster paper, green and brown markers for the vine, colored markers for fruit and labels, Bibles for reference verses, optional: real grapevine branches for observation

As a supplementary activity, students should conduct a close reading of Romans 6:1-14, which is one of the most important passages on the believer's union with Christ in His death and resurrection. Read the passage aloud together, then work through the following analysis questions: (1) What does it mean to be "baptized into his death" (v. 3)? Is Paul referring to water baptism, spiritual baptism, or both? (2) What is the "old man" that was crucified with Christ (v. 6)? How does this differ from the "new man"? (3) What does it mean that "sin shall not have dominion over you" (v. 14)? Does this mean believers never sin? If not, what does it mean? (4) How does Paul use the concept of union with Christ to argue against the idea that grace gives permission to sin (v. 1-2)? (5) What practical difference should this passage make in the way you respond to temptation today? Write your answers in your journal and discuss them with a parent, teacher, or fellow student. This kind of careful, detailed Bible study was the bread and butter of Puritan education, and it produces a depth of understanding that surface reading cannot achieve.

Additionally, students should write a personal meditation of at least two paragraphs on the theme "What It Means to Be in Christ." Draw upon the Scripture passages studied today (John 15:1-7, Galatians 2:20, Ephesians 2:4-6, Romans 6:3-5, Colossians 3:1-4) and express in your own words what it means to you personally that you are united to Christ. The Puritans excelled at this kind of devotional writing — taking theological truths and expressing them in personal, heartfelt language that brought the doctrine from the study into the prayer closet. Follow their example: do not merely explain the doctrine but respond to it with worship, gratitude, and commitment.

As you complete this activity, think about how it connects to what you studied in today's lesson. The goal is not just to do something, but to reinforce the truths you have been learning. The Puritans believed that education should engage the whole person: mind, heart, and hands.

Take your time with this activity. Quality matters more than speed. When you are finished, reflect on what you learned through the process of doing. Often we understand something far better after we have tried to put it into practice.


One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

The doctrine of union with Christ has been emphasized in different ways throughout church history. The early church fathers, the medieval mystics, the Reformers (especially Calvin), and the Puritans all developed this doctrine. Study how each tradition understood and expressed the concept of the believer's union with Christ. Note particularly how Calvin made union with Christ the central organizing principle of his theology of salvation in the Institutes of the Christian Religion. The early church father Athanasius (c. 296-373) famously wrote that "God became man so that man might become god" — not meaning that believers become divine in nature but that through union with Christ, believers participate in the divine life and share in the divine attributes of holiness, immortality, and glory. The medieval mystic Bernard of Clairvaux (1090-1153) wrote extensively about the "spiritual marriage" between Christ and the soul, drawing on the imagery of the Song of Solomon. The Reformer Martin Luther spoke of the "wonderful exchange" in which Christ takes our sin and gives us His righteousness through faith. Each of these historical expressions illuminates a different facet of the same great diamond: the believer's union with Christ. Study these historical developments and discuss how the Puritan contribution — with its characteristic emphasis on practical application and daily experience — enriched and completed the church's understanding of this central doctrine.

Literature Connection:

The metaphor of the vine and branches belongs to a rich literary tradition of using agricultural imagery to express spiritual truths. Study how poets and writers have used natural imagery — trees, gardens, seasons, planting and harvesting — to express truths about spiritual life, growth, and fruitfulness. Consider also how the concept of identity transformation — becoming someone new — is explored in literature from fairy tales (the ugly duckling becomes a swan) to great novels (the character's journey of self-discovery). John Bunyan's "The Pilgrim's Progress" powerfully illustrates union with Christ through the narrative of Christian's journey. At the very beginning of the journey, Christian is burdened by a heavy load on his back — representing the weight of sin and guilt. It is only when he comes to the cross that the burden falls away and rolls into the empty tomb. This is union with Christ in His death and resurrection: the old life of guilt and bondage dies, and the new life of freedom and joy begins. From that point forward, Christian's journey is lived in dependence upon Christ — drawing strength from the Word, finding rest in the promises, and pressing forward toward the Celestial City. Bunyan's allegory brings the abstract doctrine of union with Christ to vivid, memorable life and demonstrates the Puritan conviction that the deepest theological truths can be communicated through the power of story.

Science Connection:

The relationship between a vine and its branches provides a fascinating botanical study. In viticulture (the science of grape growing), the life of the branch depends entirely on its connection to the vine through the vascular system that carries water, nutrients, and sugars. Study the process of grafting — where a branch from one plant is inserted into the rootstock of another, forming a living union. This biological process provides a remarkable parallel to the spiritual reality of being "grafted in" to Christ (Romans 11:17-24). In grafting, the branch must be cut from its original root and joined to a new rootstock. The wound must heal, and the tissues must grow together to form a single, unified vascular system. Once the graft is successful, the branch receives its entire life — water, nutrients, growth hormones — from the new rootstock, and it bears fruit according to the nature of the rootstock, not according to its original nature. This is a stunning picture of spiritual union with Christ. We have been cut from our old root (Adam) and grafted into a new root (Christ). We now derive our spiritual life entirely from Him, and we bear fruit according to His nature (the fruit of the Spirit) rather than according to our old, fallen nature. Study the science of grafting in detail and discuss how each aspect of the process illuminates a dimension of our spiritual union with Christ.

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.

Memory Verse:

"I am the vine, ye are the branches: He that abideth in me, and I in him, the same bringeth forth much fruit: for without me ye can do nothing." -- John 15:5

Read this verse aloud five times. Then close your eyes and try to recite it from memory. Write it out by hand on a card or in your journal. Recite it to a friend, a sibling, or a parent. The Puritans memorized vast portions of Scripture, and it was one of the great sources of their spiritual strength. They understood that the promises of God, when stored in the memory and meditated upon in the heart, become living realities that sustain the soul in times of trial, comfort the heart in times of sorrow, and empower the will in times of temptation. Even one verse, truly hidden in the heart, can sustain you in the darkest hour and remind you that you are never alone — you are in Christ, and Christ is in you.

Extended Reflection Questions:

Extended Question 1: Richard Sibbes described union with Christ as a "glorious exchange" in which Christ takes our sins and gives us His righteousness. How does this exchange differ from every other kind of transaction or relationship you have experienced? What makes it unique?

Extended Question 2: Colossians 3:3 says your life is "hid with Christ in God." What does this image of hiddenness communicate about the security of the believer? How should this truth affect your response to the uncertainties and dangers of life?

Extended Question 3: The four aspects of union with Christ — federal, incarnational, spiritual, and eschatological — span from eternity past to eternity future. Which of these aspects is most new or surprising to you? How does understanding all four deepen your appreciation for what God has done?

Extended Question 4: Philippians 3:10 speaks of knowing "the fellowship of his sufferings." How can suffering deepen our experience of union with Christ? Have you or someone you know experienced this reality?

Additional Memory Verse:

"I am crucified with Christ: nevertheless I live; yet not I, but Christ liveth in me: and the life which I now live in the flesh I live by the faith of the Son of God, who loved me, and gave himself for me." -- Galatians 2:20

Closing Reflection:

As we close this lesson on union with Christ, let the words of the old Puritan hymn settle into your heart: "In Christ alone my hope is found, He is my light, my strength, my song." Every blessing you will ever receive flows from your union with Him. Every victory you will ever win is accomplished through your connection to Him. Every comfort you will ever experience derives from the fact that you are in Him and He is in you. The Puritan pastor Thomas Brooks, who lived from 1608 to 1680 and authored such beloved works as "Precious Remedies against Satan's Devices" and "Heaven on Earth," wrote: "A man's union with Christ is the ground of all his communion with Christ. As the branch receives its sap, its nourishment, its fruitfulness from the vine, so the believer receives all spiritual blessings from Christ. Cut off from Christ, we are nothing, we have nothing, we can do nothing. But in Christ, we are complete, we have everything, and we can do all things through Him who strengthens us."

Do not merely study this doctrine — live in it. Do not merely understand union with Christ — experience it. Abide in Him today, tomorrow, and every day until the day you see Him face to face. He is the vine. You are the branch. Apart from Him, you can do nothing whatsoever. In Him, you can do all things through His strength that works mightily within you. What more could you possibly need? He is your all in all — your righteousness, your sanctification, your redemption, your hope, your life, and your eternal joy.

Excellent work today at the Puritan Academy. Remember that all true knowledge leads us to know God better. May your studies continue to deepen your love for Christ and His truth. Abide in the vine, bear much fruit, and walk in the newness of life that is yours in Christ. Goodnight, students.
