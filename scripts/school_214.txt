SEGMENT 1: MORNING DEVOTION
----------------------------------------

Good morning. Every day is a gift from God, and every lesson is an opportunity to see His glory more clearly. Welcome to the Puritan Academy. Let us open this day in prayer.

Father, we thank You for the privilege of education. Not everyone has the opportunity we have today. Help us to steward it well, to study with excellence, and to use what we learn in service of Your kingdom. In Jesus' name. Amen.

Let us begin with a time of prayerful reading, in the Puritan tradition.

Our Scripture for today is John 15:1-7:

"I am the true vine, and my Father is the husbandman. Every branch in me that beareth not fruit he taketh away: and every branch that beareth fruit, he purgeth it, that it may bring forth more fruit. Now ye are clean through the word which I have spoken unto you. Abide in me, and I in you. As the branch cannot bear fruit of itself, except it abide in the vine; no more can ye, except ye abide in me. I am the vine, ye are the branches: He that abideth in me, and I in him, the same bringeth forth much fruit: for without me ye can do nothing. If a man abide not in me, he is cast forth as a branch, and is withered; and men gather them, and cast them into the fire, and they are burned. If ye abide in me, and my words abide in you, ye shall ask what ye will, and it shall be done unto you."

As you begin this day, remember this truth: you are in Christ, and Christ is in you. This is not a distant hope or a future promise — it is your present reality. The same life that raised Jesus from the dead now flows through you. The same Spirit that empowered His ministry now indwells you. You are a branch grafted into the true vine, and as long as you abide in Him, you will bear fruit. Do not try to live this day in your own strength. Draw your strength from Christ. Do not try to produce fruit by your own effort. Let the life of Christ flow through you. Abide in Him, and He will abide in you. Apart from Him, you can do nothing. In Him, you can do all things.

As we prepare for today's lesson, remember that everything we study flows from this one fountain: the Word of God. Our lesson today is part of The Work of Christ, and it is titled: Union with Christ — Living in Him. Let us proceed with minds engaged and hearts open.

SEGMENT 2: MAIN LESSON PART 1 -- BIBLICAL FOUNDATION
----------------------------------------

It is time for our main lesson. Give this your full attention.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes in John 15:1-7.

Union with Christ is, in many ways, the central truth of the Christian life. It is the doctrine that ties together justification, sanctification, and glorification into a single, organic reality. All the blessings of salvation flow from our union with Christ, and apart from this union, there is no spiritual life, no spiritual fruit, and no spiritual hope. In John 15:1-7, Jesus Himself provides the most vivid and comprehensive illustration of this union: the vine and the branches.

"I am the true vine, and my Father is the husbandman." Jesus identifies Himself as the "true" vine — in contrast to Israel, which was often described as God's vine in the Old Testament (Isaiah 5:1-7, Psalm 80:8-16) but which proved faithless and unfruitful. Christ is the true vine — the one who perfectly fulfills what Israel failed to be. And the Father is the "husbandman" — the vinedresser, the gardener — who tenderly cultivates, prunes, and cares for the vine and its branches.

The central command is in verse 4: "Abide in me, and I in you." The word "abide" is the Greek "meno," which means to remain, to dwell, to stay, to continue. It speaks not of a momentary visit but of a permanent residence. The Christian is not someone who occasionally visits Christ but someone who lives in Christ — who draws life, sustenance, and strength from Christ continuously, just as a branch draws sap from the vine.

The necessity of this union is stated with absolute clarity: "As the branch cannot bear fruit of itself, except it abide in the vine; no more can ye, except ye abide in me." A branch severed from the vine is dead. It may still look like a branch for a time, but it has no life, no growth, and no fruit. In the same way, a person severed from Christ has no spiritual life, regardless of outward religious activity. And then comes the sweeping declaration: "Without me ye can do nothing." Not "without me you can do very little" or "without me you can do most things but not everything." Without Christ, we can do nothing of any spiritual value whatsoever.

Galatians 2:20 expresses the personal dimension of union with Christ: "I am crucified with Christ: nevertheless I live; yet not I, but Christ liveth in me: and the life which I now live in the flesh I live by the faith of the Son of God, who loved me, and gave himself for me." Paul describes a profound exchange: his old self has been crucified — put to death — with Christ. But he is still alive — a new life, animated by a new power. Christ Himself lives in Paul. This is not a metaphor for moral improvement but a description of a mystical, spiritual reality. The risen Christ, by the Holy Spirit, actually dwells within the believer, becoming the source and power of the new life.

Ephesians 2:4-6 reveals the cosmic scope of union with Christ: "But God, who is rich in mercy, for his great love wherewith he loved us, Even when we were dead in sins, hath quickened us together with Christ, (by grace ye are saved;) And hath raised us up together, and made us sit together in heavenly places in Christ Jesus." Because of our union with Christ, everything that happened to Christ has happened to us. He died — we died with Him. He was raised — we were raised with Him. He ascended to heaven — we are seated with Him in heavenly places. This is not something we will experience in the future; it is our present spiritual reality, accomplished by grace and received by faith.

Romans 6:3-5 applies this union specifically to Christ's death and resurrection: "Know ye not, that so many of us as were baptized into Jesus Christ were baptized into his death? Therefore we are buried with him by baptism into death: that like as Christ was raised up from the dead by the glory of the Father, even so we also should walk in newness of life. For if we have been planted together in the likeness of his death, we shall be also in the likeness of his resurrection." Union with Christ means death to sin and life to God. The old life of rebellion and self-will has been crucified. The new life of obedience and holiness has been raised. We are to "walk in newness of life" — our daily conduct should reflect the reality of our spiritual union with the risen Christ.

The fruit of this union is described in John 15:5: "He that abideth in me, and I in him, the same bringeth forth much fruit." Spiritual fruit — love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control (Galatians 5:22-23) — is not produced by human effort alone but by the life of Christ flowing through the believer. Just as a branch produces grapes not by straining but by remaining connected to the vine, so the Christian produces spiritual fruit not by trying harder but by abiding more deeply in Christ.

The promise attached to this union is extraordinary: "If ye abide in me, and my words abide in you, ye shall ask what ye will, and it shall be done unto you" (v. 7). This is not a blank check for selfish desires but a promise for those who are so united with Christ that His words shape their desires. When Christ's words abide in us, our prayers align with His will, and prayers aligned with God's will are always answered.

This biblical foundation is essential. Without it, everything else we study today would be like a house built on sand. But with it, we stand on solid rock.

SEGMENT 3: MAIN LESSON PART 2 -- PURITAN TEACHING
----------------------------------------

Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. John Flavel wrote extensively on this subject in "The Fountain of Life." The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life.

John Flavel considered the doctrine of union with Christ to be one of the most important and most neglected truths in all of Christian theology. In "The Fountain of Life" and in his treatise "The Method of Grace," Flavel devoted extensive attention to explaining what it means to be united to Christ and what practical difference this union should make in the daily life of the believer.

Flavel began by asserting that union with Christ is the foundation of every other spiritual blessing. Justification, sanctification, adoption, perseverance, and glorification all flow from union with Christ. A person who is not united to Christ has none of these blessings, regardless of their religious activity. A person who is united to Christ possesses all of them, regardless of their feelings or circumstances. Flavel wrote: "Union with Christ is the first and fundamental blessing, from which all other blessings of the covenant of grace flow to us."

Flavel used multiple analogies to illustrate the nature of this union. He compared it to the union of a head and body — Christ is the head, and believers are the members of His body, receiving direction, coordination, and life from Him (Ephesians 1:22-23). He compared it to the union of a foundation and a building — Christ is the foundation, and believers are the living stones built upon Him (1 Peter 2:4-5). He compared it to the union of husband and wife — Christ is the bridegroom, and the church is His bride, joined to Him in a covenant of love (Ephesians 5:25-32). Each analogy highlights a different aspect of the union: the head and body emphasize vital connection and shared life; the foundation and building emphasize stability and security; the husband and wife emphasize love, intimacy, and covenant commitment.

Flavel was particularly insightful in explaining how union with Christ is effected. He taught that the Holy Spirit is the agent of union. It is the Spirit who regenerates the sinner, creating new spiritual life and joining the believer to Christ. And faith is the instrument of union — the means by which the sinner grasps Christ and is united to Him. Flavel was careful to insist that faith does not create the union; it receives the union that the Spirit creates. Faith is the hand that receives the gift, not the power that produces it.

The practical implications of union with Christ, as Flavel developed them, are far-reaching. First, union with Christ means that all of Christ's riches belong to the believer. Just as a wife shares in her husband's estate, so the believer shares in Christ's spiritual wealth — His righteousness, His holiness, His peace, His joy, His power. Flavel wrote with characteristic warmth: "When you were united to Christ, all that is His became yours. His righteousness is your covering. His blood is your cleansing. His Spirit is your strength. His Father is your Father. His heaven is your home."

Second, union with Christ means that all of the believer's burdens are borne by Christ. Just as a vine bears the weight of its branches, so Christ bears the weight of His people's sins, sorrows, and weaknesses. This does not mean that believers never experience difficulty, but it means they never experience it alone. Christ shares in every burden and sustains His people through every trial.

Third, union with Christ means that the believer's identity is fundamentally changed. The believer is no longer defined by his past, his sin, his failures, or his weaknesses. He is defined by his union with Christ. Paul captured this new identity in Galatians 2:20: the old Paul — the Pharisee, the persecutor, the self-righteous legalist — has been crucified. The new Paul — the apostle, the servant, the beloved child of God — lives by the faith of the Son of God. This transformation of identity is the deepest and most liberating aspect of union with Christ.

The Puritans as a whole placed enormous emphasis on union with Christ. They saw it not as an abstract doctrine but as the most practical truth in the Christian life. When a believer struggles with sin, the answer is not to try harder in his own strength but to abide more deeply in Christ, who is his sanctification. When a believer struggles with guilt, the answer is not to earn forgiveness through good works but to rest in his union with Christ, who is his justification. When a believer struggles with fear, the answer is not to summon more courage but to draw near to Christ, who is his security and peace.

Flavel concluded his treatment of union with Christ with an earnest appeal: "Come to Christ. Abide in Christ. Find in Christ everything your soul needs — pardon for guilt, strength for weakness, comfort for sorrow, wisdom for confusion, and hope for despair. He is the vine; you are the branch. Apart from Him, you can do nothing. In Him, you can do all things. Let this truth be the anchor of your soul and the joy of your heart. You are in Christ, and Christ is in you. What more do you need?

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.

SEGMENT 4: DISCUSSION AND APPLICATION
----------------------------------------

Discussion time. Engage with these questions carefully and honestly.

Discussion Question 1: Jesus says 'without me ye can do nothing' (John 15:5). How does this statement challenge our natural tendency toward self-reliance and independence?

Discussion Question 2: What does it mean practically to 'abide' in Christ? What spiritual disciplines help us maintain a living connection with Him?

Discussion Question 3: Galatians 2:20 describes being 'crucified with Christ.' How does this change the way we think about our old habits, desires, and patterns of sin?

Discussion Question 4: Flavel said that union with Christ means all of Christ's riches belong to the believer. How should this truth affect the way we face our daily challenges and needs?

Discussion Question 5: How does the vine and branches metaphor help us understand the relationship between effort and grace in the Christian life?

Take time with each question. The Puritans valued what they called "holy conference," the practice of discussing spiritual truths together. Do not settle for quick, surface answers. Push deeper. Ask follow-up questions. Listen carefully to one another.

Application is where truth becomes life. It is not enough to know what John 15:1-7 says. We must ask: how does this change the way I live today? How does this shape my thoughts, my words, my actions? The Puritans insisted that a truth that does not transform is a truth that has not been truly understood.

SEGMENT 5: AFTERNOON ACTIVITY
----------------------------------------

The best learning happens when we do something with what we have heard. Here is your activity.

Students will create a visual diagram illustrating the doctrine of union with Christ. Using a large vine and branches drawing as the central image, students will show how each spiritual blessing flows from union with Christ. The vine trunk represents Christ; the branches represent individual believers; the roots represent the Holy Spirit who unites us to Christ; and the fruit represents the spiritual benefits that flow from this union. Students should label specific fruits with their corresponding blessings: justification, sanctification, adoption, peace with God, access to prayer, spiritual power, hope of glory, and others. Around the margin, students should write key Scripture references for each blessing. This visual theology exercise helps students see the centrality and comprehensiveness of union with Christ.

Materials needed: Large poster paper, green and brown markers for the vine, colored markers for fruit and labels, Bibles for reference verses, optional: real grapevine branches for observation

As you complete this activity, think about how it connects to what you studied in today's lesson. The goal is not just to do something, but to reinforce the truths you have been learning. The Puritans believed that education should engage the whole person: mind, heart, and hands.

Take your time with this activity. Quality matters more than speed. When you are finished, reflect on what you learned through the process of doing. Often we understand something far better after we have tried to put it into practice.

SEGMENT 6: CROSS-SUBJECT CONNECTIONS
----------------------------------------

One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

The doctrine of union with Christ has been emphasized in different ways throughout church history. The early church fathers, the medieval mystics, the Reformers (especially Calvin), and the Puritans all developed this doctrine. Study how each tradition understood and expressed the concept of the believer's union with Christ. Note particularly how Calvin made union with Christ the central organizing principle of his theology of salvation in the Institutes of the Christian Religion.

Literature Connection:

The metaphor of the vine and branches belongs to a rich literary tradition of using agricultural imagery to express spiritual truths. Study how poets and writers have used natural imagery — trees, gardens, seasons, planting and harvesting — to express truths about spiritual life, growth, and fruitfulness. Consider also how the concept of identity transformation — becoming someone new — is explored in literature from fairy tales (the ugly duckling becomes a swan) to great novels (the character's journey of self-discovery).

Science Connection:

The relationship between a vine and its branches provides a fascinating botanical study. In viticulture (the science of grape growing), the life of the branch depends entirely on its connection to the vine through the vascular system that carries water, nutrients, and sugars. Study the process of grafting — where a branch from one plant is inserted into the rootstock of another, forming a living union. This biological process provides a remarkable parallel to the spiritual reality of being 'grafted in' to Christ (Romans 11:17-24).

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.

SEGMENT 7: EVENING REVIEW AND MEMORY WORK
----------------------------------------

We have reached the final segment of today's lesson. The Puritans placed great value on review and memorization. They knew that what is not reviewed is soon forgotten. Let us take time now to consolidate what we have learned.

Review Questions:

Review Question 1: In John 15:1-7, what does the vine represent, what do the branches represent, and what does the Father do as the husbandman?

Review Question 2: What does Galatians 2:20 teach about the believer's union with Christ in His death and resurrection?

Review Question 3: According to Ephesians 2:4-6, what three things have happened to believers because of their union with Christ?

Review Question 4: How did Flavel describe union with Christ as the foundation of all other spiritual blessings?

Review Question 5: What three analogies did Flavel use to illustrate union with Christ, and what does each emphasize?

Write out your answers to these questions. Use complete sentences and support your answers with what you learned in today's lesson. If you are unsure of an answer, go back and review that section of the lesson.

Memory Verse:

"I am the vine, ye are the branches: He that abideth in me, and I in him, the same bringeth forth much fruit: for without me ye can do nothing." -- John 15:5

Read this verse aloud five times. Then close your eyes and try to recite it from memory. Write it out by hand on a card or in your journal. The Puritans memorized vast portions of Scripture, and it was one of the great sources of their spiritual strength. Even one verse, truly hidden in the heart, can sustain you in times of trial.

Excellent work today at the Puritan Academy. Remember that all true knowledge leads us to know God better. May your studies continue to deepen your love for Christ and His truth. Goodnight, students.
