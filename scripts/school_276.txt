PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 16
"The Resurrection of Jesus"
Apologetics for Young Christians — Lesson 16


Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we examine what the Apostle Paul called the very foundation of the Christian faith — the bodily resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead. Without the resurrection, Christianity crumbles into dust. But if the resurrection truly happened, then Jesus is who He claimed to be, and the entire Christian message stands vindicated. This is, without exaggeration, the most important event in the history of the world. Let us pray and begin.

Lord God Almighty, Thou who didst raise up Thy Son from the grave on the third day, open the eyes of our understanding as we study this glorious truth. Give us minds that are sharp, hearts that are willing, and spirits that are humble before Thy Word. We ask that this lesson would strengthen the faith of every student who hears it, and that the reality of the risen Christ would become more precious to us than ever before. In the mighty name of Jesus Christ our Lord, Amen.


Students, today we arrive at what is arguably the single most important lesson in our entire apologetics unit. Everything we have studied thus far — the existence of God, the reliability of Scripture, the person and work of Christ — all of it converges upon one decisive question: Did Jesus Christ rise bodily from the dead on the third day after His crucifixion? If He did not, then as the Apostle Paul wrote to the Corinthians in approximately AD 55, "If Christ be not risen, then is our preaching vain, and your faith is also vain... ye are yet in your sins" (1 Corinthians 15:14, 17). But if Christ did rise, then the entire Christian faith is established upon an unshakable foundation, and every promise God has ever made is guaranteed by the power that conquered death itself.

Let us begin by understanding the historical context in which this event took place. Jesus of Nazareth was crucified under the Roman governor Pontius Pilate, most likely on Friday, April 3, AD 33, though some scholars have argued for April 7, AD 30. The crucifixion took place just outside the walls of Jerusalem, at a site called Golgotha, meaning "the place of a skull." The Roman method of crucifixion was one of the most brutal and humiliating forms of execution ever devised by human cruelty. The victim was stripped, beaten, nailed through the wrists and feet to a wooden cross, and left to die a slow, agonizing death by a combination of blood loss, exposure, exhaustion, and ultimately asphyxiation as the body's weight made it increasingly impossible to breathe.

The Gospel accounts record the events of Jesus's death with remarkable specificity. Matthew, who was one of the twelve apostles and an eyewitness, tells us: "And when they were come unto a place called Golgotha, that is to say, a place of a skull, They gave him vinegar to drink mingled with gall: and when he had tasted thereof, he would not drink. And they crucified him, and parted his garments, casting lots: that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the prophet, They parted my garments among them, and upon my vesture did they cast lots" (Matthew 27:33-35). Mark records that Jesus was placed on the cross at "the third hour," which corresponds to approximately nine o'clock in the morning, and that He died at "the ninth hour," approximately three o'clock in the afternoon (Mark 15:25, 34). Luke records His final words: "Father, into thy hands I commend my spirit: and having said thus, he gave up the ghost" (Luke 23:46). John records that Roman soldiers confirmed Jesus's death by piercing His side with a spear, and "forthwith came there out blood and water" (John 19:34), a detail that modern medical science recognizes as consistent with death by cardiac failure and the accumulation of pericardial fluid.

It is critical to establish beyond reasonable doubt that Jesus actually died on the cross, because without a genuine death, there can be no genuine resurrection. The so-called "swoon theory" — the notion that Jesus merely fainted on the cross and later revived in the cool of the tomb — has been thoroughly discredited by medical and historical analysis. The Roman soldiers who carried out crucifixions were professional executioners. Their lives depended on ensuring that the condemned person actually died. The breaking of legs, which was done to the two criminals crucified alongside Jesus but was unnecessary for Jesus because He was already dead, was a standard Roman procedure to hasten death. The spear thrust into Jesus's side was a death-confirming measure, not a superficial wound. Furthermore, even if Jesus had somehow survived the crucifixion — after being scourged, beaten, nailed to a cross for six hours, pierced with a spear, and then wrapped in burial linens and placed in a sealed tomb — the idea that He could have unwrapped Himself, rolled away a massive stone, overpowered Roman guards, and then appeared to His disciples as the triumphant conqueror of death stretches credulity far beyond its breaking point.

After Jesus died, His body was taken down from the cross by Joseph of Arimathaea, a wealthy and respected member of the Jewish Sanhedrin who had become a secret follower of Jesus. Joseph went to Pontius Pilate and requested the body, which Pilate granted after confirming with the centurion that Jesus was indeed dead (Mark 15:43-45). Joseph, together with Nicodemus — another secret disciple and a Pharisee who had come to Jesus by night (John 3:1-2) — wrapped the body in linen cloths with approximately seventy-five pounds of myrrh and aloes, in accordance with Jewish burial customs (John 19:39-40). They placed the body in Joseph's own new tomb, which had been hewn out of solid rock, and they rolled a great stone across the entrance. Matthew tells us that the chief priests and Pharisees then went to Pilate and asked for the tomb to be made secure, saying, "Sir, we remember that that deceiver said, while he was yet alive, After three days I will rise again. Command therefore that the sepulchre be made sure until the third day, lest his disciples come by night, and steal him away, and say unto the people, He is risen from the dead" (Matthew 27:63-64). Pilate responded: "Ye have a watch: go your way, make it as sure as ye can. So they went, and made the sepulchre sure, sealing the stone, and setting a watch" (Matthew 27:65-66).

This detail is enormously significant. The enemies of Jesus took precautions specifically to prevent any claim of resurrection. They sealed the tomb with an official Roman seal — to break which was a capital offense — and posted a guard of Roman soldiers to watch over it. Yet despite all these measures, on the morning of the third day, the tomb was empty.

Now let us turn to the evidence for the resurrection itself. Scholars who have studied this question — both Christian and non-Christian — have identified several facts that are accepted by the vast majority of historians, regardless of their theological commitments. These facts form the bedrock of the historical case for the resurrection.

The first fact is the empty tomb. On the Sunday morning following the crucifixion, a group of women — including Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James, and Salome — went to the tomb to anoint the body with spices. When they arrived, they found the stone rolled away and the tomb empty. Mark records: "And entering into the sepulchre, they saw a young man sitting on the right side, clothed in a long white garment; and they were affrighted. And he saith unto them, Be not affrighted: Ye seek Jesus of Nazareth, which was crucified: he is risen; he is not here: behold the place where they laid him" (Mark 16:5-6).

The empty tomb is one of the best-attested facts in ancient history. Consider the evidence. First, the location of the tomb was known to both friend and foe. Joseph of Arimathaea was a prominent public figure whose tomb could easily be identified and checked. If the tomb had not been empty, the Jewish authorities could have simply produced the body and put an end to the Christian movement before it began. They never did so, because they could not. The body was gone.

Second, the earliest Jewish response to the Christian proclamation was not to deny that the tomb was empty but to offer an alternative explanation for its emptiness. Matthew records that the chief priests bribed the soldiers to say, "His disciples came by night, and stole him away while we slept" (Matthew 28:13). This is a crucial admission. The opponents of Christianity conceded that the tomb was empty. They disputed only the explanation. As the great Puritan theologian John Owen (1616-1683) observed, even the enemies of the gospel became unwitting witnesses to its truth by admitting the fact they sought to deny.

Third, the empty tomb was first discovered by women. In first-century Jewish and Roman culture, the testimony of women was generally not regarded as legally credible. The Jewish historian Josephus wrote that women were not permitted to serve as witnesses in court. If the early Christians had invented the story of the empty tomb, they would never have made women the primary witnesses. The fact that all four Gospels unanimously report women as the first witnesses is a powerful indicator of authenticity. The early church preserved this detail because it was true, even though it was embarrassing by the standards of their culture.

The second major fact is the post-resurrection appearances of Jesus. After His resurrection, Jesus appeared to numerous individuals and groups over a period of forty days. The Apostle Paul provides the earliest written catalogue of these appearances in 1 Corinthians 15:3-8, a passage that most scholars date to within five years of the crucifixion itself — far too early for legendary development. Paul writes: "For I delivered unto you first of all that which I also received, how that Christ died for our sins according to the scriptures; And that he was buried, and that he rose again the third day according to the scriptures: And that he was seen of Cephas, then of the twelve: After that, he was seen of above five hundred brethren at once; of whom the greater part remain unto this day, but some are fallen asleep. After that, he was seen of James; then of all the apostles. And last of all he was seen of me also, as of one born out of due time."

Notice the significance of this passage. Paul is not merely asserting that Jesus rose. He is providing a list of witnesses, including one appearance to more than five hundred people at once, and he is noting that most of those witnesses were still alive at the time he was writing. In effect, Paul is saying: "If you doubt my claim, go and ask them yourself." This is not the language of myth or legend. This is the language of testimony that can be verified.

The appearances were diverse and convincing. Jesus appeared to Mary Magdalene in the garden near the tomb (John 20:11-18). He appeared to the two disciples on the road to Emmaus, who did not recognize Him at first but knew Him when He broke bread with them (Luke 24:13-35). He appeared to the apostles in the upper room, where He invited the doubting Thomas to touch His wounds: "Reach hither thy finger, and behold my hands; and reach hither thy hand, and thrust it into my side: and be not faithless, but believing" (John 20:27). Thomas responded with the greatest confession of faith in the New Testament: "My Lord and my God" (John 20:28). Jesus appeared to seven disciples at the Sea of Galilee, where He prepared breakfast for them on the shore (John 21:1-14). He appeared to James, His own brother, who had not believed in Him during His earthly ministry but who became a leader of the Jerusalem church after the resurrection. And He appeared to Paul himself on the road to Damascus, an encounter that transformed Paul from a violent persecutor of Christians into the greatest missionary the world has ever known.

The Puritan scholar and theologian Thomas Watson (c. 1620-1686), in his magnificent work "A Body of Divinity," wrote of the resurrection: "Christ's rising from the grave is the cause of our rising from the grave of sin. If Christ be risen, then the power of death is broken, the kingdom of darkness is demolished, and the saints shall be glorified." Watson understood that the resurrection was not merely a historical curiosity but the very engine of the Christian life.

The third major fact is the radical transformation of the disciples. Before the resurrection, the disciples were a shattered, terrified, demoralized group. When Jesus was arrested, they all forsook Him and fled (Matthew 26:56). Peter, who had boasted that he would die with Jesus, denied Him three times before a servant girl (Matthew 26:69-75). After the crucifixion, the disciples hid behind locked doors "for fear of the Jews" (John 20:19). These were not men poised to launch a world-changing movement. They were broken, confused, and afraid.

And yet, within weeks, these same men were standing before the very authorities who had crucified their Master, proclaiming boldly: "This Jesus hath God raised up, whereof we all are witnesses" (Acts 2:32). Peter, who had cowered before a servant girl, now stood before the entire Sanhedrin and declared: "Be it known unto you all, and to all the people of Israel, that by the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, whom ye crucified, whom God raised from the dead, even by him doth this man stand here before you whole" (Acts 4:10). What transformed these frightened men into fearless proclaimers of the resurrection? Something happened — something so overwhelming, so undeniable, so transformative that it turned cowards into martyrs. The most plausible explanation is that they actually encountered the risen Christ.

The Puritan minister Richard Baxter (1615-1691) reflected on this transformation in his work "The Saints' Everlasting Rest." Baxter wrote: "Consider what these men endured for the testimony of the resurrection — imprisonment, beatings, poverty, exile, and death — and ask yourself whether any sane person would endure all this for what he knew to be a lie." Baxter's point is unanswerable. People will die for what they believe to be true, even if they are mistaken. But people do not die for what they know to be a fraud. The apostles were in a unique position to know whether the resurrection had actually occurred. They were eyewitnesses. If they had fabricated the story, they would have known it was false. And yet every single one of them maintained his testimony under the most extreme persecution, and nearly all of them died as martyrs rather than recant.

Church tradition records the fates of the apostles. Peter was crucified upside down in Rome under the Emperor Nero, around AD 64-68. Andrew was crucified on an X-shaped cross in Patras, Greece. James the son of Zebedee was beheaded by King Herod Agrippa I in Jerusalem around AD 44, as recorded in Acts 12:2. James the brother of Jesus was thrown from the pinnacle of the Temple in Jerusalem and then beaten to death with a fuller's club around AD 62. Philip was crucified in Hierapolis, in modern-day Turkey. Bartholomew was flayed alive and then crucified in Armenia. Thomas was speared to death in India, where he had gone as a missionary. Matthew was killed by a sword wound in Ethiopia. Thaddaeus was crucified in Edessa. Simon the Zealot was sawed in half in Persia. And the Apostle Paul was beheaded in Rome under Nero. Only John, the beloved disciple, is believed to have died of natural causes, and even he endured exile to the island of Patmos, where he received the Revelation.

Now let us consider and refute the alternative explanations that skeptics have proposed throughout history. If the resurrection did not happen, how do we explain the empty tomb, the post-resurrection appearances, and the transformation of the disciples?

The first alternative is the stolen body theory. This is the oldest objection, dating back to the very morning of the resurrection, when the Jewish authorities bribed the soldiers to spread this story (Matthew 28:11-15). But consider the difficulties. The tomb was sealed with a Roman seal and guarded by Roman soldiers — trained, disciplined, professional military men whose lives depended on fulfilling their duty. The penalty for a Roman soldier who fell asleep on watch was execution. Are we to believe that the entire guard fell asleep simultaneously, and that while they slept, the disciples — a group of frightened, demoralized fishermen — rolled away a massive stone, extracted the body, and escaped without waking a single soldier? Furthermore, if the soldiers were asleep, how could they know that the disciples stole the body? A sleeping witness is no witness at all.

The great Puritan commentator Matthew Henry (1662-1714) made this very observation in his celebrated commentary on the whole Bible: "If the soldiers were asleep, they could not know who came; if they were awake, they might have hindered the taking away of the body. Their sleeping was therefore improbable and inconsistent with itself." Henry's logic is airtight. The stolen body theory fails because it requires us to believe a series of impossibilities.

The second alternative is the hallucination theory — the notion that the disciples did not actually see the risen Jesus but merely experienced hallucinations. This theory became popular in the nineteenth century, but it has been largely abandoned by serious scholars because it faces insurmountable problems. Hallucinations are individual psychological phenomena. They do not occur simultaneously to groups of people. Jesus appeared not just to one person but to groups — to the twelve, to the five hundred, to the disciples on the road to Emmaus. Group hallucinations are a psychological impossibility.

Furthermore, hallucinations tend to occur among people who are expecting or hoping for a particular experience. The disciples were not expecting a resurrection. They were devastated and demoralized. When the women first reported that the tomb was empty, the apostles "believed them not" (Luke 24:11). Thomas refused to believe until he had placed his fingers in the nail prints (John 20:25). These are not the psychological profiles of people prone to wish-fulfillment hallucinations.

Additionally, hallucinations do not explain the empty tomb. Even if the disciples had collectively hallucinated (which, again, is psychologically impossible), the body would still have been in the tomb. The Jewish authorities could have simply produced it and ended the movement.

The third alternative is the myth theory — the claim that the story of the resurrection developed gradually as a legend over many decades or centuries, much as the legends of other ancient heroes developed. This theory fails for several reasons. First, the earliest Christian proclamation of the resurrection can be dated to within a few years of the event itself. The creedal formula that Paul quotes in 1 Corinthians 15:3-5 is universally recognized by scholars — including skeptical ones like Gerd Ludemann — as originating within the first five years after the crucifixion. This is far too early for legendary development, which typically requires generations to take shape.

Second, the Gospels bear none of the hallmarks of mythological literature. They are written in a sober, restrained, reportorial style. They include embarrassing details that no myth-maker would have invented — the cowardice of the disciples, the primacy of women as witnesses, the doubts of Thomas, and the failure of Jesus's own family to believe in Him during His ministry. C.S. Lewis (1898-1963), who was a professor of medieval and Renaissance literature at Oxford and Cambridge and therefore an expert in ancient literary genres, observed: "I have been reading poems, romances, vision-literature, legends, myths all my life. I know what they are like. I know that not one of them is like this." Lewis recognized that the Gospel narratives are qualitatively different from mythological literature. They read like eyewitness testimony because that is precisely what they are.

The fourth alternative is the wrong tomb theory — the suggestion that the women simply went to the wrong tomb on Sunday morning and found it empty. This theory is almost beneath refutation. Joseph of Arimathaea knew where his own tomb was. The women had watched the burial and knew the location (Mark 15:47). The Jewish authorities knew where the tomb was because they had posted a guard. If the women had gone to the wrong tomb, any number of people could have corrected the error by directing them to the right one. And, of course, the authorities could have produced the body from the correct tomb and ended the Christian movement immediately.

The fifth alternative is the conspiracy theory — the idea that the disciples colluded together to fabricate the entire story, knowing full well that it was false. This theory was popular among eighteenth-century skeptics but has been almost universally abandoned by modern scholarship. The conspiracy theory fails because it cannot explain the willingness of the apostles to suffer and die for their testimony. As we noted earlier, people do not die for what they know to be a lie. A conspiracy requires secrecy, coordination, and a plausible motive. The disciples had no motive to fabricate a resurrection. They gained nothing by it — no wealth, no power, no prestige. Instead, they gained persecution, imprisonment, and death. Furthermore, a conspiracy involving dozens of individuals over decades would inevitably unravel. Someone would have cracked under pressure. Someone would have recanted to save his life. But not a single apostle ever recanted. Not one.

Beyond these alternative explanations, we should also consider two additional pieces of evidence that powerfully confirm the resurrection: the conversion of skeptics and the fulfillment of Old Testament prophecy.

The conversion of James, the brother of Jesus, is particularly striking. During Jesus's earthly ministry, James did not believe in Him. The Gospel of John records that "neither did his brethren believe in him" (John 7:5). Mark records an occasion when Jesus's family tried to seize Him, thinking He was out of His mind (Mark 3:21). James was a skeptic — an unbeliever who had grown up with Jesus and was not impressed by His claims. And yet, after the resurrection, James became not only a believer but the leader of the Jerusalem church and the author of the New Testament epistle that bears his name. What transformed this skeptic into a pillar of the church? Paul tells us plainly: the risen Christ appeared to him (1 Corinthians 15:7). Only a face-to-face encounter with the resurrected Jesus can explain this dramatic reversal.

The conversion of Paul — Saul of Tarsus — is equally remarkable. Paul was not merely an unbeliever; he was an active enemy of the Christian faith. He describes himself as "a Pharisee, the son of a Pharisee" (Acts 23:6), "circumcised the eighth day, of the stock of Israel, of the tribe of Benjamin, an Hebrew of the Hebrews; as touching the law, a Pharisee; Concerning zeal, persecuting the church; touching the righteousness which is in the law, blameless" (Philippians 3:5-6). Paul presided over the stoning of Stephen, the first Christian martyr (Acts 7:58-8:1). He went house to house in Jerusalem, dragging Christians to prison (Acts 8:3). He obtained letters from the high priest authorizing him to arrest Christians in Damascus (Acts 9:1-2). This was a man passionately dedicated to the destruction of Christianity. And yet, on the road to Damascus, he encountered the risen Christ in a blinding light and heard the words: "Saul, Saul, why persecutest thou me?" (Acts 9:4). From that moment, the persecutor became the apostle. The enemy became the champion. Paul spent the rest of his life traveling the Roman Empire, planting churches, writing epistles that would become the theological backbone of the New Testament, and suffering beyond measure for the faith he had once sought to destroy. What explains this transformation? Paul himself gives us the answer: "Last of all he was seen of me also" (1 Corinthians 15:8).

The resurrection was also foretold in the Old Testament centuries before it occurred. The prophet David wrote in Psalm 16:10: "For thou wilt not leave my soul in hell; neither wilt thou suffer thine Holy One to see corruption." Peter quotes this very passage in his Pentecost sermon, arguing that David was speaking prophetically of the Messiah, whose body would not see decay but would be raised from the grave (Acts 2:25-31). The prophet Isaiah, writing approximately seven hundred years before Christ, described the suffering and death of the Messiah in astonishing detail in Isaiah 53 and then added this remarkable note: "He shall see his seed, he shall prolong his days, and the pleasure of the Lord shall prosper in his hand" (Isaiah 53:10). How can a man who has been "cut off out of the land of the living" (Isaiah 53:8) go on to "prolong his days" and see the fruit of his suffering? Only through resurrection. The prophet Hosea wrote: "After two days will he revive us: in the third day he will raise us up, and we shall live in his sight" (Hosea 6:2). Jesus Himself appealed to the sign of the prophet Jonah, who was three days and three nights in the belly of the great fish, as a prophetic type of His own burial and resurrection (Matthew 12:39-40). The resurrection was not an afterthought or an improvisation. It was the planned culmination of God's redemptive purpose, foretold by the prophets, accomplished by the Son, and confirmed by the Spirit.

None of the alternative explanations can account for the full range of evidence. Only the resurrection itself — the actual, bodily, historical rising of Jesus Christ from the dead — explains the empty tomb, the post-resurrection appearances, the transformation of the disciples, the conversion of skeptics like James and Paul, the fulfillment of Old Testament prophecy, the emergence of the Christian church, and the willingness of the apostles to suffer and die for their testimony.

Let us now consider the theological significance of the resurrection. The resurrection is not merely a remarkable historical event. It is the cornerstone of the entire Christian faith and the source of every Christian hope. Consider what the resurrection accomplishes.

First, the resurrection vindicates the claims of Jesus. During His earthly ministry, Jesus made extraordinary claims — that He was the Son of God, that He had the authority to forgive sins, that He would judge the world at the last day, that He was one with the Father. If Jesus had remained in the grave, these claims would have been exposed as the delusions of a madman or the lies of a deceiver. But the resurrection demonstrates that God the Father set His seal of approval upon everything Jesus said and did. As Paul writes in Romans 1:4, Jesus was "declared to be the Son of God with power, according to the spirit of holiness, by the resurrection from the dead."

Second, the resurrection secures our justification. Paul writes in Romans 4:25 that Jesus "was delivered for our offences, and was raised again for our justification." The death of Christ paid the penalty for our sins. The resurrection of Christ demonstrates that the payment was accepted. It is God's receipt, His confirmation that the debt has been paid in full. The Puritan theologian John Flavel (1627-1691) put it beautifully: "The resurrection of Christ is the Amen of all His promises." Every promise that Christ made — forgiveness of sins, eternal life, the gift of the Holy Spirit, a place in the Father's house — is guaranteed by the resurrection.

Third, the resurrection guarantees our future resurrection. Paul argues this point extensively in 1 Corinthians 15. "But now is Christ risen from the dead, and become the firstfruits of them that slept. For since by man came death, by man came also the resurrection of the dead. For as in Adam all die, even so in Christ shall all be made alive" (1 Corinthians 15:20-22). Christ's resurrection is the firstfruits — the initial harvest that guarantees the full harvest to come. Because He rose, all who belong to Him will rise. Death is not the end. The grave does not have the final word. For the Christian, death is merely a doorway into the presence of God, and on the last day, even our mortal bodies will be raised in glory.

Fourth, the resurrection provides the power for Christian living. Paul's great ambition, expressed in Philippians 3:10, was "That I may know him, and the power of his resurrection." The same power that raised Christ from the dead is at work in every believer. It is the power to overcome sin, to endure suffering, to love our enemies, to persevere in faith, and to live in the hope of glory. The Christian life is not lived in our own strength. It is lived in the power of the risen Christ.

The Puritan pastor Stephen Charnock (1628-1680), in his monumental work "The Existence and Attributes of God," wrote at length about the power displayed in the resurrection: "The power that raised Christ from the dead was the most glorious display of omnipotence since the creation of the world. For in creation, God brought something from nothing; but in the resurrection, God brought life from death, glory from shame, and victory from defeat. The resurrection is the crowning act of divine power, and it is this power that sustains every believer in every trial."

Fifth, the resurrection transforms our understanding of suffering and death. Because of the resurrection, Christians do not grieve as those who have no hope (1 Thessalonians 4:13). We face death with confidence because we know that death has been defeated. Paul's triumphant declaration rings through the ages: "O death, where is thy sting? O grave, where is thy victory? The sting of death is sin; and the strength of sin is the law. But thanks be to God, which giveth us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ" (1 Corinthians 15:55-57). The resurrection does not eliminate suffering in this present life, but it transforms its meaning. Our suffering is temporary; our glory is eternal. Our afflictions are light and momentary; the weight of glory that awaits us is beyond comparison (2 Corinthians 4:17).

Let us also reflect upon how the resurrection has been celebrated and cherished throughout church history. From the very beginning, Christians gathered on the first day of the week — the Lord's Day, the day of resurrection — to worship the risen Christ. The early church father Ignatius of Antioch (c. AD 35-108), who was a disciple of the Apostle John, wrote: "Those who lived according to the old order have come to a new hope, no longer observing the Sabbath but living according to the Lord's Day, on which our life sprang up through Him and through His death." The resurrection was not merely a doctrine the early Christians believed; it was a reality they experienced in their worship, their fellowship, and their daily lives.

The great church father Augustine of Hippo (AD 354-430) wrote in his "City of God": "The resurrection of the body of Christ, and His ascension into heaven, is the new testament or covenant, the fulfillment of the promise made to Abraham, that through his seed all the nations of the earth should be blessed." Augustine understood that the resurrection was not an isolated event but the climax of God's redemptive plan — the fulfillment of promises stretching back thousands of years to the covenant with Abraham.

During the Reformation, the resurrection held a central place in Protestant theology. Martin Luther (1483-1546) preached powerfully on the resurrection throughout his ministry. In one of his Easter sermons, Luther declared: "Our Lord has written the promise of the resurrection not in books alone, but in every leaf in springtime." For Luther, the entire created order testified to the power of the God who raises the dead.

John Calvin (1509-1564), in his "Institutes of the Christian Religion," devoted extensive attention to the resurrection, arguing that it is inseparable from the work of redemption. Calvin wrote: "We see that our whole salvation and all its parts are comprehended in Christ. We should therefore take care not to derive the least portion of it from anywhere else. If we seek salvation, we are taught by the very name of Jesus that it is 'of him.' If we seek the gifts of the Spirit, they will be found in his anointing. If we seek strength, it lies in his dominion; if purity, in his conception; if gentleness, it appears in his birth... If we seek redemption, it lies in his passion; if acquittal, in his condemnation; if remission of the curse, in his cross; if satisfaction, in his sacrifice; if purification, in his blood; if reconciliation, in his descent into hell; if mortification of the flesh, in his tomb; if newness of life, in his resurrection."

The Puritans, who inherited and deepened the Reformation's theology, were particularly rich in their meditations on the resurrection. Samuel Rutherford (1600-1661), the great Scottish theologian and author of the incomparable "Letters," wrote from his imprisonment in Aberdeen: "The resurrection of Christ is the morning star of a day that shall never end, the dawn of an eternity of joy that shall swallow up every night of weeping." Rutherford, who suffered greatly for his faith, drew his strength and comfort from the reality of the risen Christ.

Thomas Brooks (1608-1680), another beloved Puritan author, wrote in his work "Precious Remedies Against Satan's Devices": "Remember, Christian, that thy Saviour who died for thee is not dead but risen. He who bore thy sins upon the cross now bears thy name upon His heart in heaven. The resurrection is thy assurance that every sin is pardoned, every promise is sure, and every enemy is defeated."

Students, as we draw this lesson toward its conclusion, let us consider the practical implications of the resurrection for our daily lives.

First, the resurrection calls us to confident faith. We do not believe in a dead hero or a distant memory. We believe in a living Lord who is present with His people by His Spirit. When doubts assail you, when the world mocks your faith, when suffering presses in — remember that you serve a risen Saviour. He who conquered death can certainly handle whatever challenges you face.

Second, the resurrection calls us to bold witness. The early Christians turned the world upside down with one simple message: "He is risen." That message has not lost its power. When you share your faith with classmates, neighbors, or family members, you are not defending a dead philosophy. You are proclaiming a living Person. Take courage from the apostles, who went from fear to fearlessness because they had met the risen Christ.

Third, the resurrection calls us to eternal perspective. This world is not all there is. The sufferings of this present time are not worthy to be compared with the glory which shall be revealed in us (Romans 8:18). Because Christ rose, we too shall rise. Our bodies, which are sown in weakness, will be raised in power. Our mortal frames, which are subject to disease and decay, will be transformed into glorious, imperishable bodies like the resurrection body of Christ Himself (Philippians 3:21). This hope sustains us through every trial and gives meaning to every moment of our earthly pilgrimage.

Fourth, the resurrection calls us to holy living. Paul draws this connection explicitly in Romans 6:4: "Therefore we are buried with him by baptism into death: that like as Christ was raised up from the dead by the glory of the Father, even so we also should walk in newness of life." The resurrection is not merely a future hope; it is a present power. Because Christ rose, we are called to live as new creatures — putting off the old nature with its sinful desires and putting on the new nature that is being renewed in the image of Christ (Colossians 3:9-10).

Fifth, the resurrection calls us to unshakable hope in the face of death. For the Christian, death has been defanged. It is no longer the king of terrors but a servant that ushers us into the presence of our King. The Puritan Richard Sibbes (1577-1635) wrote: "Death is but a passage out of this prison into a palace, out of a sea of troubles into a haven of rest, out of a crowd of enemies into an innumerable company of true, loving, and faithful friends — into the presence of God the Father and of our Lord Jesus Christ." Sibbes could write these words with confidence because of the resurrection. Christ has gone before us through the valley of the shadow of death, and He has emerged triumphant on the other side.

Let us close our lesson with the magnificent words of the Apostle Paul in Romans 8:38-39: "For I am persuaded, that neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor principalities, nor powers, nor things present, nor things to come, Nor height, nor depth, nor any other creature, shall be able to separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord." This is the confidence that the resurrection gives us. Nothing in all creation — not death, not life, not any power in heaven or earth — can separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus. The resurrection is not just an event in the past. It is the guarantee of our future and the power of our present. Christ is risen. He is risen indeed.

The resurrection of Jesus Christ is the most important event in the history of the world. It is the foundation of our faith, the guarantee of our salvation, the power of our daily lives, and the hope of our eternal future. No amount of skeptical theorizing can overturn the testimony of the empty tomb, the eyewitnesses, the transformed lives of the apostles, and the prophetic witness of the Old Testament. As Thomas Watson wrote, "The resurrection of Christ is the Amen of all His promises." Every promise of forgiveness, every assurance of eternal life, every hope of glory rests securely upon this one magnificent truth: on the third day, God raised His Son from the dead. The stone was rolled away not to let Christ out but to let the world see that He was already gone — risen, victorious, and alive forevermore. Christ is risen from the dead. Build your life upon this unshakable truth, and let the power of His resurrection animate every thought, every word, and every deed of your earthly pilgrimage. Until next time, students.
