PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Doctrine Deep-Dive, Day 12
"The Doctrine of Sanctification"
Growing in Holiness Through Puritan Eyes


Good evening. A.W. Tozer said that what comes into our minds when we think about God is the most important thing about us. Tonight we think carefully, biblically, and reverently about a great doctrine of the faith.

Tonight we draw from the work of Multiple (Owen, Flavel, Brooks), specifically from "Various works on sanctification." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is 1 Thessalonians 4:3:

"For this is the will of God, even your sanctification, that ye should abstain from fornication."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


If justification is the act by which God declares us righteous, sanctification is the process by which God makes us righteous. The two must never be confused, but neither must they be separated. Every justified person is being sanctified; every sanctified person has been justified. Together, they comprise the full work of salvation.

John Owen, in his treatise on sanctification within 'Pneumatologia' (his work on the Holy Spirit), defines sanctification as 'the immediate work of the Spirit of God on the souls of believers, purifying and cleansing their natures from the pollution and uncleanness of sin, renewing in them the image of God, and thereby enabling them, from a spiritual and habitual principle of grace, to yield obedience unto God.' Several elements of this definition deserve careful attention.

First, sanctification is the work of the Spirit. Just as the Spirit regenerated the soul in the new birth, so the Spirit continues the work of transformation throughout the believer's life. Owen insists that sanctification is not a human achievement but a divine work. The believer cooperates with the Spirit (this is not passive), but the initiative, the power, and the completion belong to God.

Second, sanctification involves purification. The believer's nature, corrupted by the fall, is progressively cleansed. Owen describes this as a two-sided process: the 'putting off' of the old man and the 'putting on' of the new. Mortification (killing sin) and vivification (strengthening grace) are the two movements of sanctification, and both are necessary.

Third, sanctification renews the image of God. Humanity was created in God's image (Genesis 1:27), and the fall defaced that image without destroying it. In sanctification, the Spirit restores what sin disfigured. The believer grows in knowledge, holiness, and righteousness (Colossians 3:10; Ephesians 4:24), progressively reflecting the character of Christ.

John Flavel, in 'The Mystery of Providence,' brings a different angle: he emphasizes the role of divine providence in sanctification. Flavel argues that God uses the circumstances of life — trials, losses, successes, relationships, and even suffering — as instruments of sanctification. The believer who understands this will approach adversity differently. Instead of asking 'Why is this happening to me?' he will ask 'What is God teaching me through this?' Flavel writes that 'sanctified afflictions are spiritual promotions.' The fire that burns also purifies.

Thomas Brooks, in 'Precious Remedies Against Satan's Devices,' addresses the obstacles to sanctification. Brooks identifies the devil as the great enemy of the believer's holiness, constantly seeking to hinder the Spirit's work. Satan's devices include making sin appear small, making holiness appear burdensome, presenting the falls of others as reasons for complacency, and whispering that God's grace covers ongoing sin. Brooks provides 'remedies' for each device, all of which center on the believer's engagement with Scripture, prayer, and the fellowship of the church.

The Puritans were united in their understanding that sanctification is progressive. It is not instant (contra some holiness movements) nor is it automatic (contra some forms of quietism). It is a genuine process that takes time, involves setbacks, and requires diligent effort in cooperation with the Spirit. The Westminster Confession captures this beautifully: sanctification is 'imperfect in this life; there abiding still some remnants of corruption in every part; whence ariseth a continual and irreconcilable war, the flesh lusting against the Spirit, and the Spirit against the flesh.'

This should both humble and encourage you. Humble you, because you will never achieve perfection in this life. The fight against sin will continue until glory. Encourage you, because the same Spirit who began the work will carry it on. You are not left to sanctify yourself. The God who justified you is faithfully at work, through every trial and every triumph, conforming you to the image of His Son.


Let these questions search your heart. Answer honestly before God.

Question 1: Do I understand sanctification as primarily the Spirit's work in which I cooperate, or do I tend to view it as my own moral project?

Question 2: Can I identify ways that God has used difficult circumstances in my life as instruments of sanctification?

Question 3: Which of Satan's devices identified by Brooks am I most vulnerable to in my own pursuit of holiness?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Holy Spirit, I thank You that the work of sanctification is Yours, not mine. Continue Your purifying, renewing, transforming work in my soul. Use every circumstance, every trial, every blessing to conform me to the image of Christ. I rest in Your faithfulness. Amen.

Goodnight. Remember: the same God who spoke to the Puritans speaks to you.
