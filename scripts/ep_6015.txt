PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"Blessed Is He Whose Transgression Is Forgiven"
June 15, 2026

---

"Blessed is he whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered. Blessed is the man unto whom the Lord imputeth not iniquity, and in whose spirit there is no guile. When I kept silence, my bones waxed old through my roaring all the day long. For day and night thy hand was heavy upon me: my moisture is turned into the drought of summer. Selah." -- Psalm 32:1-4

Of all the blessings that God bestows upon the sons of men, there is none that can compare with the forgiveness of sins. Health is a great blessing, but the healthiest body must one day return to the dust. Wealth is a pleasant thing, but riches take to themselves wings and fly away. Friendship is precious, but even the dearest companion cannot accompany us through the valley of the shadow of death. But the forgiveness of sins -- this is a blessing that reaches into eternity, that outlasts the stars, that remains when heaven and earth have passed away. "Blessed is he whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered."

The apostle Paul quotes this psalm in the fourth chapter of Romans as proof that justification is by faith alone, apart from works. David does not say, "Blessed is the man who has never sinned." Nor does he say, "Blessed is the man who has made sufficient atonement for his sins by his own efforts." He says, "Blessed is the man unto whom the Lord imputeth not iniquity." Imputation is a legal term, a courtroom word. It means to charge to one's account. And what David is declaring, by the inspiration of the Holy Spirit, is that there is a blessedness that belongs to the man against whose account God has not charged his sin. The sin exists. It has been committed. It is real and grievous and worthy of condemnation. But it has not been charged to the sinner's account. It has been covered, forgiven, removed, blotted out. And the ground of this astonishing transaction is not the sinner's repentance, though repentance is certainly necessary, but the sovereign grace of God who has chosen to pardon.

Spurgeon called this psalm one of the Pauline psalms, because it teaches the doctrine of justification by faith with a clarity that anticipates the New Testament by a thousand years. David understood, by divine revelation, what many in his day did not understand: that a man is not made righteous before God by the works of the law, but by the free grace of God that covers his sin and refuses to charge his iniquity against him. And the vehicle of this grace is faith -- a faith that is characterised by transparency and honesty before God, a faith in whose spirit there is no guile.

"When I kept silence, my bones waxed old through my roaring all the day long." Here David describes the misery of unconfessed sin, and it is a description that many believers will recognise from their own experience. He had sinned, and he knew it. But instead of confessing his sin to God, he tried to suppress it, to cover it up, to pretend that it had not happened. And the result was not peace but torment. His body wasted away under the burden of hidden guilt. His spirit groaned day and night under the heavy hand of divine conviction. The moisture of his life -- his joy, his energy, his vitality -- dried up like a stream in the desert heat.

Thomas Watson wrote with piercing insight upon the torment of a guilty conscience, calling it a foretaste of hell upon earth. The man who carries unconfessed sin in his heart carries a burning coal in his bosom. He may try to ignore it, to distract himself from it, to drown it in pleasure or activity, but it will not be silenced. The voice of conscience, which is the voice of God's law written upon the heart, speaks with an authority that cannot be gainsaid. And the longer a man resists that voice, the louder it becomes, until at last the pain of silence exceeds the pain of confession, and the soul breaks open before God in penitent surrender.

"For day and night thy hand was heavy upon me." This is the hand of divine discipline, and it is a hand of love, though it feels like a hand of wrath. God does not leave His children to wallow comfortably in their sin. He presses upon them. He makes the way of transgression hard. He removes their comforts, disturbs their peace, troubles their consciences, and makes them miserable in their sin, all for the gracious purpose of driving them to repentance. John Owen taught that one of the surest marks of genuine adoption is the experience of divine chastisement. The child whom God loves, He corrects. And the correction is designed not to destroy but to restore, not to punish vindictively but to heal redemptively.

The psalm goes on, in the fifth verse, to describe the moment of confession and the flood of relief that follows. "I acknowledged my sin unto thee, and mine iniquity have I not hid. I said, I will confess my transgressions unto the Lord; and thou forgavest the iniquity of my sin." How simple it sounds, and yet how difficult it is for the proud heart to do. To stop making excuses, to stop shifting the blame, to stop minimising the offence, and simply to say, "I have sinned" -- this is the hardest and the most liberating thing a human being can do. And the moment the confession leaves the lips, the forgiveness comes. Not after a probationary period. Not after sufficient penance has been performed. Not after the sinner has proved himself worthy of pardon. Immediately, freely, fully. "Thou forgavest the iniquity of my sin."

Let us take this psalm as an invitation to come out of the shadows of hidden guilt and into the light of divine forgiveness. Let us confess our transgressions to the Lord, holding nothing back, hiding nothing, disguising nothing. For the blessedness that David describes is not reserved for some special class of saints. It is offered to every sinner who will come to God with an honest heart and a contrite spirit. Blessed is he whose transgression is forgiven, whose sin is covered. May that blessedness be ours this day and every day. Amen.
