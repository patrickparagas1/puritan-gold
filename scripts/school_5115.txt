PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 15
"The Problem of Evil and Suffering"
Apologetics for Young Christians — Lesson 15

---

Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we confront the most emotionally powerful objection to the Christian faith — the problem of evil and suffering. Let us pray and begin.

Lord, You are good, and yet suffering fills the world You made. Help us to think clearly and compassionately about this difficult question, and to find in Your Word answers that satisfy both the mind and the heart. In Christ's name, Amen.

--- Lesson ---

Of all the objections raised against the Christian faith, none is more common or more emotionally powerful than the problem of evil. The argument is usually stated like this: If God is all-powerful, He could prevent evil. If God is all-good, He would want to prevent evil. But evil exists. Therefore, either God is not all-powerful, or He is not all-good, or He does not exist.

This is a serious objection, and Christians should not dismiss it lightly. People who raise this question are often not being clever or hostile — they are hurting. They have experienced genuine suffering and are genuinely struggling to reconcile their pain with the existence of a loving God. Our response must be both intellectually rigorous and pastorally sensitive.

Let us begin with the logical version of the problem. Is there a logical contradiction between the existence of God and the existence of evil? The answer is no. The objection assumes that an all-powerful, all-good God would have no sufficient reason for permitting evil. But this assumption is unjustified. It is entirely possible — and indeed the Christian faith teaches — that God has morally sufficient reasons for permitting evil that we, with our limited perspective, may not fully understand.

Consider an analogy. A child visits the doctor and receives a painful vaccination. From the child's perspective, a good and powerful parent is allowing something painful to happen. The child may conclude that the parent is either not loving or not powerful enough to prevent the pain. But the child is wrong. The parent has a morally sufficient reason for permitting the pain: the vaccine will protect the child from a far greater harm. The parent's perspective is larger than the child's.

In the same way, God's perspective is infinitely larger than ours. He sees the end from the beginning. He knows how every event — including every evil event — fits into the grand narrative of His purposes. This does not mean that suffering is trivial or that God is indifferent to our pain. It means that He is working on a canvas so vast that we cannot see the whole picture from our limited vantage point.

The Christian faith provides several resources for addressing the problem of evil more specifically.

First, the doctrine of free will. God created human beings with genuine freedom — the ability to choose between good and evil. This freedom is a great good, but it comes with the possibility of being misused. Much of the evil in the world is the result of human beings making wicked choices — war, crime, oppression, cruelty. God could have created a world without free agents, but such a world would also have been a world without love, since love requires freedom. The possibility of evil is the price of freedom, and freedom is the prerequisite for love.

Second, the doctrine of the fall. The Christian worldview does not teach that God created a world of suffering. He created a world that was "very good." Suffering entered the world through the rebellion of the first human beings against their Creator. The pain, disease, natural disasters, and death that we experience are consequences of the fall — a world that has been broken by sin. The problem of evil, from a Christian perspective, is not a problem of God's design but a problem of humanity's rebellion.

Third, the doctrine of redemption. God is not a passive spectator of our suffering. He has entered into it. In the incarnation, God the Son took on human flesh and experienced the full range of human suffering — hunger, thirst, fatigue, rejection, betrayal, physical torture, and death. The cross of Christ is God's ultimate answer to the problem of evil. On the cross, God did not merely explain suffering — He bore it. He took the worst evil the world could produce and transformed it into the greatest good: the salvation of sinners.

Fourth, the doctrine of future glory. The Bible promises that evil and suffering are temporary. One day God will set all things right. He will wipe away every tear, heal every wound, punish every injustice, and establish a new heaven and a new earth where righteousness dwells. The problem of evil is real, but it is not the final chapter. The final chapter is glory.

It is worth noting that the problem of evil is actually a greater difficulty for atheism than for Christianity. If there is no God, then there is no objective standard of good and evil, and therefore no basis for calling anything "evil" in the first place. The very concept of evil — the sense that something is genuinely wrong with the world — presupposes a moral standard that only makes sense if God exists.

--- Review Questions ---

Review Question 1: State the logical problem of evil in your own words. Why is the assumption behind it unjustified?

Review Question 2: How do the doctrines of free will, the fall, and redemption help address the problem of evil?

Review Question 3: Why might the problem of evil actually be a greater difficulty for atheism than for Christianity?

--- Activity ---

Write a compassionate letter to an imaginary friend who is struggling with the problem of evil because of a personal tragedy. Your letter should acknowledge their pain genuinely and without dismissing it, then gently share two or three of the Christian resources for addressing the problem of evil discussed in today's lesson. Focus on being both truthful and kind. Share your letter with a parent or mentor for feedback.

--- Closing ---

The problem of evil is not a question to be answered glibly. It requires intellectual honesty, pastoral sensitivity, and a deep trust in the goodness of God. The cross of Christ is the ultimate answer — God's demonstration that He has not abandoned us in our suffering but has entered into it and overcome it. Until next time, students.
