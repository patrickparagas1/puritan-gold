PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Guided Puritan Reading, Day 27
"A Lifting Up for the Downcast"
William Bridge — Counsel for Discouraged Christians

---

Good evening, friend. Tonight we open a book that has brought comfort to discouraged believers for nearly four centuries: William Bridge's "A Lifting Up for the Downcast." Bridge was one of the great Puritan pastors, a man who knew firsthand the sorrows of exile and persecution, and who wrote with extraordinary tenderness for those whose spirits had been crushed by the weight of life and the accusations of the enemy. If you are weary tonight, this is a word for you.

--- Scripture ---

Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Psalm 42:11:

"Why art thou cast down, O my soul? and why art thou disquieted within me? hope thou in God: for I shall yet praise him, who is the health of my countenance, and my God."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.

--- Guided Reading ---

William Bridge served as a pastor in Norwich and later in exile in the Netherlands during the turbulent years of the English Civil War. He knew what it was to lose everything — his pulpit, his country, his livelihood — for the sake of conscience. And it was out of this crucible of suffering that he preached the thirteen sermons that would become "A Lifting Up for the Downcast," published in 1649.

Bridge takes his text from Psalm 42:11, where the psalmist addresses his own downcast soul. Bridge observes that it is possible for a genuine believer — a true child of God — to be deeply discouraged. This is not a sign of unbelief. It is a common experience in the Christian life. The Psalms are full of it. The prophets knew it. Even the apostle Paul confessed to being "pressed out of measure, above strength, insomuch that we despaired even of life" (2 Corinthians 1:8). Bridge's first gift to the discouraged reader is simply this: you are not alone, and your discouragement does not disqualify you from the family of God.

Bridge then identifies the most common causes of spiritual discouragement. The first is the greatness of sin. Many believers are crushed by the weight of their past transgressions or their present failures. They look at their sins and conclude that they are too far gone for grace. Bridge answers with the greatness of Christ. He writes that the blood of Jesus Christ is sufficient for all sin — not merely for small sins or respectable sins but for the worst sins a human being can commit. If your sin is great, your Savior is greater. If your guilt is deep, His mercy is deeper still.

The second cause of discouragement is the weakness of grace. The believer looks at his feeble prayers, his wavering faith, his inconsistent obedience, and concludes that his graces are too weak to be genuine. Bridge responds by pointing to the nature of saving grace. True grace, he argues, is not measured by its strength but by its reality. A small seed of living faith is infinitely more valuable than a great show of dead profession. Richard Sibbes made the same point when he wrote that Christ will not break the bruised reed. The weakest grace, if it is true grace, is as much the work of the Spirit as the strongest.

The third cause is the hiddenness of God. Sometimes the believer prays and hears nothing. He reads Scripture and feels nothing. He worships and senses nothing. God seems absent, distant, silent. Bridge acknowledges the agony of this experience and then reminds the reader of a crucial distinction: God's felt presence is not the same as His actual presence. The sun does not cease to exist when clouds cover it. God does not cease to love His children when they cannot feel His love. Bridge counsels patience and perseverance: keep praying, keep reading, keep worshiping, even when you feel nothing. The God who hides His face for a moment will return with everlasting kindness.

The fourth cause is affliction and suffering. Many believers are discouraged not by spiritual failures but by temporal hardships — illness, poverty, bereavement, persecution. Bridge does not minimize these sufferings. He takes them seriously. But he sets them against the backdrop of eternity and the purposes of God. Affliction, he argues, is one of God's most effective tools for shaping the character of His children. It is the furnace that purifies the gold. It is the pruning that produces more fruit. Thomas Watson agreed: "The same affliction that drives one man to despair drives another man to prayer."

Bridge concludes with a simple but powerful exhortation: hope in God. The psalmist does not say, "Hope in your feelings." He does not say, "Hope in your circumstances." He says, "Hope in God." God has not changed. His promises have not expired. His power has not diminished. His love for you is as strong tonight as it was the day He sent His Son to die for you. Whatever has cast you down, God is able to lift you up.

--- Application ---

First, if you are discouraged tonight, name the cause. Is it sin, weakness, God's seeming absence, or suffering? Identifying the source of your discouragement is the first step toward addressing it with the right remedy.

Second, preach the gospel to yourself. Bridge's method is essentially this: take the truth of God and apply it to the lie that discouragement is whispering. Meet the accusation of sin with the blood of Christ. Meet the despair of weakness with the sufficiency of grace. Meet the silence of God with the promises of His Word.

Third, do not isolate yourself. Bridge wrote these sermons to be heard in community. If you are downcast, seek out a faithful friend, a pastor, a brother or sister in Christ who can remind you of what is true when your own heart has forgotten it.

--- Closing Prayer ---

O Lord, Thou art the lifter of our heads. When our souls are cast down within us, remind us to hope in Thee. Forgive us for measuring Thy love by our feelings and Thy faithfulness by our circumstances. Lift us up tonight by the power of Thy Word and the comfort of Thy Spirit. Through Christ, who was acquainted with grief and who understands our sorrows, Amen.

Rest in hope tonight, dear friend. You shall yet praise Him.
