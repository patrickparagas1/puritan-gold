PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 18
"Thou Art Beautiful, O My Love"
Song of Solomon 6:4-10 — Renewed Praise After Reconciliation


Welcome, dear couple. After the dark night and the joyful reunion, the bridegroom speaks again — and his praise is even more lavish than before. This is the beauty of reconciliation: love that has survived distance often emerges stronger and more tender.

Song of Solomon 6:4-10:

"Thou art beautiful, O my love, as Tirzah, comely as Jerusalem, terrible as an army with banners. Turn away thine eyes from me, for they have overcome me: thy hair is as a flock of goats that appear from Gilead. Thy teeth are as a flock of sheep which go up from the washing, whereof every one beareth twins, and there is not one barren among them. As a piece of a pomegranate are thy temples within thy locks. There are threescore queens, and fourscore concubines, and virgins without number. My dove, my undefiled is but one; she is the only one of her mother, she is the choice one of her that bare her. The daughters saw her, and blessed her; yea, the queens and the concubines, and they praised her. Who is she that looketh forth as the morning, fair as the moon, clear as the sun, and terrible as an army with banners?"


The bridegroom's praise after reconciliation is remarkable for two reasons. First, it repeats much of what he said before. Her hair is again like a flock of goats. Her teeth are again like sheep from the washing. Her temples are again like pomegranate halves. The repetition is not laziness — it is faithfulness. He sees her with the same eyes. The separation has not diminished his admiration. His estimation of her beauty has not changed despite the difficulty they endured.

Richard Baxter wrote that one of the greatest gifts a spouse can give after conflict is unchanged affection. When you have hurt each other, when there has been distance or misunderstanding, the temptation is to withhold praise as a form of punishment — to ration your affirmation until the other person has sufficiently earned it back. The bridegroom does the opposite. He immediately returns to full-throated admiration. He does not make her grovel for his approval. He gives it freely, just as he did before.

But the second remarkable feature is that his praise goes even further than before. He adds something new: "Thou art beautiful, O my love, as Tirzah, comely as Jerusalem, terrible as an army with banners." Tirzah was an ancient city known for its beauty. Jerusalem was the holy city, the dwelling place of God. And an army with banners is a force of majesty and power. He is saying: You are not only beautiful in a gentle, delicate way — you are magnificent. You are powerful. You are awe-inspiring.

The Puritans treasured this dual image of feminine beauty. Thomas Gataker wrote that a godly wife is both "a lily for tenderness and an army for strength." She is gentle and fierce, soft and powerful, nurturing and formidable. Modern marriages sometimes flatten this complexity, expecting wives to be only nurturing or only strong. The Song celebrates the full range.

"Turn away thine eyes from me, for they have overcome me." The bridegroom is overwhelmed by his bride's gaze. Her eyes have a power over him that he cannot resist. William Gouge observed that in a healthy marriage, both spouses retain the power to move each other — not through manipulation but through the sheer force of love's presence. When your spouse looks at you with genuine love, it should still have the power to undo you. If it does not, something has been lost that needs to be recovered.

"There are threescore queens, and fourscore concubines, and virgins without number. My dove, my undefiled is but one." In a world of many beautiful women — queens, concubines, virgins without number — the bridegroom has eyes for only one. His bride is singular. She is the "only one." The Puritans applied this to the absolute exclusivity of the marriage covenant. In a culture saturated with images, options, and temptations, your spouse must know that they are your "only one." Not merely your choice but your singular delight.

"Who is she that looketh forth as the morning, fair as the moon, clear as the sun, and terrible as an army with banners?" The passage ends with a question of wonder — as if even the bridegroom himself is astonished by the beauty of the woman he married. Marriage should retain this quality of astonishment. The day you stop being surprised by your spouse is the day you have stopped paying attention.

Richard Sibbes wrote that wonder is the native language of love. When wonder dies, love languishes. But wonder can be revived. It requires only one thing: attention. Look at your spouse tonight. Look at them as if for the first time. See the morning in their face, the moon in their gentleness, the sun in their strength, the army in their courage. And be astonished.


Turn to each other and consider these questions.

Question 1: After a conflict or period of distance, how quickly do you return to affirming each other? Is there any "punishment by withholding" that needs to end?

Question 2: The bridegroom calls his bride both beautiful and "terrible as an army with banners." How do you celebrate the full range of your spouse's personality — their gentleness and their strength?

Question 3: When was the last time you were genuinely astonished by your spouse — caught off guard by their beauty, wisdom, strength, or kindness? What would help you see them with fresh eyes?


Tonight's prayer focus: Pray for renewed wonder in your marriage. Ask God to restore the ability to be astonished by each other — to see familiar faces with fresh eyes, to hear familiar voices with new ears. Pray that reconciliation in your marriage would always lead to deeper love, not cautious distance.


Fair as the moon. Clear as the sun. Terrible as an army with banners. This is the one who sleeps beside you tonight. Be astonished. Be grateful. Be in love. Goodnight, dear couple.
