PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Guided Puritan Reading, Day 19
"Directions for Killing Sin: Part Two"
John Owen — Mortification of Sin, Chapters 10-11

---

Welcome, dear listener. The practice of reading great Christian literature is a form of spiritual formation. It shapes how we think, how we pray, and how we see God. Tonight, let us be formed by what we read.

Tonight we draw from the work of John Owen, specifically from "The Mortification of Sin." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.

--- Scripture ---

Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is 2 Corinthians 12:9:

"And he said unto me, My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness. Most gladly therefore will I rather glory in my infirmities, that the power of Christ may rest upon me."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.

--- Guided Reading ---

Owen continues his specific directions for mortification with a deepening emphasis on the role of faith, the cross of Christ, and the active engagement of the soul in the fight against sin.

Owen's fifth direction: Meditate upon the attributes of God that sin offends. Owen writes that one of the most powerful weapons against sin is a lively apprehension of who God is. Sin offends God's holiness: He is of purer eyes than to behold evil (Habakkuk 1:13). Sin offends God's justice: He will by no means clear the guilty (Nahum 1:3). Sin offends God's love: the God who gave His Son for you is grieved when you turn from Him to serve idols.

Owen recommends extended meditation on specific attributes. Spend time contemplating God's omniscience: He sees every thought, every motive, every secret indulgence. There is no hiding from Him. This awareness, Owen writes, is not meant to produce terror but reverence — a healthy, filial fear that makes sin repulsive because the Father is watching.

Owen's sixth direction: Fix your faith on Christ. This is the heart of Owen's entire approach to mortification. He writes that the most effective weapon against the power of sin is a clear view of the crucified Christ. Owen's logic is this: every sin was laid upon Christ at the cross. The sins you are fighting today were part of the burden He bore. When you see your sin in the light of Calvary, two things happen: you are horrified by what your sin cost your Savior, and you are assured that it has already been atoned for.

Owen explains that this faith-act has two effects on sin. First, it weakens sin's appeal. The man who is gazing upon the beauty and glory of Christ finds the allurements of sin growing dim. Owen writes that 'the more the soul is fixed upon the cross, the less room there is for the world and the flesh.' Second, it strengthens the will to resist. The man who is filled with gratitude for what Christ endured for him will not lightly run back to the sins that caused that suffering.

Owen develops this with a rich exposition of Galatians 6:14: 'God forbid that I should glory, save in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ, by whom the world is crucified unto me, and I unto the world.' Owen sees in this verse the secret of Paul's victory over the world: the cross had so captivated his soul that the world lost its power to attract. The cross did not merely save Paul from the penalty of sin; it saved him from the power of sin by replacing worldly desires with a supreme desire for Christ.

Owen's seventh direction: Fill your mind with thoughts of the gospel promises that attend obedience. Owen writes that the believer fights sin not only by fleeing from evil but by running toward good. God has attached glorious promises to holiness: peace of conscience, communion with God, usefulness in service, joy in the Spirit, and assurance of eternal life. When the soul is full of these promises, the false promises of sin are exposed as the counterfeits they are.

Owen lists specific promises to meditate upon: 'Blessed are the pure in heart: for they shall see God' (Matthew 5:8). 'If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness' (1 John 1:9). 'My grace is sufficient for thee' (2 Corinthians 12:9). 'The Lord is faithful, who shall stablish you, and keep you from evil' (2 Thessalonians 3:3). These promises are not wishful thinking; they are the sworn word of a covenant-keeping God.

Owen's eighth direction: Bring your sin to its full end in your mind. Trace the trajectory of your temptation to its logical conclusion. Where will this sin lead if you indulge it? Owen writes that sin always promises pleasure but delivers misery. The momentary gratification of lust leads to shame, broken trust, and spiritual deadness. The temporary satisfaction of revenge leads to bitterness, isolation, and a hardened heart. If you will only follow the sin in your imagination to its final destination, you will find the journey less attractive.

Owen closes these chapters with a word of encouragement: the grace of Christ is sufficient. The man who feels overwhelmed by the strength of his sin must remember that the power of Christ is greater. Paul discovered this in his own thorn in the flesh: 'My grace is sufficient for thee.' This is not a promise that the struggle will end, but a promise that the resources for the struggle will never run out.

--- Reflection ---

Let these questions search your heart. Answer honestly before God.

Question 1: Am I regularly meditating on the cross as a weapon against sin, or has the cross become merely a doctrinal concept rather than a present reality?

Question 2: Which of God's promises do I need to cling to most urgently in my current battle against sin?

Question 3: Have I traced my besetting sin to its logical conclusion — do I truly see where it leads?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.

--- Closing Prayer ---

Lord Jesus, fix my eyes upon Your cross. Let me see there the horror of my sin and the sufficiency of Your grace. Fill my mind with Your promises. Weaken the power of temptation by the superior power of Your beauty and love. Your grace is sufficient. Amen.

Goodnight. Remember: the same God who spoke to the Puritans speaks to you.
