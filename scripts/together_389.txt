PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 28
"The Song's Wisdom on Intimacy"
Song of Solomon Overview — The Puritan Vision of Marital Closeness

---

Welcome, beloved couple. Tonight we gather the Song of Solomon's wisdom on intimacy — not merely physical intimacy, though that is included, but the full, multidimensional closeness that God intends for married couples. The Puritans had a remarkably rich and balanced understanding of this subject.

Let us read Song of Solomon 8:6-7 one final time:

"Set me as a seal upon thine heart, as a seal upon thine arm: for love is strong as death; jealousy is cruel as the grave: the coals thereof are coals of fire, which hath a most vehement flame. Many waters cannot quench love, neither can the floods drown it: if a man would give all the substance of his house for love, it would utterly be contemned."

--- Teaching ---

The Puritans understood that true marital intimacy operates on multiple levels simultaneously. It is not merely physical. It is not merely emotional. It is not merely spiritual. It is all of these woven together into a single garment of closeness. Tonight, let us consider each dimension.

First, physical intimacy. The Song of Solomon celebrates the physical dimension of marriage with unabashed freedom. The Puritans followed suit. William Gouge wrote that "the marriage bed is appointed by God for the mutual comfort and delight of husband and wife." He insisted that both partners have not merely a right but a responsibility to attend to each other's physical needs with joy and generosity. The Puritans rejected the notion — common in medieval Christianity — that physical pleasure in marriage was sinful. They argued instead that it was a gift, a grace, and a means of bonding that God Himself had designed.

Richard Baxter offered practical counsel on this subject. He urged couples to be patient with each other's differences in desire, to communicate openly about their needs, and to approach the physical dimension of marriage with both passion and tenderness. He warned against the extremes of either obsession or neglect. "The marriage bed should be neither an idol nor a desert," Baxter wrote, "but a garden — cultivated, enjoyed, and tended with care."

Second, emotional intimacy. The Song is as much about emotional connection as physical. The bride and bridegroom share their fears, their insecurities, their longings, their admiration. They are emotionally naked before each other. Thomas Gataker called this "the nakedness of the soul" and considered it more intimate even than physical nakedness. To allow another person to see your true feelings — your real fears, your genuine hopes, your actual state of heart — requires a level of trust that only covenant love can sustain.

The Puritans counseled couples to cultivate what they called "holy conference" — regular, deep, honest conversation about the state of their souls, their marriage, and their walk with God. This was not casual chat but intentional, vulnerable dialogue. Matthew Henry wrote that "the couple who converses deeply will love deeply." Many marriages suffer not from a lack of physical closeness but from a lack of emotional depth. You may sleep in the same bed every night and still be strangers if you never talk about what truly matters.

Third, spiritual intimacy. The Puritans considered this the highest form of marital closeness. Spiritual intimacy means praying together, reading Scripture together, confessing sin to each other, encouraging each other's faith, and holding each other accountable in holiness. Richard Sibbes wrote that "a couple who kneels together is joined in a bond that heaven itself cannot dissolve."

Spiritual intimacy is also the most neglected. Many couples who are physically active and emotionally connected have never prayed together. Many who share a bed and share their feelings have never shared their souls. The Puritans urged couples to make spiritual communion a non-negotiable practice — as essential as eating together, as habitual as sleeping together. Pray together. Read together. Worship together. Confess together. These practices create a level of closeness that no other activity can replicate.

Fourth, intellectual intimacy. The Puritans valued the life of the mind within marriage. William Gouge wrote that husbands and wives should share their thoughts — their ideas, their questions, their discoveries, their intellectual struggles. A marriage where one spouse does all the thinking and the other merely agrees is a diminished marriage. The best marriages are those where both partners are genuinely interested in each other's minds — curious about each other's thoughts, engaged with each other's ideas, stimulated by each other's perspectives.

Fifth, experiential intimacy. The Song's bride invites her beloved to "go forth into the field" and "lodge in the villages." Shared experiences — adventures, travels, projects, service, play — create a reservoir of common memory that strengthens the bond. Jeremiah Burroughs wrote that "the couple who labours and rejoices together builds a house that no storm can overthrow."

The Puritans wove all five dimensions together. They did not isolate them or prioritize one at the expense of others. Physical without emotional leads to emptiness. Emotional without spiritual leads to sentimentality. Spiritual without physical leads to abstraction. The Song of Solomon holds them all together — body and soul, heart and mind, earth and heaven — in a unified vision of what marriage can be.

--- Reflection Prompts ---

Turn to each other and consider these questions.

Question 1: Of the five dimensions of intimacy — physical, emotional, spiritual, intellectual, experiential — which is strongest in your marriage? Which is weakest? What would it take to strengthen the weakest?

Question 2: When was the last time you prayed together — not before a meal, but genuine, vulnerable, heartfelt prayer for each other and your marriage? If it has been a while, pray together tonight.

Question 3: The Puritans practiced "holy conference" — deep, honest conversation about the soul. What is the state of your soul right now? Share it with your spouse.

--- Prayer Focus ---

Tonight's prayer focus: Pray for intimacy in every dimension — physical, emotional, spiritual, intellectual, and experiential. Ask God to show you which area needs the most attention and to give you the courage and creativity to cultivate it. Pray that your closeness would reflect the comprehensive love of Christ for His church.

--- Closing ---

Set me as a seal upon thine heart. Upon every chamber of your heart — physical, emotional, spiritual, intellectual, experiential. Let no dimension of your love remain unsealed, unclaimed, uncultivated. Goodnight, dear couple. Go deep tonight.
