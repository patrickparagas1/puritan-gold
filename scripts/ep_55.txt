PURITAN GOLD -- Study Devotional, Day 24
Series: Romans
"Love Without Hypocrisy"
The marks of genuine Christian love in a hostile world


Good evening. There is no shortcut to spiritual depth. It comes through sustained, careful engagement with God's Word. That is what we are doing together in this study series, and tonight we take the next step.

We are studying the book of Romans together. Tonight's lesson is titled "Love Without Hypocrisy." Each session builds on what came before, so if you have been following along, you will see how tonight's passage connects to the larger argument of the book.


Let us read our passage for tonight. It comes from Romans 12:9-21:

"Let love be without dissimulation. Abhor that which is evil; cleave to that which is good. Be kindly affectioned one to another with brotherly love; in honour preferring one another; Not slothful in business; fervent in spirit; serving the Lord; Rejoicing in hope; patient in tribulation; continuing instant in prayer; Distributing to the necessity of saints; given to hospitality. Bless them which persecute you: bless, and curse not. Rejoice with them that do rejoice, and weep with them that weep. Be of the same mind one toward another. Mind not high things, but condescend to men of low estate. Be not wise in your own conceits. Recompense to no man evil for evil. Provide things honest in the sight of all men. If it be possible, as much as lieth in you, live peaceably with all men. Dearly beloved, avenge not yourselves, but rather give place unto wrath: for it is written, Vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord. Therefore if thine enemy hunger, feed him; if he thirst, give him drink: for in so doing thou shalt heap coals of fire on his head. Be not overcome of evil, but overcome evil with good."

Read that passage again to yourself. Mark any words or phrases that stand out. The Puritans read with a pen in hand, always ready to note what God was showing them.


The Puritans often identified a single verse as the hinge of a passage. Tonight, that verse is this:

"Be not overcome of evil, but overcome evil with good." -- Romans 12:21

This verse is the lens through which we will examine the rest of tonight's passage. Keep it in the front of your mind as we proceed.


Let us unfold the meaning of this passage together.

Richard Baxter offers valuable commentary on this passage. The Puritans were expositors of the highest order, and their insights remain remarkably fresh.

Richard Baxter, the great Puritan pastor of Kidderminster, was known above all for his practical, searching applications of Scripture to daily life. His approach to Romans 12:9-21 reflected this pastoral genius. Baxter argued that this passage provides the most comprehensive description of genuine Christian love in the entire New Testament — not love as a feeling but love as a way of life, expressed in specific, concrete, measurable behaviors.

Baxter gave particular attention to the opening command: 'Let love be without dissimulation.' The word 'dissimulation' (Greek: anupokritos) means without hypocrisy, without pretense, without mask-wearing. Baxter observed that much of what passes for Christian love is actually performance — polite smiles that conceal critical hearts, warm words that mask cold indifference, public kindness that hides private contempt. Genuine love, Baxter insisted, must flow from a heart that has been genuinely transformed by the gospel. Where there is no inner reality, outward performance is worse than useless — it is hypocrisy.

Baxter was especially forceful on the command to 'overcome evil with good' in verse 21. He argued that this is the most counter-intuitive and most powerful strategy in the Christian arsenal. The world's approach to evil is retaliation — eye for eye, blow for blow. The gospel's approach is absorption — receiving evil without returning it, and responding with active, deliberate, costly goodness. Baxter saw in this the very pattern of Christ, who 'when he was reviled, reviled not again; when he suffered, he threatened not; but committed himself to him that judgeth righteously' (1 Peter 2:23). Baxter urged believers to practice this principle in their marriages, their workplaces, their neighborhoods, and their churches — for it is in the small, daily interactions of ordinary life that the world-conquering power of love is demonstrated.

Romans 12:9-21 is one of the most practically challenging passages in the New Testament. Having called for total surrender to God (verses 1-2) and sober service within the church (verses 3-8), Paul now describes what genuine Christian love looks like in daily practice. This is not abstract theology but a detailed portrait of the Christ-shaped life.

Verse 9 sets the standard: 'Let love be without dissimulation.' All that follows flows from this command. Love must be genuine — not performed, not pretended, not cosmetic. The Greek anupokritos literally means 'un-hypocritical.' In the ancient theater, a hypocrite was an actor wearing a mask. Christian love must remove the mask. What you show on the outside must match what you feel on the inside.

Linked to genuine love is moral clarity: 'Abhor that which is evil; cleave to that which is good.' Love is not sentimental tolerance. Genuine love hates evil — the Greek apostugeo means to detest, to shrink from with horror. And it clings to good — kollao means to glue oneself to, to bind tightly. A love that does not hate evil is not genuine love; it is mere niceness.

Verses 10-13 describe love within the Christian community. 'Be kindly affectioned one to another with brotherly love' — the warmth of family affection within the church. 'In honour preferring one another' — putting others ahead of yourself, considering them more significant than yourself (Philippians 2:3). 'Not slothful in business' — diligent, not lazy. 'Fervent in spirit' — literally boiling in spirit, zealous, burning with holy passion. 'Serving the Lord' — all of life is service to Christ.

'Rejoicing in hope' — our joy is anchored in future glory, not present circumstances. 'Patient in tribulation' — enduring affliction without bitterness. 'Continuing instant in prayer' — devoted to prayer, persistent, unwavering. 'Distributing to the necessity of saints' — sharing material resources with fellow believers in need. 'Given to hospitality' — literally 'pursuing hospitality,' actively seeking opportunities to open your home and your life to others.

Verses 14-16 describe love toward the hostile world. 'Bless them which persecute you: bless, and curse not.' This echoes Jesus' teaching directly: 'Love your enemies, bless them that curse you' (Matthew 5:44). The natural response to persecution is retaliation. The gospel response is blessing — speaking good over those who speak evil over you. Notice the emphasis: 'bless, and curse not.' Not 'bless and also curse.' Not 'bless on Sunday and curse on Monday.' Bless only.

'Rejoice with them that do rejoice, and weep with them that weep.' This requires genuine empathy — entering into the emotions of others. It is often easier to weep with the weeping than to rejoice with the rejoicing, because our own envy can make others' joy a source of pain. Genuine love is free from envy and enters fully into both the joys and sorrows of others.

'Be of the same mind one toward another' — pursue unity of purpose and perspective. 'Mind not high things, but condescend to men of low estate' — do not be a social climber. Do not gravitate only toward the powerful, the wealthy, the influential. Go to the lowly, the forgotten, the marginalized. Jesus did. 'Be not wise in your own conceits' — do not trust your own judgment as though it were infallible.

Verses 17-21 address the Christian response to evil. 'Recompense to no man evil for evil.' Do not return harm for harm. 'Provide things honest in the sight of all men' — live with such integrity that even unbelievers can see your honesty. 'If it be possible, as much as lieth in you, live peaceably with all men.' Paul is realistic — it is not always possible. Some people will not be at peace with you regardless of what you do. But as far as it depends on you, pursue peace.

'Dearly beloved, avenge not yourselves, but rather give place unto wrath: for it is written, Vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord.' The reason we can release our desire for vengeance is that God has promised to handle it. He is the just Judge. He will repay every wrong perfectly — either at the cross (for those who repent) or at the judgment (for those who do not). When we take vengeance into our own hands, we are usurping God's prerogative.

Verse 20 turns the principle into positive action: 'Therefore if thine enemy hunger, feed him; if he thirst, give him drink: for in so doing thou shalt heap coals of fire on his head.' The 'coals of fire' probably refer to the burning shame of conviction — your enemy's conscience is scorched by your unexpected kindness. Love disarms hostility. Kindness to enemies is the most powerful weapon in the Christian arsenal.

Verse 21 provides the grand summary: 'Be not overcome of evil, but overcome evil with good.' There are only two options: either evil overcomes you (through bitterness, resentment, retaliation), or you overcome evil (through active, deliberate, costly goodness). The cross is the ultimate demonstration: Christ overcame the greatest evil with the greatest good.


Study without application is like a seed without soil. Let us now plant what we have learned in the ground of daily life.

Romans 12:9-21 provides a blueprint for daily Christian living. Here are specific action steps.

First, perform a love audit. Is your love genuine or performative? Do you harbor resentment behind a pleasant facade? Identify one relationship where your outward behavior does not match your inward feelings and begin the work of reconciliation. Confess the hypocrisy to God and ask Him to make your love real.

Second, practice the specific commands of this passage this week. Choose three to focus on: perhaps hospitality (invite someone into your home), generosity (meet a tangible need for a fellow believer), and blessing persecutors (speak well of someone who has spoken ill of you). These are not suggestions; they are commands. Obey them concretely.

Third, release your desire for vengeance. Is there someone who has wronged you, and you are holding onto bitterness, waiting for them to pay? Hand it over to God today. 'Vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord.' You are not the judge; He is. Let go of the grudge and trust God's justice. Then do the radical thing: if your enemy is in need, meet that need. Heap coals of fire through overwhelming kindness.

Fourth, commit to overcoming evil with good in one specific situation. Where is evil pressing against you right now? A hostile coworker? A difficult family member? A community conflict? Do not let evil set the terms of engagement. Respond with deliberate, strategic, sacrificial goodness. This is the way of the cross. This is how Christ conquered. This is how you will overcome.

Write down one concrete action you will take this week in response to tonight's study. Do not let this truth remain theoretical. The Puritans measured the success of a sermon not by how much the hearer learned but by how much the hearer changed.


Lord Jesus, You overcame the greatest evil with the greatest good — the cross. Teach us to follow Your example. Make our love genuine, without hypocrisy. Give us the courage to bless those who persecute us, to feed our enemies, to return good for evil. Deliver us from bitterness, resentment, and the desire for revenge. Help us to trust Your justice and release our grudges into Your hands. Make us people who overcome evil with good in our marriages, our workplaces, our neighborhoods, and our churches. May the world see in us the love of Christ — real, costly, and unconquerable. Amen.

Continue to meditate on tonight's passage. The more you return to it, the more it will reveal.
