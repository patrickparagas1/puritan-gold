PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"Blessed Is the Man"
May 1, 2026

---

"Blessed is the man that walketh not in the counsel of the ungodly, nor standeth in the way of sinners, nor sitteth in the seat of the scornful. But his delight is in the law of the Lord; and in his law doth he meditate day and night. And he shall be like a tree planted by the rivers of water, that bringeth forth his fruit in his season; his leaf also shall not wither; and whatsoever he doeth shall prosper." -- Psalm 1:1-3

The Holy Spirit, in His infinite wisdom, has placed this psalm at the very threshold of the entire Psalter, as a porter standing at the gate of a great palace, bidding every soul who would enter to consider first the way of blessedness. Before we hear a single note of praise, before one cry of lamentation ascends, before the harp of David strikes its first chord of worship, we are confronted with this solemn and searching question: What manner of man is truly blessed? It is as though the Lord Himself stoops down from heaven and says to every son and daughter of Adam, "Before thou goest further, settle this matter in thy soul. Wilt thou walk the way of the blessed, or the way of the ungodly?"

Charles Spurgeon, that prince of preachers, once remarked that this psalm is the preface to the whole Book of Psalms, and a most fitting preface it is. For the Psalms are the very heart-language of the redeemed, and no unregenerate soul can truly enter into their depths. The man who would sing the songs of Zion must first have his feet set upon the rock of true godliness. And so the Spirit begins not with a command, but with a benediction. "Blessed is the man." Oh, what a word is this! Not merely happy, as the world counts happiness, in the possession of fleeting pleasures and perishing goods, but blessed -- deeply, thoroughly, eternally blessed -- with a blessedness that flows from the very fountain of God's own being.

Now observe the threefold description of the way the blessed man avoids. He walketh not in the counsel of the ungodly. He standeth not in the way of sinners. He sitteth not in the seat of the scornful. Here is a fearful progression that every soul must mark with care. Sin begins with walking, with a casual step alongside those who have no regard for God. A man does not plunge headlong into the depths of iniquity all at once. No, he first entertains the counsel of the ungodly. He listens to their reasoning. He weighs their philosophy. He gives ear to their arguments against the ways of the Lord. And finding their company not altogether disagreeable, he begins to linger. Walking becomes standing. The casual acquaintance becomes a settled friendship. And at last, standing becomes sitting. He takes up his permanent abode among the scornful, those who mock at sin and make a jest of holiness.

Thomas Watson, that judicious Puritan divine, was wont to say that sin is of a creeping nature; it comes by degrees. The serpent did not begin by telling Eve to eat the forbidden fruit. He began with a question: "Yea, hath God said?" So it is with every temptation. The devil is too cunning a fisherman to show the hook at first. He baits it with the pleasant counsel of the ungodly, and before the unwary soul knows what has happened, he has swallowed the hook entire.

But the blessed man is preserved from this downward path, and the secret of his preservation is found in the second verse. "His delight is in the law of the Lord; and in his law doth he meditate day and night." Here is the positive character of the godly man, and it is this that distinguishes him from all others. It is not merely that he reads the law, or that he studies it as a scholar might study an ancient text. His delight is in it. He finds in the Word of God a sweetness that surpasses honey and the honeycomb. He turns to it as a hungry man turns to bread, as a thirsty man runs to the fountain. The law of God is not a burden to him but a banquet, not a chain but a charter of liberty.

And mark well: he meditates in this law day and night. Meditation is the very soul of profitable Bible reading. A man may read his Bible from Genesis to Revelation and be none the wiser if he does not pause to meditate upon what he reads. Meditation is to the soul what digestion is to the body. It is one thing to take food into the mouth; it is another thing altogether to have that food nourished into blood and bone and sinew. So it is with the Word of God. We must chew upon it, turn it over in our minds, press it into the chambers of our hearts, and apply it to every circumstance of our lives. The godly man does this not merely in the morning hour, when the dew is still upon the grass, but throughout the heat of the day and into the watches of the night. His mind returns again and again to the truths of Scripture as a compass needle returns to the north.

And what is the result of such a life? The third verse gives us a picture of surpassing beauty. "He shall be like a tree planted by the rivers of water." Not a wild tree, growing by chance in some barren wilderness, but a tree deliberately planted by the hand of a wise gardener in the most favourable situation. This tree has its roots thrust deep into the moist earth beside unfailing streams. When the drought comes and the sun beats down with merciless heat, this tree does not wither. Its leaves remain green. Its branches are laden with fruit in due season. Why? Because its roots have found a hidden supply of water that the scorching wind cannot touch and the burning sun cannot dry up.

So it is with the believer whose delight is in the law of the Lord. He has a hidden source of strength that the world knows nothing of. When trials come, when afflictions press upon him, when the furnace of adversity is heated seven times hotter than it was wont to be heated, he does not wither. His faith does not fail. His hope does not perish. For his roots are deep in the inexhaustible riches of God's Word, and from that Word he draws a continual supply of grace, of comfort, of wisdom, and of strength.

John Owen, that mighty theologian of the Puritan age, taught us that the stability of the believer rests not upon his own resolution or the strength of his own will, but upon the faithfulness of God as revealed in His Word. The tree does not sustain itself. It is sustained by the water. And the believer does not keep himself. He is kept by the power of God through faith. But the instrument of that keeping is the Word, beloved. It is the Word that feeds faith. It is the Word that strengthens hope. It is the Word that kindles love. And therefore the man who neglects the Word is like a tree that has been transplanted away from the riverbank into dry and sandy ground. He may stand for a time, but he cannot flourish, and in the day of testing he shall surely fall.

Let us therefore take this opening psalm as a searching examination of our own souls. Do we truly delight in the law of the Lord? Is the Bible to us what it was to the psalmist, more to be desired than gold, yea, than much fine gold, sweeter also than honey and the honeycomb? Or have we allowed the counsel of the ungodly to creep into our thinking, the way of sinners to become familiar to our feet, the seat of the scornful to seem a comfortable resting place? If so, let us repent this very hour, and let us return to the fountain of living waters. For the promise stands sure: the man whose delight is in the law of the Lord shall be blessed, not with a fading, fleeting, uncertain blessing, but with a blessing as deep as the roots of that tree by the rivers of water, and as enduring as the God who planted it there.

May the Lord grant us grace to be such trees, planted by His own hand, nourished by His own Word, and bearing fruit to His eternal glory. Amen.
