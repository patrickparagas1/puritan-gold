PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"The Lord Is Nigh"
May 25, 2026

---

"The Lord is gracious, and full of compassion; slow to anger, and of great mercy. The Lord is good to all: and his tender mercies are over all his works. All thy works shall praise thee, O Lord; and thy saints shall bless thee. The Lord is nigh unto all them that call upon him, to all that call upon him in truth. He will fulfil the desire of them that fear him: he also will hear their cry, and will save them." -- Psalm 145:8-9, 10, 18-19

The hundred and forty-fifth psalm is David's final hymn of praise, and it is fitting that this last song should be devoted entirely to the character and the works of the God whom David had served all his days. It is an acrostic psalm, each verse beginning with a successive letter of the Hebrew alphabet, as though David wished to praise God from A to Z, exhausting every resource of language in the attempt to express what is, in truth, inexpressible.

"The Lord is gracious, and full of compassion; slow to anger, and of great mercy." These words echo the great self-revelation of God to Moses on Mount Sinai, when the Lord proclaimed His own name and His own character to His trembling servant. They are not David's invention. They are God's own description of Himself. And they tell us that at the very centre of the divine nature, at the very heart of the Godhead, there is graciousness, compassion, patience, and mercy. These are not peripheral qualities, as though God were fundamentally stern and occasionally merciful. They are essential qualities, woven into the very fabric of His being.

"Slow to anger." How thankful we ought to be for this attribute! If God were quick to anger, as we are, the entire human race would have been consumed long ago. We provoke Him daily with our sins, our ingratitude, our forgetfulness, our wanderings, our rebellions. And yet He is slow to anger. He bears with us. He waits for us. He gives us space to repent. He sends His Word, His Spirit, His providences, to call us back from our waywardness before the stroke of judgment falls. Thomas Goodwin compared the patience of God to a great reservoir of water held back by a mighty dam. The sins of men are constantly filling the reservoir, and the day is coming when the dam will be opened and the waters of judgment will flow. But in the meantime, God holds back His wrath, not because He is indifferent to sin, but because He is rich in mercy and desires the repentance of the sinner rather than his destruction.

"The Lord is good to all: and his tender mercies are over all his works." Here is the universal goodness of God, what the theologians call His common grace. The rain falls upon the just and the unjust. The sun shines upon the wicked and the righteous. The harvest fills the barns of the ungodly as well as the barns of the faithful. God is good to all His creatures, even to those who deny His existence and trample upon His laws. His tender mercies, His mother-like compassion, His gentle and particular care, extend to everything He has made, from the greatest whale in the ocean to the smallest sparrow on the housetop.

This universal goodness of God should fill us with both gratitude and hope. Gratitude, because every good thing we enjoy, from the air we breathe to the food we eat, is a gift from the hand of a God who is good to all. And hope, because a God who is good to all will surely be especially good to those who are His own, to those who have been purchased by the blood of His Son and sealed by the power of His Spirit.

"The Lord is nigh unto all them that call upon him, to all that call upon him in truth." This is one of the most precious promises in all of Scripture, and it deserves to be written in letters of gold upon the walls of every believer's heart. The Lord is nigh. He is near. He is close at hand. He is not a distant deity, enthroned in some far-off heaven, unreachable and unconcerned. He is nigh unto all who call upon Him. The longest prayer travels no further than a whisper, for God is closer to the believer than the believer is to himself.

But there is a condition: "to all that call upon him in truth." The nearness of God is not experienced by the formal worshipper, the habitual reciter of prayers who goes through the motions of devotion without the reality. It is experienced by those who call upon God in truth, with genuine hearts, with sincere faith, with honest repentance, with a real desire to know Him and to be known by Him. The hypocrite may call upon God with his lips, but his heart is far from Him, and God, who sees the heart, keeps His distance. But the humble soul who cries out from the depths of genuine need finds that God is already there, already near, already reaching out His hand to help.

"He will fulfil the desire of them that fear him: he also will hear their cry, and will save them." The desires of those who fear God are not always the same as the desires of those who do not. The man who fears God desires holiness above happiness, truth above comfort, the glory of God above the applause of men. And these desires, which are themselves the work of the Holy Spirit in the heart, God delights to fulfil. He hears the cry of the humble. He responds to the prayer of the contrite. He saves those who put their trust in Him.

Let us draw near to this gracious God today with full assurance of faith. He is nigh. He is listening. He is compassionate. He is ready to fulfil every holy desire of our hearts and to save us from every trouble that besets us. Let the words of David become our own: "My mouth shall speak the praise of the Lord: and let all flesh bless his holy name for ever and ever." Amen.
