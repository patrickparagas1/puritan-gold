PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 9
"The Day of His Espousals"
Song of Solomon 3:6-11 — The Wedding Procession of Solomon


Welcome, dear couple. Tonight we witness a grand spectacle — the wedding procession of King Solomon himself. This passage is a celebration of the marriage covenant in all its glory, pomp, and sacred joy. Let your hearts be lifted as we read together.

Song of Solomon 3:6-11:

"Who is this that cometh out of the wilderness like pillars of smoke, perfumed with myrrh and frankincense, with all powders of the merchant? Behold his bed, which is Solomon's; threescore valiant men are about it, of the valiant of Israel. They all hold swords, being expert in war: every man hath his sword upon his thigh because of fear in the night. King Solomon made himself a chariot of the wood of Lebanon. He made the pillars thereof of silver, the bottom thereof of gold, the covering of it of purple, the midst thereof being paved with love, for the daughters of Jerusalem. Go forth, O ye daughters of Zion, and behold king Solomon with the crown wherewith his mother crowned him in the day of his espousals, and in the day of the gladness of his heart."


This is a wedding day. All of Jerusalem turns out to watch the procession. Pillars of smoke rise from the incense. The air is thick with myrrh and frankincense. Sixty warriors stand guard around the royal litter. The chariot itself is a masterpiece — silver pillars, golden floor, purple canopy, and the midst "paved with love." This is marriage celebrated at its most public, most joyful, most magnificent.

Do you remember your wedding day? Most couples do — the nervousness, the anticipation, the moment when you first saw each other, the vows, the tears, the joy. Your wedding day was, in a very real sense, "the day of the gladness of your heart." The Puritans took wedding days seriously. They were not merely social events but covenant ceremonies — sacred moments when two people bound themselves together before God and witnesses in an unbreakable bond.

Thomas Gataker, who wrote one of the finest Puritan treatises on marriage, said that the memory of the wedding day should serve as a perpetual anchor for married couples. When difficulties come — and they will come — you return to the vows. When temptation beckons — and it will beckon — you return to the covenant. When weariness settles over the relationship — and it will settle — you return to the day of your espousals and remember the gladness of your heart.

Notice the details of Solomon's chariot. The pillars are silver — precious, enduring, beautiful. The bottom is gold — the most valuable substance in the ancient world. The covering is purple — the color of royalty and dignity. But the most remarkable detail is this: "the midst thereof being paved with love." The interior of the chariot, the part that only the bride and groom see, is paved with love. The outward appearance may impress the crowds, but the inward reality is what sustains the marriage.

William Gouge applied this insight directly to married couples. He wrote that many marriages look impressive from the outside — beautiful home, successful careers, well-behaved children, active church involvement — while the interior is cold, loveless, and hollow. A marriage paved with love from the inside is worth more than a marriage gilded with gold on the outside. What matters is not how your marriage looks to others but how it feels to the two of you when the door is closed and the guests have gone home.

"Threescore valiant men are about it, of the valiant of Israel. They all hold swords, being expert in war: every man hath his sword upon his thigh because of fear in the night." The wedding procession has armed guards. Why? Because even on the most joyful day, there are enemies. "Because of fear in the night" — because there are threats in the darkness. The Puritans saw this as a picture of the spiritual warfare that surrounds every marriage. Your marriage has enemies — the world, the flesh, and the devil, as the old catechisms say. Satan hates marriage because marriage displays the Gospel. He will attack your unity, your intimacy, your joy.

Richard Baxter urged couples to be "soldiers as well as lovers." He wrote that marriage requires vigilance — watching for the approaches of the enemy, guarding each other's hearts, standing back to back against the assaults of temptation, discouragement, and division. The sixty valiant men remind us that your marriage is worth defending. Do not leave it unguarded.

"Go forth, O ye daughters of Zion, and behold king Solomon with the crown wherewith his mother crowned him in the day of his espousals." The passage closes with an invitation to witness. Marriage is not just a private affair; it is a public testimony. When you love each other well, the world takes notice. The "daughters of Zion" — the community around you — are watching. Your marriage preaches a sermon every day. What is that sermon saying?

Tonight, look across at your spouse and remember the day of your espousals. Remember the gladness of your heart. And recommit yourselves to paving the interior of your marriage with love.


Turn to each other and consider these questions.

Question 1: Do you remember specific moments from your wedding day that still move you? Share one with each other tonight.

Question 2: If the inside of your marriage is "paved with love," what does that love look like in the daily, ordinary moments? Is the interior matching the exterior?

Question 3: What are the greatest threats to your marriage right now — the "fears in the night" that require armed guards? How are you defending your marriage against them?


Tonight's prayer focus: Return to your wedding vows in prayer. Thank God for the day of your espousals and the gladness of your hearts. Ask Him to guard your marriage with valiant warriors of truth, grace, and faithfulness. Pray that the interior of your marriage would be paved with genuine, self-giving love.


The day of your espousals was a day of gladness. But God intends every day of your marriage to carry some echo of that joy. May the crown of covenant love rest upon your heads tonight. Goodnight, dear couple.
