PURITAN GOLD -- Melchizedek: A Complete Biblical Study, Part 4 of 8
Hebrews 6:13-7:3 — The Order of Melchizedek


Welcome back to Puritan Gold. We continue our complete biblical study of Melchizedek. In our previous episode, we heard the writer of Hebrews lay the foundation in chapter 5, declaring Christ a high priest after the order of Melchizedek and rebuking his readers for their spiritual immaturity. Now, after the solemn warning passage in the first twelve verses of chapter 6, the writer returns to his theme with renewed energy. We pick up at Hebrews 6:13, and we shall read through Hebrews 7:3 — one continuous, magnificent argument.

Let us read Hebrews 6, verses 13 through 20:

"For when God made promise to Abraham, because he could swear by no greater, he sware by himself, saying, Surely blessing I will bless thee, and multiplying I will multiply thee. And so, after he had patiently endured, he obtained the promise. For men verily swear by the greater: and an oath for confirmation is to them an end of all strife. Wherein God, willing more abundantly to shew unto the heirs of promise the immutability of his counsel, confirmed it by an oath: that by two immutable things, in which it was impossible for God to lie, we might have a strong consolation, who have fled for refuge to lay hold upon the hope set before us: which hope we have as an anchor of the soul, both sure and stedfast, and which entereth into that within the veil; whither the forerunner is for us entered, even Jesus, made an high priest for ever after the order of Melchisedec."

Now let us continue directly into Hebrews 7, verses 1 through 3:

"For this Melchisedec, king of Salem, priest of the most high God, who met Abraham returning from the slaughter of the kings, and blessed him; to whom also Abraham gave a tenth part of all; first being by interpretation King of righteousness, and after that also King of Salem, which is, King of peace; without father, without mother, without descent, having neither beginning of days, nor end of life; but made like unto the Son of God; abideth a priest continually."


Beloved, we have before us one of the richest passages in the New Testament. Let us take it in order, drawing from it every drop of theological nourishment as the Puritan fathers taught us.

The passage in Hebrews 6:13-20 is about the oath of God and the anchor of the soul. The writer reaches back to God's oath to Abraham in Genesis 22:16-17, where, after Abraham offered up Isaac, God said: "By myself have I sworn, saith the LORD, for because thou hast done this thing, and hast not withheld thy son, thine only son: that in blessing I will bless thee, and in multiplying I will multiply thy seed." This oath confirmed the promise. And the writer explains why God condescended to swear an oath at all.

Men swear by the greater, he says. When a man takes an oath, he appeals to someone above himself as a witness and guarantee. An oath is the end of all strife — it settles the matter. God, willing more abundantly to show the heirs of promise the immutability of His counsel, confirmed His promise by an oath. Now, God's bare word is already unfailing. He cannot lie. But He added an oath so that we might have even stronger assurance. The two immutable things are God's promise and God's oath — neither of which can fail, because it is impossible for God to lie.

John Owen writes that this passage reveals the extraordinary condescension of God toward the weakness of His people. He knows our frailty. He knows how prone we are to doubt. And so He does not merely promise — He swears. He binds Himself by an oath to the salvation of His people. Owen asks: what more could God do? He has pledged His own name, His own being, His own unchangeable nature as the guarantee of our salvation.

And then comes one of the most beloved images in Scripture: "Which hope we have as an anchor of the soul, both sure and stedfast, and which entereth into that within the veil." Our hope is not anchored in this world, where everything shifts and changes. It is anchored within the veil — in the heavenly holy of holies — where Christ has entered as our forerunner.

The word forerunner is deeply significant. Thomas Goodwin notes that under the old covenant, only the high priest entered the Most Holy Place, and he entered not as a forerunner but as a sole representative. No one followed him in. But Christ entered as a forerunner — meaning that where He has gone, we shall follow. He has opened the way into the very presence of God, and every believer will one day enter there as well. The anchor holds because it is fastened in heaven itself, in the throne room of God, to Christ Himself.

And there, within the veil, Christ ministers as a high priest for ever after the order of Melchisedec. This is the pivot point. The writer has been circling around this theme since chapter 5, and now at last he enters directly into the full exposition.

Hebrews 7:1 opens with a formal description of Melchizedek drawn from Genesis 14: "For this Melchisedec, king of Salem, priest of the most high God, who met Abraham returning from the slaughter of the kings, and blessed him; to whom also Abraham gave a tenth part of all."

The writer retells the Genesis account in summary — the meeting after the battle, the blessing, the tithe. Every detail matters, and he will draw theological conclusions from each one. But first, in verse 2, he gives us the meaning of the names.

"First being by interpretation King of righteousness, and after that also King of Salem, which is, King of peace."

Melchizedek means King of Righteousness. Salem means peace. So he is King of Righteousness and King of Peace — and the writer notes the order: first righteousness, then peace. This is not accidental. It reflects the order of the gospel itself. Romans 5:1 declares, "Therefore being justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ." First comes the establishment of righteousness through justification, and then comes the experience of peace. There can be no peace with God apart from the satisfaction of divine justice. Christ first fulfills all righteousness, bearing the penalty of sin and providing a perfect righteousness for His people, and only then does He become our peace.

John Owen expounds this at considerable length. He writes that the order of the names reveals the order of Christ's priestly work. As King of Righteousness, Christ establishes a righteous basis for reconciliation — He satisfies the law, He appeases the wrath of God, He provides a righteousness that is imputed to all who believe. As King of Peace, He then brings the reconciled sinner into a state of peace with God — no more enmity, no more condemnation, no more wrath. Righteousness is the foundation; peace is the fruit.

Now we come to verse 3, one of the most discussed verses in the entire epistle: "Without father, without mother, without descent, having neither beginning of days, nor end of life; but made like unto the Son of God; abideth a priest continually."

What does this mean? Was Melchizedek literally without father and mother? Was he an angel? Was he a pre-incarnate appearance of Christ Himself? These questions have occupied theologians for centuries.

The Puritan consensus, following the best Reformed interpretation, is this: the writer is speaking not of Melchizedek's actual nature but of his presentation in Scripture. In the biblical record, Melchizedek has no genealogy. Genesis gives us no father, no mother, no birth, no death. For the Levitical priests, genealogy was everything. A man had to prove his descent from Aaron before he could serve at the altar. When the exiles returned from Babylon, those who could not prove their priestly genealogy were excluded from service. But Melchizedek's priesthood rests on no genealogy at all. He appears in the text without origin and disappears without ending.

Owen writes that the Holy Spirit deliberately omitted Melchizedek's genealogy in Genesis so that he might serve as a more perfect type of Christ. The silence of Scripture is as inspired as its speech. What is not said about Melchizedek is as important as what is said. He is without father, without mother, without descent — not in historical fact, for he was certainly born of human parents, but in the inspired record — so that he might be made like unto the Son of God.

Note the direction of the comparison. The text does not say the Son of God is made like Melchizedek. It says Melchizedek is made like unto the Son of God. Christ is the original; Melchizedek is the copy. Christ is the reality; Melchizedek is the shadow. The Son of God truly is without beginning of days or end of life. He truly is eternal. Melchizedek was presented in Scripture in such a way as to resemble this eternal Son.

Matthew Henry draws from this a beautiful pastoral reflection. He writes that Melchizedek abideth a priest continually — and so does Christ. The Levitical priests came and went. Aaron died. Eleazar died. Phinehas died. Every generation saw old priests carried to the grave and new priests installed. But Christ's priesthood has no such succession, for He Himself never ceases to live and to intercede. He is the same yesterday, today, and forever. The believer who rested upon Christ's priesthood in the morning of the church age rests upon the same priesthood today. It has not changed. It cannot change. He abideth a priest continually.

Stephen Charnock adds that the perpetuity of Christ's priesthood is the ground of the believer's perpetual security. If Christ's priesthood could end, our salvation could end. If His intercession could cease, our standing before God could collapse. But because He abideth a priest continually, we abide in grace continually. The anchor holds because the Priest lives.

Beloved, we have now entered the heart of the Melchizedek exposition. The writer has established the oath of God as the foundation, the hope within the veil as the anchor, and the character of Melchizedek as the pattern for Christ's eternal priesthood. In our next episode, we shall continue through Hebrews 7, verses 4 through 17, where the writer makes his devastating argument that Melchizedek — and therefore Christ — is greater than Abraham, greater than Levi, and greater than the entire Levitical system.

Until then, let your soul rest upon the anchor. It is sure and stedfast, and it entereth into that within the veil. Amen.
