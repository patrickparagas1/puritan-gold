PURITAN GOLD -- Study Topic: The Book of Melchizedek, Part 4 of 8
"Greater Than Abraham"
The Book of Melchizedek — Hebrews 7:4-10 — The superiority of Melchizedek

---

Welcome back, dear friends, to our study of Melchizedek and the priesthood of Christ. Today we continue in Hebrews chapter 7, where the inspired writer makes one of the most audacious arguments in all of Scripture: that Melchizedek was greater than Abraham, and therefore his priesthood — and the priesthood of Christ which it foreshadows — is greater than the entire Levitical system.

Let us read Hebrews 7:4-10:

"Now consider how great this man was, unto whom even the patriarch Abraham gave the tenth of the spoils. And verily they that are of the sons of Levi, who receive the office of the priesthood, have a commandment to take tithes of the people according to the law, that is, of their brethren, though they come out of the loins of Abraham: But he whose descent is not counted from them received tithes of Abraham, and blessed him that had the promises. And without all contradiction the less is blessed of the better. And here men that die receive tithes; but there he receiveth them, of whom it is witnessed that he liveth. And as I may so say, Levi also, who receiveth tithes, payed tithes in Abraham. For he was yet in the loins of his father, when Melchisedec met him."

--- Teaching ---

"Now consider how great this man was." The writer of Hebrews calls his readers to pause and contemplate the magnitude of what happened in the valley of Shaveh. We are so accustomed to reading the Bible quickly that we often miss the staggering implications of its narratives. Here, the inspired author slows us down and says, in effect, "Do you realize what you are looking at? Do you see the significance of what Abraham did?"

Abraham was not an ordinary man. He was the patriarch — the father of the nation of Israel, the friend of God, the recipient of the covenant promises. God had said to him, "In thee shall all families of the earth be blessed" (Genesis 12:3). He was the man through whom God would bring redemption to the world. There was no one greater in the entire Old Testament economy. Abraham was the summit of human privilege and blessing.

And yet this Abraham — this incomparably great patriarch — gave tithes to Melchizedek. He recognized Melchizedek's superiority by rendering to him a tenth of the spoils of battle. In the ancient world, tithing was an act of honor and submission. One gave tithes to a superior, not to an equal or an inferior. Abraham's act of tithing was therefore an acknowledgment that Melchizedek held a higher station than himself.

The writer develops this argument with meticulous care. The Levitical priests, he notes, received tithes from their fellow Israelites "according to the law." Their right to receive tithes was established by divine commandment. But notice: both the priests who received the tithes and the people who paid them were descendants of Abraham. The Levites received tithes from their brethren — from those who, like themselves, came "out of the loins of Abraham."

But Melchizedek was different. His descent was "not counted from them" — he was not a son of Abraham, not a member of the covenant community, not part of the Levitical system. Yet he received tithes from Abraham himself. The one from outside the covenant received honor from the one who held the covenant. The man without a recorded genealogy was served by the man with the most illustrious genealogy in human history.

"And blessed him that had the promises." Not only did Melchizedek receive Abraham's tithes; he also blessed him. And then the writer states a principle that is self-evident: "Without all contradiction the less is blessed of the better." The one who bestows the blessing is greater than the one who receives it. A father blesses his children, not the other way around. A king blesses his subjects, not the reverse. Melchizedek blessed Abraham — therefore Melchizedek was greater than Abraham.

John Owen spent considerable time on this argument, recognizing its importance for the writer's larger purpose. If Melchizedek was greater than Abraham, then Melchizedek's priesthood was greater than any priesthood descended from Abraham — including the Levitical priesthood. This was a revolutionary claim for a Jewish audience steeped in reverence for the Levitical system. The temple, the sacrifices, the priestly garments, the Day of Atonement — all of these were beloved and sacred institutions. To argue that they were inferior to another priesthood was to challenge the religious foundations of Jewish identity.

Yet this is precisely what the writer of Hebrews argues, and he does so on the basis of Scripture itself. The Old Testament, properly understood, contains within itself the evidence of its own supersession. The very text that established the Levitical priesthood also records the existence of a prior and superior priesthood — the priesthood of Melchizedek. And the very psalm that celebrates the Davidic monarchy also announces that the Messiah will be a priest "after the order of Melchizedek," not after the order of Aaron.

The argument reaches its climax in verses 9 and 10: "And as I may so say, Levi also, who receiveth tithes, payed tithes in Abraham. For he was yet in the loins of his father, when Melchisedec met him." This is the principle of federal or representative headship — the same principle that underlies the doctrine of original sin and the doctrine of imputed righteousness.

Just as all humanity was "in Adam" when he sinned, and therefore all share in the consequences of his transgression (Romans 5:12-19), so Levi was "in Abraham" when Abraham paid tithes to Melchizedek. Levi had not yet been born — he was the great-grandson of Abraham — but he was present in Abraham's loins in a seminal sense. When Abraham bowed before Melchizedek and gave him tithes, Levi bowed with him. The entire Levitical priesthood, still unborn, acknowledged the superiority of Melchizedek through the act of its forefather.

This is a breathtaking argument, and it rests upon a principle that is central to all of Reformed theology: the principle that one person can act as the representative of many. Adam represented all humanity in the garden. Abraham represented all Israel — including the Levitical priests — before Melchizedek. And Christ represents all His elect before the Father. The doctrine of federal headship is not an abstract theological construct; it is woven into the very fabric of biblical history.

Thomas Goodwin, in his reflections on the covenant of redemption, drew a direct line from the federal headship of Abraham to the federal headship of Christ. Just as Abraham's act of homage before Melchizedek implicated all his descendants, so Christ's act of obedience and sacrifice before the Father secures salvation for all who are "in Him" by faith. The principle is the same: the act of the representative is credited to those he represents.

Stephen Charnock, writing on the goodness of God, observed that the superiority of Melchizedek over Abraham was not a slight upon Abraham but an exaltation of Christ. Abraham was great — surpassingly great — and yet Melchizedek was greater. How much greater, then, is Christ, of whom Melchizedek was merely a type? If the shadow exceeded the patriarch, what must the reality be? The greatness of Melchizedek serves to magnify the incomparable greatness of the Son of God.

--- Application ---

1. Let the greatness of Christ's priesthood humble your pride and strengthen your confidence. If even Abraham — the friend of God — bowed before a type of Christ, how much more should we bow before Christ Himself? And if Abraham found blessing in Melchizedek, how much more shall we find blessing in Christ?

2. Understand the principle of federal headship and apply it to your relationship with Christ. Just as Levi was "in Abraham" when Abraham paid tithes, so believers are "in Christ" when He offers His perfect sacrifice. His obedience is your obedience. His righteousness is your righteousness.

3. Do not cling to lesser things when greater things have come. The Levitical system was glorious, but Melchizedek's order was more glorious still. In your own spiritual life, do not settle for religious forms and rituals when you have access to the living Christ.

4. Recognize that the Bible is a unified book with a unified message. The argument of Hebrews 7 depends upon the details of Genesis 14 and Psalm 110. The Old and New Testaments are not in tension but in harmony, jointly testifying to the supremacy of Christ.

--- Closing ---

Almighty Father, we stand in awe of the wisdom of Thy Word, which so carefully prepared the way for Thy Son through types and shadows stretching across millennia. We bow before Christ as Abraham bowed before Melchizedek — acknowledging His greatness, His superiority, His eternal priesthood. May we never rest in anything less than the full sufficiency of our great High Priest. In His name we pray. Amen.

Grace and peace be multiplied unto you, beloved, through the knowledge of our Lord Jesus Christ. Until next time, rest in the greatness of your High Priest.
