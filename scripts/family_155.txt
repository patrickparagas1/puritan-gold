PURITAN GOLD -- Family Devotional, Day 24
"The Sweetness of Christ in Trials"
Pilgrim Families — Week 4, Day 3


Welcome, family. Whether you are gathered on the couch, around the dinner table, or riding in the car, God is here with you. Let us turn our attention to Him and see what treasure He has stored up for us tonight.

Let us bow our heads. Dear Father in heaven, what a privilege it is to gather as a family in Your presence. We need Your wisdom tonight. We need Your guidance. Open our ears to hear, our minds to understand, and our hearts to receive. We pray in the precious name of Jesus. Amen.

Let us turn together to Psalm 34:8; 1 Peter 1:6-8. Follow along in your Bible if you have one:

"O taste and see that the LORD is good: blessed is the man that trusteth in him. Wherein ye greatly rejoice, though now for a season, if need be, ye are in heaviness through manifold temptations: That the trial of your faith, being much more precious than of gold that perisheth, though it be tried with fire, might be found unto praise and honour and glory at the appearing of Jesus Christ: Whom having not seen, ye love; in whom, though now ye see him not, yet believing, ye rejoice with joy unspeakable and full of glory."

That is Psalm 34:8; 1 Peter 1:6-8. Let those words settle in your heart for a moment before we continue.


There is a sweetness in Christ that can only be tasted in the valley. David knew this when he wrote, "O taste and see that the LORD is good: blessed is the man that trusteth in him" (Psalm 34:8). Tasting is more intimate than seeing. You can see food from a distance, but you taste it only when it is close, when it is touching you, when it is inside you. David invites us to an intimate experience of God's goodness — not merely observing it from afar, but tasting it personally.

Peter writes to believers who are in "heaviness through manifold temptations" — weighed down by many trials. Yet he tells them they "greatly rejoice." How can heaviness and great rejoicing exist at the same time? Because the object of their rejoicing is not their circumstances — it is Christ. "Whom having not seen, ye love; in whom, though now ye see him not, yet believing, ye rejoice with joy unspeakable and full of glory."

Joy unspeakable. Full of glory. These words describe a joy so deep that language fails to capture it. It is a joy that comes not from having everything you want but from having the one thing that matters most — Jesus Christ. And this joy is available not despite trials but often especially during trials, because trials drive us to Christ with a desperation we do not feel in comfortable times.

Peter calls the trial of faith "much more precious than of gold that perisheth." Gold is valuable, but it is destroyed by fire. Faith tested by fire is not destroyed — it is purified. When your faith survives a trial, it emerges stronger, purer, and more precious than before. The trial that tested it becomes the proof that it is genuine.

Rutherford discovered this personally. From his exile in Aberdeen, he wrote some of the most rapturous descriptions of Christ's sweetness in all of Christian literature. He found that when every earthly comfort was stripped away, Christ's comfort was amplified. When he had nothing else, he found that Christ was everything. The prison walls could not keep Christ out. The exile could not separate him from Christ's love.

This is a mystery that many Christians never discover because they are never forced into a position where Christ must be everything. When life is comfortable, we tend to spread our affections across many things — family, career, hobbies, entertainments. Christ gets a portion of our heart, but not the whole thing. But when trial strips everything else away, our hearts turn entirely to Christ — and we discover that He is sweeter than all those other things combined.

Rutherford would not have chosen exile. Paul would not have chosen prison. Peter would not have chosen persecution. But each of them found something in suffering that they could not have found in comfort: the surpassing sweetness of Christ. They tasted and saw that the Lord is good — not when everything was going right, but when everything was going wrong.

For families, this teaching should both comfort and challenge. It comforts us because it promises that no matter what we go through, Christ will be sweet to us in it. And it challenges us because it asks: Do we know Christ only in the sunshine, or do we know Him in the storm? A faith that has never been tested is a faith that has never been proven.

Tonight, take a moment to taste the sweetness of Christ together. Read a psalm of praise. Sing a hymn. Share a testimony of how God has been good to your family. And remember: the God who is sweet to you in prosperity will be even sweeter in adversity, because adversity removes all the noise and leaves only Him.


For centuries, faithful Christians have reflected on passages like this one. Let us hear from a trusted voice of the past.

Samuel Rutherford, in his work "Letters of Samuel Rutherford," wrote these words:

"If God had told me some time ago that He was about to make me as happy as I could be in this world, and then had told me that He should begin by crippling me in arm or limb, and removing me from all my usual sources of enjoyment, I should have thought it a very strange mode of accomplishing His purpose. And yet, how is His wisdom manifest even in this!"

What Samuel Rutherford is reminding us is that the truths we have read tonight in Psalm 34:8; 1 Peter 1:6-8 are not new. They have been treasured by faithful Christians for centuries. And they are just as true and just as needed in our family today as they were hundreds of years ago. Let us take these words to heart and let them shape the way we live together as a family.


Discussion time. These questions are for every member of the family, young and old alike.

Question 1: What does it mean to 'taste and see' that the Lord is good? How is tasting different from just knowing?

Question 2: Peter says trial of faith is more precious than gold. Why would a tested faith be more valuable than wealth?

Question 3: Rutherford found more sweetness in Christ during exile than during comfort. Why might that be true?

Question 4: Can you share a time when you experienced God's goodness during a difficult period?

Take a few minutes to talk about these questions together. Let everyone share, and listen to one another with patience and love.


Now, here is our memory verse for today. Let us say it together:

"Whom having not seen, ye love; in whom, though now ye see him not, yet believing, ye rejoice with joy unspeakable and full of glory." -- 1 Peter 1:8

Try to say it out loud three times right now. Then, before bed tonight, see if you can say it from memory. Tomorrow morning, try again. The more we hide God's Word in our hearts, the more it will guide us when we need it most.


The Puritans believed that truth must be practiced, not just heard. Here is something you can do together:

Prepare a simple taste test with different foods — something sweet, something bitter, something sour. Blindfold family members and let them taste each one. Talk about how some of life's experiences are bitter, but God's goodness is always sweet. Read Psalm 34:8 together and discuss what it means to 'taste' God's goodness.


Let us bow our heads and pray. Dear Father in heaven, what a privilege it is to gather as a family around Your Word. We ask You to write these truths on our hearts. Bless our family, protect our home, and lead us in the path of righteousness for Your name's sake. In Christ we pray. Amen.

Until tomorrow, family. May the Lord bless you and keep you.
