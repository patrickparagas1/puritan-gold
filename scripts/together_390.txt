PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 29
"The Christian Directory on Marriage — Richard Baxter"
Puritan Marriage Wisdom — Richard Baxter's Counsel for Couples


Welcome, dear couple. For our final three days this month, we leave the Song of Solomon and sit at the feet of three great Puritan marriage counselors. Tonight we begin with Richard Baxter, whose Christian Directory contains some of the most extensive, practical, and pastorally sensitive counsel on marriage ever written.

Let us read Colossians 3:12-14 (KJV):

"Put on therefore, as the elect of God, holy and beloved, bowels of mercies, kindness, humbleness of mind, meekness, longsuffering; Forbearing one another, and forgiving one another, if any man have a quarrel against any: even as Christ forgave you, so also do ye. And above all these things put on charity, which is the bond of perfectness."


Richard Baxter (1615-1691) was a Puritan pastor in Kidderminster, England, whose ministry transformed an entire town. He married Margaret Charlton in 1662, and by all accounts, their marriage was one of profound mutual devotion. When she died, he wrote that he had "no such lovely importunate creature to be the daily companion of my labours and sufferings." Baxter knew marriage from the inside — its joys and its griefs — and his counsel reflects that lived experience.

Baxter organized his marriage counsel around what he called "the mutual duties of husband and wife." These duties, drawn from Scripture and tested by pastoral experience, form a comprehensive guide for married life.

First, Baxter taught the duty of delight. He wrote that husbands and wives are commanded to delight in each other — not merely to tolerate each other, not merely to fulfill obligations, but to take genuine pleasure in each other's company. He grounded this in Proverbs 5:18-19: "Rejoice with the wife of thy youth." Baxter insisted that delight is not merely a feeling that comes and goes; it is a discipline that must be cultivated. When delight fades — and it will at times — the remedy is not to seek delight elsewhere but to rediscover it in the one to whom you are covenanted. This requires attention, effort, and the willingness to see your spouse with fresh eyes.

Second, Baxter taught the duty of patience. He wrote with remarkable honesty: "You must bear with the infirmities and faults of each other. You were not married to an angel but to a sinful, imperfect creature. If you had expected perfection, you should have stayed single." Baxter saw patience as the most tested virtue in marriage and the most essential. He warned against the habit of magnifying faults — making small irritations into large grievances — and commended instead the practice of magnifying virtues. "See the best in your spouse," he wrote, "and the worst will shrink in your estimation."

Third, Baxter taught the duty of communication. He was remarkably ahead of his time in emphasizing the importance of open, honest, kind conversation between husband and wife. He urged couples to talk daily — not about household logistics alone, but about their hearts, their struggles, their joys, and their spiritual state. He warned against the "silent treatment" as a weapon of marital warfare, calling it "a cruelty worse than blows, for it wounds the soul while leaving the body untouched."

Fourth, Baxter taught the duty of mutual sanctification. Drawing on Ephesians 5, he argued that husbands and wives are called to help each other grow in holiness. This does not mean constant correction. It means modeling virtue, encouraging growth, praying for each other's weaknesses, and creating an environment where holiness is attractive rather than burdensome. Baxter wrote that "the best husband is the one whose wife becomes more Christlike because of him, and the best wife is the one whose husband becomes more godly because of her."

Fifth, Baxter taught the duty of unity in the face of opposition. He knew from his own experience — he was ejected from his pulpit and imprisoned for his faith — that external pressures can either divide a couple or unite them. He counseled couples to face outward together, presenting a united front to the world, even when they disagreed privately. "Quarrel in the closet if you must," he wrote, "but present a united face to the world."

Sixth, Baxter taught the duty of sexual faithfulness and generosity. He was explicit about the importance of physical intimacy as a safeguard against temptation and as a means of mutual comfort. He urged couples to be generous with each other — not withholding physical affection as punishment or leverage. He wrote that "the bed of marriage is a place of giving, not taking; of comfort, not control."

Baxter's wisdom is timeless because it is grounded in the character of Christ. Every duty he outlines for marriage is a reflection of how Christ treats His church: with delight, patience, communication, sanctification, unity, and generous love.


Turn to each other and consider these questions.

Question 1: Which of Baxter's six duties speaks most urgently to your marriage right now — delight, patience, communication, mutual sanctification, unity, or sexual faithfulness? Discuss honestly.

Question 2: Baxter warns against magnifying faults and commends magnifying virtues. What virtues in your spouse have you been overlooking? Name them tonight.

Question 3: Baxter says that marriage is not to an angel but to an imperfect creature. What imperfection in your spouse do you need to extend more patience toward? Can you release it tonight?


Tonight's prayer focus: Pray through Baxter's six duties together. Ask God to strengthen your delight, deepen your patience, enrich your communication, advance your mutual sanctification, fortify your unity, and guard your faithfulness. Pray specifically for the area where your marriage is weakest.


Richard Baxter loved his wife well, and when she was gone, the ache of her absence testified to the depth of their bond. May your marriage be such that the world would look at your love and say: there is a flame that could not be quenched. Goodnight, dear couple.
