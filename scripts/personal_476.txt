PURITAN GOLD -- My Growth, Day 15
"The Doctrine of Eschatology — The Last Things"
Theological Day — Death, Judgment, Heaven, and Hell


Welcome. Today is a Theological day. We examine the Puritan understanding of the last things — eschatology — the doctrines of death, judgment, heaven, and hell that form the horizon toward which all of history is moving.

Let us read 2 Corinthians 5:10 and Revelation 21:3-4 (KJV):

"For we must all appear before the judgment seat of Christ; that every one may receive the things done in his body, according to that he hath done, whether it be good or bad." "And I heard a great voice out of heaven saying, Behold, the tabernacle of God is with men, and he will dwell with them, and they shall be his people, and God himself shall be with them, and be their God. And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away."


The Puritans thought deeply about the last things because they believed that the end of the story shapes the living of the story. How you think about death, judgment, heaven, and hell profoundly affects how you live today.

On death, the Puritans were neither morbid nor evasive. They faced death with the sober realism of Psalm 90:12: "So teach us to number our days, that we may apply our hearts unto wisdom." Richard Baxter, who lived much of his life in poor health, wrote extensively about the preparation for death. He counseled Christians to think about death regularly — not to produce despair but to produce urgency. The life that may end tomorrow must not be wasted on trivialities today.

The Puritans taught that for the believer, death is not destruction but transition. To be absent from the body is to be present with the Lord. Death is the doorway through which the Christian passes from the country of faith to the country of sight. Thomas Watson wrote that death is the last enemy, but Christ has defeated it. For the believer, death has lost its sting. It remains an enemy, but a conquered one.

On judgment, the Puritans held that there will be a final, universal judgment at which all people will appear before Christ. Believers will be judged — not for condemnation, for there is no condemnation to those in Christ Jesus — but for reward. The works done in the body will be evaluated, and rewards will be given accordingly. This is not salvation by works but the recognition of faithful service within the context of salvation by grace.

The Puritans used the doctrine of judgment as a motivation for holy living. If every idle word will be brought into account, then every word matters. If every action done in the body will be evaluated, then every action matters. This is not a burden but a dignity — it means that your daily life has eternal significance. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is forgotten. Everything counts.

On heaven, the Puritans wrote with soaring eloquence. Richard Baxter's Saints' Everlasting Rest is one of the most magnificent visions of heaven in all of Christian literature. Baxter argued that heaven is not primarily a place of rest from labor but a place of rest in God — the eternal enjoyment of the One for whom we were made. Every longing that has gone unsatisfied in this life will be fulfilled. Every question that has gone unanswered will be resolved. Every tear that has been shed will be wiped away. And the vision of God — the beatific vision — will be the inexhaustible source of eternal joy.

Jonathan Edwards added to this vision his characteristic emphasis on the dynamic nature of heavenly experience. He argued that heaven will not be static but progressively glorious — an eternity of ever-increasing knowledge, love, and delight as the finite soul plunges deeper and deeper into the infinite ocean of God's beauty. There will be no boredom in heaven because God is inexhaustible.

On hell, the Puritans were equally solemn. They believed, because Scripture teaches, that those who die without Christ face an eternity of conscious punishment — separation from God's gracious presence and subjection to His just wrath. This doctrine is severe, but the Puritans refused to soften it because they believed it was true and because they believed it served a vital pastoral purpose: it motivated evangelism, urgency in preaching, and seriousness about sin.

Thomas Boston, in his Human Nature in Its Fourfold State, wrote that the contemplation of hell should produce in believers not terror but compassion — compassion for the lost, urgency in witness, and gratitude for the grace that has spared us from what we deserve.

The Puritans held all four of these doctrines together in a unified vision. Death reminds us of urgency. Judgment reminds us of accountability. Heaven reminds us of hope. And hell reminds us of the seriousness of the Gospel. Together, they form the horizon toward which we walk — the final chapter of the story that gives meaning to every previous chapter.


The last things are not far off — they are approaching. Live today in light of eternity. Lord, teach me to number my days. Let the reality of judgment make me serious, the promise of heaven make me hopeful, and the reality of hell make me compassionate. I look forward to the day when every tear is wiped away and You are all in all. Amen.
