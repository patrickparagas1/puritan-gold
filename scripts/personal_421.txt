PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Doctrine Deep-Dive, Day 21
"The Assurance of Salvation"
How Can I Know That I Am Truly Saved?

---

Good evening. There is a reason the church has always valued catechesis, the systematic teaching of Christian truth. Without it, faith becomes shallow and vulnerable. Tonight we go deeper.

Tonight we draw from the work of Multiple (Brooks, Owen, Goodwin), specifically from "Various works on assurance." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.

--- Scripture ---

Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is 2 Peter 1:10:

"Wherefore the rather, brethren, give diligence to make your calling and election sure: for if ye do these things, ye shall never fall."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.

--- Doctrine Study ---

Few questions trouble the Christian soul more than this: How can I know that I am truly saved? The Puritans gave extensive attention to this question, and their answers remain among the most helpful in the history of the church.

The Westminster Confession of Faith (18.1-2) provides the foundational framework. It teaches that assurance is possible: believers 'may, in this life, be certainly assured that they are in the state of grace.' It is not presumption to possess assurance; it is the believer's birthright. But the Confession also teaches that assurance is not automatic: it is 'not so joined with faith but that a true believer may wait long, and conflict with many difficulties, before he be partaker of it.' Some believers possess strong assurance; others struggle with doubt for extended seasons.

Thomas Brooks, in 'Heaven on Earth,' devotes an entire treatise to the subject of assurance. Brooks defines assurance as 'the reflex act of the soul, by which a believer knows that he believes.' It is not saving faith itself but a confidence added to faith: the settled persuasion that Christ is mine and I am His.

Brooks identifies three grounds of assurance. The first is the promises of God. God has promised that all who come to Christ will not be cast out (John 6:37), that whoever believes on the Son has everlasting life (John 3:36), and that nothing shall separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus (Romans 8:38-39). These promises, believed and applied by faith, are the primary foundation of assurance. Brooks writes that the man who doubts his salvation should begin not by examining himself but by contemplating the promises.

The second ground is the internal witness of the Holy Spirit. Paul teaches in Romans 8:16 that 'the Spirit itself beareth witness with our spirit, that we are the children of God.' This is a direct, experiential testimony: the Spirit impresses upon the believer's consciousness the truth that he belongs to God. Brooks acknowledges that this witness varies in intensity and is not always felt with equal strength, but he insists that it is real and that every true believer has experienced it to some degree.

The third ground is the evidence of grace in one's life. This is where self-examination comes in. Brooks, drawing from 1 John, identifies marks of true grace: love for the brethren, hatred of sin, delight in God's Word, obedience to God's commands, and a desire for holiness. These marks are not the ground of salvation (that is Christ's work alone) but they are the evidence that salvation has occurred.

John Owen, in his treatment of assurance in 'Communion with God,' emphasizes the relational dimension. Assurance, for Owen, is not merely intellectual certainty but experiential communion. The man who walks closely with God, who delights in His presence, who feels the movements of His Spirit, has an assurance that goes beyond logical deduction. He knows God, and in knowing God, he knows that he is known by God.

Thomas Goodwin, in 'The Object and Acts of Justifying Faith,' addresses the problem of the believer who cannot seem to attain assurance. Goodwin counsels patience. He compares the soul seeking assurance to a man walking through a fog: the sun is there, behind the fog, even when he cannot see it. The absence of felt assurance does not mean the absence of true faith. Goodwin writes that many of the most genuine believers are the most troubled by doubt, precisely because they take their faith seriously enough to examine it rigorously.

Goodwin also warns against two errors. The first is false assurance: the presumptuous confidence of one who claims salvation without any evidence of grace. This man never doubts because he has never truly examined himself. The second is unnecessary despair: the agonized self-questioning of one who has genuine marks of grace but refuses to believe the evidence. This man always doubts because he has set the bar of assurance impossibly high.

The Puritan counsel to the man who lacks assurance is threefold: trust the promises, use the means of grace (Word, prayer, sacraments, fellowship), and examine the fruit. And above all, look to Christ. Assurance is not found by looking inward (though self-examination has its place) but by looking outward to the One who finished the work of salvation and sits at the right hand of God, interceding for you.

--- Reflection ---

Now let us turn the lens inward. What is God saying to you through tonight's study?

Question 1: Do I possess a settled assurance of my salvation, or do I wrestle with significant doubt?

Question 2: Which of the three grounds of assurance — God's promises, the Spirit's witness, or the evidence of grace — do I most need to attend to?

Question 3: Am I in danger of either false assurance (presumption without examination) or unnecessary despair (refusal to believe genuine evidence of grace)?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.

--- Closing Prayer ---

Lord, grant me the assurance that is my birthright as Your child. If I am truly Yours, let me know it. If there is reason for doubt, let me examine myself honestly. Help me to rest in Your promises, attend to Your Spirit, and look to Christ as the author and finisher of my faith. Amen.

Press on, dear saint. The race is worth running, and the prize is Christ Himself.
