PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 16
"Honor Your Father and Mother"
Christian Ethics & Moral Philosophy — Lesson 16

---

Welcome back, students, to another lesson in our Puritan Gold series. Today we take up one of the most far-reaching and practically consequential of all the Ten Commandments — the fifth commandment: "Honour thy father and thy mother: that thy days may be long upon the land which the LORD thy God giveth thee" (Exodus 20:12). This commandment occupies a pivotal position in the Decalogue, serving as a bridge between the first four commandments, which govern our relationship with God, and the remaining five commandments, which govern our relationships with our fellow human beings. As we shall see, the Puritans understood this commandment as far more than a simple instruction about family manners. They saw it as the foundational principle of all human authority, the cornerstone of civil society, and one of the most searching tests of true Christian obedience.

--- Lesson ---

PART ONE: THE POSITION AND PROMISE OF THE FIFTH COMMANDMENT

Let us begin by examining the unique position of this commandment within the Decalogue. The Ten Commandments were given by God to Moses on Mount Sinai approximately 1446 years before the birth of our Lord Jesus Christ, during the great Exodus of Israel from Egyptian bondage. They were written, the Scriptures tell us, by the very finger of God upon two tablets of stone. The first tablet contained the four commandments that address our duties toward God — having no other gods, making no graven images, not taking God's name in vain, and remembering the Sabbath. The second tablet contained the six commandments that address our duties toward our neighbor — and the fifth commandment stands at the head of this second tablet.

This placement is deeply significant. The Puritans recognized that the fifth commandment serves as a kind of hinge upon which all other social commandments turn. Thomas Watson, the great Puritan divine who served as rector of St. Stephen's, Walbrook, in London during the 1650s and who published his masterful exposition of the Ten Commandments in 1692, observed that the fifth commandment is "the first commandment of the second table, and it is fitly placed there, because the keeping of this commandment helps us to keep all the rest." Watson's reasoning was profound: if a person learns to honor authority in the home — if he learns reverence, submission, gratitude, and obedience within the family — he will carry those virtues into every other sphere of life. He will be less likely to murder, to commit adultery, to steal, to lie, or to covet, because he has learned the fundamental discipline of submitting his will to a higher authority.

The apostle Paul, writing to the Ephesians from his imprisonment in Rome around AD 61, drew special attention to the promise attached to this commandment: "Honour thy father and mother; which is the first commandment with promise; That it may be well with thee, and thou mayest live long on the earth" (Ephesians 6:2-3). This is remarkable. Of all the Ten Commandments, the fifth is the only one that carries a specific, stated blessing. God promises that those who honor their parents will experience long life and prosperity. The Puritans understood this promise both individually and corporately. Individually, the child who honors his parents positions himself under God's blessing — he benefits from the wisdom and guidance of those who have gone before him, and he avoids the destructive consequences of rebellion and self-will. Corporately, the society that preserves the principle of honor and authority will endure and flourish, while the society that abandons it will crumble from within.

William Gouge, a prominent Puritan pastor who served at St. Ann Blackfriars in London for forty-five years from 1608 until his death in 1653, wrote extensively about this in his monumental work "Of Domestical Duties," first published in 1622. Gouge argued that the family is the nursery of the church and the commonwealth. "Well-ordered families," he wrote, "naturally produce a good order in other societies. If the family be not under a wise and holy government, it will be hard for any other society to be kept in good order." When children learn to honor their parents at home, they learn the habits of heart that make them faithful church members, obedient citizens, diligent workers, and honest neighbors. When that foundational habit breaks down, every other institution suffers.

PART TWO: THE MEANING OF HONOR — WATSON'S THREEFOLD ANALYSIS

What does it mean to "honour" one's father and mother? The Hebrew word translated "honour" is kavad, which carries the fundamental meaning of "to be heavy" or "to give weight to." To honor someone is to recognize their significance, to treat them as weighty and important, to take them seriously. It is the opposite of treating someone lightly, dismissively, or with contempt. The Puritans explored the implications of this rich concept with characteristic thoroughness.

Thomas Watson identified three essential components of the honor owed to parents. The first is reverence — an inward attitude of respect and esteem that manifests itself in outward behavior. Leviticus 19:3 commands: "Ye shall fear every man his mother, and his father, and keep my sabbaths: I am the LORD your God." The word "fear" here does not mean terror but rather a profound respect that shapes how we speak to our parents, how we speak about them, how we carry ourselves in their presence, and how we respond to their instruction. Watson noted that children should "rise up before their parents," a reference to Leviticus 19:32, which instructs the young to rise in the presence of the aged as a sign of respect. Even in small gestures — in the tone of our voice, the attentiveness of our listening, the willingness to set aside our own preferences — we demonstrate the reverence that the fifth commandment requires.

Watson cited the example of Solomon, the wisest of Israel's kings, who, when his mother Bathsheba came to speak with him, "rose up to meet her, and bowed himself unto her, and sat down on his throne, and caused a seat to be set for the king's mother; and she sat on his right hand" (1 Kings 2:19). Here was the most powerful man in Israel, yet he rose to honor his mother, provided a seat for her at his right hand, and gave her a respectful hearing. Solomon understood that his royal authority did not exempt him from the duty of filial reverence.

The second component is obedience — the willing submission to the lawful commands of those in authority. "Children, obey your parents in the Lord: for this is right" (Ephesians 6:1). Obedience is the practical expression of honor. It is not enough to feel respect for our parents if we disregard their instructions. The Puritans were careful to note the qualifying phrase "in the Lord," which establishes the limits of obedience. We are to obey our parents in all things that are consistent with God's law. If a parent commands something that violates the clear teaching of Scripture, the child's ultimate allegiance to God takes precedence. But the Puritans also warned against using this exception as an excuse for disobedience in ordinary matters. The vast majority of parental instructions — about chores, studies, behavior, associations, and daily responsibilities — fall well within the bounds of lawful authority, and in these matters, cheerful and prompt obedience is the Christian duty of every child.

Richard Baxter, the prolific Puritan author who served as minister at Kidderminster from 1641 to 1660 and transformed that congregation from spiritual indifference to remarkable godliness, addressed the topic of obedience in his "Christian Directory," published in 1673. Baxter wrote that children should obey their parents "not grudgingly, or with murmuring, or with an unwilling mind, but with a ready, cheerful, and willing obedience." He compared the obedience of children to the obedience of servants to their masters, noting that both are "doing the will of God from the heart" (Ephesians 6:6). Baxter was insistent that the manner of obedience mattered as much as the act itself. Sullen compliance, delayed response, and obedience accompanied by complaint all fall short of the honor that the fifth commandment requires.

The third component Watson identified is gratitude — the recognition of the enormous debt we owe to our parents for giving us life, nurturing us through infancy, providing for our needs, instructing us in the ways of God, and bearing the countless burdens that come with raising a child. Watson wrote with great tenderness about the sacrifices of parents, particularly mothers, noting that they endure the pain of childbirth, the sleepless nights of infancy, the anxieties of childhood illnesses, and the long labor of moral and spiritual instruction — all without expectation of repayment, motivated only by love. To dishonor such parents, Watson declared, is to commit an act of profound ingratitude against those who have given more for our welfare than we can ever repay.

The practical expression of gratitude, the Puritans taught, includes caring for parents in their old age. Paul wrote to Timothy: "But if any widow have children or nephews, let them learn first to shew piety at home, and to requite their parents: for that is good and acceptable before God" (1 Timothy 5:4). The word "requite" means to repay — to return to our parents some measure of the care and provision they gave to us. Jesus Himself condemned the Pharisees for their practice of declaring their property "Corban" (a gift devoted to God) as a way of avoiding the obligation to support their aging parents: "Full well ye reject the commandment of God, that ye may keep your own tradition. For Moses said, Honour thy father and thy mother; and, Whoso curseth father or mother, let him die the death: But ye say, If a man shall say to his father or mother, It is Corban, that is to say, a gift, by whatsoever thou mightest be profited by me; he shall be free. And ye suffer him no more to do ought for his father or his mother; Making the word of God of none effect through your tradition, which ye have delivered: and many such like things do ye" (Mark 7:9-13). Jesus made clear that no religious tradition or practice can excuse the failure to honor and care for one's parents. The fifth commandment remains binding in every stage of life.

PART THREE: THE BROADER SCOPE — ALL LEGITIMATE AUTHORITY

While the fifth commandment specifically names fathers and mothers, the Puritans and the broader Reformed tradition understood it to encompass all forms of legitimate human authority. The Westminster Larger Catechism, completed in 1648 by the Assembly of Divines at Westminster, provides the most comprehensive treatment of this extended application. Question 124 asks: "Who are meant by father and mother in the fifth commandment?" The answer states that by "father and mother" are meant "not only natural parents, but all superiors in age and gifts, and especially such as, by God's ordinance, are over us in place of authority, whether in family, church, or commonwealth."

This principle is of enormous importance for understanding Puritan social thought. The Puritans identified three primary spheres of authority ordained by God: the family, the church, and the state. In each sphere, God has appointed individuals to exercise authority for the good of those under their care, and in each sphere, the fifth commandment applies.

In the family, the authority of parents over children is the most natural and most immediate application of the commandment. But the Puritans also extended the principle to the relationship between husband and wife, between masters and servants, and between older and younger siblings. Every household, they believed, should be a little commonwealth, ordered by the principles of love, authority, and mutual obligation.

In the church, the fifth commandment governs the relationship between pastors and people. The Scriptures are clear that congregations owe their ministers honor, obedience, and material support. "Obey them that have the rule over you, and submit yourselves: for they watch for your souls, as they that must give account, that they may do it with joy, and not with grief: for that is unprofitable for you" (Hebrews 13:17). "Let the elders that rule well be counted worthy of double honour, especially they who labour in the word and doctrine. For the scripture saith, Thou shalt not muzzle the ox that treadeth out the corn. And, The labourer is worthy of his reward" (1 Timothy 5:17-18). The great Puritan pastor John Owen, who served as vice-chancellor of Oxford University under Oliver Cromwell and who published over eighty works of theology during his lifetime, insisted that the pastoral office carries a sacred authority derived from Christ Himself. To dishonor a faithful pastor, Owen argued, is to dishonor the Lord who appointed him.

In the commonwealth — what we would call civil government — the fifth commandment establishes the duty of citizens to honor and obey their magistrates. The apostle Paul addressed this duty directly in his Epistle to the Romans, written around AD 57: "Let every soul be subject unto the higher powers. For there is no power but of God: the powers that be are ordained of God. Whosoever therefore resisteth the power, resisteth the ordinance of God: and they that resist shall receive to themselves damnation. For rulers are not a terror to good works, but to the evil. Wilt thou then not be afraid of the power? do that which is good, and thou shalt have praise of the same: For he is the minister of God to thee for good. But if thou do that which is evil, be afraid; for he beareth not the sword in vain: for he is the minister of God, a revenger to execute wrath upon him that doeth evil" (Romans 13:1-4).

The Puritans took this passage with utmost seriousness. They believed that civil magistrates are God's deputies, appointed to maintain order, protect the innocent, punish the wicked, and promote the conditions necessary for human flourishing. Samuel Rutherford, the Scottish Presbyterian theologian who served as professor of divinity at the University of St. Andrews and who published his landmark work "Lex, Rex" in 1644, explored the relationship between the authority of God and the authority of earthly rulers with extraordinary depth. The title itself — "Lex, Rex," meaning "The Law is King" — was revolutionary. Rutherford argued that no human ruler stands above the law of God, that all political authority is derived from and limited by divine authority, and that when a ruler systematically violates the law of God, the people have the right and indeed the duty to resist.

This principle was of enormous practical significance during the English Civil War of the 1640s, when Puritan members of Parliament and the New Model Army took up arms against King Charles I, whom they accused of governing tyrannically and of attempting to impose religious practices contrary to Scripture. The Puritans did not take this step lightly. They agonized over the tension between the duty to honor magistrates and the duty to obey God. But they concluded that the fifth commandment, while it requires honor and obedience to legitimate authority, does not require blind submission to tyranny. When the higher authority of God's law conflicts with the commands of a human ruler, the Christian must obey God rather than men, as the apostles declared in Acts 5:29: "We ought to obey God rather than men."

PART FOUR: THE DUTIES OF SUPERIORS — AUTHORITY AS SERVICE

One of the most distinctive features of the Puritan treatment of the fifth commandment was their insistence that authority carries obligation as well as privilege. The commandment is not merely addressed to those under authority; it speaks with equal force to those who exercise authority. Parents, pastors, magistrates, employers, and teachers all bear solemn responsibilities toward those entrusted to their care.

The Westminster Larger Catechism, in Questions 129 and 130, lists the duties of superiors with impressive specificity. Superiors are required to love, pray for, and bless those under their care; to instruct, counsel, and admonish them; to commend, reward, and encourage them when they do well; to rebuke, correct, and discipline them when they do wrong; to protect and provide for them; and to set a consistent example of godly living. The Puritans were adamant that authority is a form of stewardship — a trust given by God, to be exercised for the benefit of others, and for which a strict accounting will be required.

William Gouge, in his "Of Domestical Duties," devoted hundreds of pages to the specific obligations of parents toward their children. He wrote that parents must provide for their children's physical needs — food, clothing, shelter, and health care. They must provide for their intellectual development — education in reading, writing, arithmetic, and a useful trade or profession. Above all, they must provide for their spiritual welfare — teaching them the Scriptures, catechizing them in the doctrines of the faith, praying with them and for them, taking them to public worship, and modeling before them a life of genuine godliness. Gouge warned that parents who neglect the spiritual instruction of their children are committing a terrible sin: "It is the cruelest neglect in the world to let a child go to hell while you take care only of his body."

Cotton Mather, the prominent New England Puritan minister who served at the North Church in Boston from 1685 until his death in 1728, echoed these sentiments in his famous essay "A Family Well-Ordered," published in 1699. Mather argued that the family is the primary institution for the transmission of the faith from one generation to the next. He wrote with great urgency about the duty of fathers to catechize their children — to teach them the great doctrines of Scripture, to explain the meaning of the sacraments, to impress upon them the reality of sin and the necessity of a Savior, and to point them to Christ as the only hope of salvation. Mather drew from Deuteronomy 6:6-7: "And these words, which I command thee this day, shall be in thine heart: And thou shalt teach them diligently unto thy children, and shalt talk of them when thou sittest in thine house, and when thou walkest by the way, and when thou liest down, and when thou risest up."

The Puritans also stressed that authority must be exercised with tenderness and restraint. Paul's instruction to fathers is pointed: "And, ye fathers, provoke not your children to wrath: but bring them up in the nurture and admonition of the Lord" (Ephesians 6:4). The word "nurture" here includes both instruction and discipline, while "admonition" refers to verbal correction and warning. But the overarching direction is one of love, patience, and gentleness. Discipline that is excessive, arbitrary, or administered in anger violates the fifth commandment just as surely as disobedience does. Baxter counseled parents to "rule their children by love rather than by fear," and to remember that "your authority is given you by God, not for your own ease or honor, but for the good of those whom God has placed in your care."

PART FIVE: THE SINS AGAINST THE FIFTH COMMANDMENT

The Puritans catalogued the sins against the fifth commandment with their characteristic thoroughness, examining failures on both sides of the authority relationship.

On the part of those under authority, the sins include: disobedience to lawful commands; contempt or disrespect shown through words, gestures, or attitudes; ingratitude for the sacrifices and care of those in authority; cursing or reviling parents and other superiors; stubbornness and refusal to accept correction; rebellion against legitimate authority; neglect of aging parents; and bringing shame upon one's family through wicked behavior.

The Scriptures speak with devastating directness about the sin of dishonoring parents. "The eye that mocketh at his father, and despiseth to obey his mother, the ravens of the valley shall pick it out, and the young eagles shall eat it" (Proverbs 30:17). "Whoso curseth his father or his mother, his lamp shall be put out in obscure darkness" (Proverbs 20:20). Under the Mosaic law, the penalty for striking or cursing a parent was death: "And he that smiteth his father, or his mother, shall be surely put to death" (Exodus 21:15). "And he that curseth his father, or his mother, shall surely be put to death" (Exodus 21:17). While the Puritans did not advocate applying these penalties literally under the New Covenant, they pointed to them as evidence of how seriously God regards the sin of dishonoring parents. The severity of the penalty reveals the gravity of the offense.

On the part of those in authority, the sins include: tyranny and oppressive rule; negligence in providing instruction and care; partiality and favoritism among children or subordinates; excessive severity in discipline; failure to pray for and encourage those under one's care; setting a bad example through one's own behavior; and exploiting one's position of authority for personal gain. Eli the priest is the great biblical example of a parent who failed in his duties. Although he served faithfully as Israel's high priest, he was tragically negligent in disciplining his sons Hophni and Phinehas, who "knew not the LORD" (1 Samuel 2:12) and committed terrible sins in the tabernacle. God's judgment upon Eli's house was severe: "For I have told him that I will judge his house for ever for the iniquity which he knoweth; because his sons made themselves vile, and he restrained them not" (1 Samuel 3:13). The lesson is sobering: authority that is not exercised faithfully brings judgment not only upon those who abuse it but upon those who neglect it.

PART SIX: BIBLICAL EXAMPLES OF HONORING AND DISHONORING PARENTS

The Scriptures provide us with vivid examples of both the honoring and dishonoring of parents, and the Puritans drew upon these examples extensively in their teaching.

Ruth, the Moabitess, provides one of the most beautiful examples of honoring a parent figure in all of Scripture. After the death of her husband, Ruth clung to her mother-in-law Naomi with these immortal words: "Intreat me not to leave thee, or to return from following after thee: for whither thou goest, I will go; and where thou lodgest, I will lodge: thy people shall be my people, and thy God my God: Where thou diest, will I die, and there will I be buried: the LORD do so to me, and more also, if ought but death part thee and me" (Ruth 1:16-17). Ruth gave up her homeland, her people, and her prospects to care for her aging mother-in-law — and God rewarded her faithfulness beyond measure, making her the great-grandmother of King David and an ancestress of our Lord Jesus Christ.

Joseph, the son of Jacob, provides another powerful example. After his brothers sold him into slavery and he endured years of suffering in Egypt, Joseph rose to become the second most powerful man in the known world. When his aged father Jacob finally came to Egypt, Joseph "made ready his chariot, and went up to meet Israel his father, to Goshen, and presented himself unto him; and he fell on his neck, and wept on his neck a good while" (Genesis 46:29). Joseph, despite his exalted position, honored his father with tenderness, provision, and filial affection throughout the remainder of Jacob's life.

By contrast, Absalom, the son of King David, stands as a terrible example of a child who dishonored his father in the most extreme way possible. Not content with rebellion, Absalom conspired to overthrow his father's kingdom, drove David from Jerusalem in fear for his life, and publicly defiled his father's concubines on the rooftop of the palace in the sight of all Israel (2 Samuel 16:22). Absalom's rebellion brought civil war, devastation, and death upon the entire nation. And his end was as tragic as his rebellion was wicked: caught by his hair in the branches of an oak tree, he was slain by David's general Joab. David's lament over his fallen son remains one of the most poignant passages in all of Scripture: "O my son Absalom, my son, my son Absalom! would God I had died for thee, O Absalom, my son, my son!" (2 Samuel 18:33).

Ham, the son of Noah, provides yet another cautionary example. When Noah lay uncovered in his tent after drinking wine, Ham "saw the nakedness of his father, and told his two brethren without" (Genesis 9:22). Rather than covering his father's shame, Ham exposed it and apparently spoke of it with disrespect. His brothers Shem and Japheth, by contrast, "took a garment, and laid it upon both their shoulders, and went backward, and covered the nakedness of their father; and their faces were backward, and they saw not their father's nakedness" (Genesis 9:23). The consequences of Ham's dishonor fell upon his descendants, while Shem and Japheth received their father's blessing. The lesson is clear: how we treat the failings and vulnerabilities of our parents — whether we cover them with love or expose them with contempt — reveals the true condition of our hearts.

PART SEVEN: THE FIFTH COMMANDMENT AND THE GOSPEL

The Puritans never tired of pointing their readers and listeners to the gospel in every exposition of the law. The fifth commandment, they observed, reveals our need for Christ in at least three ways.

First, it convicts us of sin. Who among us has perfectly honored our parents? Who has never spoken disrespectfully, obeyed grudgingly, or failed in gratitude? The fifth commandment, like all the commandments, exposes the depth of our sinfulness and drives us to seek the forgiveness that is found only in Christ.

Second, it reveals Christ as the perfect example of filial obedience. Jesus Christ, the eternal Son of God, honored His heavenly Father with perfect, unbroken obedience throughout His earthly life. "I do always those things that please him," Jesus declared (John 8:29). And He honored His earthly parents as well. Luke records that after the incident in the temple when Jesus was twelve years old, "he went down with them, and came to Nazareth, and was subject unto them" (Luke 2:51). The God of the universe, in the person of His incarnate Son, submitted Himself to the authority of a carpenter and his wife. Even from the cross, as He bore the sins of the world, Jesus remembered His duty to His mother, committing her to the care of the beloved disciple: "Woman, behold thy son! Then saith he to the disciple, Behold thy mother!" (John 19:26-27). In all of this, Christ fulfilled the fifth commandment perfectly on our behalf.

Third, the fifth commandment points us to our relationship with God as our heavenly Father. The entire structure of the commandment — the call to honor, reverence, obey, and give thanks to those who have given us life and cared for us — finds its ultimate fulfillment in our relationship with God. He is our Creator, our Provider, our Protector, and our Guide. To Him we owe an infinite debt of honor, gratitude, and obedience. And in the gospel, we are adopted into His family as His children: "For ye have not received the spirit of bondage again to fear; but ye have received the Spirit of adoption, whereby we cry, Abba, Father" (Romans 8:15). The honor we learn to give our earthly parents is training for the far greater honor we owe to our heavenly Father.

PART EIGHT: PURITAN APPLICATION TO DAILY LIFE

The Puritans were never content with mere theoretical exposition. They insisted on bringing the commandments to bear upon the daily realities of life. Let us consider several practical applications of the fifth commandment that the Puritans frequently addressed.

First, regarding speech. The Puritans taught that the way we speak to and about our parents is one of the most immediate tests of whether we are keeping the fifth commandment. Do we speak respectfully? Do we listen attentively when they speak? Do we refrain from mocking, contradicting, or dismissing their words? Do we speak well of them to others, or do we complain about them behind their backs? Watson observed that "the tongue is the index of the heart," and that the words we use about our parents reveal our true attitude toward them.

Second, regarding obedience in the small things. The Puritans taught that faithfulness in small acts of obedience is the proving ground for faithfulness in great matters. The child who cheerfully does his chores, completes his studies, and responds promptly to his parents' instructions is building a character that will serve him well throughout his life. The child who is negligent in small duties, who must be reminded repeatedly, and who obeys only under compulsion is developing habits of heart that will plague him in every sphere of life.

Third, regarding care for aging parents. As we noted earlier, the fifth commandment does not expire when a child reaches adulthood. The duty to honor parents continues throughout their lifetime and includes the obligation to provide for their physical needs, to visit them, to show them affection, and to ensure that they are treated with dignity and respect in their declining years. The Puritans would have had strong words for a culture that warehouses its elderly in impersonal institutions while their children pursue personal comfort and convenience.

Fourth, regarding the intersection with other commandments. The Puritans frequently pointed out how the fifth commandment reinforces and supports the other commandments. A child who learns to honor his parents will be more likely to honor God (the first four commandments). He will be less inclined to violence (the sixth commandment), sexual immorality (the seventh commandment), theft (the eighth commandment), dishonesty (the ninth commandment), and covetousness (the tenth commandment). The fifth commandment, in this sense, is the gateway to the entire second table of the law.

PART NINE: THE FIFTH COMMANDMENT IN EDUCATION

As homeschool students, you are in a unique position to appreciate the fifth commandment's relevance to education. The Puritans were among the most passionate advocates for education in the history of Western civilization. They founded Harvard College in 1636, just six years after the founding of the Massachusetts Bay Colony, with the express purpose of training an educated ministry and an informed citizenry. The founding document of Harvard declared that the college was established "to advance learning and perpetuate it to posterity, dreading to leave an illiterate ministry to the churches, when our present ministers shall lie in the dust."

The Puritans viewed education as an extension of the fifth commandment. Parents have a God-given responsibility to educate their children — not merely in reading, writing, and arithmetic, but in the knowledge of God, the truths of Scripture, and the principles of godly living. The teacher stands in loco parentis — in the place of the parent — and exercises a delegated authority that deserves the same honor and respect that the fifth commandment requires toward parents.

John Milton, the great Puritan poet who wrote "Paradise Lost" and who served as Secretary for Foreign Tongues under Oliver Cromwell, published a treatise titled "Of Education" in 1644 in which he argued that "the end of learning is to repair the ruins of our first parents by regaining to know God aright, and out of that knowledge to love Him, to imitate Him, to be like Him." For Milton, education was not merely the accumulation of knowledge but the formation of character — the shaping of young minds and hearts to know, love, and serve God. This is the fifth commandment applied to education: the student honors his teachers by diligent study, respectful attention, and the faithful application of what he has learned.

PART TEN: THE FIFTH COMMANDMENT AND CHURCH HISTORY

Throughout church history, the fifth commandment has played a central role in shaping the relationship between Christians and the institutions of authority that surround them. The early church fathers, including Augustine of Hippo, who lived from AD 354 to 430, taught that the fifth commandment required Christians to honor not only their biological parents but also the church as their spiritual mother. Augustine wrote in his work "On the Morals of the Catholic Church" that the church nourishes, teaches, and guides believers just as a mother nourishes, teaches, and guides her children, and that the honor due to the church is a natural extension of the honor due to parents.

John Calvin, the great Reformer of Geneva who lived from 1509 to 1564 and whose "Institutes of the Christian Religion" became the foundational theological text of the Reformed tradition, devoted careful attention to the fifth commandment in his exposition of the Decalogue. Calvin wrote that God "has been pleased to direct us to those whom He has distinguished by some eminence to pay them honor, obedience, and gratitude." Calvin emphasized that the honor required by this commandment must be "sincere" — not merely outward and formal, but flowing from a genuine heart of respect and love. He warned that "those who stubbornly refuse to bend before their parents are monsters, and not men."

The Puritans carried Calvin's teaching forward with their characteristic combination of theological depth and practical application. They saw the fifth commandment as the foundation of social order and spent enormous effort teaching it to their children, preaching it from their pulpits, and applying it in their communities. The remarkable stability and productivity of Puritan societies — both in England and in New England — can be attributed in no small measure to their serious commitment to the principles embodied in this commandment.

PART ELEVEN: THE FIFTH COMMANDMENT IN PURITAN NEW ENGLAND

The practical outworking of the fifth commandment was nowhere more visible than in the Puritan settlements of New England. The Massachusetts Bay Colony, founded in 1630 under the leadership of Governor John Winthrop, established laws that directly reflected the fifth commandment's principles. In 1646, the colony enacted the famous "Old Deluder Satan Act," which required every town of fifty households to appoint a teacher and every town of one hundred households to establish a grammar school — all because the Puritans believed that parents and civil authorities had a divine obligation to educate children, an obligation rooted in the fifth commandment's extension of parental authority to all who bear responsibility for the young.

The colony's legal code also included provisions for dealing with children who were persistently rebellious or disobedient to their parents. While the extreme penalties of the Mosaic law were rarely if ever enforced, the mere presence of these laws in the statute books reveals how seriously the Puritans took the fifth commandment. They understood that a society that tolerates the widespread dishonoring of parents will eventually dissolve into chaos and moral anarchy.

Jonathan Edwards, the great preacher of Northampton, Massachusetts, who is often regarded as the greatest theologian America has produced, and who served as pastor of the Northampton congregation from 1729 to 1750, preached frequently on the duties of parents and children. Edwards stressed that the fifth commandment is not merely a commandment about external compliance but about the affections of the heart. A child who obeys his parents outwardly while harboring resentment and contempt inwardly has not truly kept the commandment. Edwards urged parents to win the hearts of their children through love, instruction, and consistent godly example, and he urged children to recognize in their parents the very hand of God's providence — for it was God who chose their parents, placed them in their family, and appointed the circumstances of their upbringing.

The New England Primer, first published around 1690 and used as the standard textbook in Puritan schools for over a century, included catechism questions that reinforced the fifth commandment. Children were taught from their earliest years that honoring parents was not merely a social convention but a divine command, carrying the weight of God's own authority. The Primer taught the alphabet itself through moral instruction, with the letter "F" illustrated by the couplet: "The idle Fool / Is whipt at School." Even in the most basic elements of education, the principles of authority, obedience, and correction were woven into the fabric of a child's learning.

Increase Mather, the father of Cotton Mather and himself a renowned minister who served as president of Harvard College from 1685 to 1701, published a treatise in 1679 called "A Call from Heaven" in which he lamented the declining respect for parental authority that he observed in the second generation of New England Puritans. Mather warned that when children cease to honor their parents, and when parents cease to discipline and instruct their children, the spiritual vitality of a community will rapidly deteriorate. His warning proved prophetic: the spiritual decline that Mather identified would continue through subsequent generations, contributing to the conditions that made the Great Awakening of the 1730s and 1740s necessary.

CONCLUSION

As we bring this lesson to a close, let us remember that the fifth commandment is not merely about external behavior — it is about the condition of the heart. It calls us to cultivate an inward disposition of honor, gratitude, and reverence toward those whom God has placed in authority over us. It reminds us that authority is a divine institution — not a human invention — and that it is designed for our good and for the good of society. It challenges us to examine our own attitudes and behavior: Are we honoring our parents — not merely with our outward actions, but with our hearts? Are we grateful for the sacrifices they have made on our behalf? Are we submitting to their authority with cheerfulness and love? Are we caring for them as they age and need our support?

And ultimately, the fifth commandment points us to the greatest authority relationship of all — our relationship with God the Father, who created us, sustains us, redeems us, and calls us to honor Him with every thought, word, and deed. As we honor our earthly parents faithfully, we are learning the habits of heart that will prepare us for an eternity of honoring our heavenly Father in the courts of glory.
