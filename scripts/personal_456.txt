PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Guided Puritan Reading, Day 25
"The Wisdom of God"
Stephen Charnock — The Attributes of God: Wisdom


Good evening. There is a reason the Puritans read so many books alongside their Bibles. They knew that God raises up teachers in every generation to illuminate His Word. Tonight we sit with one of those teachers and listen carefully.

Tonight we draw from the work of Stephen Charnock, specifically from "The Existence and Attributes of God." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Romans 11:33-36:

"O the depth of the riches both of the wisdom and knowledge of God! how unsearchable are his judgments, and his ways past finding out! For who hath known the mind of the Lord? or who hath been his counsellor? Or who hath first given to him, and it shall be recompensed unto him again? For of him, and through him, and to him, are all things: to whom be glory for ever. Amen."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


The wisdom of God is that attribute by which He orders all things in the most perfect manner toward the most perfect ends by the most perfect means. If omniscience is God's knowledge of all things, wisdom is His ability to use that knowledge perfectly. Omniscience knows everything; wisdom knows what to do with everything. The two attributes are closely related but distinct, and Charnock explores the distinction with characteristic care.

Charnock defines divine wisdom as 'the rational ordering of all things to their proper end.' God does not merely know all things; He arranges all things with infinite skill. Every event, every circumstance, every detail of creation and history is part of a grand design that is moving toward a determined purpose. There are no accidents in God's universe, no wasted moments, no purposeless suffering. Everything — including the things that seem most random, chaotic, or evil — is woven into a pattern of breathtaking wisdom.

Charnock illustrates God's wisdom from three spheres. First, the wisdom displayed in creation. The natural world reveals an intelligence so vast and intricate that the most brilliant human minds can only scratch its surface. The structure of a single cell, the orbit of the planets, the ecosystem of a coral reef, the migration of birds — each displays a wisdom that far exceeds anything human beings have ever conceived. Charnock writes that the study of nature is the study of God's wisdom written in visible form.

Second, the wisdom displayed in providence. God governs the affairs of nations and individuals with a wisdom that is often hidden in the moment but revealed in retrospect. Joseph told his brothers, 'Ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good' (Genesis 50:20). The events that seemed most tragic — the betrayal, the slavery, the imprisonment — were all instruments of a divine wisdom that was working toward an end that none of the human actors could see. Charnock argues that this pattern repeats throughout Scripture and throughout the lives of believers: what looks like disaster at one point is revealed as wisdom at another.

Third, and most gloriously, Charnock extols the wisdom displayed in redemption. The plan of salvation is the supreme exhibition of divine wisdom. How could God remain just while justifying the ungodly? How could He punish sin without destroying the sinner He loved? How could He maintain His holiness while extending His mercy? The answer is the cross of Christ — the place where justice and mercy, holiness and love, wrath and grace meet in perfect harmony. Charnock writes that the cross is the wisdom of God in its most concentrated, most glorious expression. It is the solution to a problem that no created mind could have conceived, executed by a power that no created being possesses.

Charnock draws several practical applications from the wisdom of God. First, it teaches humility. If God's wisdom is infinite and ours is finite, then we should expect that we will often not understand His ways. The proper response to incomprehensible providence is not complaint but worship: 'O the depth of the riches both of the wisdom and knowledge of God!' The man who demands to understand every act of God has made his own intellect the measure of divinity.

Second, God's wisdom teaches trust. If God orders all things wisely, then the believer can trust Him even when the path is dark. The surgeon who operates on a patient does things that would seem cruel to an uninformed observer: cutting, removing, suturing. But the patient trusts the surgeon's skill, knowing that the pain is purposeful. So the believer trusts the wisdom of God, knowing that the painful providences serve a purpose too wise for present comprehension.

Third, Charnock argues that the wisdom of God should produce prayer. Because God is infinitely wise, we can ask Him for wisdom with confidence that He will give it generously. James 1:5 promises, 'If any of you lack wisdom, let him ask of God, that giveth to all men liberally, and upbraideth not; and it shall be given him.' The God who possesses infinite wisdom is delighted to share it with His children who ask.

Charnock also explores the relationship between God's wisdom and human suffering with particular pastoral sensitivity. He acknowledges that suffering is the great challenge to our confidence in God's wisdom. When the righteous suffer and the wicked prosper, the temptation is to conclude that God either does not know, does not care, or does not govern wisely. But Charnock answers that suffering is often the very instrument of God's deepest wisdom. The gold must pass through fire to be purified. The vine must be pruned to bear more fruit. The athlete must endure pain to build strength. In each case, what appears to be destruction is actually construction, and only the wise know the difference.

Let the wisdom of God be your confidence today. You do not need to understand everything. You do not need to see the whole picture. You need only trust the One who sees it all and orders it all with perfect, infinite, unsearchable wisdom.


The Puritans kept spiritual journals. Consider writing your answers to these questions.

Question 1: Am I willing to trust God's wisdom when I cannot understand His ways, or do I demand explanations before I will submit?

Question 2: How does the wisdom displayed at the cross — where justice and mercy meet in perfect harmony — deepen my worship?

Question 3: Am I regularly asking God for wisdom in the specific decisions and challenges I face, or am I relying on my own understanding?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


O God of infinite wisdom, I worship You for the depth and riches of Your knowledge. Your judgments are unsearchable, and Your ways past finding out. Teach me to trust Your wisdom when I cannot see Your hand. Teach me to worship rather than complain when Your providences are beyond my understanding. And grant me wisdom for the decisions I face today, for I am foolish and You are infinitely wise. Through Christ, in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge, Amen.

Go in peace. The God who began a good work in you will bring it to completion.
