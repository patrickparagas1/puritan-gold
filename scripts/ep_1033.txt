PURITAN GOLD -- Study Topic: The Book of Melchizedek, Part 3 of 8
"Without Father, Without Mother"
The Book of Melchizedek — Hebrews 7:1-3 — The eternal priesthood

---

Grace and peace to you, beloved friends. We have traced Melchizedek from his sudden appearance in Genesis 14 through the prophetic declaration of Psalm 110. Now we arrive at the place where the full theological significance of this mysterious figure is revealed — the Epistle to the Hebrews, chapter 7. Over the next four episodes, we shall walk through this remarkable chapter verse by verse, allowing the inspired writer to unfold for us the glory of Christ's priesthood as it is foreshadowed in Melchizedek.

Let us read Hebrews 7:1-3:

"For this Melchisedec, king of Salem, priest of the most high God, who met Abraham returning from the slaughter of the kings, and blessed him; To whom also Abraham gave a tenth part of all; first being by interpretation King of righteousness, and after that also King of Salem, which is, King of peace; Without father, without mother, without descent, having neither beginning of days, nor end of life; but made like unto the Son of God; abideth a priest continually."

--- Teaching ---

The writer of Hebrews begins his exposition of Melchizedek by recounting the essential facts from Genesis 14: this man was both king and priest, he met Abraham after the slaughter of the kings, he blessed Abraham, and Abraham paid him tithes. But then the writer does something extraordinary — he interprets the names and the silences of the Genesis account with a theological precision that reveals the hand of the Holy Spirit at work in the original narrative.

"First being by interpretation King of righteousness." The writer parses the Hebrew name Melchizedek — melek-tsedeq — and draws out its meaning. This is not merely an etymological observation; it is a theological declaration. The Messiah whom Melchizedek foreshadows is the King of Righteousness. He is the one of whom Jeremiah prophesied: "In his days Judah shall be saved, and Israel shall dwell safely: and this is his name whereby he shall be called, THE LORD OUR RIGHTEOUSNESS" (Jeremiah 23:6). Christ is righteous in His person and the source of righteousness for His people. As Paul would write, God "hath made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin; that we might be made the righteousness of God in him" (2 Corinthians 5:21).

"And after that also King of Salem, which is, King of peace." Salem, as we noted in our first episode, is related to shalom — peace. The order is significant, and the writer of Hebrews is careful to note it: first righteousness, then peace. There can be no true peace without righteousness. The peace that Christ brings is not a false peace built upon the suppression of justice, but a true and lasting peace founded upon the satisfaction of divine justice. At the cross, "mercy and truth are met together; righteousness and peace have kissed each other" (Psalm 85:10). Christ first established righteousness by bearing the penalty for sin, and then He became our peace, reconciling us to God.

John Owen expounded this point with characteristic depth. He wrote that the order of the names was not accidental but divinely arranged to teach a fundamental truth about the gospel. Righteousness must precede peace. Until the demands of God's law are fully satisfied, there can be no peace between God and sinners. But in Christ, those demands have been met. His active obedience fulfilled every requirement of the law; His passive obedience bore every penalty for transgression. And because righteousness has been established, peace flows freely and eternally.

Then comes the most extraordinary statement in the passage: "Without father, without mother, without descent, having neither beginning of days, nor end of life; but made like unto the Son of God; abideth a priest continually."

This verse has puzzled and fascinated readers for two millennia. What does it mean that Melchizedek was "without father, without mother"? Several interpretations have been proposed throughout church history.

Some have suggested that Melchizedek was a theophany — a pre-incarnate appearance of Christ Himself. This view has a long pedigree, going back to some of the early church fathers and finding advocates among certain Puritan writers as well. If Melchizedek was Christ appearing in human form before the incarnation, then the descriptions of being "without father, without mother" and "having neither beginning of days, nor end of life" would be literally true.

However, the majority of Reformed interpreters, including John Owen and John Calvin, have rejected this identification. The key phrase is "made like unto the Son of God." The writer says Melchizedek was "made like" Christ — that is, he was a type or figure who resembled the Son of God. If Melchizedek actually was the Son of God, the writer would not say he was "made like" Him; he would say he was Him. The language of resemblance implies distinction. Melchizedek was a real historical person who served as a type of Christ, not Christ Himself.

Owen argued persuasively that the phrases "without father, without mother, without descent" refer not to Melchizedek's actual nature but to the manner of his presentation in the Genesis narrative. The Bible, uniquely among ancient Near Eastern literature, is meticulous about genealogies. Every major figure in Genesis is introduced with a genealogical notice — who his father was, who his mother was, where he came from. But Melchizedek has no such notice. No father is named. No mother is named. No genealogy is provided. No birth is recorded. No death is recorded.

This silence, Owen maintained, was a deliberate act of the Holy Spirit. The Spirit who inspired the writing of Genesis chose to withhold this information so that Melchizedek's literary presentation would mirror the eternal nature of the Son of God. Just as the reader encounters Melchizedek without any beginning or ending, so the Son of God truly has no beginning of days and no end of life. The type points to the reality.

This is a profound hermeneutical principle. The silences of Scripture are as inspired as its statements. What God chose not to say about Melchizedek is as theologically significant as what He chose to say. The omission of genealogical data was not an oversight of the human author but an intention of the divine Author — a gap deliberately left in the narrative to create a typological portrait of the eternal Christ.

"Abideth a priest continually." Because no death is recorded for Melchizedek, the inspired writer draws the typological conclusion that he "abideth a priest continually." His priesthood, as presented in the text, has no termination. And this is precisely the point: Christ's priesthood has no termination. He is a priest forever. The Levitical priests served until death; Christ serves unto eternity. They were replaced by successors; He has no successor, for He needs none. He is alive forevermore, and His priesthood is as permanent as His person.

Thomas Goodwin, reflecting on this passage, wrote with great tenderness about the comfort this affords to believers. If Christ's priesthood is eternal, then His intercession is eternal. If His intercession is eternal, then the preservation of every believer is certain. No one for whom Christ intercedes can ever perish, for His intercession is as effectual as His atoning sacrifice and as enduring as His immortal life.

--- Application ---

1. Embrace Christ as your King of Righteousness first, and then receive Him as your King of Peace. Do not seek peace with God apart from the righteousness that Christ provides. Rest your conscience not upon your own efforts but upon His perfect obedience and atoning death.

2. Marvel at the Holy Spirit's design in Scripture. Even the silences of the Bible are purposeful. What is omitted is as inspired as what is included. Let this deepen your reverence for the Word of God and your confidence in its divine authorship.

3. Distinguish between the type and the reality. Melchizedek was a great and glorious type, but Christ is infinitely greater. Do not stop at the shadow; press on to the substance. Every type in Scripture is an arrow pointing to Jesus.

4. Rest in the permanence of Christ's priestly ministry. He does not grow weary. He does not forget. He does not fail. At this very moment, He is interceding for you at the right hand of the Father — and He will continue to do so forever.

--- Closing ---

Lord Jesus Christ, our eternal High Priest — King of Righteousness and King of Peace — we worship Thee. Thou art without beginning of days and without end of life. Thy priesthood shall never pass to another, and Thy intercession shall never cease. We rest our souls upon Thy unchangeable ministry and upon the Father's unbreakable oath. All glory be unto Thee, now and forever. Amen.

Until next time, beloved, may the peace that flows from Christ's righteousness guard your hearts and minds. Grace be with you all.
