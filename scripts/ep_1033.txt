PURITAN GOLD -- Melchizedek: A Complete Biblical Study, Part 3 of 8
Hebrews 5:1-14 — Christ Appointed High Priest


Welcome back to Puritan Gold and our complete biblical study of Melchizedek. We have now read the two Old Testament foundations: Genesis 14, where Melchizedek appeared in history, and Psalm 110, where God swore with an oath that the Messiah would be a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek. Now we turn to the New Testament, where the writer of Hebrews takes up these texts and constructs from them the most magnificent exposition of Christ's priesthood in all of Scripture.

We begin with Hebrews chapter 5 — the entire chapter. Here the apostolic writer lays the groundwork. He establishes the qualifications of a high priest, shows how Christ meets every one of them, and then introduces the Melchizedek theme that will dominate chapters 6 and 7. Let us read every verse.

Hebrews 5, verses 1 through 14:

"For every high priest taken from among men is ordained for men in things pertaining to God, that he may offer both gifts and sacrifices for sins: who can have compassion on the ignorant, and on them that are out of the way; for that he himself also is compassed with infirmity. And by reason hereof he ought, as for the people, so also for himself, to offer for sins. And no man taketh this honour unto himself, but he that is called of God, as was Aaron."

"So also Christ glorified not himself to be made an high priest; but he that said unto him, Thou art my Son, to day have I begotten thee. As he saith also in another place, Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchisedec. Who in the days of his flesh, when he had offered up prayers and supplications with strong crying and tears unto him that was able to save him from death, and was heard in that he feared; though he were a Son, yet learned he obedience by the things which he suffered; and being made perfect, he became the author of eternal salvation unto all them that obey him; called of God an high priest after the order of Melchisedec."

"Of whom we have many things to say, and hard to be uttered, seeing ye are dull of hearing. For when for the time ye ought to be teachers, ye have need that one teach you again which be the first principles of the oracles of God; and are become such as have need of milk, and not of strong meat. For every one that useth milk is unskilful in the word of righteousness: for he is a babe. But strong meat belongeth to them that are of full age, even those who by reason of use have their senses exercised to discern both good and evil."


Let us now work through this passage with the thoroughness it demands.

Verses 1 through 4 establish the qualifications of a high priest under the old covenant. The writer identifies four essential characteristics. First, the high priest is taken from among men. He is human. He represents humanity before God. Second, he is ordained for men in things pertaining to God — his ministry is Godward, bringing the needs of men into the presence of God. Third, he must be able to have compassion on the ignorant and those who are out of the way, because he himself is compassed with infirmity. The Levitical priest could sympathize with sinners because he was himself a sinner. Fourth, and crucially, no man taketh this honour unto himself, but he that is called of God, as was Aaron. The priestly office is not self-appointed. It is divinely bestowed. Aaron did not volunteer. God called him.

John Owen draws from these verses a foundational principle: every legitimate priesthood must be divinely appointed. Any priesthood that men create for themselves is an abomination. This is why Korah's rebellion was so severely punished — he presumed to take priestly authority without divine calling. And this is why, Owen argues, all human religious systems that appoint their own mediators between God and man are illegitimate. There is one Mediator, and He was appointed by God Himself.

Now, in verses 5 and 6, the writer applies these qualifications to Christ. "So also Christ glorified not himself to be made an high priest." Christ did not seize the priesthood. It was given to Him by the Father. And the writer quotes two Old Testament passages as proof. The first is from Psalm 2:7 — "Thou art my Son, to day have I begotten thee." This establishes Christ's divine appointment and sonship. The second is from Psalm 110:4 — "Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchisedec." This establishes Christ's priestly office.

Observe carefully: the writer links Psalm 2 and Psalm 110 together. The Son of God is the priest of God. His sonship is the foundation of His priesthood. Thomas Goodwin meditates at length on this connection. He writes that Christ's priesthood is not a mere office externally conferred but flows from the very relationship between Father and Son within the Godhead. The eternal Son, who shares the Father's nature, becomes in the incarnation the High Priest who shares our nature — and thus He stands perfectly between God and man, fully God and fully man, the one Mediator.

Verses 7 through 10 are among the most profound in the entire epistle: "Who in the days of his flesh, when he had offered up prayers and supplications with strong crying and tears unto him that was able to save him from death, and was heard in that he feared; though he were a Son, yet learned he obedience by the things which he suffered; and being made perfect, he became the author of eternal salvation unto all them that obey him; called of God an high priest after the order of Melchisedec."

This is the doctrine of Christ's perfecting through suffering. It does not mean that Christ was imperfect in a moral sense — He was sinless from the womb. Rather, it means He was perfected as a High Priest. His qualifications for the priestly office were completed through the actual experience of suffering. He did not merely know about human suffering theoretically; He endured it personally. The strong crying and tears refer to Gethsemane, where He sweat drops of blood and pleaded with the Father. He was heard in that He feared — not that He was delivered from death itself, but that He was sustained through it by the Father's strengthening grace.

John Owen devotes many pages to the phrase "learned he obedience by the things which he suffered." How can the eternal Son of God learn anything? Owen explains that Christ learned obedience not in the sense of acquiring knowledge He previously lacked but in the sense of experiential acquisition. There is a difference between knowing a thing and experiencing it. Christ, in His divine nature, knew all things. But in His human nature, He walked the path of obedience step by step, learning its cost and weight through personal endurance. This is what qualifies Him to sympathize with us. He does not merely understand our trials from a distance. He has walked through them Himself.

And being made perfect — having completed the full course of priestly qualification through suffering, death, and resurrection — He became the author of eternal salvation. Not temporary salvation. Not conditional salvation. Eternal salvation. The word author here means originator, source, cause. Christ is the fountainhead from which all saving grace flows to all them that obey Him.

And then the climactic declaration in verse 10: called of God an high priest after the order of Melchisedec. Here the Melchizedek theme returns with full force. Christ's priesthood is not of the Levitical order. It is of the Melchizedekian order — superior, eternal, established by oath.

Now comes a remarkable turn. In verses 11 through 14, the writer interrupts his exposition with a rebuke. He has much more to say about Melchizedek — many things, and hard to be uttered — but his readers are not ready. They are dull of hearing. They should be teachers by now, but they need to be taught again the first principles. They are babes who need milk, not mature believers who can handle strong meat.

Matthew Henry notes the seriousness of this rebuke. Spiritual immaturity is not merely unfortunate; it is dangerous. These Hebrew Christians were in danger of apostasy — of falling back into Judaism — precisely because they had never grown into a deep understanding of Christ's priesthood. If they had grasped the glory of the Melchizedekian priesthood, they would never have been tempted to return to the shadows of the Levitical system.

The Puritans applied this with great pastoral urgency. Richard Baxter warned that lazy hearers make themselves vulnerable to every wind of false doctrine. Stephen Charnock insisted that Christians must press beyond the elementary doctrines into the deep things of God. The doctrine of Christ's priesthood after the order of Melchizedek is not optional theology for advanced students. It is essential doctrine for every believer's assurance and perseverance.

The writer's frustration is palpable, and yet his love is evident. He will not abandon the teaching merely because his readers are immature. He will press on — after a solemn warning in chapter 6 — to give the fullest exposition of Melchizedek in all of Scripture. That exposition begins with the closing verses of chapter 6 and continues through the entirety of chapter 7.

Note well the phrase in verse 14: "But strong meat belongeth to them that are of full age, even those who by reason of use have their senses exercised to discern both good and evil." Spiritual maturity comes by reason of use — by practice, by exercise, by the habitual engagement with Scripture and doctrine. Owen writes that the faculties of the soul are like the muscles of the body: they grow strong only through regular, disciplined use. The Christian who feeds daily on the Word, who wrestles with difficult texts, who meditates on the deep doctrines of grace — this is the Christian who grows into full stature.

Beloved, Hebrews 5 has laid the foundation. Christ meets every qualification of a true High Priest. He is taken from among men. He is ordained for men in things pertaining to God. He can have compassion, for He has suffered. He did not appoint Himself but was called by God with the words of Psalm 2 and Psalm 110. And He has been perfected through suffering to become the author of eternal salvation.

In our next episode, we shall follow the writer into the closing verses of Hebrews 6 and the opening of Hebrews 7, where the full exposition of the order of Melchizedek begins in earnest. Prepare your hearts for strong meat. Amen.
