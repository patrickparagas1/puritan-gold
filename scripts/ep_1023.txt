PURITAN GOLD -- Study Topic: The Book of Enoch, Part 3 of 10
"The Fall of the Watchers (1 Enoch 6-16)"
The Book of Enoch — The sons of God and daughters of men

---

Dear friends in Christ, we continue our journey through the Book of Enoch, and today we come to one of its most dramatic and theologically significant sections — the detailed account of the fall of the Watchers in chapters 6 through 16. This is the heart of the Book of the Watchers, and it contains the narrative that most directly connects to the canonical Scriptures.

Let us read from Genesis 6:1-4:

"And it came to pass, when men began to multiply on the face of the earth, and daughters were born unto them, That the sons of God saw the daughters of men that they were fair; and they took them wives of all which they chose. And the LORD said, My spirit shall not always strive with man, for that he also is flesh: yet his days shall be an hundred and twenty years. There were giants in the earth in those days; and also after that, when the sons of God came in unto the daughters of men, and they bare children to them, the same became mighty men which were of old, men of renown."

--- Teaching ---

In our previous episode, we introduced the concept of the Watchers — the angelic beings who, according to the Enochic tradition, abandoned their heavenly post. Now we come to the full account of their descent and its terrible consequences. Chapters 6 through 16 of 1 Enoch are among the oldest portions of the entire work, with fragments dating to the third century before Christ found among the Dead Sea Scrolls. This means that this tradition about fallen angels was well established in Jewish thought centuries before the birth of our Lord.

The narrative begins with a pact. Two hundred Watchers, under the leadership of Semjaza, bind themselves by mutual oath upon Mount Hermon to descend to earth and take human wives. The text names twenty leaders of this rebellion, each of whom is said to have taught humanity forbidden arts. Azazel taught the making of weapons of war and the arts of cosmetic adornment. Amazarak taught sorcery and the cutting of roots. Armaros taught the resolving of enchantments. Baraqijal taught astrology. Kokabel taught the constellations. Ezeqeel taught the knowledge of the clouds. Araqiel taught the signs of the earth. Shamsiel taught the signs of the sun. Sariel taught the course of the moon.

Now, we must approach this with discernment. The specific names and the catalog of forbidden knowledge are literary and theological devices, not necessarily historical reportage. But the theological point is profound and deeply consonant with Scripture: the corruption of humanity was not merely a matter of human choice, but involved the intervention of malevolent spiritual beings who introduced forbidden knowledge and practices into the world. This is remarkably consistent with the biblical portrait of Satan as the tempter and deceiver who seeks to lead humanity astray.

The offspring of the Watchers and human women are described in 1 Enoch as giants — the Nephilim of Genesis 6:4. The Hebrew word nephilim is of uncertain etymology, but it is traditionally connected to the verb naphal, meaning "to fall," and thus the "fallen ones." In 1 Enoch, these giants are portrayed as creatures of immense appetite and violence who devoured the produce of the earth, then turned to consuming human beings, and finally one another. They are depicted as a monstrous corruption of the created order — neither fully angelic nor fully human, but an abomination that provoked the judgment of God.

This narrative serves a profound theological function. It explains the origin and pervasiveness of evil in the antediluvian world — the world that was so wicked that God determined to destroy it with a flood. Genesis 6:5 tells us that "GOD saw that the wickedness of man was great in the earth, and that every imagination of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually." The Enochic tradition provides a dramatic backstory for this universal corruption: evil was not merely the fruit of human sin, but was compounded by the interference of fallen spiritual beings.

The cry of the earth reaches heaven. In 1 Enoch, the archangels Michael, Uriel, Raphael, and Gabriel look down upon the bloodshed and bring the matter before God. Here we see a theme that resounds throughout Scripture — the cry of the oppressed ascending to heaven. Abel's blood cried from the ground (Genesis 4:10). The cry of Sodom and Gomorrah was great before the Lord (Genesis 18:20). The children of Israel cried out from their bondage in Egypt, "and God heard their groaning" (Exodus 2:24). God is not indifferent to the suffering of His creatures.

God responds with judgment. He sends the angel Uriel to warn Noah of the coming flood. He commands Raphael to bind Azazel hand and foot, to cast him into darkness, and to cover him with rough and jagged rocks until the day of judgment. He commands Gabriel to destroy the giants by turning them against one another. And He commands Michael to bind Semjaza and his associates for seventy generations in the valleys of the earth until the day of their final judgment.

The parallels with the New Testament are striking. Peter writes that God "spared not the angels that sinned, but cast them down to hell, and delivered them into chains of darkness, to be reserved unto judgment" (2 Peter 2:4). The word Peter uses for "cast down to hell" is tartaroo — a term drawn not from the Old Testament but from Greek mythology, where Tartarus was the deepest abyss beneath Hades. Yet Peter's theology is not Greek but Hebraic, and the closest parallel to his description is found in the Enochic account of the binding and imprisonment of the fallen Watchers.

Jude likewise describes angels "reserved in everlasting chains under darkness unto the judgment of the great day" (Jude 6), and he places this reference immediately alongside the judgment of Sodom and Gomorrah, which he says suffered "in like manner" — that is, giving themselves over to unnatural desire. The connection between angelic transgression and sexual sin is explicit in both Jude and 1 Enoch.

Chapters 12 through 16 present Enoch as an intercessor. The fallen Watchers, unable to approach God themselves, ask Enoch to plead their case before the Almighty. Enoch ascends to heaven and presents their petition, but God's response is a devastating rebuke. The Watchers, who were created as spiritual beings dwelling in heaven, had no need to take wives or produce offspring — that was the lot of mortal men. They have transgressed their boundary and must bear the consequences. There is no pardon for them.

This refusal of pardon for the fallen angels stands in stark contrast with the gospel of grace offered to fallen humanity. The epistle to the Hebrews makes this very point: "For verily he took not on him the nature of angels; but he took on him the seed of Abraham" (Hebrews 2:16). Christ became man, not an angel, in order to redeem. The fallen Watchers, having sinned against greater light and from a higher station, are left without remedy. This should fill us with wonder at the grace of God toward sinful men and women — grace that provided a Savior where none was owed.

Thomas Goodwin meditated deeply on this theme. In his treatise on election, he marveled that God passed over the fallen angels and chose to redeem fallen humanity. It was not because humans were more worthy, but because it pleased God in His sovereign mercy to set His love upon the children of Adam. The Enochic tradition, though extra-canonical, powerfully illustrates the same truth.

--- Application ---

1. Understand that sin always exceeds its intended boundaries. The Watchers intended only to take wives, but the result was the corruption of the entire earth. So it is with every sin — it always brings consequences far beyond what we imagined.

2. Marvel at the grace of God toward humanity. The fallen angels received no offer of redemption, yet God sent His Son to die for sinful men and women. Let this truth fill you with gratitude and humble worship.

3. Be warned that forbidden knowledge is not always beneficial knowledge. The arts taught by the Watchers — warfare, sorcery, vanity — brought destruction, not blessing. Not every advance in knowledge is a gift from God; some knowledge leads only to ruin.

4. Trust that God hears the cry of the oppressed. Just as the cry of the earth reached heaven in Enoch's account, so your prayers ascend before the throne of grace. God is not deaf to the suffering of His people.

--- Closing ---

O Lord, who alone art worthy of all worship and praise, we stand in awe of Thy justice toward the fallen angels and Thy mercy toward fallen humanity. We do not deserve Thy grace, yet Thou hast freely given it in Christ Jesus. Help us never to take lightly the gift of redemption, purchased at so great a cost. In the name of our Savior we pray. Amen.

May the peace of Christ guard your hearts and minds, beloved, until we gather again for our next study.
