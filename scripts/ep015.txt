PURITAN GOLD — Episode 15
"The Fear of the Lord is the Beginning of Wisdom"
Week 3, Day 1 (Monday) — Proverbs 9

---

"The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom: and the knowledge of the holy is understanding." Proverbs 9:10.

Let that verse settle over you for a moment. Don't rush past it. Let it land.

The fear of the Lord... is the beginning of wisdom.

Not the middle. Not the advanced course. The beginning. The very starting place. The foundation stone upon which everything else — every insight, every moral choice, every act of love, every moment of clarity — is built.

This is Puritan Gold, and today we open the ninth chapter of Proverbs together. And friend, what a chapter it is. Because Proverbs 9 doesn't just teach us about wisdom in the abstract. It sets before us two feasts, two invitations, two women calling out from the high places of the city. And it asks us, with unmistakable urgency... which table will you sit down at?

But before we get to those two feasts, I want us to linger on this phrase — the fear of the Lord. Because I think we often misunderstand it. We either soften it into something that barely means anything... or we harden it into something cold and terrifying that drives us away from God rather than toward Him.

The Puritans understood this tension beautifully. They lived in it. They breathed it.

Thomas Watson once wrote that the fear of God is "a holy affection whereby the child of God, seeing the grandeur and holiness of the Almighty, is filled with awe and a desire to glorify Him." Notice that — it's not the terror of a slave cowering before a cruel master. It's the awe of a child who knows his Father is both infinitely powerful and infinitely good.

John Bunyan put it this way in his work on the fear of God — he said there are two kinds of fear. There's a slavish fear, which drives you from God. And there's a filial fear — a child-like fear — which draws you toward God. Slavish fear says, "God will destroy me." Filial fear says, "God is so holy, so beautiful, so overwhelmingly good... that I tremble to offend Him. Not because He'll crush me, but because I love Him."

Do you see the difference? One fear sends you running into the darkness. The other fear sends you running into His arms.

And that's what Proverbs 9:10 is talking about. This holy, reverent, wonder-filled fear of the Lord... is where wisdom begins. You can read every book in the library. You can earn every degree the university offers. You can travel the world and sit at the feet of the most brilliant minds of every generation. But if you don't begin here — with a trembling, grateful, awe-struck reverence for the living God — you haven't even started.

You're still standing outside the door.

Now... let's look at these two feasts. Because Solomon, under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit, paints one of the most vivid pictures in all of Scripture.

Look at the first six verses of Proverbs 9:

"Wisdom hath builded her house, she hath hewn out her seven pillars: She hath killed her beasts; she hath mingled her wine; she hath also furnished her table. She hath sent forth her maidens: she crieth upon the highest places of the city, Whoso is simple, let him turn in hither: as for him that wanteth understanding, she saith to him, Come, eat of my bread, and drink of the wine which I have mingled. Forsake the foolish, and live; and go in the way of understanding."

What a feast. Seven pillars — a house of completeness, of perfection. The meat is prepared. The wine is mixed. The table is set. And wisdom doesn't just sit there waiting for people to find her. She sends out invitations. She calls from the highest places. She's not hiding. She's not exclusive. She says, "Whoever is simple — whoever knows they need help — come. Come and eat. Come and live."

There's such grace in that, isn't there? Wisdom doesn't say, "Come to me, you who have it all together." She says, "Come to me, you who are simple. You who lack understanding. You who've been wandering."

It reminds me of our Lord's own words: "Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest."

The feast of wisdom is a feast of life. Of nourishment. Of substance.

But now... look at the other feast. Verses 13 through 18:

"A foolish woman is clamorous; she is simple, and knoweth nothing. For she sitteth at the door of her house, on a seat in the high places of the city, To call passengers who go right on their ways: Whoso is simple, let him turn in hither: and as for him that wanteth understanding, she saith to him, Stolen waters are sweet, and bread eaten in secret is pleasant. But he knoweth not that the dead are there; and that her guests are in the depths of hell."

Do you notice something chilling? Folly uses almost the same words as wisdom. She also calls out to the simple. She also sits in the high places. She also offers a meal. But her table... her table leads to the grave. Her guests are in the depths of Sheol, and they don't even know it.

This is how sin works, friend. It mimics. It imitates. It dresses itself up to look like the real thing. The devil, as the old Puritans loved to remind us, is not a creative genius — he's a counterfeiter. He doesn't invent anything good. He takes what God has made and twists it. He takes the feast of wisdom and sets up a cheap imitation down the street, with brighter lights and louder music... and a trapdoor in the floor.

"Stolen waters are sweet," folly says. And you know what? In the moment... they are. Sin does taste sweet. Temptation wouldn't be temptation if it didn't. The forbidden fruit looked good to Eve. It was pleasant to the eyes. It was desirable to make one wise. Nobody is ever tempted by something that looks obviously disgusting and deadly from the outside.

That's the deception. That's the trap.

John Owen, that great Puritan pastor and theologian, understood this deeply. He wrote extensively about how sin presents itself to us with promises of pleasure, of satisfaction, of fulfillment — and then delivers nothing but death. Owen would say that we must learn to see through the disguise. We must learn to look past the sweetness of the stolen water and see the dead who lie beneath folly's floor.

And how do we do that? How do we develop the eyes to see through the counterfeit?

We come back to verse 10. The fear of the Lord.

Because when your heart is filled with a genuine, reverent, loving fear of God... the counterfeits lose their power. When you've tasted the feast of wisdom — when you've sat at the Lord's table and eaten the bread of life and drunk the wine of His grace — the stolen waters of folly taste... thin. Hollow. Empty.

It's like someone who's eaten a magnificent meal at the finest table in the land. You don't go rummaging through garbage cans afterward. Not because you're forcing yourself not to — but because you've been satisfied. You've been filled.

This is what the Puritans meant when they talked about the "expulsive power of a new affection." Thomas Chalmers preached a famous sermon on this idea — that the way to drive out the love of sin is not by sheer willpower, not by gritting your teeth and saying "I won't, I won't, I won't." It's by being captivated by a greater love. A higher feast. A deeper satisfaction.

The fear of the Lord gives you that. It reorients your entire being. It recalibrates your desires. When you stand in awe of who God is — His holiness, His mercy, His justice, His tenderness, His sovereignty — the glittering trinkets of the world grow strangely dim.

Now, what does this look like practically? Because I don't want us to leave this in the realm of abstract theology. The Puritans were never content with mere head knowledge. They wanted heart knowledge — knowledge that works its way down into the hands and the feet and the daily choices of ordinary life.

So what does godly fear look like on a Monday morning?

It looks like pausing before you speak — because you know that God hears every word.

It looks like honesty in your business dealings — even when dishonesty would be more profitable — because you reverence the One who sees all things.

It looks like gentleness with your spouse and your children — because you know that the God who entrusted them to you is watching how you steward that trust.

It looks like turning away from that website, that conversation, that relationship that you know is leading you toward folly's house — not because you're terrified of punishment, but because you love the One who calls you to wisdom's feast.

It looks like getting on your knees in the morning and saying, "Lord, I am small, and You are great. I am foolish, and You are wise. Lead me today. Keep me today. Let me walk in Your ways."

Richard Baxter, in his Christian Directory, gave this counsel: let the fear of God be the first thing that meets you when you open your eyes in the morning, and the last thing that accompanies you when you close them at night. Let it be the lens through which you see everything — your work, your relationships, your pleasures, your sufferings. When the fear of God is the atmosphere in which you live... wisdom follows naturally.

And notice — verse 10 says the fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom, and the knowledge of the Holy One is understanding. Knowledge of the Holy One. This is personal. This is relational. This isn't about knowing theological propositions, though those matter. It's about knowing a Person. The Holy One. The living God.

The Puritans often spoke of "experimental religion" — by which they meant experiential faith. Not just knowing about God, but knowing God. Tasting and seeing that the Lord is good. Having a living, breathing, daily communion with the Almighty.

John Owen said that all true knowledge of God is transformative. If your knowledge of God hasn't changed you — if it hasn't made you more humble, more tender, more courageous, more joyful — then you don't yet truly know Him. You know facts about Him, perhaps. But you don't know Him.

And knowing Him... that's where understanding comes from. That's the deep well from which wisdom draws.

So here we are, friend. Two feasts are set before you. Every single day, you face this choice. Wisdom calls — come, eat of my bread, drink of my wine, forsake the foolish and live. And folly calls — stolen waters are sweet, bread eaten in secret is pleasant.

Which table will you sit at today?

I'll tell you this — if you sit at wisdom's table, you'll find that the food satisfies. It nourishes. It strengthens you for the road ahead. And the longer you sit there, the more you realize that the Host of that feast is Christ Himself — the Wisdom of God, the Bread of Life, the One who says, "I am the way, the truth, and the life."

If you sit at folly's table... well, you already know what's waiting there. You may have sat there before. We all have. And we know the emptiness that follows. The shame. The hollowness.

But here's the good news — wisdom is still calling. Right now. Today. Even if you've been at folly's table for years, wisdom has not stopped sending invitations. "Whoso is simple, let him turn in hither."

Turn. That's all. Just turn. Leave the one table and come to the other.

The fear of the Lord is the beginning. And it's never too late to begin.

Let me pray for us.

Gracious Father, we stand in awe of You. Not the awe of terror, but the awe of love. You are holy beyond our comprehension, and yet You invite us — sinners, wanderers, the simple and the foolish — to Your table. Teach us the fear of the Lord. Not a fear that drives us from You, but a fear that draws us ever closer. Give us wisdom, Lord. Give us eyes to see through the counterfeits, and hearts that are satisfied with the feast You have prepared. We choose Your table today. In the name of Jesus Christ, our Wisdom and our Life. Amen.
