PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 22
"O That Thou Wert as My Brother"
Song of Solomon 8:1-4 — The Longing for Open Affection


Welcome, dear couple. Tonight we enter the final chapter of the Song of Solomon, where the love story reaches its climax. The bride begins with a surprising wish — a longing for the freedom to express her love openly and without restraint.

Song of Solomon 8:1-4:

"O that thou wert as my brother, that sucked the breasts of my mother! when I should find thee without, I would kiss thee; yea, I should not be despised. I would lead thee, and bring thee into my mother's house, who would instruct me: I would cause thee to drink of spiced wine of the juice of my pomegranate. His left hand should be under my head, and his right hand should embrace me. I charge you, O daughters of Jerusalem, that ye stir not up, nor awake my love, until he please."


"O that thou wert as my brother!" This is a surprising opening. Why would the bride wish her beloved were her brother? In the ancient Near East, public displays of affection between husband and wife were considered improper, but siblings could embrace publicly without social censure. The bride longs for the freedom to show her love openly — to kiss her beloved in public without being "despised" by onlookers.

The Puritans found in this passage a meditation on the relationship between private and public love. William Gouge acknowledged that while modesty is a virtue, married couples should not be ashamed of showing appropriate affection in the presence of others. He wrote that a couple who never demonstrates any warmth before their family and community sends a message that something is lacking. While the most intimate expressions of love belong to the enclosed garden, there should be visible evidence of affection — a hand held, a kind word spoken in company, a look exchanged across a room that says, "You are mine."

Thomas Gataker went further. He wrote that children who grow up in homes where their parents show affection to each other carry into adulthood a deep sense of security. Conversely, children who never see their parents touch, laugh together, or speak tenderly to each other grow up with a distorted view of love. Your public affection — modest, appropriate, but real — is a gift to the next generation.

"I would lead thee, and bring thee into my mother's house." The bride wants to bring her beloved into the most intimate space she knows — her mother's house, the place of her upbringing, the home of her identity. Richard Sibbes interpreted this as the desire to integrate all dimensions of life. The bride does not want a compartmentalized love — private here, public there, hidden here, revealed there. She wants a love so full, so confident, so unashamed that it occupies every room, every space, every relationship.

"I would cause thee to drink of spiced wine of the juice of my pomegranate." She wants to serve him, to delight him, to offer him the very best she has. Spiced wine and pomegranate juice were luxuries — not everyday fare but special preparations. The Puritans valued the practice of intentional hospitality within marriage. Baxter wrote that husbands and wives should treat each other as honored guests — not with formal distance but with the kind of generous, attentive care that says, "You are worth my best effort."

How often do we give our best to strangers and our leftovers to the people closest to us? We dress up for work and wear pajamas at home. We use our most patient voice with colleagues and our most irritable voice with our spouse. We prepare elaborate meals for company and heat leftovers for family. The bride reverses this. She saves the spiced wine for her beloved. Her best is reserved for him.

"His left hand should be under my head, and his right hand should embrace me." This tender image of physical closeness appears for the third time in the Song. It is the posture of rest, comfort, and security — one arm cradling, the other embracing. The repetition tells us that this is not a one-time moment but a pattern, a way of life. The embrace is not occasional but habitual. The comfort is not extraordinary but ordinary. The Puritans taught that the best marriages are built not on grand gestures but on daily patterns of tenderness.

"I charge you, O daughters of Jerusalem, that ye stir not up, nor awake my love, until he please." The solemn charge appears for the third and final time. It bookends the Song's three major movements: the beginning of love, the crisis of separation, and the restoration of intimacy. Each time, the message is the same: love has its own rhythm. Do not force it. Do not rush it. Trust its timing.

Tonight, consider the longing of the bride: a love so free, so full, so unashamed that it fills every space — public and private, family and community, the mother's house and the garden. Is your love expanding or contracting? Are you giving your best to your beloved or saving it for others?


Turn to each other and consider these questions.

Question 1: Do you show appropriate affection in public — in front of your children, your friends, your community? Or has modesty become a cover for emotional distance?

Question 2: Does your spouse get the "spiced wine" — your best energy, your finest attention, your most generous spirit — or do they get the leftovers after everyone else has been served?

Question 3: What would it look like for your love to be more integrated — filling every room, every relationship, every dimension of your shared life?


Tonight's prayer focus: Pray for a love that is unashamed, uncontained, and generous. Ask God to break down any walls of false modesty, emotional reserve, or misplaced propriety that keep your love confined. Pray that your affection for each other would bless not only yourselves but everyone who witnesses it.


May your love overflow its banks tonight — into every room, every relationship, every corner of your life together. You were made for a love this big. Goodnight, dear couple.
