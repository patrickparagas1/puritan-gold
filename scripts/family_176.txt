PURITAN GOLD -- Family Devotional, Day 15
"The Commentator's Household"
Matthew Henry — Family Worship and Bible Study


Good evening, dear family. Welcome to our third week of Puritan Gold family devotions for May. This week our guide is Matthew Henry — perhaps the most beloved Bible commentator in the English language. His six-volume commentary on the entire Bible has fed the souls of pastors, teachers, and families for over three hundred years. But tonight we discover that Henry's passion for Scripture began not in a study but in a home. Let us draw near to God together.

Let us pray. Lord God, we thank You for the gift of Your Word. We thank You for men like Matthew Henry who devoted their lives to opening its treasures for others. As we study his example this week, kindle in our hearts a fresh love for Scripture and a fresh commitment to studying it together as a family. In Christ's name, Amen.

Our Scripture reading this evening comes from Psalm 119:97-104. Let us hear the Word of the Lord:

"O how love I thy law! it is my meditation all the day. Thou through thy commandments hast made me wiser than mine enemies: for they are ever with me. I have more understanding than all my teachers: for thy testimonies are my meditation. I understand more than the ancients, because I keep thy precepts. I have refrained my feet from every evil way, that I might keep thy word. I have not departed from thy judgments: for thou hast taught me. How sweet are thy words unto my taste! yea, sweeter than honey to my mouth! Through thy precepts I get understanding: therefore I hate every false way."

That is Psalm 119:97-104. Let those words settle in your heart for a moment before we continue.


Matthew Henry was born in 1662, the son of the ejected Puritan minister Philip Henry. When the Act of Uniformity expelled over two thousand Puritan pastors from the Church of England in 1662, Philip Henry was among them. He lost his pulpit, his income, and his standing in the established church. But he did not lose his faith, and he did not lose his family. Instead, Philip Henry turned his home into a school of godliness, and it was in that home — gathered around the family table with an open Bible — that young Matthew learned to love the Scriptures with a passion that would shape the rest of his life.

Matthew Henry later wrote that the most formative influence in his spiritual life was his father's practice of family worship. Every morning and every evening, the Henry family gathered together. Philip Henry would read a portion of Scripture, explain it in simple terms, apply it to the circumstances of the family, and then lead them in prayer. This was not a dry, formal exercise. It was the living heartbeat of the household — the daily rhythm around which everything else revolved.

Henry carried this practice into his own home and his own ministry. When he became pastor of a Presbyterian congregation in Chester, he not only preached the Word from the pulpit but urged every household in his congregation to practice family worship. He wrote a treatise called "A Church in the House" in which he laid out his vision for the Christian family as a worshiping community. His argument was simple and powerful: if the church is the household of God, then the household should be a church.

Henry identified several essential elements of family Bible study. The first is regularity. He urged families to read Scripture together every day, not just when it was convenient. The habit of daily Bible reading, he wrote, is like the habit of daily bread — if you neglect it, the soul grows weak and sickly. He recommended reading through the Bible systematically, so that the family would hear the full sweep of God's revelation over time.

The second element is simplicity. Henry was famous for his ability to explain the deepest truths of Scripture in simple, vivid, memorable language. He urged family leaders to do the same. When reading the Bible at home, do not aim for scholarly sophistication. Aim for clarity. Ask: What does this passage say? What does it mean? How does it apply to our lives? These three questions, faithfully pursued, will unlock the treasures of any passage.

The third element is application. Henry believed that the Bible was never meant to be studied as an academic exercise. It was given to change lives. Every passage has a practical application — something to believe, something to do, something to avoid, something to hope for. Henry urged families to ask themselves after every reading: what difference will this passage make in our lives today?

The fourth element is prayer. Bible reading and prayer belong together, Henry insisted. Reading without prayer is like planting without watering — the seed may be sown, but it will not grow. After reading Scripture together, the family should pray together — thanking God for what they have learned, asking Him to apply it to their hearts, and seeking His help to obey what they have heard.

Matthew Henry's own commentary is the fruit of a lifetime spent in this kind of intimate, devotional engagement with Scripture. It is not a commentary of cold analysis but of warm application. Page after page, Henry draws out the spiritual nourishment of each passage and serves it up in language that feeds the soul. Spurgeon said that every minister should read Matthew Henry's commentary through at least once. But Henry would have said that every family should read the Bible through together — not once, but continually, as the daily bread of the household.

Let this word challenge us tonight. Do we read the Bible together as a family? Do we do so regularly, simply, with application, and with prayer? If we do, we are walking in the footsteps of one of the greatest Bible lovers the church has ever known. If we do not, let us begin — and let us begin tonight.


Let us pause and talk as a family. Here are tonight's discussion questions. Everyone gets a turn to share.

Question 1: Matthew Henry learned to love the Bible through family worship in his father's home. What practices in our home have helped us love Scripture?

Question 2: Henry says the three key questions for any Bible passage are: What does it say? What does it mean? How does it apply? Can we practice these three questions with tonight's Scripture reading from Psalm 119?

Question 3: Henry says reading without prayer is like planting without watering. How does prayer help us receive and apply what we read in the Bible?

Take a few minutes to talk about these questions together. Let everyone share, and listen to one another with patience and love.


Now, here is our memory verse for today. Let us say it together:

"O how love I thy law! it is my meditation all the day." -- Psalm 119:97

Try to say it out loud three times right now. Then, before bed tonight, see if you can say it from memory.


Let us pray together. Lord God, give us a love for Your Word that burns in our hearts all day long. Help us to read it faithfully, to understand it clearly, to apply it practically, and to pray over it earnestly. Make our home a house where the Bible is opened daily and where its truths shape everything we say and do. In Jesus' name, Amen.

Sleep well tonight, knowing that God watches over your family.
