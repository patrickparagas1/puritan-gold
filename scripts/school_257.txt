PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 17
"You Shall Not Kill"
Christian Ethics & Moral Philosophy — Lesson 17

---

Welcome back, students, to our continuing study of the Ten Commandments in the Puritan Gold series. Today we take up one of the most solemn and searching commandments in all of Scripture — the sixth commandment: "Thou shalt not kill" (Exodus 20:13). These three words, the shortest utterance in the entire Decalogue, contain within them an ocean of moral instruction. The Puritans found in this commandment not merely a prohibition against the physical act of murder, but a comprehensive ethic of life that addresses the heart, the tongue, the hands, and every dimension of our responsibility toward persons made in the image of God. This is a lesson that will challenge you to think deeply about what it means to value human life as God values it.

--- Lesson ---

PART ONE: THE FOUNDATION — THE IMAGE OF GOD IN MAN

To understand the sixth commandment, we must begin where the Bible begins — with the creation of man in the image of God. In the opening chapter of Genesis, we read: "And God said, Let us make man in our image, after our likeness: and let them have dominion over the fish of the sea, and over the fowl of the air, and over the cattle, and over all the earth, and over every creeping thing that creepeth upon the earth. So God created man in his own image, in the image of God created he him; male and female created he them" (Genesis 1:26-27). This passage is the theological bedrock upon which the entire sixth commandment rests. Every human being — regardless of age, race, nationality, physical ability, mental capacity, social standing, or any other distinguishing characteristic — bears the image of the living God. This is the source of human dignity. This is why human life is sacred. And this is why the destruction of human life is an offense not merely against the person killed but against the God whose image that person bears.

After the great Flood, God established the principle of capital punishment for murder, and He grounded that principle explicitly in the doctrine of the image of God: "Whoso sheddeth man's blood, by man shall his blood be shed: for in the image of God made he man" (Genesis 9:6). The severity of the penalty reveals the gravity of the offense. To murder a human being is to assault the very image of God — to destroy a living representation of the Creator Himself. No other creature on earth bears this image. No animal, however beautiful or remarkable, possesses the unique dignity that belongs to human beings by virtue of their creation in God's likeness.

Thomas Watson, writing in his exposition of the Ten Commandments published in 1692, began his treatment of the sixth commandment with this foundational truth. Watson observed that the image of God in man includes the capacity for reason, the capacity for moral judgment, the capacity for relationship with God, and the capacity for eternal existence. When a human being is murdered, all of these capacities are violently extinguished. The murderer does not merely end a biological process — he destroys a being who was made to know God, to love God, to serve God, and to enjoy God forever. This is what makes murder the uniquely horrifying crime that it is.

The Puritans were careful to distinguish the image of God, which belongs to all human beings by creation, from the likeness of God in holiness, which was lost in the Fall and is restored only through regeneration. Even fallen, sinful human beings retain the image of God — they remain rational, moral, relational creatures with eternal souls. This is why murder is always and everywhere wrong, regardless of the character or condition of the victim. The worst sinner on earth still bears the image of God, and his life is therefore sacred.

PART TWO: THE BREADTH OF THE COMMANDMENT — THE WESTMINSTER EXPOSITION

The Westminster Larger Catechism, completed in 1648 by the Assembly of Divines meeting at Westminster Abbey in London, provides what is arguably the most comprehensive exposition of the sixth commandment ever written in the Reformed tradition. Questions 135 and 136 address the duties required and the sins forbidden by this commandment, and the breadth of their treatment is truly remarkable.

The sins forbidden by the sixth commandment, according to the Westminster divines, include: "the taking away the life of ourselves, or of others, except in case of public justice, lawful war, or necessary defence; the neglecting or withdrawing the lawful or necessary means of preservation of life; sinful anger, hatred, envy, desire of revenge; all excessive passions, distracting cares; immoderate use of meat, drink, labour, and recreations; provoking words, oppression, quarreling, striking, wounding, and whatsoever else tends to the destruction of the life of any."

Notice the extraordinary range of this list. It begins with the obvious — murder and suicide — but extends far beyond to encompass hatred, envy, anger, revenge, oppressive behavior, quarreling, reckless conduct, excessive indulgence, neglect of health, and even "distracting cares" — what we might call excessive worry and anxiety. The Puritans understood that any attitude, behavior, or habit that tends toward the destruction or diminishment of human life — our own or others' — falls within the scope of the sixth commandment.

The duties required by the sixth commandment are equally expansive. The Westminster Larger Catechism states that we are required to exercise "all careful studies, and lawful endeavours, to preserve the life of ourselves and others by resisting all thoughts and purposes, subduing all passions, and avoiding all occasions, temptations, and practices, which tend to the unjust taking away the life of any; by just defence thereof against violence, patient bearing of the hand of God, quietness of mind, cheerfulness of spirit; a sober use of meat, drink, physic, sleep, labour, and recreations; by charitable thoughts, love, compassion, meekness, gentleness, kindness; peaceable, mild, and courteous speeches and behaviour; forbearance, readiness to be reconciled, patient bearing and forgiving of injuries, and requiting good for evil; comforting and succouring the distressed, and protecting and defending the innocent."

This is a breathtaking vision of what the sixth commandment requires. It calls us not merely to refrain from killing but to actively preserve, protect, and promote human life in every way we can. It addresses not only our physical conduct but our emotional state, our mental habits, our speech, our attitudes, and even our diet and recreation. The sixth commandment, properly understood, is a comprehensive charter for the sanctity of life.

PART THREE: JESUS AND THE SIXTH COMMANDMENT — THE SERMON ON THE MOUNT

Our Lord Jesus Christ, in His great Sermon on the Mount, delivered on a hillside near the Sea of Galilee early in His public ministry around AD 28, took the sixth commandment and drove it straight into the deepest chambers of the human heart. "Ye have heard that it was said by them of old time, Thou shalt not kill; and whosoever shall kill shall be in danger of the judgment: But I say unto you, That whosoever is angry with his brother without a cause shall be in danger of the judgment: and whosoever shall say to his brother, Raca, shall be in danger of the council: but whosoever shall say, Thou fool, shall be in danger of hell fire" (Matthew 5:21-22).

Jesus was not contradicting the Old Testament law — He was revealing its full depth and intention. The scribes and Pharisees had reduced the sixth commandment to a mere prohibition against the physical act of murder. As long as a man did not actually kill someone, he considered himself innocent. But Jesus declared that the commandment reaches far beyond the hand to the heart. Anger, contempt, and verbal abuse are violations of the sixth commandment because they are the seeds from which murder grows. Hatred is murder in embryo. Every act of violence begins with an angry thought, a resentful feeling, a contemptuous word.

The word "Raca" that Jesus used is an Aramaic term of contempt, meaning something like "empty-headed" or "worthless." It was a term used to dismiss another person as insignificant, as beneath notice, as undeserving of respect. Jesus warned that even this seemingly minor expression of contempt places a person in danger of divine judgment. And to call someone "Thou fool" — to pronounce a verdict of moral and intellectual worthlessness upon another person — places one in danger of "hell fire," the most severe penalty Jesus ever mentioned.

The Puritan commentators drew profound implications from this teaching. John Owen, the great Puritan theologian who served as dean of Christ Church, Oxford, under Oliver Cromwell and who published his monumental works on the Holy Spirit and the mortification of sin, wrote that Christ's teaching in the Sermon on the Mount reveals that "sin is not measured by outward actions alone, but by the inward motions of the soul." Owen argued that a man who has never committed murder but who harbors murderous anger in his heart is guilty before God of violating the sixth commandment. The standard is not merely behavioral — it is dispositional. God requires not just clean hands but a clean heart.

The apostle John reinforced this teaching with stark clarity in his first epistle, written near the end of the first century: "Whosoever hateth his brother is a murderer: and ye know that no murderer hath eternal life abiding in him" (1 John 3:15). Hatred, John declares, is the moral equivalent of murder. The man who hates has already committed the essential sin of the sixth commandment, even if circumstances, cowardice, or prudence prevent him from carrying out the physical act. The difference between the hater and the murderer is a difference of degree, not of kind.

PART FOUR: THOMAS WATSON ON THE THREE LEVELS OF PRESERVING LIFE

Thomas Watson, in his characteristically organized fashion, taught that the sixth commandment requires us to preserve life at three distinct levels, and each level demands careful examination.

The first level is the preservation of our own lives. Watson argued that we are stewards, not owners, of the bodies God has given us. Our lives belong to God, and we have no right to destroy what He has made. The sin of suicide, Watson taught, is a grievous violation of the sixth commandment — a usurpation of God's prerogative as the Lord of life and death. Watson acknowledged the reality of despair, sorrow, and mental anguish, but he insisted that no degree of suffering justifies the taking of one's own life. The Christian who is tempted to despair must remember that God is sovereign over every circumstance, that His grace is sufficient for every trial, and that no suffering in this world is beyond the reach of His redeeming purpose.

Beyond the prohibition of suicide, the duty to preserve our own lives includes the positive obligation to care for our bodies through proper nutrition, adequate rest, appropriate exercise, the avoidance of harmful substances, and the moderation of our passions. Watson listed "immoderate grief" and "excessive worry" as sins against the sixth commandment because they damage the health that God has entrusted to our stewardship. Paul reminded the Corinthians: "What? know ye not that your body is the temple of the Holy Ghost which is in you, which ye have of God, and ye are not your own? For ye are bought with a price: therefore glorify God in your body, and in your spirit, which are God's" (1 Corinthians 6:19-20).

The second level is the preservation of the physical lives of others. The sixth commandment requires us to protect, defend, and provide for the bodily welfare of our fellow human beings. The parable of the Good Samaritan, told by Jesus in Luke 10:30-37, is the classic illustration of this duty. A certain man traveling from Jerusalem to Jericho fell among thieves, who stripped him, wounded him, and left him half dead by the roadside. A priest came by and saw the wounded man — but passed by on the other side. A Levite came by and saw him — and also passed by on the other side. Finally, a Samaritan — a member of a despised ethnic group — came by, and "when he saw him, he had compassion on him, And went to him, and bound up his wounds, pouring in oil and wine, and set him on his own beast, and brought him to an inn, and took care of him" (Luke 10:33-34).

Jesus asked: "Which now of these three, thinkest thou, was neighbour unto him that fell among the thieves?" The answer was obvious: "He that shewed mercy on him." And Jesus said: "Go, and do thou likewise" (Luke 10:36-37).

The Puritans drew a powerful lesson from this parable. The priest and the Levite did not attack the wounded man. They did not participate in his beating. They committed no act of violence against him. But their sin was no less real: they failed to act when they had the ability to help. The sixth commandment can be violated by omission as well as by commission — by failing to act when action is needed as well as by acting wrongly. When we see a person in need and have the ability to help but choose instead to pass by on the other side, we have violated the spirit of the sixth commandment.

William Ames, the influential Puritan theologian who lived from 1576 to 1633 and whose work "The Marrow of Theology" became a standard textbook at Harvard College, elaborated on this principle at length. Ames argued that the sixth commandment imposes a duty of active benevolence toward all persons. We are not merely prohibited from harming others; we are positively required to seek their good. This includes feeding the hungry, clothing the naked, sheltering the homeless, visiting the sick and imprisoned, defending the oppressed, and speaking up for those who cannot speak for themselves. Proverbs 31:8-9 commands: "Open thy mouth for the dumb in the cause of all such as are appointed to destruction. Open thy mouth, judge righteously, and plead the cause of the poor and needy."

The third level Watson identified is the preservation of the spiritual lives of others. This, Watson argued, is the highest and most important application of the sixth commandment. The body will eventually die regardless of how well we care for it, but the soul is eternal. The greatest act of life-preservation we can perform is to bring the gospel to those who are perishing in their sins. When we share the good news of salvation in Jesus Christ, when we pray for the conversion of the lost, when we teach the truths of Scripture to the ignorant, and when we warn the wicked of the judgment to come, we are fulfilling the sixth commandment in its deepest and most glorious sense.

Watson cited the words of the prophet Ezekiel: "When I say unto the wicked, Thou shalt surely die; and thou givest him not warning, nor speakest to warn the wicked from his wicked way, to save his life; the same wicked man shall die in his iniquity; but his blood will I require at thine hand. Yet if thou warn the wicked, and he turn not from his wickedness, nor from his wicked way, he shall die in his iniquity; but thou hast delivered thy soul" (Ezekiel 3:18-19). To know the way of salvation and to withhold it from those who are perishing is, in Watson's view, a form of spiritual murder — a failure to preserve the most precious kind of life, the life of the immortal soul.

PART FIVE: THE SIXTH COMMANDMENT AND THE TONGUE

The Puritans recognized that one of the most common and most overlooked ways of violating the sixth commandment is through our words. James, the brother of our Lord, wrote with characteristic bluntness about the destructive power of the tongue: "And the tongue is a fire, a world of iniquity: so is the tongue among our members, that it defileth the whole body, and setteth on fire the course of nature; and it is set on fire of hell" (James 3:6). The tongue can wound more deeply than a sword, destroy more thoroughly than a fire, and kill more certainly than a weapon.

The Puritans identified several specific sins of the tongue that violate the sixth commandment. First, there is the sin of verbal abuse — speaking to another person with cruelty, contempt, or deliberate intent to wound. This includes name-calling, mockery, public humiliation, and the kind of biting sarcasm that tears down rather than builds up. When we use our words to diminish, degrade, or destroy another person's sense of worth, we are doing violence to a creature made in the image of God.

Second, there is the sin of slander — spreading false information about another person with the intent to damage their reputation. A person's reputation, the Puritans taught, is a form of property — indeed, it is among the most valuable things a person possesses. "A good name is rather to be chosen than great riches, and loving favour rather than silver and gold" (Proverbs 22:1). To destroy a person's reputation through lies is to commit a form of murder — killing not the body but the social identity that gives a person standing in the community.

Third, there is the sin of incitement — using words to provoke others to anger, violence, or destructive behavior. The person who stirs up strife, who fans the flames of conflict, who uses inflammatory language to set people against each other, is guilty of violating the sixth commandment even if he never lifts a hand himself. Proverbs 6:16-19 lists among the things that the Lord hates "he that soweth discord among brethren."

Richard Baxter, in his "Christian Directory" published in 1673, devoted an extensive section to the duties of the tongue under the sixth commandment. Baxter counseled believers to "speak always as in the presence of God," remembering that every word we utter is heard by the One who will judge us: "But I say unto you, That every idle word that men shall speak, they shall give account thereof in the day of judgment. For by thy words thou shalt be justified, and by thy words thou shalt be condemned" (Matthew 12:36-37). Baxter urged Christians to cultivate speech that is truthful, kind, edifying, and life-giving — speech that builds up rather than tears down, that heals rather than wounds, that reconciles rather than divides.

PART SIX: THE ETHICS OF WAR, CAPITAL PUNISHMENT, AND SELF-DEFENSE

The sixth commandment raises profound questions about the ethics of war, capital punishment, and self-defense — questions that the Puritans addressed with characteristic seriousness and theological depth.

Regarding capital punishment, the Puritans generally held that the state has the God-given right and duty to punish certain crimes with death. They grounded this position in Genesis 9:6, which establishes the principle of capital punishment for murder, and in Romans 13:1-4, where Paul declares that the civil magistrate "beareth not the sword in vain: for he is the minister of God, a revenger to execute wrath upon him that doeth evil." The Puritans argued that the sixth commandment does not prohibit the lawful taking of life by the state in the administration of justice; it prohibits the unlawful taking of life by private individuals acting on their own authority. The Hebrew word used in Exodus 20:13 is ratsach, which is more accurately translated "murder" — the deliberate, unjustified killing of an innocent person — rather than "kill" in the broadest sense.

However, the Puritans also insisted that capital punishment must be exercised with the utmost gravity and restraint. It is not something to be administered lightly, casually, or with a spirit of revenge. Every execution is a solemn event — a reminder of the terrible consequences of sin and of the awesome responsibility that God has placed upon the civil magistrate. Samuel Bolton, who served as vice-chancellor of Cambridge University in 1651, wrote that the magistrate who orders an execution "must do it as God's servant, with a heavy heart and a trembling hand, knowing that he is taking a life that bears the image of God."

Regarding warfare, the Puritans generally held to a form of just war theory, drawing from both Scripture and the broader Christian tradition. They believed that defensive warfare — taking up arms to protect the innocent, defend the commonwealth, and resist tyrannical aggression — was a legitimate exercise of the magistrate's God-given authority. The Old Testament provides numerous examples of God commanding or approving warfare undertaken for the defense of His people and the advancement of His purposes. The Puritans were not pacifists; they believed that there are circumstances in which the failure to fight is itself a violation of the sixth commandment, because it allows the innocent to be destroyed when they could have been protected.

At the same time, the Puritans were deeply aware of the moral dangers of warfare. They insisted that war must be a last resort, undertaken only when all peaceful means of resolution have been exhausted. They demanded that the conduct of war must be governed by the principles of justice and mercy — that noncombatants must be protected, that excessive force must be avoided, and that the goal of warfare must always be the restoration of peace and justice, not mere conquest or revenge. Oliver Cromwell, the great Puritan general and Lord Protector of England from 1653 to 1658, is recorded as having said before the Battle of Naseby in 1645: "I could not riding alone about my business, but smile out to God in praises, in assurance of victory, because God would, by things that are not, bring to naught things that are." Cromwell believed that warfare undertaken in God's cause and conducted according to God's principles could be an act of obedience to the sixth commandment — a defense of life against the forces that sought to destroy it.

Regarding self-defense, the Puritans generally recognized the right of individuals to defend themselves and their families against unjust violence. Exodus 22:2 provides a basis for this position: "If a thief be found breaking up, and be smitten that he die, there shall no blood be shed for him." The Puritans argued that the duty to preserve life — which the sixth commandment requires — includes the duty to defend our own lives and the lives of those entrusted to our care when they are unjustly threatened. However, they also cautioned against excessive force in self-defense and warned that the line between legitimate defense and sinful vengeance can be very thin.

PART SEVEN: THE SIXTH COMMANDMENT AND THE INNER LIFE

Perhaps the most challenging aspect of the Puritan exposition of the sixth commandment is its application to the inner life — to the emotions, passions, and dispositions that dwell within the human heart. The Puritans, following Jesus' teaching in the Sermon on the Mount, insisted that the sixth commandment addresses not only our outward actions but our inward attitudes.

Anger is the most obvious and most dangerous of the passions that the sixth commandment addresses. The Puritans distinguished between righteous anger — the holy indignation that responds to genuine injustice and moral evil — and sinful anger — the self-serving rage that arises from wounded pride, frustrated desires, and personal offenses. Righteous anger is directed at sin, not at persons; it is controlled, proportionate, and motivated by love for God and concern for justice. Sinful anger is directed at persons; it is often disproportionate, vindictive, and motivated by self-interest.

Paul counseled the Ephesians: "Be ye angry, and sin not: let not the sun go down upon your wrath: Neither give place to the devil" (Ephesians 4:26-27). The Puritans took this passage as a recognition that anger itself is a natural human emotion that is not inherently sinful — even God is described as being angry with sin. But anger becomes sinful when it is directed at persons rather than at injustice, when it is nursed and harbored rather than resolved, and when it leads to bitterness, revenge, and the desire to harm.

Envy is another passion that the Puritans identified as a violation of the sixth commandment. To envy another person is to resent their prosperity, their talents, their relationships, or their blessings — and resentment, if unchecked, can easily develop into the desire to see them brought low. The story of Cain and Abel, the first murder recorded in Scripture, illustrates this progression with terrible clarity. Cain was envious because "the LORD had respect unto Abel and to his offering: But unto Cain and to his offering he had not respect. And Cain was very wroth, and his countenance fell" (Genesis 4:4-5). God warned Cain: "If thou doest well, shalt thou not be accepted? and if thou doest not well, sin lieth at the door" (Genesis 4:7). But Cain refused to heed the warning, and his envy ripened into murder: "And Cain talked with Abel his brother: and it came to pass, when they were in the field, that Cain rose up against Abel his brother, and slew him" (Genesis 4:8).

The Puritans used this narrative as a solemn warning. Murder does not spring fully formed from the human heart. It grows gradually — from envy to resentment, from resentment to anger, from anger to hatred, and from hatred to violence. The sixth commandment, by prohibiting the final act, also prohibits every step in the chain that leads to it. To uproot murder, we must uproot the envy, the anger, and the hatred that give it birth.

Jeremiah Burroughs, the Puritan minister who served as a pastor in Norfolk and later in London, and who published his beloved work "The Rare Jewel of Christian Contentment" in 1648, addressed the relationship between discontent and the violation of the sixth commandment. Burroughs argued that a discontented heart is a restless heart, and a restless heart is a dangerous heart. When we are consumed with dissatisfaction — when we compare our lot in life to others' and find it wanting — we are cultivating the soil in which envy, anger, and violence take root. Contentment, by contrast, produces peace — peace with God, peace with others, and peace within ourselves. The contented soul has no reason to envy, no impulse to harm, and no desire for revenge.

PART EIGHT: THE SIXTH COMMANDMENT AND THE DUTY OF COMPASSION

The positive requirements of the sixth commandment — the duty to actively preserve and promote human life — find their highest expression in the Christian virtue of compassion. The Puritans taught that compassion is not merely a feeling but an active disposition — a readiness to perceive suffering, to be moved by it, and to take action to alleviate it.

Matthew Henry, the great Puritan Bible commentator who lived from 1662 to 1714 and whose six-volume "Exposition of the Old and New Testaments" remains one of the most widely read commentaries in the English language, wrote extensively about the sixth commandment's requirement of compassion. Henry observed that Jesus Himself was moved with compassion when He saw the multitudes who were "scattered abroad, as sheep having no shepherd" (Matthew 9:36). This compassion was not passive — it led Jesus to teach, to heal, to feed, and ultimately to lay down His life for the salvation of the world. Henry argued that Christians are to follow their Master's example, showing compassion to all who suffer and using every means at their disposal to relieve suffering and preserve life.

The Puritans put this principle into practice with remarkable energy. They established hospitals, almshouses, schools, and charitable societies. They cared for orphans, widows, the aged, and the disabled. They insisted that the poor had a legitimate claim upon the generosity of the wealthy, and they organized their congregations to provide systematic relief for those in need. Cotton Mather, in his influential essay "Bonifacius" or "Essays to Do Good," published in 1710, urged every Christian to ask himself daily: "What good may I do in the world?" Mather proposed that every person should actively seek opportunities to relieve suffering, promote education, strengthen the weak, comfort the afflicted, and advance the kingdom of God in practical ways.

PART NINE: THE SIXTH COMMANDMENT AND THE GOSPEL

As with every commandment, the Puritans ultimately pointed their readers to the gospel of Jesus Christ. The sixth commandment, they taught, serves three vital functions in relation to the gospel.

First, it serves as a mirror that reveals our sinfulness. When we examine our hearts honestly in the light of the sixth commandment — not merely asking whether we have committed murder, but whether we have harbored anger, envy, hatred, contempt, or indifference toward others — we discover that we are all guilty. We have all violated the spirit of the sixth commandment in countless ways. This conviction of sin is essential to the work of grace, because it drives us to seek the forgiveness that is found only in Christ.

Second, the sixth commandment reveals the enormity of what Christ endured on our behalf. At the cross, the sinless Son of God was murdered — put to death by wicked hands, in the most violent and unjust act in the history of the world. Yet this murder was also, in the mysterious providence of God, the means of our salvation. Peter declared on the day of Pentecost: "Him, being delivered by the determinate counsel and foreknowledge of God, ye have taken, and by wicked hands have crucified and slain" (Acts 2:23). The death of Christ was both the ultimate violation of the sixth commandment and the ultimate fulfillment of God's plan of redemption. Through His death, Christ bore the penalty for all our violations of God's law — including our violations of the sixth commandment — and purchased for us the forgiveness, the righteousness, and the eternal life that we could never earn for ourselves.

Third, the sixth commandment reveals the power of grace to transform the human heart. The same Paul who once "consented unto" the murder of Stephen (Acts 8:1) — who "breathed out threatenings and slaughter against the disciples of the Lord" (Acts 9:1) — was transformed by the grace of God into the apostle of love who wrote: "Love worketh no ill to his neighbour: therefore love is the fulfilling of the law" (Romans 13:10). The gospel does not merely forgive our violations of the sixth commandment — it transforms us into people who love life, who cherish others, and who seek to preserve and promote the welfare of every person we encounter.

PART TEN: THE SIXTH COMMANDMENT AND THE TREATMENT OF ANIMALS

It may surprise you to learn that the Puritans included the treatment of animals within the scope of the sixth commandment. While the commandment specifically addresses human life, the Puritans argued that the principle of cherishing life extends to God's creatures as well. Proverbs 12:10 declares: "A righteous man regardeth the life of his beast: but the tender mercies of the wicked are cruel." The way a person treats animals, the Puritans believed, reveals something profound about the condition of his heart. A person who is cruel to animals — who inflicts unnecessary suffering upon creatures that cannot defend themselves — is cultivating a hardness of heart that will inevitably express itself in cruelty toward human beings as well.

The Massachusetts Body of Liberties, adopted in 1641 — one of the earliest legal codes in the American colonies — included a remarkable provision protecting animals from cruelty. Liberty 92 stated: "No man shall exercise any tyranny or cruelty toward any brute creature which are usually kept for man's use." This was one of the first animal cruelty laws in the English-speaking world, and it was enacted by Puritans who saw the sixth commandment's principle of life-preservation as extending even to the animal kingdom.

Thomas Watson reinforced this teaching by noting that God Himself shows care for His creatures. "The Lord is good to all: and his tender mercies are over all his works" (Psalm 145:9). If God, the sovereign Creator, shows mercy and care toward the animals He has made, how much more should we, His image-bearers, treat the creatures under our dominion with kindness and compassion? The Puritans did not sentimentalize animals or place them on the same level as human beings — they fully recognized the vast distinction between creatures made in the image of God and those that are not. But they insisted that the dominion God has given us over the animal creation is a dominion of stewardship, not of tyranny, and that wanton cruelty toward any living creature is inconsistent with the spirit of the sixth commandment.

PART ELEVEN: THE SIXTH COMMANDMENT IN PURITAN HISTORY

The Puritans' commitment to the sanctity of life found expression in numerous practical ways throughout their history. In Puritan New England, communities organized to care for the sick, the orphaned, and the destitute. The town of Boston established one of the earliest public welfare systems in the American colonies, providing food, shelter, and medical care to those who could not provide for themselves. These charitable efforts were not seen as optional acts of generosity but as moral obligations rooted in the sixth commandment's requirement to preserve and protect human life.

The Puritan emphasis on education was itself an expression of the sixth commandment. By establishing schools and colleges — including Harvard College in 1636, Yale College in 1701, and numerous grammar schools and dame schools throughout New England — the Puritans were investing in the intellectual and spiritual life of the rising generation. They understood that ignorance is a form of death — that the person who lacks knowledge of God, of Scripture, and of the skills necessary for productive living is in a very real sense impoverished and endangered. Education, for the Puritans, was a life-giving enterprise, and their passionate commitment to it was rooted in the same principle that undergirds the sixth commandment: the sacred obligation to preserve and promote human flourishing.

The Puritan approach to medicine and public health was likewise shaped by the sixth commandment. Cotton Mather, despite considerable public opposition, championed the practice of smallpox inoculation during the devastating Boston epidemic of 1721. Mather argued that using available medical knowledge to prevent death was not a violation of God's sovereignty but a faithful exercise of the stewardship that the sixth commandment requires. His courage in the face of public hostility — his home was actually firebombed by opponents of inoculation — demonstrated the depth of his conviction that the preservation of life was a sacred duty.

PART TWELVE: APPLICATION FOR OUR TIME

The sixth commandment speaks with urgent relevance to our own age. We live in a time when human life is often devalued, when violence is glorified in entertainment, when angry and contemptuous speech fills public discourse, and when many people struggle with the kind of deep-seated anger, envy, and bitterness that the sixth commandment addresses.

Consider the world of social media and digital communication. The anonymity and distance that screens provide can embolden people to say things they would never say face to face — cruel mockery, vicious personal attacks, relentless public shaming, and the kind of sustained verbal harassment that has driven vulnerable individuals to despair and even to self-destruction. The Puritans would have recognized these behaviors immediately as violations of the sixth commandment. The medium may be new, but the sin is ancient: the use of words as weapons to destroy the dignity and well-being of persons made in the image of God.

Consider also the epidemic of loneliness and isolation that afflicts so many people in our society. The elderly are often neglected, the sick are sometimes abandoned, and countless individuals live in quiet desperation, feeling invisible and forgotten. The sixth commandment calls us to see these people — to notice them, to reach out to them, to bring the warmth of human connection and the hope of the gospel into their lives. Every act of kindness toward a lonely person is an act of obedience to the sixth commandment. Every visit to the sick, every meal shared with the grieving, every word of encouragement spoken to the discouraged is a fulfillment of the duty to preserve and promote human life.

As students of the Puritan tradition, you are called to be counter-cultural — to stand against the tide of a culture that too often treats human life as cheap and disposable. This means valuing every person you encounter as an image-bearer of the living God. It means guarding your heart against anger, envy, and hatred. It means using your words to build up rather than to tear down. It means showing compassion to those who suffer and speaking up for those who cannot speak for themselves. It means caring for your own body and mind as the temple of the Holy Spirit. And it means bringing the gospel — the message of eternal life — to those who are perishing without it.

The Puritan vision of the sixth commandment is a vision of life — abundant, flourishing, God-honoring life. It calls us to be people who treasure every human being, who actively seek the welfare of others, who guard our hearts against the passions that destroy, and who point the world to the One who said: "I am come that they might have life, and that they might have it more abundantly" (John 10:10).

--- Review Questions ---

Review Question 1: What is the theological foundation of the sixth commandment, and why does the image of God in man make human life sacred? In your answer, reference both Genesis 1:26-27 and Genesis 9:6, and explain why the Puritans insisted that even fallen, sinful human beings retain the image of God.

Review Question 2: How does Jesus deepen the sixth commandment in the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5:21-22)? What does this teach us about the relationship between anger and murder, and why did the Puritans insist that the commandment addresses the heart as well as the hand?

Review Question 3: Thomas Watson taught that the sixth commandment requires us to preserve life at three levels — our own physical lives, the physical lives of others, and the spiritual lives of others. Explain each level in detail and provide at least one Scripture reference that supports each.

Review Question 4: The Puritans identified the sins of the tongue — verbal abuse, slander, and incitement — as violations of the sixth commandment. Using James 3:6 and Matthew 12:36-37, explain why words can be as destructive as physical violence and how Christians should govern their speech.

Review Question 5: How does the sixth commandment point us to the gospel of Jesus Christ? In your answer, discuss the cross as both the ultimate violation of the sixth commandment and the ultimate fulfillment of God's plan of redemption.

--- Closing ---

The sixth commandment is ultimately a call to love — to value every person as God values them and to treat every life as the sacred, irreplaceable gift that it is. It calls us to guard not only our hands but our hearts, not only our actions but our attitudes, not only our deeds but our words. May God give us hearts that cherish life, tongues that speak life, and hands that preserve life — all for the glory of the One who is Himself the Author and Sustainer of all life.
