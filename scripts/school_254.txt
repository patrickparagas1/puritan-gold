PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 14
"Hallowing God's Name"
Christian Ethics & Moral Philosophy — Lesson 4

---

Welcome back, students. Today we study the third commandment — the commandment that governs the most powerful thing a human being possesses: the ability to speak the name of God. In our previous lessons, we learned who we are to worship (the first commandment) and how we are to worship (the second commandment). Today we learn how we are to speak of the God we worship — and we will discover that this commandment reaches far deeper and wider than most people imagine.

--- Lesson ---

Part One: The Commandment and Its Unique Warning

"Thou shalt not take the name of the Lord thy God in vain; for the Lord will not hold him guiltless that taketh his name in vain" (Exodus 20:7).

The third commandment is unique among the Ten in a striking and sobering way. It is the only commandment that carries an explicit and specific warning: "The Lord will not hold him guiltless that taketh his name in vain." No other commandment contains such a direct statement of divine response. The commandment against murder does not say "the Lord will not hold him guiltless who kills." The commandment against theft does not say "the Lord will not hold him guiltless who steals." But this commandment — the commandment about God's name — carries a special and solemn declaration that God takes the misuse of His name with absolute seriousness.

This unique warning should cause us to sit up and pay attention. It tells us that the misuse of God's name is not a minor infraction, not a venial sin, not a culturally relative matter of etiquette. It is a direct offense against the majesty, holiness, and authority of the Creator of the universe. It strikes at the very heart of the relationship between creature and Creator. And God Himself has declared that He will not overlook it.

The Westminster Larger Catechism, Question 112, identifies the sins forbidden by the third commandment in exhaustive detail. It lists "not using God's name as is required" as well as "an ignorant, vain, irreverent, profane, superstitious, or wicked mentioning or otherwise using his titles, attributes, ordinances, or works; by blasphemy, perjury; all sinful cursings, oaths, vows, and lots; violating of our oaths and vows, if lawful; fulfilling them, if of things unlawful; murmuring and quarrelling at, curious prying into, and misapplying of God's decrees and providences; misinterpreting, misapplying, or any way perverting the word, or any part of it, to profane jests, curious or unprofitable questions, vain janglings, or the maintaining of false doctrines; abusing it, the creatures, or any thing contained under the name of God, to charms, or sinful lusts and practices; the maligning, scorning, reviling, or any wise opposing of God's truth, grace, and ways; making profession of religion in hypocrisy, or for sinister ends; being ashamed of it, or a shame to it, by unconformable, unwise, unfruitful, and offensive walking, or backsliding from it."

The sheer length of this answer tells us something important: the third commandment covers far more territory than the casual profanity that most people associate with it. It addresses the entire range of human speech and conduct as it relates to the name, reputation, and honor of God.

Part Two: The Significance of God's Name in Scripture

To understand the third commandment, we must first understand what the Bible means by "the name of God." In the ancient world, and especially in the culture of the Old Testament, a person's name was not merely a label or identifier. It was a revelation of character, authority, and identity. When God revealed His name to Moses at the burning bush, He was revealing His very nature.

"And Moses said unto God, Behold, when I come unto the children of Israel, and shall say unto them, The God of your fathers hath sent me unto you; and they shall say to me, What is his name? what shall I say unto them? And God said unto Moses, I AM THAT I AM: and he said, Thus shalt thou say unto the children of Israel, I AM hath sent me unto you" (Exodus 3:13-14).

The name "I AM THAT I AM" — in Hebrew, Ehyeh Asher Ehyeh — is related to the divine name YHWH (Yahweh or Jehovah), which is the covenant name of God used throughout the Old Testament. This name reveals God as the self-existent, eternal, unchanging, all-sufficient Being — the One who depends on nothing outside Himself for His existence, who was and is and ever shall be, who is the source and sustainer of all that exists. To speak this name is to invoke the reality behind it. To misuse this name is to treat the reality behind it as nothing.

Throughout the Old Testament, God revealed Himself through a rich array of names, each disclosing an aspect of His character. He is El Elyon — God Most High. He is El Shaddai — God Almighty. He is Adonai — Lord and Master. He is Jehovah-Jireh — the Lord who provides. He is Jehovah-Rapha — the Lord who heals. He is Jehovah-Nissi — the Lord our Banner. He is Jehovah-Shalom — the Lord our Peace. He is Jehovah-Tsidkenu — the Lord our Righteousness. He is Jehovah-Shammah — the Lord who is present.

The "name of God," therefore, encompasses everything that God has revealed about Himself — His character, His attributes, His promises, His works, His ordinances, His word, and His glory. The third commandment protects all of these. To take God's name in vain is to treat any aspect of God's self-revelation as empty, trivial, common, or unworthy of reverence.

The ancient Israelites understood this so profoundly that they developed the practice of not even speaking the name YHWH aloud, substituting the title Adonai (Lord) whenever they encountered it in the reading of Scripture. While the Puritans did not adopt this specific practice — they used the names of God freely in worship, prayer, and preaching — they shared the underlying conviction: the name of God is holy and must be treated with the utmost reverence.

Part Three: The Many Violations of the Third Commandment

Thomas Watson, in his exposition of the third commandment in "The Ten Commandments," identified numerous ways in which this commandment is violated. Let us examine these carefully, for they reveal how far-reaching this commandment truly is.

The first and most obvious violation is profanity — the casual, contemptuous, or careless use of God's name as an exclamation, a curse, or a filler word. This practice, tragically common in every age and epidemic in our own, treats the name of the Creator of the universe as though it were nothing — an empty syllable to be thrown away in moments of surprise, frustration, or casual conversation. Watson wrote that profanity is a sin that yields no profit to the sinner — unlike theft, which at least gains the thief something, or adultery, which at least satisfies a sinful desire, profanity gains nothing. It is sin for sin's sake — the gratuitous dishonoring of the most sacred name in the universe.

The Puritans considered profanity one of the most grievous sins, precisely because it reveals a heart that has no reverence for God. A culture that uses God's name as a common expletive is a culture that has lost its sense of the sacred. And the loss of the sense of the sacred is the beginning of moral collapse, for when God is not reverenced, nothing else is safe.

The second violation is blasphemy — the deliberate and malicious reviling of God's name, character, or works. Blasphemy is the most extreme form of third-commandment violation. It is not merely careless speech but hostile speech — speech that directly attacks the honor and dignity of God. In the Old Testament, blasphemy was punishable by death: "And he that blasphemeth the name of the Lord, he shall surely be put to death, and all the congregation shall certainly stone him" (Leviticus 24:16).

The third violation is perjury — swearing falsely by God's name. In ancient Israel, an oath sworn in God's name was the most solemn form of promise. It was an invocation of God as a witness to the truth of one's words. To swear falsely in God's name was to drag the Holy One into the service of falsehood — to make God a party to a lie. This was an act of unspeakable impiety that struck at the very foundation of social trust, since oaths were the guarantors of contracts, treaties, and judicial proceedings.

The Puritans extended this principle to all forms of dishonesty that invoke or implicate God's name. False prophecy — claiming to speak in God's name when God has not spoken — is a form of perjury against the divine name. False teaching — misrepresenting God's Word to others — is a form of perjury against the divine name. Using religious language to deceive or manipulate — "God told me to tell you..." or "The Lord revealed to me that..." when no such revelation has occurred — is among the most serious violations of the third commandment.

The fourth violation is hypocrisy — professing the name of God while living in a manner that contradicts His character. When a person claims to be a Christian but lives in persistent, unrepentant sin, that person is taking God's name in vain — wearing the name of Christ as a label while denying its reality by their conduct. The prophets of Israel thundered against this sin with relentless fury.

God said through Isaiah: "This people draw near me with their mouth, and with their lips do honour me, but have removed their heart far from me, and their fear toward me is taught by the precept of men" (Isaiah 29:13). God said through Ezekiel: "And they come unto thee as the people cometh, and they sit before thee as my people, and they hear thy words, but they will not do them: for with their mouth they shew much love, but their heart goeth after their covetousness" (Ezekiel 33:31). Paul wrote to the Romans: "For the name of God is blasphemed among the Gentiles through you" (Romans 2:24), indicating that the hypocritical conduct of those who profess God's name actually causes unbelievers to dishonor that name.

Watson wrote that the hypocrite commits a double sin: he sins against God by his conduct, and he sins against God's name by associating that name with his sinful conduct. The drunkard who calls himself a Christian, the liar who professes the name of Christ, the thief who sits in the pew on Sunday morning — all of these take God's name in vain in a way that is arguably more damaging than the profanity of the openly ungodly, because they bring reproach upon the very name they claim to honor.

The fifth violation is false worship — using God's name in religious ceremonies, prayers, or rituals that are not authorized by His Word or that are performed without genuine faith and sincerity. Worship that is merely external — words without meaning, rituals without faith, prayers without sincerity — is a form of taking God's name in vain. It invokes God's name in an act that God has not authorized or in a spirit that God does not accept.

The sixth violation, more subtle but no less serious, is irreverent theology — speaking about God carelessly, flippantly, or without proper awe. The Puritans insisted that all our speech about God — in sermons, in conversations, in written works, in classroom discussions — should be marked by accuracy, reverence, and humility. We are speaking about the infinite, eternal, omnipotent Creator of the universe. Our words should reflect the gravity of the subject.

Stephen Charnock, in "The Existence and Attributes of God," wrote that we should never speak of God without first pausing to consider the infinite weight of what we are saying. Even our theology — our study of God — can become a form of third-commandment violation if it degenerates into mere intellectual exercise without awe, or into casual speculation without reverence. The Puritans studied theology on their knees, and they taught it with trembling lips, because they knew that they were handling the most sacred subject in the universe.

Part Four: The Positive Duty — Hallowing God's Name

On the positive side, the third commandment requires that we honor God's name in all that we say and do. The Westminster Larger Catechism, Question 111, identifies the duties required by the third commandment with characteristic comprehensiveness: "The duties required in the third commandment are, the holy and reverend use of God's names, titles, attributes, ordinances, word, and works; and in answering and acting, according to the name and authority of God, in making and keeping vows, and swearing faithfully, when required."

This means, first, that we must speak God's name with reverence in worship and prayer. When we pray "Our Father, which art in heaven, hallowed be thy name" (Matthew 6:9), we are not merely expressing a wish — we are making a commitment. We are saying: let Your name be treated as holy in my speech, in my conduct, in my worship, in my teaching, in my relationships, in my business dealings — in everything I am and everything I do.

Second, it means living in a way that brings credit to God's name rather than shame. Paul exhorted the Colossians: "And whatsoever ye do in word or deed, do all in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to God and the Father by him" (Colossians 3:17). Every action of the Christian is performed "in the name of the Lord Jesus" — as a representative of Christ, bearing His name before the watching world. This places an enormous responsibility upon us. Our conduct either adorns the gospel or disgraces it. Our behavior either attracts people to Christ or repels them from Him. The third commandment demands that we live as those who take seriously the name we bear.

Third, it means teaching our children to treat God's name as holy. The Puritans placed enormous emphasis on the catechetical instruction of children — not merely teaching them facts about God but instilling in them a deep, heartfelt reverence for God's name and character. William Gouge, the Puritan pastor who served at St. Ann Blackfriars in London for over forty-five years from 1608 to 1653 and whose work "Of Domesticall Duties," published in 1622, became one of the most influential treatises on family life in the English language, wrote that parents have a sacred obligation to teach their children to speak of God with reverence from the earliest age. A child who grows up hearing God's name used carelessly will develop a careless attitude toward God Himself. A child who grows up hearing God's name spoken with awe and love will develop a reverent heart.

Fourth, it means standing up for the honor of God's name when it is profaned by others — not with self-righteous indignation or angry confrontation, but with the quiet, firm conviction that the name above every name deserves the respect that belongs to it. The Puritans did not believe in passive acceptance of blasphemy. They believed that loyalty to God required them to speak — gently but clearly — when His name was dishonored.

Part Five: Vows, Oaths, and the Solemn Use of God's Name

The third commandment has particular application to the practice of making vows and oaths — solemn promises in which God's name is invoked as a witness. The Westminster Confession of Faith, Chapter 22, addresses this subject at length, affirming that a lawful oath — one taken in truth, righteousness, and judgment — is a part of religious worship, since it involves the solemn acknowledgment of God's omniscience and sovereignty.

The Bible records many instances of lawful oaths and vows. Abraham made his servant swear an oath when sending him to find a wife for Isaac (Genesis 24:2-3). Jacob made a vow to God at Bethel (Genesis 28:20-22). Ruth made a solemn vow to Naomi (Ruth 1:16-17). Paul frequently invoked God as a witness to the truth of his statements (Romans 1:9; 2 Corinthians 1:23; Galatians 1:20; Philippians 1:8).

The Puritans taught that oaths and vows must be entered into with the utmost gravity and must be kept with the utmost fidelity. To make a vow to God and then break it is a particularly serious violation of the third commandment, because it treats God's name — invoked as a witness — with contempt. "When thou vowest a vow unto God, defer not to pay it; for he hath no pleasure in fools: pay that which thou hast vowed. Better is it that thou shouldest not vow, than that thou shouldest vow and not pay" (Ecclesiastes 5:4-5).

At the same time, the Puritans warned against rash and unnecessary oaths — the casual invocation of God's name to strengthen everyday assertions. Jesus addressed this in the Sermon on the Mount: "But I say unto you, Swear not at all; neither by heaven; for it is God's throne: nor by the earth; for it is his footstool: neither by Jerusalem; for it is the city of the great King. Neither shalt thou swear by thy head, because thou canst not make one hair white or black. But let your communication be, Yea, yea; Nay, nay: for whatsoever is more than these cometh of evil" (Matthew 5:34-37).

The Puritans understood this passage not as a prohibition of all oaths — which would contradict the many instances of lawful oaths in Scripture and the Westminster Confession's affirmation of their legitimacy — but as a prohibition of casual, unnecessary, and frivolous swearing. Our ordinary speech should be so honest and reliable that oaths are unnecessary. We should not need to invoke God's name to convince people that we are telling the truth. Our "yea" should be yea and our "nay" should be nay. This is the standard of truthfulness that the third commandment requires.

Part Six: The Third Commandment and the Lord's Prayer

The connection between the third commandment and the Lord's Prayer is direct and profound. The very first petition of the prayer that Jesus taught His disciples is: "Hallowed be thy name" (Matthew 6:9). This petition is not a statement of fact — God's name is already holy, whether we hallow it or not. It is a prayer and a commitment — a request that God would cause His name to be reverenced throughout the world, and a dedication of ourselves to the work of hallowing that name in our own lives.

Thomas Manton, the Puritan minister who served as one of the clerks of the Westminster Assembly and later pastored a congregation in Covent Garden, London, preached an extensive series of sermons on the Lord's Prayer — 190 sermons in all, filling three thick volumes in the standard edition of his works. Manton's exposition of "Hallowed be thy name" is one of the richest treatments of the third commandment in all of Puritan literature.

Manton taught that this petition covers three aspects of hallowing God's name. First, we pray that God would hallow His own name — that He would make Himself known in the world through His works of creation, providence, and redemption. Second, we pray that others would hallow God's name — that the gospel would spread, that unbelievers would come to know and revere the true God, and that the nations would bow before His authority. Third, we pray that we ourselves would hallow God's name — that our speech, our conduct, our worship, and our entire manner of life would bring honor to the God whose name we bear.

This third aspect is the most searching and personal. When we pray "Hallowed be thy name," we are asking God to make us the kind of people who hallow His name — people whose every word is marked by reverence, whose every action is marked by integrity, whose every relationship is marked by the character of Christ. It is a prayer that strikes at the root of hypocrisy, carelessness, and irreverence, and it commits us to the lifelong pursuit of a life that brings glory to God.

Part Seven: Historical Examples and Warnings

The Bible provides sobering examples of the consequences of taking God's name in vain. When the son of Shelomith blasphemed the name of the Lord during a quarrel in the camp of Israel, God commanded that he be taken outside the camp and stoned to death by the entire congregation (Leviticus 24:10-16). This extreme punishment reveals the extreme seriousness with which God regards the misuse of His name.

During the reign of King Sennacherib of Assyria, around 701 B.C., the Assyrian general Rabshakeh stood before the walls of Jerusalem and blasphemed the God of Israel, comparing Him to the powerless gods of the nations Assyria had already conquered: "Who are they among all the gods of the countries, that have delivered their country out of mine hand, that the Lord should deliver Jerusalem out of mine hand?" (2 Kings 18:35). King Hezekiah took this blasphemy before the Lord in prayer, and God responded through the prophet Isaiah with words of devastating judgment. That very night, the angel of the Lord struck down 185,000 Assyrian soldiers, and Sennacherib fled back to Nineveh, where he was later assassinated by his own sons (2 Kings 19:35-37). God vindicated His name against those who blasphemed it.

In the New Testament, the story of Ananias and Sapphira (Acts 5:1-11) provides a sobering example of the consequences of lying to the Holy Spirit — which is a form of taking God's name in vain. When they sold a piece of property and secretly kept back a portion of the proceeds while claiming to have given the full amount, they were not merely lying to the apostles. They were lying to God. Peter said to Ananias: "Why hath Satan filled thine heart to lie to the Holy Ghost?" (Acts 5:3). Both Ananias and his wife fell dead — not because their sin was the worst ever committed, but because God was establishing a principle at the very beginning of the church's life: His name is holy, and those who invoke it must do so with truth and sincerity.

Part Eight: The Third Commandment and the Tongue — James's Warning

The third commandment is closely connected to the broader biblical teaching about the power and danger of the human tongue. The apostle James devoted an entire section of his epistle to this subject, and his words deserve careful consideration in connection with the third commandment.

"And the tongue is a fire, a world of iniquity: so is the tongue among our members, that it defileth the whole body, and setteth on fire the course of nature; and it is set on fire of hell. For every kind of beasts, and of birds, and of serpents, and of things in the sea, is tamed, and hath been tamed of mankind: but the tongue can no man tame; it is an unruly evil, full of deadly poison. Therewith bless we God, even the Father; and therewith curse we men, which are made after the similitude of God. Out of the same mouth proceedeth blessing and cursing. My brethren, these things ought not so to be" (James 3:6-10).

James makes the remarkable observation that the same tongue that blesses God also curses men made in God's image. This is a direct application of the third commandment. When we use the tongue that was created to praise God in order to slander, deceive, manipulate, or wound — we are profaning the instrument that was designed for holy speech. And when we use God's name in the same conversation where we use cutting, dishonest, or cruel words — we are demonstrating the very inconsistency that James condemns.

The Puritans took the sins of the tongue with great seriousness. Jeremiah Burroughs, the author of "The Rare Jewel of Christian Contentment," published in 1648, and one of the most beloved of all Puritan pastors, preached extensively on the sanctification of speech. Burroughs taught that the tongue is the index of the heart — that what comes out of the mouth reveals what is inside the soul. "Out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh," Jesus said (Matthew 12:34). If our speech is profane, it is because our hearts lack reverence. If our speech is careless about God, it is because our hearts are careless about God. The third commandment, therefore, is ultimately a heart commandment. It addresses speech, but it reaches through speech to the inner condition of the soul.

Richard Baxter, in his "Christian Directory," devoted extensive attention to the governance of the tongue. He provided practical guidance for Christians who wished to honor the third commandment in daily conversation. He urged believers to pause before speaking — to ask whether their words would honor God, edify their neighbor, and reflect the truth. He warned against idle speech — not merely profanity but the endless torrent of trivial, meaningless, time-wasting words that fill so much of human conversation. "But I say unto you, That every idle word that men shall speak, they shall give account thereof in the day of judgment. For by thy words thou shalt be justified, and by thy words thou shalt be condemned" (Matthew 12:36-37). If every idle word will be judged, how much more the misuse of God's sacred name?

Part Nine: Christ and the Third Commandment

Jesus Christ perfectly fulfilled the third commandment throughout His entire earthly life. He spoke of the Father with unfailing reverence and love. He used the Father's name with absolute truthfulness and sincerity. He never spoke a careless word about God, never made a false promise in God's name, never used religious language to deceive or manipulate. His speech was always "yea and amen" — perfectly truthful, perfectly reverent, perfectly aligned with the character and will of the Father.

In the high priestly prayer of John chapter 17, Jesus says to the Father: "I have manifested thy name unto the men which thou gavest me out of the world: thine they were, and thou gavest them me; and they have kept thy word" (John 17:6). And again: "I have declared unto them thy name, and will declare it: that the love wherewith thou hast loved me may be in them, and I in them" (John 17:26). The ministry of Jesus was, in its essence, the hallowing of the Father's name — the making known of who God truly is so that people could worship Him in spirit and in truth.

Christ also bore the consequences of our third-commandment violations. On the cross, He bore the curse that our profane, blasphemous, hypocritical, and deceitful speech deserved. "Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us: for it is written, Cursed is every one that hangeth on a tree" (Galatians 3:13). The One who never took the Father's name in vain took upon Himself the full penalty for every instance in which we have.

And through His Spirit, Christ now empowers us to hallow the Father's name as He did. The same Spirit who gave Jesus wisdom and reverence in His speech now dwells in every believer, producing the fruit of speech that is truthful, reverent, edifying, and grace-filled. "Let your speech be alway with grace, seasoned with salt, that ye may know how ye ought to answer every man" (Colossians 4:6). The third commandment is fulfilled not merely by our own willpower but by the transforming power of the Holy Spirit, who progressively sanctifies our tongues as He sanctifies our hearts.

Part Ten: The Third Commandment and the Name of Jesus

Under the new covenant, the third commandment takes on an additional dimension through the name of Jesus Christ. The name of Jesus is "the name which is above every name: that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, of things in heaven, and things in earth, and things under the earth; and that every tongue should confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father" (Philippians 2:9-11).

Christians bear the name of Christ — we are called "Christians," Christ-ones, people who belong to Christ and carry His name. The third commandment, therefore, applies not only to the name of God in the Old Testament sense but to the name of Christ in the New Testament sense. To profess Christ's name while living in contradiction to His character is to take His name in vain. To invoke Christ's name in prayer without sincerity is to take His name in vain. To use Christ's name as a casual or cultural label without genuine faith is to take His name in vain.

The early church understood this profoundly. When the apostles were brought before the Sanhedrin and commanded not to teach in the name of Jesus, they replied: "We ought to obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29). They counted it a privilege to suffer for the name of Christ: "And they departed from the presence of the council, rejoicing that they were counted worthy to suffer shame for his name" (Acts 5:41). The name of Jesus was not a mere label to the early Christians — it was a treasure worth dying for.

Throughout church history, millions of martyrs have gone to their deaths rather than deny the name of Christ. During the persecution under Diocletian in 303-313 A.D., Christians who refused to hand over their Scriptures or burn incense to the emperor were tortured and killed. During the Marian persecutions in England from 1553 to 1558, approximately three hundred Protestants — including Thomas Cranmer, the Archbishop of Canterbury, Hugh Latimer, the Bishop of Worcester, and Nicholas Ridley, the Bishop of London — were burned at the stake for their refusal to renounce their Protestant faith. As Latimer said to Ridley as the flames were lit: "Be of good comfort, Master Ridley, and play the man. We shall this day light such a candle, by God's grace, in England, as I trust shall never be put out."

These martyrs understood the third commandment at its deepest level. They understood that the name of God and the name of Christ are not cheap words to be discarded when the cost of bearing them becomes too high. They are sacred trusts to be upheld even unto death. The third commandment calls us to the same commitment — not necessarily to martyrdom, but to a life that honors the name we bear in every circumstance, at any cost.

Part Eleven: The Third Commandment in Connection with the Other Commandments

The third commandment has a vital connection to every other commandment in the Decalogue. When we worship false gods (first commandment), we dishonor God's name. When we worship through false means (second commandment), we profane God's name. When we violate the Sabbath (fourth commandment), we show contempt for God's holy day and thus for God's holy name. When we dishonor our parents (fifth commandment), we dishonor the God who appointed them as His representatives. When we murder, steal, commit adultery, or bear false witness, we bring reproach on the name of the God whose law we profess to follow.

In this sense, the third commandment is violated whenever any other commandment is violated by someone who bears God's name. The Christian who sins publicly does not merely harm himself — he harms the reputation of Christ. The church member who lives in open contradiction to the gospel does not merely damage his own credibility — he damages the credibility of the gospel itself. This is why Paul wrote with such urgency: "For the name of God is blasphemed among the Gentiles through you" (Romans 2:24).

The Puritans understood this interconnection deeply. They taught that every sin committed by a professing Christian is a double sin — a sin against the law of God and a sin against the name of God. This understanding produced in them a profound sensitivity to the public witness of their lives. They lived not only for their own salvation but for the honor of the name they bore. They conducted their business with scrupulous honesty, their relationships with unfailing integrity, and their worship with reverential awe — because they knew that the watching world was forming its opinion of God based on the conduct of God's people.

Part Nine: Practical Application — Hallowing God's Name Today

The third commandment speaks with urgent relevance to our own age. We live in a culture that uses God's name as a casual expletive, that treats the sacred as entertainment, and that has largely lost its sense of the holy. The third commandment calls us to be different — to swim against the cultural current and to treat the name of God with the reverence it deserves.

Practically, this means several things. It means guarding our own speech — refusing to use God's name carelessly, eliminating euphemisms that are thinly veiled references to divine names, and cultivating the habit of speaking about God with accuracy, reverence, and humility. It means examining our worship — asking whether our prayers are sincere, whether our praise is heartfelt, whether we approach God with the awe that is due to His majesty. It means examining our lives — asking whether our conduct adorns or disgraces the name of Christ, whether the watching world sees in us something that makes the gospel attractive or something that makes it repulsive.

It also means recovering the practice of meditation on the names of God — spending time in Scripture studying what God has revealed about Himself through His names, and allowing that knowledge to deepen our reverence and strengthen our faith. When we know that God is Jehovah-Jireh — the Lord who provides — we can trust Him in times of need. When we know that God is Jehovah-Rapha — the Lord who heals — we can turn to Him in times of sickness. When we know that God is Jehovah-Shalom — the Lord our Peace — we can rest in Him in times of turmoil. The names of God are not just theological data to be memorized. They are living realities to be experienced, and the third commandment calls us to engage with them reverently and gratefully.

Furthermore, the third commandment should shape how we engage with the broader culture. When God's name is used profanely in entertainment, in literature, in casual conversation, or on social media, the Christian who understands the third commandment cannot simply ignore it. This does not mean we must mount angry campaigns against every instance of profanity — the Puritans were not zealots for outward conformity without inward conviction. But it does mean that we should be grieved by the casual profanation of God's name, that we should guard our own households against its influence, and that we should model a different way of speaking — a way that treats the sacred as sacred and the holy as holy.

The Puritan John Flavel wrote that the Christian's speech should be so consistently reverent and truthful that it becomes a witness in itself. When the world hears a Christian speak of God with genuine awe, with heartfelt gratitude, with theological precision, and with personal warmth — it encounters something it rarely hears: the living reality of a relationship with the God whose name is spoken. The third commandment is not merely a prohibition of bad speech. It is an invitation to holy speech — speech that reflects the beauty and majesty of the God we serve, speech that makes the invisible God visible through the testimony of our words.

Finally, the third commandment calls us to integrity in our use of religious language. In an age where spiritual vocabulary is often co-opted for marketing purposes, where "blessing" and "prayer" and even "God" are used as brand-building tools, and where religious sentiment is manufactured for commercial or political advantage, the third commandment demands that our use of God's name be genuine. When we say "God bless you," do we mean it? When we say "I'll pray for you," do we actually pray? When we invoke God's name in our conversations, are we speaking from a heart of faith or merely following a cultural convention? The third commandment searches beneath our words to the reality — or lack of reality — they represent.

--- Review Questions ---

Review Question 2: Why did the Puritans consider hypocrisy — professing God's name while living contrary to His character — a particularly damaging violation of the third commandment? How does hypocrisy affect the reputation of God among unbelievers?

--- Activity ---

For one full day, pay careful attention to how God's name is used in the media, conversations, and entertainment you encounter. Keep a written log noting each instance, the context, and whether the usage was reverent or irreverent. At the end of the day, write a reflection (300-400 words) on what you observed and what it reveals about our culture's attitude toward the sacred. Then, as a second exercise, spend fifteen minutes meditating on three names of God from the Old Testament (choose from the list discussed in today's lesson). For each name, write a brief paragraph explaining what it reveals about God's character and how that knowledge should shape your reverence for His name.

As a third exercise, look up the Westminster Larger Catechism, Questions 111 and 112, which detail the duties required and sins forbidden by the third commandment. Compare the catechism's comprehensive list with the categories discussed in today's lesson. Write a brief paragraph (100-150 words) identifying which aspect of third-commandment obedience you find most challenging in your own life and what practical steps you can take to grow in that area.

Materials needed: Small notebook or note-taking device, pen, Bible, Westminster Larger Catechism (available in free online editions), time for end-of-day reflection and meditation.

--- Closing ---

The name of God is the most sacred word a human tongue can speak. It is the name above every name, the name at which every knee shall bow and every tongue confess. It is the name by which we are saved, the name in which we are baptized, the name through which we pray, and the name that we will praise for all eternity. The third commandment calls us to treat this name with the reverence it deserves — not merely in our speech but in our worship, our conduct, our theology, and our daily walk before the watching world. As the Psalmist prayed: "O Lord our Lord, how excellent is thy name in all the earth! who hast set thy glory above the heavens" (Psalm 8:1). Let us hallow that excellent name with every word we speak and every life we live.

Let me leave you with one final thought from the Puritan tradition. Thomas Watson, near the end of his exposition of the third commandment, wrote that the day is coming when every tongue will speak God's name with perfect reverence — when sin will be no more, when profanity will be silenced forever, and when the redeemed of the Lord will praise His name without interruption, without distraction, and without end. "And they shall see his face; and his name shall be in their foreheads" (Revelation 22:4). In that day, the third commandment will not be a struggle but a delight — the spontaneous, joyful, eternal hallowing of the name that is above every name. Let us begin that eternal worship now, in the time that God has given us, with the tongues He has entrusted to our care. May our speech, our lives, and our worship hallow His glorious name from this day forward and forevermore. Until next time, students.
