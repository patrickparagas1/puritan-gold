PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 15
"I Opened to My Beloved, but He Was Gone"
Song of Solomon 5:2-8 — The Pain of Missed Connection

---

Welcome, beloved couple. Tonight we enter the most difficult passage in the Song of Solomon — a scene of missed connection, regret, and painful separation. If you have ever experienced the sting of a missed opportunity in your marriage, tonight's passage will resonate deeply.

Song of Solomon 5:2-8:

"I sleep, but my heart waketh: it is the voice of my beloved that knocketh, saying, Open to me, my sister, my love, my dove, my undefiled: for my head is filled with dew, and my locks with the drops of the night. I have put off my coat; how shall I put it on? I have washed my feet; how shall I defile them? My beloved put in his hand by the hole of the door, and my bowels were moved for him. I rose up to open to my beloved; and my hands dropped with myrrh, and my fingers with sweet smelling myrrh, upon the handles of the lock. I opened to my beloved; but my beloved had withdrawn himself, and was gone: my soul failed when he spake: I sought him, but I could not find him; I called him, but he gave me no answer. The watchmen that went about the city found me, they smote me, they wounded me; the keepers of the walls took away my veil from me. I charge you, O daughters of Jerusalem, if ye find my beloved, that ye tell him, that I am sick of love."

--- Teaching ---

This passage is painfully familiar to every married couple. The beloved comes knocking. He has been out in the cold — his head wet with dew, his locks dripping with the night's moisture. He has been working, traveling, enduring hardship. And now he comes home and knocks at the door of the one who loves him. "Open to me."

And the bride hesitates. "I have put off my coat; how shall I put it on? I have washed my feet; how shall I defile them?" She has already settled in for the night. She is comfortable. She is warm. Opening the door means getting up, getting dressed, dirtying her feet on the cold floor. In other words: she is unwilling to be inconvenienced.

This is one of the most honest portraits of marriage in all of Scripture. How often does one spouse reach out — with an invitation, a conversation starter, a bid for connection, a need for comfort — and the other refuses, not out of malice but out of sheer convenience? "Not now. I am tired. I have already settled in. Can it wait until tomorrow?" The Puritans warned fiercely against this complacency. Richard Baxter wrote that "the coldest marriages are not those that began without love, but those where love's invitations were repeatedly refused until the lover stopped knocking."

Then comes the turning point. "My beloved put in his hand by the hole of the door, and my bowels were moved for him." He reaches through the door — one last attempt, one more gesture of pursuit. And her heart is stirred. She realizes what she is doing. She rises to open. "My hands dropped with myrrh, and my fingers with sweet smelling myrrh, upon the handles of the lock." She has prepared herself, she is ready to receive him, she opens the door with fragrant hands.

But it is too late. "I opened to my beloved; but my beloved had withdrawn himself, and was gone." He is no longer there. The moment has passed. The invitation was offered and delayed, and now the one who knocked has left.

Every couple knows the weight of this moment. The conversation that should have happened last night but did not. The apology that was needed yesterday but was put off until today, when the damage had already hardened. The physical touch that was offered and rebuffed, and now the offering has ceased. William Gouge wrote with great urgency: "Do not delay to answer love's call. The moment is brief, and what is lost by delay can require much pain to recover."

"My soul failed when he spake: I sought him, but I could not find him; I called him, but he gave me no answer." Now the bride is in agony. She who hesitated now pursues desperately. She who was too comfortable to open the door now runs through the city streets. The irony is painful: earlier in the Song, she searched and found him quickly. Now she searches and finds only silence.

Thomas Gataker observed that the pain of regret in marriage is sharper than almost any other suffering. "To know that you had love within your grasp and let it slip through lazy fingers — that is a wound that goes deep." But Gataker also offered hope: the Song does not end here. Reconciliation is coming. The silence is not permanent. But the path to reunion is harder now than it would have been if the door had been opened the first time.

"The watchmen that went about the city found me, they smote me, they wounded me." The bride's search leads her into danger. She is struck and wounded by the very people who should protect her. When marriages are in crisis, well-meaning outsiders sometimes make things worse — advice that misses the mark, judgments that add shame, counsel that increases the distance rather than closing it. Not all help is helpful. The Puritans urged couples to seek counsel only from the wise, the experienced, and the truly godly.

Tonight, this passage asks a direct question: Is your beloved knocking? Is there a bid for connection — emotional, physical, spiritual — that you have been putting off because it is inconvenient? Do not wait. Open the door. The myrrh is on the handle. The moment is now.

--- Reflection Prompts ---

Turn to each other and consider these questions.

Question 1: Has there been a recent moment when one of you reached out and the other was too comfortable, too tired, or too distracted to respond? Can you talk about it honestly tonight?

Question 2: The bride's delay turned a simple moment of connection into a painful ordeal. What patterns of delay or avoidance exist in your marriage that might be causing similar damage?

Question 3: How can you create a mutual commitment to answer each other's knocking — to make responsiveness a non-negotiable value in your marriage?

--- Prayer Focus ---

Tonight's prayer focus: Pray for attentiveness and responsiveness in your marriage. Ask God to keep your hearts awake even when your bodies are tired. Pray that you would never be too comfortable, too settled, or too preoccupied to open the door when your beloved knocks.

--- Closing ---

The door is before you tonight. Your beloved is near. Do not delay. Open with willing hands and a warm heart. The moment is brief, and love is worth the inconvenience. Goodnight, dear couple.
