PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"Create in Me a Clean Heart"
May 7, 2026

---

"Have mercy upon me, O God, according to thy lovingkindness: according unto the multitude of thy tender mercies blot out my transgressions. Wash me throughly from mine iniquity, and cleanse me from my sin. For I acknowledge my transgressions: and my sin is ever before me. Against thee, thee only, have I sinned, and done this evil in thy sight." -- Psalm 51:1-4

This psalm is the deepest and most thorough expression of repentance to be found anywhere in the Word of God. It was wrung from the heart of David after the prophet Nathan confronted him with the terrible sin he had committed against Uriah and Bathsheba. For nearly a year, the king had concealed his transgression, and during that long season of unconfessed sin, his bones waxed old through his roaring all the day long. The hand of the Lord was heavy upon him. His moisture was turned into the drought of summer. But when Nathan's parable pierced through the layers of self-deception and David cried out, "I have sinned against the Lord," then at last the dam broke, and the pent-up anguish of a convicted soul poured forth in these immortal words.

"Have mercy upon me, O God, according to thy lovingkindness." David does not begin his prayer by making excuses. He does not attempt to minimize his guilt or to shift the blame onto others. He does not plead his past faithfulness or his former services to the kingdom. He throws himself upon the bare mercy of God, and upon nothing else. This is always the mark of genuine repentance. The truly penitent soul has nothing to offer but its sin, nothing to plead but the mercy of God. It comes with empty hands and a broken heart, and it casts itself upon the lovingkindness of the Almighty as a drowning man casts himself upon the ocean.

And what a word is this, lovingkindness! It speaks of a mercy that is not cold or reluctant, but warm, tender, and overflowing. It is the mercy of a Father toward His erring child, the mercy that yearns over the wanderer and longs for his return. David knew that he had no claim upon the justice of God. Justice could only condemn him. But he appealed to something deeper than justice: the covenant lovingkindness of a God who had promised to be merciful to the thousandth generation of those who love Him.

"Wash me throughly from mine iniquity, and cleanse me from my sin." Observe the urgency and the thoroughness of this plea. Not a superficial rinsing, but a thorough washing. David felt the filthiness of his sin as a man feels the contamination of leprosy upon his flesh. Every part of him seemed defiled. His thoughts were polluted. His affections were disordered. His conscience was stained with guilt. And he knew that nothing short of a divine cleansing could make him whole again.

Thomas Watson, in his masterful treatise on repentance, identified several ingredients of true repentance, and every one of them is present in this psalm. There is sight of sin: "I acknowledge my transgressions." There is sorrow for sin: "My sin is ever before me." There is confession of sin: "Against thee, thee only, have I sinned." There is shame for sin, a turning from sin, and a hatred of sin that runs through every verse like a crimson thread. This is not the superficial regret of a man who is sorry he was caught. This is the deep, soul-rending grief of a man who has seen his sin as God sees it, and who is utterly undone by the sight.

"Against thee, thee only, have I sinned, and done this evil in thy sight." Now, David had certainly sinned against Bathsheba, against Uriah, against his own family, and against the nation of Israel. How then can he say, "Against thee only"? Because David understood, with a clarity that only the Holy Spirit can give, that all sin is ultimately and primarily an offense against God. Every transgression of God's law is a personal affront to the Lawgiver. Every act of rebellion is a declaration of war against the throne of heaven. Uriah may be dead and buried, but the sin against God remains, living and burning, until it is dealt with by the mercy of God alone.

This understanding is essential to genuine repentance. The man who is merely sorry for the consequences of his sin has not truly repented. The man who is sorry only because he has hurt other people, though his sorrow is commendable, has not yet reached the depths of repentance. True repentance is grief over the offense committed against God Himself. It is the cry of the prodigal son: "Father, I have sinned against heaven, and in thy sight." Until a man sees his sin primarily as a transgression against the holy, just, and good God who made him and redeemed him, he has not truly repented.

"Behold, thou desirest truth in the inward parts: and in the hidden part thou shalt make me to know wisdom." Here David acknowledges that God's requirement goes far deeper than outward conformity to a set of rules. God desires truth in the inward parts, in the secret chambers of the heart where no human eye can penetrate. It is possible to maintain an impeccable exterior while the inward parts are full of corruption. David had done precisely this for nearly a year, and he had discovered that the attempt to live a double life is the most exhausting and miserable occupation a man can undertake. God will not be mocked. He sees the heart. And He demands truth there, in the hidden depths, where only He and the individual soul have access.

But alongside this searching demand, there is a gracious promise: "In the hidden part thou shalt make me to know wisdom." God does not merely demand truth in the inward parts; He supplies it. He does not merely require a clean heart; He creates it. "Create in me a clean heart, O God; and renew a right spirit within me." The word "create" is the same word used in Genesis for the creation of the heavens and the earth. It is a work that only God can do. No man can create a clean heart within himself by his own effort, any more than he can create a universe by the word of his mouth. This is the sovereign work of the Holy Spirit, and it is the birthright of every penitent sinner who cries out to God for mercy.

Let us take heart, beloved. If the God who forgave David can forgive us, then no sin is too great for His mercy to cover, and no heart is too filthy for His grace to cleanse. The blood of Jesus Christ, God's Son, cleanseth us from all sin. Let us come boldly to the throne of grace, confessing our transgressions, and let us receive the mercy and the cleansing that the Lord so freely offers to every broken and contrite heart. Amen.
