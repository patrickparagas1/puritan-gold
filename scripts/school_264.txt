PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 4
"Of God's Eternal Decree"
The Westminster Standards — Lesson 4


Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we explore one of the most awe-inspiring and sometimes challenging doctrines in the Westminster Standards — God's eternal decree. The Westminster Confession addresses this subject in Chapter 3, titled "Of God's Eternal Decree," and it is a chapter that has provoked more discussion, debate, and devotion than perhaps any other in the entire Confession. We will examine what the Confession teaches, why it teaches it, and how these truths should affect our hearts and lives. Let us pray and begin.

Sovereign Lord, Thy decrees are from everlasting, and Thy counsels are established forever. Give us wisdom and humility as we study these deep truths. Help us to trust what we cannot fully comprehend, to worship what we cannot exhaustively explain, and to rest in Thy sovereign goodness even when Thy ways are past finding out. In Christ's name, Amen.


PART ONE: ENTERING DEEP WATERS

Before we open Chapter 3 of the Confession, let us acknowledge that we are entering deep theological waters. The doctrine of God's eternal decree — which encompasses the doctrines of predestination, election, and reprobation — has been debated by Christians for centuries. It has divided theologians, stirred controversies, and tested the faith of many believers. But it is a doctrine that the Bible teaches, that the Westminster divines affirmed, and that has brought immense comfort and strength to countless Christians throughout the ages.

The Apostle Paul, who taught this doctrine more extensively than any other biblical writer, recognized both its depth and its importance. After expounding the doctrine of God's sovereign election in Romans chapters 9 through 11, Paul burst into a doxology of wonder: "O the depth of the riches both of the wisdom and knowledge of God! how unsearchable are his judgments, and his ways past finding out! For who hath known the mind of the Lord? or who hath been his counsellor? Or who hath first given to him, and it shall be recompensed unto him again? For of him, and through him, and to him, are all things: to whom be glory for ever. Amen" (Romans 11:33-36, KJV).

Paul's response to the doctrine of God's decree was not frustration or fear but worship. And that is precisely the response the Westminster divines intended to cultivate in those who study this chapter. We are not meant merely to understand these truths (though understanding is essential). We are meant to worship the God who has decreed all things according to the counsel of His own will.

The great Puritan theologian John Owen wrote: "The decrees of God are not a subject for idle speculation but for humble adoration. They are the foundation of all our comfort and the spring of all our hope. Without the sovereign decree of God, we have no assurance of salvation, no certainty of perseverance, and no confidence that anything in our lives has ultimate meaning and purpose."

PART TWO: THE CONFESSION'S TEACHING — CHAPTER 3, SECTION 1

The Westminster Confession, Chapter 3, Section 1, provides the foundational statement on God's decree:

"God from all eternity did by the most wise and holy counsel of his own will, freely and unchangeably ordain whatsoever comes to pass; yet so as thereby neither is God the author of sin; nor is violence offered to the will of the creatures, nor is the liberty or contingency of second causes taken away, but rather established."

This is one of the most carefully worded statements in the entire Confession, and every clause matters. The divines labored over this sentence with extraordinary care, knowing that imprecise language could lead to serious theological error. Let us examine each clause in detail.

"God from all eternity did...ordain whatsoever comes to pass." Before the foundation of the world, before time itself began, before there were any creatures to observe or participate in events, God determined everything that would happen throughout the entire course of history. The word "ordain" is a strong word. It means to appoint, to establish, to fix with certainty. And the scope of God's ordaining is comprehensive: "whatsoever comes to pass" — not merely some things but all things. Nothing takes God by surprise. Nothing falls outside His sovereign plan. The rise and fall of empires, the flight of every sparrow, the decisions of kings and peasants, the details of your daily life, the number of hairs on your head — all of it was ordained by God from eternity.

The Scripture testifies to this truth with unmistakable clarity. The prophet Isaiah records God's own declaration of His sovereign decree: "Remember the former things of old: for I am God, and there is none else; I am God, and there is none like me, declaring the end from the beginning, and from ancient times the things that are not yet done, saying, My counsel shall stand, and I will do all my pleasure" (Isaiah 46:9-10, KJV). God declares the end from the beginning. He does not merely foresee what will happen; He determines what will happen. His counsel stands. His pleasure is accomplished.

The Psalmist affirmed the same truth: "The LORD of hosts hath sworn, saying, Surely as I have thought, so shall it come to pass; and as I have purposed, so shall it stand" (Isaiah 14:24, KJV). And again: "Our God is in the heavens: he hath done whatsoever he hath pleased" (Psalm 115:3, KJV). The book of Proverbs states: "The LORD hath made all things for himself: yea, even the wicked for the day of evil" (Proverbs 16:4, KJV). And the Apostle Paul declares that God "worketh all things after the counsel of his own will" (Ephesians 1:11, KJV).

"By the most wise and holy counsel of his own will." God's decrees are not arbitrary or capricious. They are not the random decisions of a cosmic dictator. They flow from His infinite wisdom and perfect holiness. God always decrees what is wisest and best, and His decrees are always consistent with His holy character. He never decrees anything foolish, unjust, or evil. The Puritan pastor Thomas Manton explained: "God's will is the rule of all righteousness. What He wills is right, not because some external standard of rightness is imposed upon Him, but because His will is the eternal fountain of all that is right and good."

"Freely and unchangeably." God was under no compulsion to decree as He did. No one advised Him. No one persuaded Him. No one forced His hand. He decreed freely, according to His own sovereign pleasure. "Who hath directed the Spirit of the LORD, or being his counsellor hath taught him? With whom took he counsel, and who instructed him, and taught him in the path of judgment, and taught him knowledge, and shewed to him the way of understanding?" (Isaiah 40:13-14, KJV). The answer to each of these questions is: no one. God's decrees are the product of His own sovereign will, uninfluenced by any external consideration.

And having decreed, God does not change His mind. His decrees are as eternal and unchangeable as He Himself is. "The counsel of the LORD standeth for ever, the thoughts of his heart to all generations" (Psalm 33:11, KJV). This is of immense comfort to the believer. The God who has decreed your salvation has not changed His mind. The God who has promised to bring you safely to glory will not revise His plan. His decree is unchangeable.

PART THREE: THE CRUCIAL QUALIFICATIONS

The second half of Section 1 provides three critical qualifications that protect the doctrine of God's decree from serious misunderstandings.

"Yet so as thereby neither is God the author of sin." This is the first and most important qualification. While God ordains all that comes to pass — including events that involve sin — He is not the cause of evil. God does not tempt anyone to sin. He does not compel anyone to sin. He does not approve of sin or take pleasure in it. "God cannot be tempted with evil, neither tempteth he any man" (James 1:13, KJV). Sin is real, and sinners are genuinely responsible for their sinful choices.

How can God ordain all things, including events that involve sin, without being the author of sin? This is one of the deep mysteries of theology, and the Confession does not attempt to explain the mechanism fully. It simply affirms both truths: God is sovereign, and God is holy. The Bible holds these truths together without contradiction. Consider the crucifixion of Christ — the most momentous event in history. Peter, preaching on the day of Pentecost, declared: "Him, being delivered by the determinate counsel and foreknowledge of God, ye have taken, and by wicked hands have crucified and slain" (Acts 2:23, KJV). The crucifixion was accomplished by "the determinate counsel and foreknowledge of God" — it was God's eternal decree. And yet it was also accomplished by "wicked hands" — the sinful actions of sinful men. God ordained it; men did it wickedly. God is sovereign; men are responsible.

The early church in Jerusalem, praying after the release of Peter and John, expressed this same paradox: "For of a truth against thy holy child Jesus, whom thou hast anointed, both Herod, and Pontius Pilate, with the Gentiles, and the people of Israel, were gathered together, for to do whatsoever thy hand and thy counsel determined before to be done" (Acts 4:27-28, KJV). Herod, Pilate, the Gentiles, and the people of Israel — they all acted freely, according to their own sinful wills. And yet they did "whatsoever thy hand and thy counsel determined before to be done." God's decree and human responsibility are both real.

The great Reformed theologian John Calvin addressed this mystery with characteristic care. Calvin taught that God relates to sin not as its efficient cause (He does not make people sin) but through His permission and governance. God permits creatures to sin according to their own corrupted natures, and He governs the consequences of their sin to serve His own wise and holy purposes. But God Himself is never tainted by the sin He permits. As James says, "Let no man say when he is tempted, I am tempted of God: for God cannot be tempted with evil, neither tempteth he any man: but every man is tempted, when he is drawn away of his own lust, and enticed" (James 1:13-14, KJV).

"Nor is violence offered to the will of the creatures." This is the second qualification. God's decree does not turn human beings into robots or puppets. People make real choices, and those choices are genuinely their own. When you choose to believe the gospel, that is a real choice — not a programmed response. When a sinner chooses to reject Christ, that is a real choice — and a culpable one. God's sovereign decree does not destroy human agency. It upholds it.

The Westminster divines were careful to distinguish their position from both fatalism and determinism. Fatalism teaches that events happen by blind, impersonal necessity, without purpose or meaning. The Westminster doctrine teaches that events happen according to the wise and holy decree of a personal, loving God — which is an entirely different thing. Determinism, in its mechanistic form, teaches that human choices are merely the product of physical causes — chemical reactions in the brain, environmental conditioning, genetic programming. The Westminster doctrine teaches that human choices are real, meaningful, morally significant acts for which human beings are accountable before God.

The Puritan theologian William Perkins, whose works profoundly influenced the Westminster divines, wrote: "God doth not force the wills of men, as a man doth force an ass to go where he would not go. God moves the wills of men sweetly and powerfully, according to their own natures, so that they freely and willingly do what God hath decreed they shall do." This is the Reformed understanding of compatibilism — the conviction that God's sovereignty and human freedom are not contradictory but complementary. God's decree works through human choices, not against them.

"Nor is the liberty or contingency of second causes taken away, but rather established." This third qualification addresses the role of secondary causes in God's plan. God does not usually accomplish His purposes through direct, miraculous intervention. Instead, He works through the ordinary processes of nature, the decisions of human beings, and the complex web of cause and effect that constitutes the created order. These secondary causes are real and operative. Farmers plant seeds and harvest crops. Doctors prescribe medicines and patients recover. Teachers instruct students and learning occurs. These secondary causes are not illusions. They are the means by which God ordinarily carries out His eternal decree.

Indeed, the Confession says that God's decree does not take away the liberty or contingency of secondary causes but rather "establishes" them. God's sovereignty is not opposed to secondary causes; it is the ground of their reality. The regularity of nature, the reliability of cause and effect, the freedom of human choice — all of these exist because God has decreed them and sustains them. Without God's sustaining decree, there would be no order, no causation, no freedom — only chaos.

The story of Joseph in the book of Genesis provides a vivid illustration of this principle. Joseph's brothers sold him into slavery out of jealousy and hatred. Potiphar's wife falsely accused him out of wounded pride. These were real, sinful human actions carried out by real, morally responsible human agents. Yet at the end of the story, Joseph said to his brothers, "But as for you, ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save much people alive" (Genesis 50:20, KJV). The brothers' evil intention and God's good intention coexisted in the same events. The secondary causes — human choices — were real and responsible. But God's decree — His "meaning" or purpose in those events — was also real and sovereign. God's decree worked through the secondary causes, not by destroying them but by governing them toward His appointed end.

PART FOUR: THE DECREE OF ELECTION — SECTIONS 3 THROUGH 7

Having established the general doctrine of God's decree in Section 1, the Confession turns in Sections 3 through 7 to the specific decree of election — God's eternal choice of certain individuals for salvation.

Section 3 states: "By the decree of God, for the manifestation of his glory, some men and angels are predestinated unto everlasting life, and others foreordained to everlasting death."

Section 5 elaborates on election: "Those of mankind that are predestinated unto life, God, before the foundation of the world was laid, according to his eternal and immutable purpose, and the secret counsel and good pleasure of his will, hath chosen in Christ unto everlasting glory, out of his mere free grace and love, without any foresight of faith or good works, or perseverance in either of them, or any other thing in the creature, as conditions, or causes moving him thereunto; and all to the praise of his glorious grace."

The Shorter Catechism presents this doctrine more concisely. Question 20 asks, "Did God leave all mankind to perish in the estate of sin and misery?" and answers, "God, having out of his mere good pleasure, from all eternity, elected some to everlasting life, did enter into a covenant of grace to deliver them out of the estate of sin and misery, and to bring them into an estate of salvation by a Redeemer."

Several truths stand out from these statements. First, election is God's act, not man's. It is God who chooses, not man who chooses God. The Lord Jesus Himself declared, "Ye have not chosen me, but I have chosen you" (John 15:16, KJV). And again, "No man can come to me, except the Father which hath sent me draw him" (John 6:44, KJV).

Second, election is from eternity. It was not a decision God made in time, in response to anything man did. It was made "before the foundation of the world" (Ephesians 1:4, KJV). Before you existed, before the world existed, God had already chosen you for salvation — if you are indeed His elect.

Third, election is unconditional. It is not based on any foreseen faith, good works, or merit in the creature. The Confession explicitly rejects the Arminian view that God chose those whom He foresaw would believe. Rather, God's election is "out of his mere free grace and love" — it is entirely gracious, entirely undeserved, and entirely a product of God's sovereign will. The Apostle Paul makes this point with unmistakable clarity in his letter to the Romans: "For the children being not yet born, neither having done any good or evil, that the purpose of God according to election might stand, not of works, but of him that calleth; it was said unto her, The elder shall serve the younger. As it is written, Jacob have I loved, but Esau have I hated" (Romans 9:11-13, KJV). Before Jacob and Esau were born, before they had done anything good or bad, God chose Jacob — not because Jacob was better but because God's sovereign purpose of election might stand.

Fourth, election is "in Christ." God did not choose us apart from Christ. He chose us in Christ — united to Christ, redeemed by Christ, and destined to be conformed to the image of Christ. "Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who hath blessed us with all spiritual blessings in heavenly places in Christ: according as he hath chosen us in him before the foundation of the world, that we should be holy and without blame before him in love" (Ephesians 1:3-4, KJV).

Fifth, the purpose of election is "the praise of his glorious grace." Election exists to display the glory of God's grace — His unmerited, undeserved, free favor toward sinners who deserve nothing but judgment. Every soul that is saved is a trophy of grace, a living demonstration of God's mercy, and an occasion for eternal praise.

PART FIVE: THE DIFFICULT DOCTRINE OF REPROBATION — SECTION 7

The Confession also addresses the difficult doctrine of reprobation — the decree concerning those who are not elected to salvation. Section 7 states:

"The rest of mankind, God was pleased, according to the unsearchable counsel of his own will, whereby he extendeth or withholdeth mercy as he pleaseth, for the glory of his sovereign power over his creatures, to pass by, and to ordain them to dishonour and wrath for their sin, to the praise of his glorious justice."

This is undoubtedly one of the most challenging statements in the Confession. It teaches that those not elected to salvation are "passed by" and "ordained to dishonour and wrath" — but note the crucial phrase: "for their sin." Reprobation is not the mirror image of election. In election, God actively chooses sinners and works in them to bring them to faith and salvation. In reprobation, God passes by sinners and leaves them in the sin they have freely chosen. The elect are saved by grace; the reprobate are condemned by justice. Both outcomes display the glory of God — His grace in the salvation of the elect and His justice in the condemnation of the wicked.

The Apostle Paul addresses this very question in Romans 9: "What shall we say then? Is there unrighteousness with God? God forbid. For he saith to Moses, I will have mercy on whom I will have mercy, and I will have compassion on whom I will have compassion. So then it is not of him that willeth, nor of him that runneth, but of God that sheweth mercy" (Romans 9:14-16, KJV). Paul anticipates the objection that God's sovereign election is unfair and answers it with the declaration of God's sovereign freedom to show mercy as He pleases. He then continues: "Thou wilt say then unto me, Why doth he yet find fault? For who hath resisted his will? Nay but, O man, who art thou that repliest against God? Shall the thing formed say to him that formed it, Why hast thou made me thus?" (Romans 9:19-20, KJV).

The Puritan pastor Jeremiah Burroughs, known for his gentle and pastoral spirit, addressed the difficulty of this doctrine with characteristic wisdom: "When we come to the deep things of God, we must remember that we are creatures standing before the infinite Creator. We do not have the right to demand explanations from God, but we do have the privilege of trusting His character. If God is infinitely wise, then His decrees are wise. If God is infinitely just, then His judgments are just. If God is infinitely good, then His ways are good — even when they are beyond our understanding."

PART SIX: THE DECREE AND THE KNOWLEDGE OF GOD — SECTION 2

Before addressing election specifically, the Confession makes an important statement in Section 2 about the relationship between God's decree and His knowledge:

"Although God knows whatsoever may or can come to pass, upon all supposed conditions; yet hath he not decreed any thing because he foresaw it as future, or as that which would come to pass, upon such conditions."

This section addresses a crucial distinction that the divines wished to make absolutely clear. God possesses what theologians call "middle knowledge" — knowledge of all possible scenarios, all counterfactual situations, all "what if" hypotheticals. God knows what every creature would do in every conceivable circumstance. His knowledge is infinite and comprehensive. But — and this is the key point — God did not decree things because He foresaw them. He did not look ahead, see what would happen, and then simply ratify what He saw. His decree is the cause of events, not a response to them.

This distinction is critical because it addresses the fundamental difference between the Reformed and Arminian understandings of election. The Arminian view, sometimes called the "prescient" view, teaches that God looked ahead through time, foresaw who would believe and who would not, and then "elected" those whom He foresaw would believe. On this view, election is essentially God's recognition of human faith — it is the creature's choice that determines God's decree. The Westminster Confession explicitly rejects this view. God's decree is not based on foresight of human actions. It is based on "the good pleasure of his will" (Ephesians 1:5).

The Puritan theologian William Ames, whose Marrow of Theology was a standard textbook in the Puritan academies, explained this point with clarity: "If God's decree were grounded upon His foresight of our faith, then our faith would be the cause of God's decree, and God would be dependent upon the creature for His eternal counsels. But this is absurd and contrary to Scripture, which everywhere ascribes election to God's mere grace and sovereign will, not to any condition foreseen in the creature."

PART SEVEN: THE HISTORICAL CONTEXT — THE ARMINIAN CONTROVERSY

The Westminster Confession's teaching on God's eternal decree must be understood in its historical context. The divines were not writing in a vacuum. They were responding to a major theological controversy that had shaken the Reformed world: the Arminian controversy.

Jacobus Arminius (1560-1609) was a Dutch theologian who had been trained in the Reformed tradition at the University of Leiden and at Geneva under Theodore Beza, Calvin's successor. But Arminius came to question several key tenets of Reformed theology, particularly the doctrines of unconditional election and irresistible grace. Arminius taught that God's election was conditional — based on God's foreknowledge of who would believe — and that God's grace could be resisted by the human will.

After Arminius's death, his followers published the Remonstrance of 1610, which articulated their positions in five points: conditional election, universal atonement, total depravity (but with the ability to respond to grace), resistible grace, and the possibility of falling from grace. These teachings provoked a massive theological response. The Synod of Dort, held in the Netherlands from 1618 to 1619, examined the Arminian positions and responded with what have become known as the five points of Calvinism, summarized by the acronym TULIP: Total Depravity, Unconditional Election, Limited (or Definite) Atonement, Irresistible (or Effectual) Grace, and Perseverance of the Saints.

The Westminster Assembly convened just twenty-five years after the Synod of Dort, and the influence of Dort on the Assembly was significant. Many of the Westminster divines were deeply familiar with the Arminian controversy and were committed to articulating a thoroughly Reformed doctrine of God's decree. The language of Chapter 3 — particularly the explicit rejection of foreseen faith as the ground of election — was crafted with the Arminian position in view.

The Puritan theologian John Owen, who was closely associated with the Westminster Assembly and whose works represent the finest flowering of Puritan theology, wrote extensively against Arminianism. In his treatise A Display of Arminianism, published in 1643 — the very year the Assembly began — Owen argued that Arminian theology undermined the sovereignty of God, the efficacy of grace, and the security of the believer. Owen contended that if election is conditional, then salvation ultimately depends on the creature rather than the Creator, and the praise of salvation belongs in part to man rather than entirely to God.

The English Puritans followed the Arminian controversy with great interest and alarm. William Twisse, who would later serve as the prolocutor of the Westminster Assembly, wrote extensively against Arminianism in works such as The Riches of God's Love and A Discovery of D. Jackson's Vanity. The controversy was not merely academic; it struck at the very heart of the gospel. If salvation ultimately depends on the human will rather than God's sovereign grace, then the glory of salvation belongs in part to man, and the security of salvation is always in doubt. The Westminster divines were determined to craft a confession that articulated the Reformed doctrine of God's decree with precision, clarity, and biblical fidelity.

It should also be noted that the Arminian controversy was not merely a Protestant debate. The questions raised by Arminius — about the relationship between divine sovereignty and human freedom, about the basis of election, and about the extent of the atonement — were questions that had been debated within the Christian church since at least the fifth century, when Augustine of Hippo and Pelagius clashed over the doctrines of grace. Augustine taught that salvation was entirely of God's grace, that fallen man could not save himself, and that God's election was unconditional and irrevocable. Pelagius taught that human beings had the natural ability to obey God and that grace was merely an aid, not a necessity. The Council of Ephesus in 431 AD condemned Pelagianism, and the Second Council of Orange in 529 AD condemned semi-Pelagianism. The Westminster divines stood squarely in the Augustinian tradition, and they saw Arminianism as a subtle form of the semi-Pelagian error that the church had already condemned.

PART EIGHT: THE PASTORAL COMFORT OF THE DECREE

While the doctrine of God's eternal decree is often presented as a source of controversy, the Westminster divines understood it primarily as a source of comfort. Consider the implications.

If God has chosen you from eternity, then your salvation does not depend on the strength of your faith, the quality of your obedience, or the consistency of your perseverance. It depends on the unchangeable purpose of God. "The foundation of God standeth sure, having this seal, The Lord knoweth them that are his" (2 Timothy 2:19, KJV).

If God has decreed your salvation, then nothing can separate you from His love. The Apostle Paul's triumphant declaration in Romans 8 is built entirely upon the doctrine of God's decree: "For whom he did foreknow, he also did predestinate to be conformed to the image of his Son...Moreover whom he did predestinate, them he also called: and whom he called, them he also justified: and whom he justified, them he also glorified...Who shall separate us from the love of Christ?...For I am persuaded, that neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor principalities, nor powers, nor things present, nor things to come, nor height, nor depth, nor any other creature, shall be able to separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord" (Romans 8:29-30, 35, 38-39, KJV).

The chain of salvation — foreknowledge, predestination, calling, justification, glorification — is unbreakable. Not a single link can fail. Every person whom God has predestinated will be called. Every person who is called will be justified. Every person who is justified will be glorified. This is the golden chain of redemption, and it is forged by the eternal decree of God.

The Puritan pastor Samuel Rutherford, writing from his prison cell in Aberdeen, found deep comfort in the doctrine of election. In one of his famous letters, he wrote: "I am persuaded that the decrees of God are the Christian's pillow. When I lay my head upon the decree of God, I can sleep in peace, knowing that He who hath begun a good work in me will perform it until the day of Jesus Christ."

Thomas Watson, in his exposition of the Shorter Catechism, wrote: "Election is the golden chain that holds the Christian fast to God. If God hath chosen thee, thou art safe forever. Though the devil rage, though the world frown, though thy own heart condemn thee — if God hath chosen thee, nothing shall pluck thee out of His hand."

Consider also the comfort this doctrine brings in the face of death. The believer who knows that God has decreed his salvation from eternity can face death with confidence, knowing that death itself is part of God's wise and loving plan. The Puritans were remarkable in their ability to die well, and the doctrine of God's sovereign decree was central to their dying confidence. When the great Puritan preacher John Knox lay on his deathbed in 1572, he was asked whether he had any doubts about his salvation. Knox replied, "I have no thought of doubt. My confidence is in the eternal decree of God, who hath chosen me in Christ before the foundation of the world. How can that decree fail which was made by the unchangeable God?"

The Puritan pastor Richard Baxter, reflecting on the comfort of God's decree for dying believers, wrote: "When thou comest to die, it will be thy comfort to know that thy salvation was not left to the changeable will of man but was secured by the unchangeable decree of God. His purpose standeth sure. His covenant faileth not. His love altereth not. And therefore thy soul is safe, though thy body fail."

Let us also observe that the doctrine of God's decree brings comfort in the face of persecution and opposition. The early church endured fierce persecution under the Roman Empire. The Puritans themselves suffered greatly for their faith — silenced from pulpits, imprisoned in cells, driven from their homeland. What sustained them was not a vague hope that things might work out but a settled conviction that God had decreed all things according to His wise and holy purpose. The Lord Jesus told His persecuted disciples, "Fear not them which kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul: but rather fear him which is able to destroy both soul and body in hell. Are not two sparrows sold for a farthing? and one of them shall not fall on the ground without your Father. But the very hairs of your head are all numbered. Fear ye not therefore, ye are of more value than many sparrows" (Matthew 10:28-31, KJV). If not a sparrow falls without the Father's decree, then surely no harm can befall His children that is not part of His eternal plan.

PART NINE: THE DECREE AND THE SHORTER CATECHISM

The Shorter Catechism presents the doctrine of God's decree in a form that is more accessible for younger students, yet no less profound. Question 7 asks, "What are the decrees of God?" and answers, "The decrees of God are his eternal purpose, according to the counsel of his will, whereby, for his own glory, he hath foreordained whatsoever comes to pass."

Notice the phrase "for his own glory." The ultimate purpose of all God's decrees is the display of His glory — His wisdom, power, justice, and mercy revealed for the wonder and worship of His creatures. God did not decree all things for the benefit of the creature alone but for the manifestation of His own perfections. This is not divine selfishness — it is divine love, for the greatest gift God can give His creatures is the revelation of Himself. When we see God's glory, we experience the supreme joy for which we were created. "In thy presence is fulness of joy; at thy right hand there are pleasures for evermore" (Psalm 16:11, KJV).

Question 8 asks, "How doth God execute his decrees?" and answers, "God executeth his decrees in the works of creation and providence." This connects the doctrine of God's decree to the doctrines of creation and providence that we will study in our next lesson. God's decree is eternal — it was formed before time. But God executes His decree in time — through the works of creation (bringing the world into existence) and providence (governing and directing all things toward their appointed end).

Thomas Watson, expounding this catechism answer, wrote with pastoral warmth: "The decrees of God are like a golden chain let down from heaven to earth. Every link is connected to the one above it and the one below it. The decree of God in eternity is the first link. The execution of that decree in creation and providence is the chain as it descends through time. And the consummation of all things in the final judgment is the last link. From first to last, it is one unbroken chain of divine purpose, and not a single link can be broken."

PART TEN: PRACTICAL APPLICATION

How should these truths affect our lives? Let me suggest several applications.

First, the doctrine of God's decree should produce humility. If your salvation is entirely of grace, entirely of God's free choice, then you have no grounds for boasting. "For who maketh thee to differ from another? and what hast thou that thou didst not receive? now if thou didst receive it, why dost thou glory, as if thou hadst not received it?" (1 Corinthians 4:7, KJV). The doctrine of election crushes human pride and magnifies divine grace.

Second, the doctrine of God's decree should produce gratitude. If God chose you before the foundation of the world — not because of anything you did or would do, but solely out of His free love — then what response is appropriate but overwhelming, ceaseless gratitude? Every morning you wake as a child of God, you wake as the object of an eternal, unconditional love that chose you before time began.

Third, the doctrine of God's decree should produce assurance. If your salvation depends on God's unchangeable purpose rather than your changeable heart, then your salvation is secure. You may stumble. You may struggle. You may doubt. But God's decree will not fail. He who has begun a good work in you will perform it until the day of Jesus Christ (Philippians 1:6).

Fourth, the doctrine of God's decree should produce evangelistic zeal, not apathy. Some people wrongly conclude that if God has already determined who will be saved, then evangelism is unnecessary. The Westminster divines would have been horrified by this conclusion. God has not only decreed the ends (the salvation of the elect) but also the means (the preaching of the gospel). The Apostle Paul, who taught the sovereignty of God more clearly than anyone, was also the greatest missionary the church has ever known. He labored tirelessly to preach the gospel to every creature, precisely because he knew that God had elect people scattered throughout the world who would be saved through the hearing of the Word. Paul wrote to Timothy, "Therefore I endure all things for the elect's sakes, that they may also obtain the salvation which is in Christ Jesus with eternal glory" (2 Timothy 2:10, KJV).

Fifth, the doctrine of God's decree should produce patience in suffering. If God has ordained all things — including your trials — according to His wise and holy will, then your suffering is not meaningless. It is part of God's eternal plan for your life. He has a purpose in every affliction, a lesson in every loss, and a glory that will be revealed in due time. "And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose" (Romans 8:28, KJV).

Sixth, the doctrine of God's decree should produce prayerfulness. Some might think that if God has already decreed all things, prayer is pointless. But the opposite is true. God has decreed not only the ends but also the means — and prayer is one of the chief means by which God accomplishes His purposes. God has decreed that certain blessings will come to His people through prayer, and therefore prayer is both meaningful and necessary. The Apostle James wrote, "Ye have not, because ye ask not" (James 4:2, KJV). The decree of God does not eliminate the need for prayer; it establishes it. We pray because God has decreed to work through our prayers to accomplish His purposes.
