PURITAN GOLD -- Family Devotional, Day 3
"Speaking God's Name with Reverence"
Thomas Watson — The Ten Commandments


Good evening, dear family. Welcome back to Puritan Gold. Tonight Thomas Watson guides us through the third commandment — the sacred duty of honoring God's name in our family's speech. Let us begin with prayer.

Let us pray together. Holy God, Your name is above every name. Forgive us for the times we have spoken Your name carelessly. Teach us tonight to hold Your name in the highest reverence, and help our family to honor You with every word we speak. In Christ's name, Amen.

Our Scripture reading this evening comes from Exodus 20:7. Let us hear the Word of the Lord:

"Thou shalt not take the name of the LORD thy God in vain; for the LORD will not hold him guiltless that taketh his name in vain."

That is Exodus 20:7. Let those words settle in your heart for a moment before we continue.


The third commandment brings the law of God into the realm of our speech, and Thomas Watson understood that speech is one of the most powerful forces in family life. Words build up or tear down. Words bless or curse. Words reveal the true condition of the heart. And of all the words a family speaks, none are more significant than the words we speak about God and to God.

Watson taught that the third commandment forbids not only the obvious sin of using God's name as a curse word, but a much wider range of offenses. We take God's name in vain whenever we speak of Him thoughtlessly, whenever we pray without sincerity, whenever we make promises in His name that we do not intend to keep, and whenever we claim to be His people while living in a way that dishonors Him. In short, the third commandment requires that everything associated with God's name be treated with the utmost reverence and care.

For families, Watson's teaching has profound implications. Consider the atmosphere of speech in your home. How do family members speak about God? Is He mentioned with awe and gratitude, or has familiarity bred a casual indifference? Do parents speak of God only when correcting children, so that God's name becomes associated with punishment rather than with love? Do we invoke God's name in trivial expressions — "Oh my God," "Good Lord" — without even realizing that we are handling holy things carelessly?

Watson would press deeper still. He would ask: Do we pray with sincerity or merely with routine? Family prayer can become so habitual that the words are spoken without any engagement of the heart. When we say "Dear Lord" at the beginning of a meal, do we truly mean it? When we pray at bedtime, are we speaking to the living God, or are we performing a ritual? Watson taught that formal, heartless prayer is a species of taking God's name in vain — it uses holy words without holy meaning.

But Watson also had a positive vision for the use of God's name in the family. He believed that God's name should be the sweetest word in the home. Parents should speak of God's attributes with wonder — His wisdom, His power, His mercy, His faithfulness. Children should grow up hearing God's name in contexts of love, gratitude, and praise. The dinner table should be a place where the family recounts God's goodness. Bedtime should be a time when God's name is the last word on every lip, spoken in prayer and thanksgiving.

Watson also connected the third commandment to our witness before the world. A family that bears God's name — a Christian family — is a living testimony. When the family's speech is marked by kindness, truth, and reverence for God, the watching world takes notice. When the family's speech is marked by gossip, harshness, and casual irreverence, the watching world concludes that Christianity makes no real difference in how people live.

There is also the matter of integrity. Watson taught that one form of taking God's name in vain is hypocrisy — professing to know God while living as though He does not exist. A family that goes to church on Sunday but lives by entirely different values the rest of the week is, in Watson's view, guilty of profaning the name of God. The third commandment calls us to consistency — to be the same people in private that we claim to be in public, and to let God's name be honored in every room of the house, not just in the sanctuary.

Tonight, let your family commit to making your home a place where God's name is hallowed. Let every word spoken about Him be a word of reverence. Let every prayer be a prayer of sincerity. Let every mention of His name carry the weight of glory that it deserves.


Let us pause and talk as a family. Here are tonight's discussion questions. Everyone gets a turn to share.

Question 1: Watson said we take God's name in vain not only by cursing but by speaking of Him carelessly or praying without sincerity. In what subtle ways might our family be guilty of this?

Question 2: How can we create a home atmosphere where God's name is spoken with reverence, wonder, and love rather than with casual indifference?

Question 3: What is the connection between how we speak God's name and how we live? How does our daily conduct either honor or dishonor the name of God?

Take a few minutes to talk about these questions together. Let everyone share, and listen to one another with patience and love.


Now, here is our memory verse for today. Let us say it together:

"Thou shalt not take the name of the LORD thy God in vain; for the LORD will not hold him guiltless that taketh his name in vain." -- Exodus 20:7

Try to say it out loud three times right now. Then, before bed tonight, see if you can say it from memory.


Let us pray together. Holy Father, we hallow Your name tonight. Forgive us for every careless word, every empty prayer, every moment of irreverence. Fill our home with a spirit of awe and gratitude, so that Your name is always spoken with the love and honor it deserves. In Jesus' name, Amen.

Sleep well tonight, knowing that the God whose name you bear watches over you with tender care.
