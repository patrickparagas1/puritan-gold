PURITAN GOLD -- Family Devotional, Day 29
"Using Time Wisely"
Richard Baxter — The Christian Directory


Good evening, dear family. Welcome to tonight's family devotional from Puritan Gold. Thank you for gathering together around God's Word. Let us quiet our hearts and prepare to listen to what the Lord has for us.

Let us begin with prayer. Heavenly Father, we thank You for this family. We thank You for bringing us safely through another day. Now we ask that You would open our eyes to see wonderful things in Your Word. Speak to each heart gathered here, from the oldest to the youngest. In Jesus' name we pray. Amen.

Tonight we open our Bibles to Ephesians 5:15-16. Listen carefully as I read our passage:

"See then that ye walk circumspectly, not as fools, but as wise, Redeeming the time, because the days are evil."

That is Ephesians 5:15-16. Let those words settle in your heart for a moment before we continue.


We come now to our final three days, guided by the great Richard Baxter. Baxter was one of the most prolific and practical Puritan writers. His Christian Directory is an enormous work of practical theology — a guide to how Christians should live in every area of life. Baxter was a pastor who cared deeply about the daily, practical walk of ordinary believers.

Today's Scripture from Paul's letter to the Ephesians is a call to wise living. 'See then that ye walk circumspectly, not as fools, but as wise, Redeeming the time, because the days are evil.'

The word 'circumspectly' means carefully, exactly, with precision. Paul says we should walk through life with our eyes open, paying attention, not stumbling along carelessly. The contrast is between fools and wise people. A fool lives without thought for consequences. A wise person considers every step.

The phrase 'redeeming the time' is particularly rich. The Greek word 'exagorazo' means to buy up, to seize an opportunity, to make the most of. The image is of a shrewd merchant at a market who sees a valuable item at a low price and immediately snaps it up before it is gone. Paul says we should treat time the same way — seizing every opportunity, making the most of every moment, because time is precious and limited.

Why? 'Because the days are evil.' We live in a world full of distractions, temptations, and pressures that conspire to waste our time. If we are not intentional, the urgent will crowd out the important. We will spend hours on trivial things and have no time left for what truly matters.

Psalm 90:12 gives us this prayer: 'So teach us to number our days, that we may apply our hearts unto wisdom.' Numbering our days means being aware that our time on earth is limited. This is not morbid — it is liberating. When you realize that every day is a gift, you become more intentional about how you use it.

James 4:14 reminds us how brief life is: 'For what is your life? It is even a vapour, that appeareth for a little time, and then vanisheth away.' Like a puff of steam or morning mist, our lives are here for a moment and then gone. This should not make us anxious but should make us purposeful.

Jesus modeled perfect time management. He was never rushed, never frantic, yet He accomplished everything the Father gave Him to do. In John 9:4, He said, 'I must work the works of him that sent me, while it is day: the night cometh, when no man can work.' Jesus was aware that His time was limited, and He used every moment purposefully.

Richard Baxter took this subject very seriously. He wrote that time is a talent given by God, and that God will call us to account for how we spend it. Baxter urged his readers to spend time as they would spend gold — wisely, purposefully, and with eternity in view.

Baxter offered very practical counsel about time management. He urged Christians to begin each day by considering what God would have them do. He counseled against idleness — not because rest is wrong (God commands rest) but because wasting time through laziness is a form of stealing from God. He distinguished between rest (which is necessary and good) and sloth (which is sinful and destructive).

Baxter also warned against the opposite extreme — busyness without purpose. Some people fill every moment with activity but accomplish nothing of eternal value. They are busy being busy. Baxter urged his readers to evaluate their activities not by how busy they make you but by how much they contribute to God's kingdom and the good of others.

Baxter suggested a simple test for any activity: will this matter in eternity? If yes, give it priority. If not, give it less time or eliminate it. This does not mean that only 'spiritual' activities matter. Loving your family, doing honest work, serving your community — these are all eternal investments. But endless scrolling, purposeless entertainment, and trivial pursuits that consume hours without producing anything of value — these are the time-wasters Baxter warned against.

For our youngest ones: time is like a gift box — once you open it and use it, you cannot get it back. So let us use our time for good things! For older children: think about how you spend your free time. Are you using it wisely, or do hours disappear without anything meaningful happening? For the whole family: let us ask God to help us be wise stewards of the time He gives us — making the most of each day for His glory.


Now let us hear from one of the great Puritan teachers of the faith.

Richard Baxter, in his work "The Christian Directory," wrote these words:

"Time is a talent given us by God, and He will call us to account for the spending of it. Spend it as you would spend gold — wisely, purposefully, and with eternity in view."

What Richard Baxter is reminding us is that the truths we have read tonight in Ephesians 5:15-16 are not new. They have been treasured by faithful Christians for centuries. And they are just as true and just as needed in our family today as they were hundreds of years ago. Let us take these words to heart and let them shape the way we live together as a family.


Now it is time for our family discussion. Here are some questions to talk about together. Take your time with each one.

Question 1: What does 'redeeming the time' mean? How is time like a treasure?

Question 2: What are some things that waste our time without us even realizing it?

Question 3: How did Jesus use His time on earth? What can we learn from His example?

Question 4: If you could add one hour to your day, how would you spend it? Does that answer reveal what you value most?

Take a few minutes to talk about these questions together. Let everyone share, and listen to one another with patience and love.


Now, here is our memory verse for today. Let us say it together:

"See then that ye walk circumspectly, not as fools, but as wise, Redeeming the time, because the days are evil." -- Ephesians 5:15-16

Try to say it out loud three times right now. Then, before bed tonight, see if you can say it from memory. Tomorrow morning, try again. The more we hide God's Word in our hearts, the more it will guide us when we need it most.


Here is a family activity you can do this week to help these truths take root:

Have each family member track how they spend one full day — writing down what they do each hour. At the end of the day, review together. Were there surprises? Were there blocks of time that could have been used more wisely? Make a family plan for 'redeeming the time' this week.


Let us close in prayer. Heavenly Father, thank You for gathering our family together in Your Word tonight. Thank You for the truth we have heard from Ephesians 5:15-16. Help us to live out what we have learned. Guard our hearts, guide our steps, and fill our home with Your love and peace. We pray in the name of Jesus Christ. Amen.

Goodnight, dear ones. Walk in wisdom tomorrow.
