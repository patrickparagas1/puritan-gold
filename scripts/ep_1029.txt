PURITAN GOLD -- Study Topic: The Book of Enoch, Part 9 of 10
"The Epistle of Enoch (1 Enoch 91-105)"
The Book of Enoch — Woes, blessings, and the final judgment

---

Welcome, dear friends, to our ninth study in this series on the Book of Enoch. Today we examine the final major section of the work — the Epistle of Enoch, contained in chapters 91 through 105. This section represents a dramatic shift in literary form from what we have encountered so far. Where the earlier sections presented visions and journeys, the Epistle is a work of moral exhortation — a letter from Enoch to his children and to future generations, calling the righteous to faithfulness and pronouncing woes upon the wicked.

Let us read from Ecclesiastes 12:13-14:

"Let us hear the conclusion of the whole matter: Fear God, and keep his commandments: for this is the whole duty of man. For God shall bring every work into judgment, with every secret thing, whether it be good, or whether it be evil."

--- Teaching ---

The Epistle of Enoch opens with an exhortation from Enoch to his son Methuselah and to all future generations. The tone is that of a father addressing his children — warm yet urgent, tender yet uncompromising. Enoch speaks as one who has seen the hidden things of God and now must communicate what he has learned to those who will come after him. The sense of intergenerational responsibility is palpable: the faith must be passed down, the truth must be preserved, the warning must be sounded.

This theme of passing the faith from one generation to the next is deeply biblical. Moses commanded Israel: "And these words, which I command thee this day, shall be in thine heart: And thou shalt teach them diligently unto thy children, and shalt talk of them when thou sittest in thine house, and when thou walkest by the way, and when thou liest down, and when thou risest up" (Deuteronomy 6:6-7). Paul wrote to Timothy: "And the things that thou hast heard of me among many witnesses, the same commit thou to faithful men, who shall be able to teach others also" (2 Timothy 2:2). The Puritans were passionate about this duty. Cotton Mather, in his writings on family religion, urged parents to make the instruction of their children a daily priority — not merely in the catechism but in the application of divine truth to every area of life.

The most famous passage within the Epistle is the Apocalypse of Weeks, found in chapters 93 and 91:12-17 (the text is somewhat disordered in the manuscripts). This remarkable prophecy divides the entirety of human history into ten "weeks" — periods of time that span from the creation to the final judgment. The first week begins with Enoch himself. The second week sees the growth of wickedness and the coming of the flood. The third week sees the calling of Abraham. The fourth week sees the giving of the law at Sinai. The fifth week sees the building of Solomon's temple. The sixth week sees the exile and the destruction of the temple. The seventh week sees a period of apostasy. The eighth week sees the triumph of the righteous. The ninth week sees the revelation of righteous judgment to all the world. The tenth week sees the final judgment and the beginning of eternity.

This schematic view of history, while highly stylized, reflects a profound theological conviction: history is not random but purposeful. It has a beginning, a middle, and an end, and it moves according to God's sovereign plan. The canonical Scriptures teach the same truth. God declared the end from the beginning (Isaiah 46:10). The Lamb was slain from the foundation of the world (Revelation 13:8). All of history is the unfolding of God's eternal decree.

The Epistle also contains a series of woes and blessings that bear a striking resemblance to the literary forms found throughout the canonical Scriptures. The woes are directed against the wealthy who oppress the poor, against those who build their houses with sin, against those who trust in their own righteousness, and against those who pervert the words of truth. Compare these with the woes pronounced by Isaiah: "Woe unto them that join house to house, that lay field to field" (Isaiah 5:8). Compare them with the woes of our Lord: "Woe unto you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites!" (Matthew 23:13). The literary form is the same, and the moral vision is consistent.

The blessings are directed toward the righteous who endure suffering with patience, who hold fast to the truth in the midst of persecution, and who trust in God's coming vindication. These blessings anticipate the Beatitudes of our Lord's Sermon on the Mount: "Blessed are they which are persecuted for righteousness' sake: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven" (Matthew 5:10). The Epistle of Enoch promises that the suffering of the righteous is temporary and their vindication is certain — a theme that pervades the entire New Testament.

One of the most powerful passages in the Epistle addresses the problem of theodicy — the question of why the righteous suffer while the wicked prosper. This question runs throughout the canonical wisdom literature, from Job to the Psalms to Ecclesiastes. The Epistle's answer is forthright: the present world is disordered by sin, but God will set all things right in the final judgment. The wicked who prosper now will be condemned then; the righteous who suffer now will be exalted then. "Fear not, ye righteous, when ye see the sinners growing strong and prospering in their ways: be not companions with them, but keep afar from their violence; for ye shall become companions of the hosts of heaven."

The Puritan pastor Richard Baxter addressed the same question in his great work The Saints' Everlasting Rest. Baxter argued that the sufferings of this present time are not worthy to be compared with the glory that shall be revealed. The rest that awaits the people of God is so immeasurably great that all present trials are rendered insignificant by comparison. The Epistle of Enoch, centuries before Baxter, makes essentially the same argument.

The Epistle also contains a remarkable passage on the fate of the righteous dead. Enoch assures his readers that the spirits of the righteous who have died are preserved in joy and peace. They are not annihilated; they are not forgotten. They rest in the presence of God, awaiting the final resurrection. This teaching is consonant with Paul's assurance to the Thessalonians: "But I would not have you to be ignorant, brethren, concerning them which are asleep, that ye sorrow not, even as others which have no hope" (1 Thessalonians 4:13).

The Epistle concludes with an exhortation to courage and faithfulness. The righteous are not to despair, even when the world seems utterly given over to wickedness. God sees all things. God knows all things. God will act in His appointed time. This is the message of the Epistle — and it is the message of the entire Bible: "Be thou faithful unto death, and I will give thee a crown of life" (Revelation 2:10).

--- Application ---

1. Take seriously your responsibility to pass the faith to the next generation. The Epistle of Enoch is a letter to children and grandchildren. Your highest duty as a parent, grandparent, or mentor is to transmit the truth of God to those who come after you.

2. Do not be dismayed by the prosperity of the wicked. Their success is temporary; their judgment is certain. God's justice may be delayed, but it will not be denied.

3. Hold fast to the truth even when it is unpopular. The Epistle pronounces woes upon those who "pervert the words of truth." In a world that increasingly calls good evil and evil good, the faithful must cling to God's Word with unwavering resolve.

4. Take comfort in the certainty of God's plan for history. The Apocalypse of Weeks reminds us that history is not random. God is working out His purposes through every age, and He will bring all things to their appointed end.

--- Closing ---

Faithful God, who hast ordered the ages and appointed the times, we trust in Thy sovereign plan. When the world seems dark and the wicked seem to prosper, remind us that Thou seest all and that Thy justice shall prevail. Give us courage to hold fast to the truth, to pass the faith to the next generation, and to wait with patience for the coming of our Lord. In the name of Jesus Christ, who is the same yesterday, today, and forever. Amen.

Until our final study on Enoch, beloved, stand firm. The end is sure, and the Judge is at the door. Grace and peace be with you all.
