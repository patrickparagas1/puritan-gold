Lesson 20: "Sharing Your Faith with Confidence and Grace"
Apologetics for Young Christians


Welcome to a new day of learning at the Puritan Academy. The Puritans believed that all truth is God's truth, and that the pursuit of knowledge is a form of worship. Let us worship Him through our studies today.

Heavenly Father, we dedicate this day of learning to You. We confess that apart from You we can understand nothing rightly. We have spent many lessons studying the defense of the faith — learning the arguments, examining the evidence, considering the objections. Now, as we come to this lesson, we ask You for something beyond knowledge. We ask for courage and compassion. We ask for the wisdom to know when to speak and when to listen, when to press forward and when to wait. Help us to share our faith not as those who look down upon the unbelieving, but as beggars telling other beggars where to find bread. Give us ears to listen before we speak, hearts to love before we argue, and the humility to remember that we ourselves were once in darkness before Your light found us. Remind us that we are not the Savior — we are the messengers. Our task is to be faithful, clear, and loving. The Holy Spirit's task is to open blind eyes, change hard hearts, and bring dead souls to life. In Christ's name. Amen.

Before we open our lesson books, let us sit quietly with a passage from God's Word.

Our Scripture for today is 1 Peter 3:15-16; Colossians 4:5-6; 2 Corinthians 5:14-15; 2 Timothy 2:24-25:

"But sanctify the Lord God in your hearts: and be ready always to give an answer to every man that asketh you a reason of the hope that is in you with meekness and fear: Having a good conscience; that, whereas they speak evil of you, as of evildoers, they may be ashamed that falsely accuse your good conversation in Christ. Walk in wisdom toward them that are without, redeeming the time. Let your speech be alway with grace, seasoned with salt, that ye may know how ye ought to answer every man. For the love of Christ constraineth us; because we thus judge, that if one died for all, then were all dead: And that he died for all, that they which live should not henceforth live unto themselves, but unto him which died for them, and rose again. And the servant of the Lord must not strive; but be gentle unto all men, apt to teach, patient, In meekness instructing those that oppose themselves; if God peradventure will give them repentance to the acknowledging of the truth."

These verses contain the entire philosophy of Christian witness. Peter commands us to be ready — prepared with answers, grounded in truth. But he immediately qualifies the manner in which we are to give those answers: with meekness and fear. Paul tells us to walk in wisdom toward outsiders, to let our speech be seasoned with grace and salt — grace that is kind and winsome, salt that preserves truth and adds flavor to our words. And the motivation behind all of it is not intellectual pride or the desire to win arguments, but the constraining love of Christ — the love that took Him to the cross, the love that refuses to let us live for ourselves any longer. You are about to go into the world as an ambassador of the King of kings. Let His love be your fuel, His truth be your foundation, and His Spirit be your guide.

As we prepare for today's lesson, remember that everything we study flows from this one fountain: the Word of God. Our lesson today is part of Apologetics for Young Christians, and it is titled: Sharing Your Faith with Confidence and Grace. Let us proceed with minds engaged and hearts open.


With our hearts prepared, let us move into the substance of today's lesson.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes in 1 Peter 3:15-16; Colossians 4:5-6; 2 Corinthians 5:14-15; 2 Timothy 2:24-25.

We have spent many lessons building a foundation for defending the Christian faith. We have examined arguments for God's existence, evidence for the reliability of the Bible, the case for the resurrection of Jesus Christ, responses to the problem of evil and suffering, the relationship between science and faith, the uniqueness of Christ as the only way to God, answers to moral objections, and the coherence and comprehensiveness of the Christian worldview. These are powerful tools, and they will serve you well throughout your life.

But tools are only as effective as the person using them. A hammer in the hands of a carpenter builds a house. A hammer in the hands of a vandal destroys one. In the same way, apologetic arguments in the hands of a wise, loving, humble Christian can remove obstacles to faith and open doors for the gospel. But the same arguments in the hands of an arrogant, combative, insensitive Christian can build walls, alienate people, and bring disrepute to the name of Christ. Today we learn how to use our tools wisely. We will discuss five principles of effective apologetic conversation, and then we will consider practical strategies for sharing your faith in the real situations you encounter every day.

The biblical foundation for this lesson rests upon several crucial texts that define both the content and the character of Christian witness.

First Peter 3:15-16 is the charter verse of Christian apologetics. Peter writes, "But sanctify the Lord God in your hearts: and be ready always to give an answer to every man that asketh you a reason of the hope that is in you with meekness and fear: Having a good conscience; that, whereas they speak evil of you, as of evildoers, they may be ashamed that falsely accuse your good conversation in Christ." Notice the structure of Peter's instruction. Before he tells us to give an answer, he tells us to sanctify the Lord God in our hearts. The foundation of effective witness is not intellectual preparation — it is a heart that is set apart for God, a life that is yielded to Christ's lordship. Out of that sanctified heart flows the readiness to give an answer. And that answer is to be given with meekness and fear — not arrogance and aggression, not condescension and contempt, but genuine humility before God and genuine respect for the person asking.

The word Peter uses for "answer" is the Greek word apologia, from which we derive the English word "apologetics." An apologia was a legal term — it was the defense that a person on trial would give before a court. Peter is telling Christians that they should be prepared to defend their faith — to give a reasoned, articulate, compelling account of what they believe and why they believe it. This is not optional. It is a command. Every Christian is called to be an apologist — not necessarily a professional philosopher or theologian, but a person who can explain and defend the hope that is within them.

But Peter's emphasis on meekness and fear is equally important. The word "meekness" does not mean weakness — it means strength under control. Moses was called the meekest man on earth (Numbers 12:3), and he was anything but weak. Meekness is the quality of a powerful horse brought under the control of a skilled rider. The meek apologist has strong arguments, deep convictions, and firm confidence — but all of it is held under the control of love, wisdom, and sensitivity to the person in front of him. The word "fear" here does not refer to being afraid of the questioner but to reverential awe before God — the awareness that you are handling holy things, speaking about the eternal destiny of souls, and representing the King of the universe.

Paul's instruction in Colossians 4:5-6 adds further depth: "Walk in wisdom toward them that are without, redeeming the time. Let your speech be alway with grace, seasoned with salt, that ye may know how ye ought to answer every man." Notice that Paul says "every man" — each individual person. He does not say, "Give the same answer to every man." He says, "Know how ye ought to answer every man." Different people need different approaches. The person who is intellectually curious about the existence of God needs a different approach than the person who is emotionally wounded by suffering. The person who has been hurt by the hypocrisy of the church needs a different response than the person who is philosophically committed to materialism. The person who is genuinely seeking needs a different conversation than the person who is merely looking for an argument.

Paul's metaphor of salt is instructive. Salt in the ancient world served two functions: it preserved food from decay, and it added flavor. Christian speech should preserve truth — it should never water down the gospel or compromise essential doctrines to avoid offending people. But it should also be flavorful — attractive, winsome, interesting, appealing. Truth without grace is harsh and repellent. Grace without truth is empty and sentimental. The effective apologist combines both.

Second Corinthians 5:14-15 reveals the motivation behind all Christian witness: "For the love of Christ constraineth us; because we thus judge, that if one died for all, then were all dead: And that he died for all, that they which live should not henceforth live unto themselves, but unto him which died for them, and rose again." The word "constraineth" means to press from all sides, to compel, to leave no escape. Paul is saying that the love of Christ so overwhelms him that he cannot keep silent — he is compelled, driven, constrained to share the gospel. This is the difference between apologetics as an intellectual hobby and apologetics as a passion born of love. When you truly understand what Christ has done for you — that He died for you when you were His enemy, that He bore your sins in His body on the tree, that He rose again to give you eternal life — you cannot help but want others to know this same Savior.

Second Timothy 2:24-25 describes the character of the faithful witness: "And the servant of the Lord must not strive; but be gentle unto all men, apt to teach, patient, In meekness instructing those that oppose themselves; if God peradventure will give them repentance to the acknowledging of the truth." The word "strive" means to quarrel or fight. Paul is telling Timothy — and through him, all Christians — that our goal is not to win arguments but to win people. We must be gentle, not aggressive. Apt to teach, not eager to criticize. Patient, not impatient with people who are slow to understand. And we must instruct in meekness — that same quality Peter emphasized — knowing that ultimately it is God who gives repentance and opens hearts to receive the truth.

The Lord Jesus Himself modeled this approach perfectly. Consider His conversation with the Samaritan woman at the well in John chapter four. Jesus did not begin with a theological lecture. He began by asking for a drink of water — a simple, human request that opened the door to conversation. He listened to her responses. He asked probing questions that revealed the deeper needs of her heart. He spoke with gentleness but also with penetrating honesty. He addressed her specific situation — her broken relationships, her spiritual thirst, her misunderstandings about worship — and He led her step by step from curiosity to conviction to faith. By the end of the conversation, she had been transformed from a skeptic into an evangelist, running to tell her entire town about the Messiah she had encountered.

Consider also Jesus' conversation with Nicodemus in John chapter three. Nicodemus was a Pharisee, a ruler of the Jews, an educated and sincere religious leader who came to Jesus by night with genuine questions. Jesus did not dismiss him or berate him for his ignorance. He engaged with him seriously and respectfully, but He also challenged his assumptions — insisting that being born again is necessary for entrance into the kingdom of God, even though this concept was confusing and troubling to Nicodemus. Jesus was patient, clear, and honest — and He used the conversation as an opportunity to deliver one of the most profound statements of the gospel in all of Scripture: "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life" (John 3:16).

And consider Jesus' encounter with Thomas after the resurrection in John 20:24-29. Thomas had doubted the reality of the resurrection — he demanded physical evidence before he would believe. Jesus did not rebuke Thomas for his doubts. He met Thomas on Thomas's own terms — inviting him to touch His wounds and see the evidence for himself. "Reach hither thy finger, and behold my hands; and reach hither thy hand, and thrust it into my side: and be not faithless, but believing" (John 20:27). Jesus took Thomas's doubts seriously and provided the evidence Thomas needed. And Thomas responded with one of the greatest confessions of faith in the New Testament: "My Lord and my God" (John 20:28).

In each of these conversations, Jesus demonstrated the principles we will study today: He listened. He asked questions. He was honest. He kept the main thing — the kingdom of God, the gospel, His own identity as the Messiah — at the center. He embodied what He professed. And He trusted in the power of the Holy Spirit to accomplish what only God can accomplish.

This biblical foundation is essential. Without it, everything else we study today would be like a house built on sand. But with it, we stand on solid rock.


Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life. Their writings on the art of spiritual conversation and gospel witness provide us with wisdom that is as relevant today as it was in the seventeenth century.

The first principle of effective apologetics is to listen before you speak.

One of the most common and most damaging mistakes Christians make in conversations about faith is jumping to answers before they understand the questions. We hear a friend say, "I don't believe in God," and we immediately launch into the cosmological argument or the teleological argument or some other pre-packaged response. But we have not paused to ask: Why does this person not believe in God? What experiences have shaped their view? What is the real concern behind their statement?

Every person you talk to has a unique story, unique concerns, unique wounds, and unique reasons for their doubts or objections. If you take the time to listen — really listen, with genuine interest and genuine care — you will be far more effective than if you launch into a rehearsed argument that may or may not address their actual concern.

The Puritan pastor Richard Baxter, born in 1615 in Rowton, Shropshire, England, wrote about the importance of understanding the person before you in his great work The Reformed Pastor. Baxter argued that effective ministry — whether preaching, counseling, or evangelism — requires a deep understanding of the person you are addressing. You must understand their specific concerns, their specific temptations, their specific objections, and their specific needs. A one-size-fits-all approach to apologetics is no more effective than a one-size-fits-all approach to medicine. The wise physician diagnoses before he prescribes. The wise apologist listens before he speaks. Baxter practiced this principle with extraordinary thoroughness — he personally visited every family in his congregation in Kidderminster, spending time with each household, listening to their concerns, answering their questions, and pointing them to Christ. The result was one of the most remarkable spiritual transformations of any community in English history.

Ask questions. Learn the art of the good question — the question that shows genuine interest, that invites the other person to share more, and that helps you understand what they really think and why. "What do you mean by that?" is one of the most powerful questions in apologetics. It clarifies terms, uncovers assumptions, and often reveals that the person's real objection is different from the one they initially stated. "Can you tell me more about why you think that?" shows respect for the other person's reasoning and gives you valuable information about how to respond. "What experiences have shaped your views?" acknowledges that beliefs are not merely intellectual but experiential — that people's views about God are often shaped as much by their experiences as by their arguments.

Many people who say, "I don't believe in God," are really saying, "I'm angry at God because of something painful that happened to me." The intellectual argument is a mask for an emotional wound. If you respond with a philosophical argument when what they need is compassion, you will miss the mark entirely. But if you listen carefully, you will discern the real issue and be able to respond with both truth and tenderness.

The book of Proverbs teaches this principle clearly: "He that answereth a matter before he heareth it, it is folly and shame unto him" (Proverbs 18:13). And James instructs, "Wherefore, my beloved brethren, let every man be swift to hear, slow to speak, slow to wrath" (James 1:19). Listening is not merely a communication technique. It is a biblical virtue — a way of honoring the person in front of you as someone made in the image of God, whose thoughts and experiences are worthy of your attention.

The Puritan pastor Thomas Brooks, born in 1608 in London, England, wrote about the art of spiritual conversation in Precious Remedies Against Satan's Devices. Brooks argued that the effective communicator of truth must first understand the "devices" — the specific strategies, arguments, and emotional states — that are keeping the other person from receiving the truth. Only after understanding these obstacles can the communicator apply the appropriate "remedy." Brooks's approach was not one-size-fits-all. It was deeply personal, deeply attentive, and deeply compassionate — a model for every apologetic conversation.

The second principle is to be honest about what you know and what you do not know.

You do not need to have an answer to every question. No one does. The history of human thought is full of questions that even the greatest minds have not been able to answer fully. It is perfectly acceptable — and indeed it is far more credible — to say, "That is a great question. I do not have a complete answer, but here is what I do know," or "Let me think about that and get back to you." Pretending to know everything undermines your credibility instantly. Honesty builds trust over time.

The Puritan theologian John Owen, born in 1616 in Stadham, Oxfordshire, England, wrote about the proper posture of the Christian mind in his work On the Reason of Faith. Owen argued that Christian faith is not blind — it is based on evidence, argument, and the testimony of the Holy Spirit. But Owen also acknowledged that our understanding is always partial, always growing, and always subject to correction and refinement. There are mysteries in the Christian faith that we do not fully comprehend — the Trinity, the relationship between divine sovereignty and human responsibility, the precise mechanisms of the atonement. Acknowledging these mysteries is not a weakness. It is an expression of intellectual humility before an infinite God whose thoughts are higher than our thoughts and whose ways are higher than our ways. As God Himself declares through the prophet Isaiah: "For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways, saith the LORD. For as the heavens are higher than the earth, so are my ways higher than your ways, and my thoughts than your thoughts" (Isaiah 55:8-9).

When you are asked a question you cannot answer, do not panic. Do not make something up. Do not change the subject. Simply be honest. Say, "I do not know the answer to that, but I would like to find out." Then follow up. Research the question. Consult books, pastors, and other mature Christians. And when you find an answer — or discover that the question has no easy answer — report back to your friend. This kind of intellectual honesty and genuine curiosity is deeply attractive and deeply persuasive. It shows that your faith is not fragile, not dependent on having every question answered, but robust enough to engage with difficult questions openly and honestly.

The third principle is to keep the main thing the main thing.

The main thing in Christian apologetics is not winning a debate. It is not proving that you are smarter than the other person. It is not scoring intellectual points. The main thing is introducing people to Jesus Christ. Apologetics is a servant of the gospel, not a substitute for it. Arguments can clear obstacles. They can remove intellectual barriers. They can address objections and answer questions. But arguments do not save souls. The gospel saves souls. And every apologetic conversation should eventually point to the gospel — to the cross, to the empty tomb, to the God who loved the world so much that He gave His only begotten Son, "that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life" (John 3:16).

The apostle Paul expressed this priority with characteristic directness: "For I determined not to know any thing among you, save Jesus Christ, and him crucified" (1 Corinthians 2:2). Paul was a brilliant philosopher, a trained rabbi, a skilled debater. He could argue with the best minds of his generation — and he did, in Athens, in Corinth, in Rome. But he subordinated all of his intellectual gifts to the proclamation of the gospel. Arguments are useful. The gospel is powerful. Never let your arguments replace your gospel witness.

The Puritan pastor and evangelist George Whitefield, born in 1714 in Gloucester, England, understood this principle with burning clarity. Whitefield was one of the most brilliant and persuasive communicators in the history of Christianity, capable of moving enormous crowds to tears with the power of his voice alone. But Whitefield's aim was never to impress people with his brilliance. It was always to bring them face to face with the crucified and risen Christ. Every sermon Whitefield preached, regardless of its specific topic, pointed to the cross. Whitefield understood that apologetics without the gospel is like a road without a destination — it may be interesting, but it leads nowhere.

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.


Let us now press deeper into the theology of faithful witness by examining the remaining principles and drawing from the broader Puritan tradition.

The fourth principle is to embody what you profess.

Your life is your most powerful argument. People are watching how you handle difficulty, how you treat others, how you respond to injustice, how you face fear, how you deal with failure, and how you handle success. If your life reflects the love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control of the Holy Spirit (Galatians 5:22-23), your words will carry enormous weight — because people can see that the gospel you profess has actually changed your life. If your life contradicts your message — if you talk about love but are cruel, if you talk about truth but are dishonest, if you talk about humility but are arrogant — then no amount of clever argumentation will compensate for the discrepancy.

The Puritan pastor Richard Sibbes, born in 1577 in Tostock, Suffolk, England, wrote about the power of a holy life as a testimony to the truth of the gospel. In his work The Bruised Reed and Smoking Flax, Sibbes argued that the gentleness, kindness, and compassion of Christ should be visibly reflected in the lives of those who follow Him. Sibbes wrote that a harsh, judgmental, self-righteous Christian is a contradiction in terms — a living argument against the very gospel he professes. But a Christian who embodies the gentleness of Christ — who is patient with the weak, compassionate toward the suffering, humble in the face of disagreement, and generous in the midst of conflict — is a living argument for the reality and power of the gospel. Such a life speaks louder than any syllogism. Sibbes observed that Christ Himself was the perfect example of this principle: He did not break the bruised reed or quench the smoking flax. He handled wounded, struggling, doubting souls with infinite tenderness. And His followers must do the same.

The apostle Peter confirms this principle: "Having your conversation honest among the Gentiles: that, whereas they speak against you as evildoers, they may by your good works, which they shall behold, glorify God in the day of visitation" (1 Peter 2:12). The word "conversation" in the King James Version means "manner of life." Peter is saying that the way you live is itself a form of evangelism — a visible testimony to the truth of the gospel that even hostile observers cannot ignore. Live in such a way that your life confirms your message.

The Puritan pastor John Flavel, born around 1627 in Bromsgrove, Worcestershire, England, developed this theme further in The Method of Grace. Flavel argued that the Christian who has genuinely experienced the grace of God in his own life possesses a kind of evidence that no amount of philosophical reasoning can produce or refute — the evidence of a changed heart, a transformed character, and a living relationship with the God who saved him. Flavel urged believers to examine their own lives regularly to ensure that their conduct did not contradict their confession. He wrote that the world watches Christians with a keen and critical eye, and that a single act of hypocrisy can undo years of faithful testimony. Conversely, a life of consistent, genuine, humble godliness can break through defenses that a thousand arguments could never penetrate. Flavel declared that the experience of the saints is itself a clear and convincing testimony to the truth of the gospel. Your life, lived faithfully and honestly before a watching world, is itself an argument for the reality of the God you worship.

The fifth principle is to trust the Holy Spirit.

Apologetics is not a purely human endeavor. You are not operating alone. The Holy Spirit is at work in every genuine conversation about faith — convicting of sin, illuminating truth, drawing hearts toward God, and breaking down the barriers that keep people from receiving the gospel. Jesus Himself promised this: "And when he is come, he will reprove the world of sin, and of righteousness, and of judgment" (John 16:8). The Holy Spirit is the ultimate apologist. He is the one who opens blind eyes, softens hard hearts, and brings dead souls to life. You are not the Savior. You are the messenger. Your job is to be faithful, clear, and loving. The Spirit's job is to do what only God can do — to regenerate the human heart and bring about the miracle of saving faith.

This truth takes enormous pressure off you. You do not have to be brilliant. You do not have to have perfect answers to every question. You do not have to win every argument. You have to be faithful. You have to be available. You have to be willing to speak the truth in love, even when it is uncomfortable. And then you have to trust God with the results.

The Puritan theologian John Owen wrote extensively about the work of the Holy Spirit in his magisterial work A Discourse Concerning the Holy Spirit, published in 1674. Owen demonstrated from Scripture that every aspect of the Christian life — from regeneration to sanctification, from illumination to assurance — is the work of the Holy Spirit. Owen argued that no human argument, however brilliant, can convert a soul apart from the sovereign work of the Spirit. Conversely, even a weak and imperfect presentation of the gospel, when attended by the power of the Spirit, can bring about the most profound and lasting conversion. This is both humbling and liberating. It is humbling because it reminds us that we are instruments, not the source of power. It is liberating because it frees us from the burden of thinking that the effectiveness of our witness depends on our eloquence or brilliance.

The Puritan pastor William Perkins, born in 1558 in Marston Jabbet, Warwickshire, England, wrote about the art of prophesying — preaching and teaching — in his work The Arte of Prophecying. Perkins argued that the effective communicator of truth must rely not on human wisdom or rhetorical skill but on the power of the Holy Spirit working through the Word of God. Perkins wrote that the preacher's task is to present the truth of Scripture plainly, clearly, and faithfully — and then to trust the Spirit to apply that truth to the hearts of the hearers. The same principle applies to every apologetic conversation. Present the truth with clarity and love. And trust the Spirit to do His work.

The Puritan pastor Jonathan Edwards, born in 1703 in East Windsor, Connecticut, experienced the power of the Holy Spirit in extraordinary ways during the Great Awakening — a period of widespread spiritual revival that swept through the American colonies in the 1730s and 1740s. Edwards observed that the conversions that occurred during the Awakening were not the result of clever arguments or emotional manipulation. They were the work of the Spirit of God, who opened blind eyes and changed hard hearts through the faithful preaching of the Word. Edwards wrote about these experiences in his work A Faithful Narrative of the Surprising Work of God, published in 1737. Edwards's observations confirm the principle that the effectiveness of our witness depends ultimately on the power of the Spirit, not on the skill of the messenger. Our job is to be faithful. God's job is to save.

Let us now consider additional theological dimensions of faithful witness that the Puritan tradition illuminates with particular clarity.

The Puritan pastor William Bridge, born in 1600 in Cambridgeshire, England, who served as one of the Dissenting Brethren at the Westminster Assembly, wrote about the importance of adapting the presentation of truth to the condition of the hearer. In his work A Lifting Up for the Downcast, Bridge demonstrated remarkable pastoral sensitivity in addressing different kinds of spiritual struggles. He did not apply the same remedy to every ailment. To the person struggling with doubt, he offered evidence and assurance. To the person crushed by guilt, he offered the comfort of the gospel. To the person tempted by worldliness, he offered the superior beauty of Christ. Bridge understood that effective communication requires not merely knowing the truth but knowing the person — and knowing how to bring the right truth to bear on the right need at the right time. This principle has profound implications for apologetic conversations. The wise apologist is not merely a walking encyclopedia of arguments. He is a spiritual physician who diagnoses before he prescribes — who listens, understands, and then applies the appropriate truth to the specific need.

The Puritan pastor Matthew Henry, born in 1662 in Flintshire, Wales, wrote about Jesus's method of engagement in his commentary on the Gospels. Henry observed that Jesus's conversations were always perfectly calibrated to the condition of the person He was addressing. With the proud, Jesus was challenging. With the broken, He was gentle. With the sincere seeker, He was patient and encouraging. With the dishonest, He was penetrating and exposing. Henry wrote that Jesus's example teaches us that effective witness requires not merely knowing the truth but knowing the person — and having the wisdom and sensitivity to bring the right truth to bear at the right moment.

The Puritan tradition also emphasized the vital role of prayer in apologetic witness. Effective apologetics is not merely a human activity — it is a spiritual activity that requires spiritual preparation. Before engaging in conversations about faith, pray for wisdom. Pray for the person you will be speaking with. Pray for the Holy Spirit to prepare their heart and to give you the right words at the right time. The Puritan pastor David Clarkson, born in 1622 in Bradford, Yorkshire, England, who served as co-pastor with John Owen at a congregation in Leadenhall Street, London, wrote about the necessity of prayer in all spiritual work. Clarkson argued that the Christian who attempts to do God's work without prayer is like a soldier who goes to battle without weapons — he may have courage, but he lacks the power to prevail. Prayer is the channel through which God's power flows into our efforts, and the apologist who prays faithfully will find that his words carry a weight and authority that no human eloquence can produce.

The Puritan pastor John Bunyan, imprisoned for twelve years for preaching the gospel without a license, demonstrated the relationship between prayer and effective witness in his own life. Bunyan was not a university-educated theologian. He was a tinker — a mender of pots and pans — from a humble background with no formal training. Yet his preaching and writing were so powerful that they moved thousands to faith and produced one of the greatest works of Christian literature ever written in The Pilgrim's Progress. The source of Bunyan's power was not his education or his eloquence. It was his deep, intimate, dependent relationship with God in prayer. Bunyan declared that prayer is a shield to the soul, a sacrifice to God, and a scourge to Satan. The apologist who prays is the apologist who prevails — not in his own strength, but in the strength of the God who answers prayer.

The Puritan pastor Thomas Goodwin, born in 1600 in Rollesby, Norfolk, England, wrote about God's patience and persistence in drawing sinners to Himself. Goodwin observed that God often works over long periods of time, gradually breaking down resistance, answering objections, softening hearts, and preparing souls for the moment of conversion. Goodwin wrote that the Christian evangelist must share in God's patience — not becoming discouraged when results are not immediate, but continuing to pray, to love, to speak truth, and to trust God's sovereign timing. "The Lord is not slack concerning his promise, as some men count slackness; but is longsuffering to us-ward, not willing that any should perish, but that all should come to repentance" (2 Peter 3:9).

The Puritan pastor Thomas Watson wrote in his Body of Divinity about the inseparable connection between knowing God and making God known. Watson argued that the person who truly knows God — who has experienced His grace, tasted His goodness, and been transformed by His love — will naturally and inevitably want to share that knowledge with others. True knowledge of God produces true zeal for God's glory. And true zeal for God's glory produces true evangelistic passion. Watson wrote that the knowledge of God is not merely a notional thing, but a thing of power. The apologist who truly knows God will speak about Him with a conviction, a warmth, and a reality that no amount of mere intellectual preparation can produce.


Before we move to our discussion questions, let us apply the theology of faithful witness to our daily lives through specific, practical strategies.

First, when someone raises an objection, resist the urge to get defensive. Defensiveness signals insecurity — it suggests that you are threatened by the question and not confident in your position. Instead, respond with curiosity and confidence. "That is an interesting point. Here is how I think about it..." or "I appreciate you raising that. Let me share my perspective..." These responses show that you take the question seriously, that you respect the person asking it, and that you are confident enough in your faith to engage with difficult questions openly.

Second, when conversations become heated, slow down. Lower your voice. Ask a question. Redirect the conversation from argument to dialogue. Show that you care more about the person than about winning. Remember the proverb: "A soft answer turneth away wrath: but grievous words stir up anger" (Proverbs 15:1). The temperature of a conversation is often more important than its content. If the other person feels respected, heard, and cared for, they will be far more receptive to your message — even if they disagree with it — than if they feel attacked, belittled, or dismissed.

Third, when you sense that the conversation has gone as far as it can for now, know when to stop. Not every conversation will end in conversion. Not every question will receive a satisfying answer in a single sitting. Plant seeds and trust God to give the growth. Paul wrote, "I have planted, Apollos watered; but God gave the increase. So then neither is he that planteth any thing, neither he that watereth; but God that giveth the increase" (1 Corinthians 3:6-7). Sometimes your role is to plant. Sometimes your role is to water. Sometimes your role is to harvest. You may never know which role you are playing in any given conversation. But every seed planted faithfully is a seed that the Spirit can bring to fruition in His own time and in His own way.

The Puritan pastor Jeremiah Burroughs, born in 1600 in Essex, England, wrote about the virtue of patience in spiritual matters. In his work The Rare Jewel of Christian Contentment, Burroughs argued that the Christian must learn to be content with God's timing — trusting that God's purposes are being accomplished even when we cannot see the results. This is especially important in evangelism and apologetics. We want to see immediate results. We want our conversations to produce instant conversions. But God often works slowly, gradually, and invisibly — preparing hearts over months and years for the moment when they will finally be ready to receive the gospel. Our job is to be faithful in every conversation and to trust God with the timing of the harvest.

Fourth, build genuine relationships with non-Christians. Apologetics is most effective not in formal debates but in the context of authentic, caring friendships. When a person knows that you genuinely care about them — not as a "project" to be converted but as a human being to be loved — they will be far more open to hearing about your faith. Eat meals together. Share experiences together. Be present in their lives during both joyful and difficult times. Let them see your faith lived out in the ordinary moments of everyday life. And when opportunities arise — as they inevitably will — to talk about matters of faith, those conversations will be rooted in a relationship of trust and mutual respect.

Richard Baxter emphasized this principle repeatedly. In The Reformed Pastor, Baxter argued that personal visitation, individual attention, and genuine care for people's lives were more effective instruments of evangelism than even the most brilliant sermons. Baxter practiced what he preached — he personally visited every family in his congregation, spending time with each one, listening to their concerns, answering their questions, and pointing them to Christ. The result was one of the most remarkable spiritual transformations of any community in English history. The town of Kidderminster, where Baxter served, was transformed from a spiritually indifferent community into a vibrant center of Christian faith and life. Baxter's example teaches us that the most powerful apologetic is not a brilliant argument delivered from a stage but a genuine friendship lived out in the ordinary rhythms of daily life.

Fifth, use your personal testimony as a powerful apologetic tool. Your own experience of God's grace — how He found you, saved you, changed you, and sustains you — is something no one can argue with. They can dispute your philosophical arguments. They can challenge your historical evidence. But they cannot deny your personal experience. The apostle Paul frequently used his own conversion story as an apologetic tool (Acts 22, Acts 26). The man born blind whom Jesus healed used the simplest of testimonies: "One thing I know, that, whereas I was blind, now I see" (John 9:25). Do not underestimate the power of your own story. When shared with humility and authenticity, it can be the most compelling argument you possess.

Sixth, do not neglect the power of hospitality and ordinary kindness. Some of the most effective apologetic work happens not in formal conversations about theology but in the informal, everyday expressions of love and service that characterize a Christian life. Invite people into your home. Share meals together. Help neighbors with practical needs. Be present during times of crisis. Show up for celebrations. Let people see your faith not as an abstract system of beliefs but as a living reality that shapes how you treat others, how you spend your time, and how you respond to the ordinary challenges of daily life.

The early church's explosive growth was fueled not only by the apostles' preaching but by the extraordinary love and generosity of ordinary Christians. The pagans exclaimed of the early believers, "See how they love one another!" The visibility of Christian love was itself a powerful apologetic — a demonstration that the gospel was not merely a set of ideas but a transforming power that produced genuine, visible, undeniable love between people who would otherwise have had no reason to care for one another.

Seventh, prepare yourself for the long haul. Conversion is rarely instantaneous. Most people who come to faith in Christ do so after a long process of questioning, searching, doubting, exploring, and gradually being drawn toward the truth. Your role in this process may be small — a single conversation, a single question answered, a single act of kindness — or it may extend over years of patient, faithful friendship. Either way, you are participating in a work that is ultimately God's work, not yours. The Lord Jesus told His disciples, "Herein is that saying true, One soweth, and another reapeth. I sent you to reap that whereon ye bestowed no labour: other men laboured, and ye are entered into their labours" (John 4:37-38). You stand in a long chain of faithful witnesses, and the results of your labors may not be visible until long after you have moved on.

Eighth, remember that the most important preparation for apologetic witness is not intellectual but spiritual. The knowledge you need is important. The arguments you have learned are valuable. The historical evidence you can cite is powerful. But none of these will be effective unless your own heart is right with God — unless you are walking in daily fellowship with Christ, growing in holiness, saturated in Scripture, and dependent on prayer. The apologist whose heart is cold, whose prayer life is dry, whose walk with God is shallow, will be a hollow vessel — technically competent, perhaps, but lacking the spiritual power that makes the difference between mere debate and genuine witness.

The Puritan pastor Samuel Rutherford, born in 1600 in Nisbet, Scotland, wrote from his exile in Aberdeen about the privilege and responsibility of being a witness for Christ in a hostile world. Rutherford's Letters overflow with a passion for Christ and a desire to make Him known to others, even at great personal cost. Rutherford declared that he would not exchange his Master's cross for all the world's crown. The cost of following Christ and bearing witness to Him may be high. But the privilege of being His ambassador — of carrying the message that can save souls from death and bring them into the eternal joy of knowing God — is infinitely greater than any sacrifice it may require.

Learning by doing was central to Puritan education. Here is today's hands-on activity.

With a family member or friend, practice a role-play conversation about faith. Have the other person play the role of a thoughtful, respectful non-Christian who raises one of the objections studied in this apologetics unit — the problem of evil, the reliability of the Bible, the exclusivity of Christ, the relationship between science and faith, or a moral objection to Christianity.

Practice responding using all five principles discussed today: listen first and ask questions before answering; be honest about what you know and do not know; keep the conversation focused on Christ and the gospel; be gentle and respectful in your tone and manner; and trust the Holy Spirit rather than relying on your own brilliance.

After the first role-play, spend ten minutes discussing what went well, what felt natural, what felt difficult, and what could be improved. Then switch roles or choose a different objection and repeat the exercise.

After completing both role-plays, write a one-page reflection addressing these questions: What was the hardest part of the conversation? Did you find yourself wanting to "win" rather than to love and serve the other person? Were you tempted to get defensive when your arguments were challenged? How did it feel to say "I don't know" when you did not have an answer? What would you do differently next time?

Materials needed: Printed list of common objections studied throughout the apologetics unit for reference, lined paper for reflection essay, Bible (KJV) for looking up relevant passages during the conversation, timer for structuring the role-play sessions (ten minutes per conversation, ten minutes for discussion), notebook for recording lessons learned and areas for improvement.

As you complete this activity, think about how it connects to what you studied in today's lesson. The goal is not just to do something, but to reinforce the truths you have been learning. The Puritans believed that education should engage the whole person: mind, heart, and hands.

Take your time with this activity. Quality matters more than speed. When you are finished, reflect on what you learned through the process of doing. Often we understand something far better after we have tried to put it into practice.


One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

The history of Christian apologetics stretches back to the earliest days of the church. Study the apologists of the second and third centuries — Justin Martyr, who addressed his First Apology to the Roman Emperor Antoninus Pius around 155 AD, defending Christians against charges of atheism and immorality; Tertullian, who wrote his Apologeticus around 197 AD, making a legal case for the toleration of Christianity; and Augustine of Hippo, whose City of God, written between 413 and 426 AD, provided a comprehensive defense of Christianity after the fall of Rome. Trace the apologetic tradition through the medieval period, where Thomas Aquinas developed his famous Five Ways of proving God's existence, through the Reformation, where Luther and Calvin defended Protestant theology against Roman Catholic objections, and into the Puritan era, where pastors like John Owen, Richard Baxter, and Stephen Charnock wrote extensive defenses of Christian truth. Examine how the challenges facing Christian apologists have changed from era to era — from pagan polytheism in the ancient world, to Islamic philosophy in the medieval period, to scientific materialism and moral relativism in the modern era — and how faithful Christians have always risen to meet those challenges with Scripture, reason, and love.

Literature Connection:

Read and analyze the apologetic writings of the great Puritans alongside other examples of persuasive writing. Study the rhetorical techniques used by Paul in his address to the Athenians on Mars Hill (Acts 17:22-31) — how he began by affirming what was true in their worship, quoted their own poets, and then moved from common ground to the distinctive claims of the gospel, culminating in the proclamation of the resurrection of Christ. Compare Paul's approach with the strategies recommended by Puritan writers such as William Perkins in The Arte of Prophecying and Richard Baxter in The Reformed Pastor. Study the art of persuasive writing — how to structure an argument, how to anticipate and address objections, how to appeal to both the intellect and the emotions, and how to write with clarity, force, and conviction. Write your own apologetic essay or letter addressed to an imaginary friend who has raised a specific objection to Christianity, practicing the principles discussed in today's lesson. Pay attention not only to the content of your argument but to the tone — making sure that your writing reflects the meekness, grace, and love that Peter and Paul commanded.

Science Connection:

Explore the relationship between scientific methodology and apologetic reasoning. Both science and apologetics rely on evidence, hypothesis, and logical argument. Both require intellectual humility — the willingness to follow the evidence wherever it leads, even when the conclusions are surprising or inconvenient. Study the lives of scientists who were also committed Christians and effective apologists for the faith — Blaise Pascal, born in 1623 in Clermont-Ferrand, France, who made groundbreaking contributions to mathematics and physics while writing his Pensees as a defense of Christianity; Robert Boyle, the father of modern chemistry, who funded apologetic lectures and Bible translations; and Isaac Newton, who wrote extensively on biblical prophecy and considered his theological writings to be at least as important as his scientific work. Discuss how these scientists understood the relationship between their scientific work and their Christian convictions, and what their example teaches us about engaging with scientific objections to Christianity today. Consider also how the scientific method itself — the belief that the universe is orderly, rational, and intelligible — depends upon assumptions that are best explained by the Christian worldview: that the universe was created by a rational God whose character guarantees the reliability of the natural order.

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.

Memory Verse:

"But sanctify the Lord God in your hearts: and be ready always to give an answer to every man that asketh you a reason of the hope that is in you with meekness and fear." -- 1 Peter 3:15

Read this verse aloud five times. Then close your eyes and try to recite it from memory. Write it out by hand on a card or in your journal. This verse is the charter of Christian apologetics — the single verse that defines the calling, the content, and the character of faithful witness. It should be engraved upon your heart for the rest of your life.

Notice the three elements of Peter's instruction. First, "sanctify the Lord God in your hearts" — set apart Christ as Lord in the depths of your being. This is the spiritual foundation. No amount of intellectual preparation can substitute for a heart that is wholly devoted to Christ. Second, "be ready always to give an answer" — be prepared, equipped, armed with knowledge and understanding. This is the intellectual preparation. Study the Scriptures. Learn the arguments. Examine the evidence. Be ready. Third, "with meekness and fear" — deliver your defense with gentleness, humility, and reverence. This is the character requirement. The manner of your witness is as important as the matter of your witness. These three elements — a sanctified heart, a prepared mind, and a humble spirit — are the threefold cord of effective Christian apologetics, and a threefold cord is not quickly broken (Ecclesiastes 4:12).

Additional Memory Verse:

"Let your speech be alway with grace, seasoned with salt, that ye may know how ye ought to answer every man." -- Colossians 4:6

Paul's image of speech "seasoned with salt" is vivid and practical. Salt in the ancient world was essential for two purposes: preservation and flavor. Your speech should preserve truth — never compromising essential doctrines to avoid offending people. But it should also add flavor — making the truth attractive, interesting, and appealing. Bland speech that avoids controversy is as useless as salt that has lost its savor. Harsh speech that drives people away is as harmful as salt poured into an open wound. The wise Christian learns to season his words with the right amount of salt — enough to preserve truth and add flavor, but not so much as to burn or repel. Jesus warned His disciples, "Salt is good: but if the salt have lost his savour, wherewith shall it be seasoned?" (Luke 14:34). Let your words carry the savor of divine truth and the sweetness of divine love.

Supplemental Reading Assignment:

For students who wish to go deeper, read selected passages from Richard Baxter's The Reformed Pastor, particularly the sections where Baxter discusses the art of personal conversation and individual spiritual care. Baxter wrote with extraordinary practical wisdom about how to engage with different types of people — the ignorant, the doubting, the hostile, the indifferent, and the seeking. Notice how Baxter adapts his approach to the specific condition of each person, applying the right truth to the right need at the right time. Compare Baxter's approach with Paul's instruction in Colossians 4:6 to "know how ye ought to answer every man." Consider also reading the chapter on apologetics in John Owen's On the Reason of Faith, where Owen discusses how the Christian can be confident that the Bible is the Word of God and how to communicate that confidence to others. Read also the section in Thomas Brooks's Precious Remedies Against Satan's Devices where Brooks identifies the specific "devices" or strategies that the enemy uses to keep people from receiving the truth, and the "remedies" — the specific truths and approaches — that can overcome those devices. These three Puritan works together provide a comprehensive guide to the art of faithful, wise, loving, and effective Christian witness that will serve you well for the rest of your life.

Well done, students. Today you have engaged with deep truths from Scripture and from the great Puritan tradition. You are now equipped to understand, defend, and share the Christian faith with confidence and grace. But remember that the greatest apologetic is not an argument — it is a life lived in the power of the Holy Spirit. Let your light shine before men, that they may see your good works and glorify your Father which is in heaven (Matthew 5:16). As the Puritan pastor Thomas Manton, born in 1620 in Lawrence-Lydiat, Somerset, England, observed, God has chosen to accomplish His saving purposes through the weak, imperfect, ordinary testimony of His people. This is both a staggering privilege and a solemn responsibility. God does not need us — He could save the world without any human assistance. But He has chosen to use us — to entrust the message of eternal salvation to jars of clay (2 Corinthians 4:7), so that the surpassing power might be seen to belong to God and not to us. Go forth, young apologists. The world needs the message you carry. Share it with all your heart — with confidence that comes from knowing the truth, and with humility that comes from knowing that you yourself are a sinner saved by grace. Be ready. Be honest. Be gentle. Be brave. And trust the God who has called you to be His witnesses unto the uttermost parts of the earth. "For the love of Christ constraineth us; because we thus judge, that if one died for all, then were all dead: And that he died for all, that they which live should not henceforth live unto themselves, but unto him which died for them, and rose again" (2 Corinthians 5:14-15). Carry these truths with you. Let them shape the way you think, the way you act, and the way you worship. Until next time, students.
