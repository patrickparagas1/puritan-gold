PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Guided Puritan Reading, Day 28
"The Holiness of God"
Stephen Charnock — The Attributes of God: Holiness


Welcome. Tonight we read together. Not quickly, not casually, but with the kind of attentive, worshipful reading that the Puritans practiced every day. Open your heart as we open this text.

Tonight we draw from the work of Stephen Charnock, specifically from "The Existence and Attributes of God." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Isaiah 6:1-5:

"In the year that king Uzziah died I saw also the Lord sitting upon a throne, high and lifted up, and his train filled the temple. Above it stood the seraphims: each one had six wings; with twain he covered his face, and with twain he covered his feet, and with twain he did fly. And one cried unto another, and said, Holy, holy, holy, is the LORD of hosts: the whole earth is full of his glory. And the posts of the door moved at the voice of him that cried, and the house was filled with smoke. Then said I, Woe is me! for I am undone; because I am a man of unclean lips, and I dwell in the midst of a people of unclean lips: for mine eyes have seen the King, the LORD of hosts."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


Holiness is the attribute of God that the seraphim single out for threefold repetition: 'Holy, holy, holy.' No other attribute receives this emphasis in Scripture. The angels do not cry 'Love, love, love' or 'Power, power, power' — though God is infinitely loving and infinitely powerful. They cry 'Holy, holy, holy,' because holiness is, as Charnock will argue, the attribute that crowns all the others. It is the beauty of all God's attributes, the glory of His glory.

Charnock's discourse on holiness is among the longest and most exalted of his entire work. He begins by defining what holiness means when applied to God. In its most fundamental sense, holiness means separation — God's absolute distinction from and elevation above all created things, and particularly His complete separation from and opposition to all moral evil. God is not merely free from sin; He is incapable of sin. He does not merely avoid evil; He is the antithesis of evil in every dimension of His being.

Charnock argues that holiness is not merely one attribute among many but the attribute that qualifies all the others. God's power is a holy power — He never uses His omnipotence for evil ends. God's love is a holy love — He never loves in a way that compromises righteousness. God's wisdom is a holy wisdom — He never designs ends that are morally corrupt. God's justice is a holy justice — He never punishes arbitrarily or unjustly. Remove holiness and every other attribute becomes dangerous: power without holiness is tyranny, love without holiness is indulgence, wisdom without holiness is cunning.

Charnock then explores the effects of encountering God's holiness. Isaiah's experience is paradigmatic. When the prophet saw the Lord in His holiness, his immediate response was not delight but devastation: 'Woe is me! for I am undone.' The vision of God's holiness exposed Isaiah's own unholiness with a clarity that was shattering. This is always the effect of a genuine encounter with the Holy One. The man who claims to have seen God and was not humbled has not truly seen Him. The holiness of God is the mirror that shows us what we truly are.

But Charnock does not leave the matter there. After the devastation comes the cleansing. A seraph takes a live coal from the altar and touches Isaiah's lips: 'Thine iniquity is taken away, and thy sin purged.' The holiness of God does not merely expose sin; through the atoning work of Christ (prefigured by the altar), it provides the remedy. Charnock writes that the same holiness that condemns the sinner provides, through the cross, the means of the sinner's purification.

Charnock develops an extended meditation on the relationship between God's holiness and the cross of Christ. At the cross, God's holiness is simultaneously most terrifyingly displayed and most beautifully vindicated. The cross shows what holiness requires: the death of God's own Son as the penalty for sin. It also shows what holiness provides: the righteousness of Christ credited to the sinner, so that the Holy God can receive unholy sinners without compromising His holiness. Charnock writes that the cross is the place where God's holiness and His love kiss each other — where the full demand of the one is met so that the full expression of the other might flow.

Charnock also addresses the believer's call to holiness. 'Be ye holy, for I am holy' (1 Peter 1:16). Because God is holy, His people must pursue holiness. This is not merely a command; it is a consequence of belonging to Him. The child bears the family likeness. The branches partake of the vine's nature. Those who are united to the Holy One will increasingly manifest His holiness in their character and conduct.

Charnock concludes with a searching application. The holiness of God, he writes, should produce four things in the believer: humility before God (for we are unholy in the presence of the Holy One), gratitude for the cross (for without it, God's holiness would consume us), hatred of sin (for sin is an offense against infinite holiness), and earnest pursuit of personal holiness (for we are called to reflect the character of our Father).

Charnock makes one final application that is especially relevant for those who serve in leadership within the church or the home. He writes that those who represent God to others bear a special responsibility to reflect His holiness. The father who leads his family, the elder who shepherds the flock, the teacher who instructs the young — each is called to be a visible expression of the God he represents. This does not mean sinless perfection, which is impossible in this life. It means a genuine, earnest, visible pursuit of holiness that demonstrates to others what it means to take God seriously. The leader whose life does not reflect the holiness of the God he proclaims is undermining his own message.

As we near the end of our journey through Charnock, let the holiness of God do its work in your soul. Let it humble you, cleanse you, and transform you. For the God who is holy, holy, holy is also the God who makes His people holy.


Now let us turn the lens inward. What is God saying to you through tonight's study?

Question 1: When I think about God, does His holiness feature prominently, or have I domesticated Him into a comfortable, unthreatening deity?

Question 2: How does the holiness of God displayed at the cross — where sin was punished and sinners were pardoned — shape my understanding of the gospel?

Question 3: Am I earnestly pursuing personal holiness as a reflection of God's character, or have I settled for a comfortable level of moral respectability?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Holy, holy, holy Lord God Almighty! I stand before You as Isaiah stood — undone by the vision of Your holiness and the awareness of my own uncleanness. But I thank You for the coal from the altar — the sacrifice of Christ that cleanses me from all sin. Purify me, O God. Make me holy as You are holy. Let the pursuit of holiness not be a burden but a delight, for in Your holiness I see the beauty that my soul was made to reflect. Through Christ, my purification, Amen.

Press on, dear saint. The race is worth running, and the prize is Christ Himself.
