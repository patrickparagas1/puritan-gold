PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"By the Rivers of Babylon"
May 28, 2026

---

"By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, yea, we wept, when we remembered Zion. We hanged our harps upon the willows in the midst thereof. For there they that carried us away captive required of us a song; and they that wasted us required of us mirth, saying, Sing us one of the songs of Zion. How shall we sing the Lord's song in a strange land? If I forget thee, O Jerusalem, let my right hand forget her cunning." -- Psalm 137:1-5

This psalm is the song of the exile, the lament of a people torn from their homeland and forced to live among strangers in a strange land. It was written during or shortly after the Babylonian captivity, that seventy-year period during which the people of God were carried away from Jerusalem and compelled to dwell beside the rivers of a pagan empire. And it expresses, with a rawness and an honesty that few other passages of Scripture can match, the anguish of the soul that finds itself separated from the place where God is worshipped and the people among whom God is known.

"By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, yea, we wept, when we remembered Zion." The rivers of Babylon were the Tigris, the Euphrates, and the network of canals that crisscrossed the flat and fertile plains of Mesopotamia. They were beautiful rivers, by all accounts, and the land through which they flowed was prosperous and abundant. But for the captives from Judah, those rivers held no beauty. They sat beside those foreign waters and wept, because every glance at those alien streams reminded them of the streams of their own beloved Zion that they would never see again.

There is a grief that goes deeper than the loss of property or the deprivation of physical comfort. It is the grief of spiritual displacement, the sorrow of being cut off from the means of grace, from the fellowship of the saints, from the public worship of the true and living God. The Babylonians could offer the captives every material advantage: food, shelter, employment, even a measure of freedom. But they could not replace what had been lost. They could not rebuild the temple. They could not restore the sacrifices. They could not re-create the sacred fellowship of the congregation of the Lord. And without these things, all the prosperity of Babylon was ashes in the mouth.

"We hanged our harps upon the willows." The harps of Israel were instruments of praise. They had accompanied the psalms of David. They had sounded in the courts of the temple. They had lifted the hearts of the faithful in a thousand services of worship. But now they hung silent upon the weeping willows of Babylon, as useless and as desolate as the souls of those who owned them. When the heart is broken, the harp is silent. When the joy of the Lord is gone, the song of praise is impossible.

"For there they that carried us away captive required of us a song; and they that wasted us required of us mirth, saying, Sing us one of the songs of Zion." The cruelty of this request is almost beyond comprehension. The very men who had destroyed Jerusalem, who had burned the temple, who had slaughtered the priests and dragged the people into captivity, now demanded entertainment. "Sing us one of your songs. Give us some of that famous Jewish music. Let us hear what the religion of your God sounds like." It was the cruelty of the cat that toys with the mouse, the callousness of the oppressor who not only takes everything from his victim but demands that the victim smile while being robbed.

"How shall we sing the Lord's song in a strange land?" This question is not merely a refusal. It is a confession of spiritual impossibility. The songs of Zion were not performance pieces to be trotted out for the amusement of pagans. They were sacred expressions of faith, love, and devotion, addressed to the living God. To sing them before the mockers of Babylon would have been a profanation of the holiest things. Some things are too sacred to be shared with those who cannot understand them, and the worship of God is chief among them.

And yet, there is a deeper question here, one that every believer in exile must face. How do we worship God when we find ourselves in circumstances that seem to make worship impossible? How do we maintain our faith when we are surrounded by unbelief? How do we keep the flame of devotion alive when everything around us conspires to extinguish it? The captives by the rivers of Babylon faced this question in its most extreme form, and their answer, though it came with tears, was profoundly instructive.

"If I forget thee, O Jerusalem, let my right hand forget her cunning. If I do not remember thee, let my tongue cleave to the roof of my mouth; if I prefer not Jerusalem above my chief joy." They could not sing in Babylon. But they would not forget Jerusalem. They bound themselves by the most solemn oath not to allow the memory of Zion to fade from their hearts. The right hand would lose its skill before it forgot to reach toward Jerusalem in prayer. The tongue would be struck dumb before it ceased to speak of the holy city. Even in exile, even in captivity, even by the rivers of Babylon, the love of God's dwelling place would burn on in their hearts like a fire that no flood could quench.

This is the resolution that every Christian exile must make. For we are all, in a sense, captives in Babylon. This present world is not our home. We are strangers and pilgrims here. The culture around us is foreign to our faith. The values of this age are hostile to our God. And we are tempted, constantly and powerfully, to hang our harps upon the willows and give up the song of praise. But we must not. We must not forget our Jerusalem, our heavenly city, the city whose builder and maker is God. We must keep the flame alive. We must remember Zion. And we must wait, with patience and with hope, for the day when our captivity shall be turned, and we shall go home at last to the city of our God, and the harps shall be taken down from the willows, and the songs of Zion shall ring out once more in the courts of the Lord. Amen.
