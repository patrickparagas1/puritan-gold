PURITAN GOLD -- Family Devotional, Day 4
"The Power of Parental Example"
John Angell James — A Help to Domestic Happiness

---

Good evening, beloved family. The Puritans believed that the family altar was the most important altar in all the land. Tonight we gather at that altar together. Welcome to Puritan Gold.

Let us come before the Lord. Gracious God, thank You for the gift of family. Thank You that we can come to You together. We pray that You would teach us from Your Word this evening. Let every word sink deep into our hearts. We love You, Lord. In Jesus' name, Amen.

The Word of God is a lamp to our feet and a light to our path. Tonight that light shines from Ephesians 6:4:

"And, ye fathers, provoke not your children to wrath: but bring them up in the nurture and admonition of the Lord."

That is Ephesians 6:4. Let those words settle in your heart for a moment before we continue.

--- Teaching ---

Today we come to a verse that speaks directly to parents — especially fathers. The Apostle Paul writes in Ephesians 6:4, 'And, ye fathers, provoke not your children to wrath: but bring them up in the nurture and admonition of the Lord.'

This verse has two parts, and both are vital. Let us take the first part: 'provoke not your children to wrath.' The Greek word for 'provoke' means to irritate, exasperate, or stir up to anger. Paul is warning fathers against a harsh, unreasonable, or hypocritical style of parenting that embitters children rather than drawing them to God.

What provokes children to wrath? Inconsistency — when parents demand obedience but live however they please. Harshness without love — discipline that is angry rather than corrective. Unreasonable expectations — demanding perfection from little ones who are still learning. Neglect — being so absent or distracted that children feel they do not matter. Hypocrisy — telling children to be kind, honest, and prayerful while the parents are none of these things.

The second part gives the positive instruction: 'bring them up in the nurture and admonition of the Lord.' As we discussed on Day 2, 'nurture' means discipline and training — the whole process of shaping character through instruction, correction, and encouragement. 'Admonition' means verbal instruction, counsel, and warning. Together, these words describe a parent who lovingly and consistently teaches, corrects, and guides their children in the ways of God.

But here is the key: the phrase 'of the Lord' tells us that this nurture and admonition must be centered on God, not on the parent's ego or preferences. We are not raising children to serve us. We are raising children to serve the Lord. The standard is not our opinions but God's Word.

The Apostle Paul himself modeled this when he wrote to the Thessalonians in 1 Thessalonians 2:11-12, 'As ye know how we exhorted and comforted and charged every one of you, as a father doth his children, That ye would walk worthy of God, who hath called you unto his kingdom and glory.' Notice the three actions — exhorting, comforting, and charging. A good parent does all three: encourages, consoles, and instructs.

Now, John Angell James had much to say about parental example. He observed that example is the school of mankind and that children take far more notice of what their parents do than of what they say. This is a sobering truth. You can preach the finest sermons at the dinner table, but if your life does not match your words, your children will notice the gap.

James told the story of a father who faithfully conducted family worship every evening but was harsh and impatient with his wife and children throughout the day. His children grew up resenting religion because they associated it with their father's hypocrisy. The family worship was right and good, but the lack of a consistent example undermined it.

On the other hand, James celebrated parents whose quiet, consistent godliness spoke louder than any sermon. A mother who prays with genuine faith when the family faces hardship teaches her children more about trust in God than a hundred lectures on the subject. A father who apologizes when he is wrong models the humility and repentance that he wants his children to learn.

James wrote that the most powerful argument for Christianity that a child will ever encounter is a godly parent. Not a perfect parent — there are none. But a parent who genuinely loves God, honestly confesses sin, and consistently seeks to grow in grace.

For our little ones: Did you know that your parents are learning too? Nobody is perfect, but we can all try to show God's love by how we act, not just what we say. For older children: Think about the people who have taught you the most about God. Was it mostly what they said, or how they lived? And for parents hearing this: Let us be honest with our children about our failures. Let us apologize when we fall short. Our children do not need perfect parents — they need real ones who are genuinely following Jesus.

--- Puritan Wisdom ---

The wisdom of the Puritans remains as fresh today as when it was first written. Listen to this insight.

John Angell James, in his work "A Help to Domestic Happiness," wrote these words:

"Example is the school of mankind; they will learn at no other. Children take more notice of what their parents do than of what they say."

What John Angell James is reminding us is that the truths we have read tonight in Ephesians 6:4 are not new. They have been treasured by faithful Christians for centuries. And they are just as true and just as needed in our family today as they were hundreds of years ago. Let us take these words to heart and let them shape the way we live together as a family.

--- Family Discussion ---

Here are some questions for us to think about and discuss together. There are no wrong answers here, just honest hearts.

Question 1: What does it mean to 'provoke' someone? Can you think of a time someone made you really frustrated?

Question 2: Why does the Bible say parents should not make their children angry?

Question 3: Why do you think what people DO matters more than what they SAY?

Question 4: How can parents show they are real and honest about their own faith, even when they make mistakes?

Take a few minutes to talk about these questions together. Let everyone share, and listen to one another with patience and love.

--- Memory Verse ---

Now, here is our memory verse for today. Let us say it together:

"And, ye fathers, provoke not your children to wrath: but bring them up in the nurture and admonition of the Lord." -- Ephesians 6:4

Try to say it out loud three times right now. Then, before bed tonight, see if you can say it from memory. Tomorrow morning, try again. The more we hide God's Word in our hearts, the more it will guide us when we need it most.

--- Family Activity ---

Here is a hands-on way to remember what we have learned tonight:

Play 'Follow the Leader' as a family. Take turns being the leader while everyone else copies exactly what the leader does. After the game, discuss: Was it easier to follow the leader's words or actions? How does this connect to parental example?

--- Closing ---

Father, we have heard Your Word tonight, and we are grateful. We confess that we cannot live it out in our own strength. Fill us with Your Spirit. Give us wisdom for tomorrow, patience with one another, and a deep love for You. Keep our family close to You always. In Jesus' name. Amen.

Rest well tonight. God is faithful, and His wisdom is sure.
