PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"Blessed Is He That Considereth the Poor"
June 19, 2026

---

"Blessed is he that considereth the poor: the Lord will deliver him in time of trouble. The Lord will preserve him, and keep him alive; and he shall be blessed upon the earth: and thou wilt not deliver him unto the will of his enemies. The Lord will strengthen him upon the bed of languishing: thou wilt make all his bed in his sickness." -- Psalm 41:1-3

The world measures a man's blessedness by what he possesses. The Scripture measures it by what he gives. The world says, "Blessed is the man who has accumulated wealth, who has secured his future, who has built a wall of material comfort around himself." But the Holy Spirit says, "Blessed is he that considereth the poor." Not merely he who gives to the poor, though that is certainly included, but he who considers them -- who thinks about them, who enters into their situation with sympathy and understanding, who does not merely toss a coin in their direction and walk on, but who pauses to understand their need and to respond to it with genuine compassion.

Spurgeon preached with great tenderness upon this verse, reminding his congregation that the Lord Jesus Christ is the supreme example of one who considered the poor. He did not remain in the comfort of heaven, surveying the poverty of fallen humanity from a safe and comfortable distance. He came down. He entered into our poverty. He became poor for our sakes, that we through His poverty might be rich. He considered not merely the material poverty of the hungry and the homeless, but the far deeper poverty of souls lost in sin, alienated from God, bankrupt of all spiritual wealth, and He gave Himself for their redemption. And therefore every act of compassion that a believer shows to the poor is an imitation of Christ, a participation in the ministry of the One who considered the poorest of the poor and gave Himself for them.

"The Lord will deliver him in time of trouble." Here is the promise attached to the blessing. The man who considers the poor shall himself be considered by God in the day of his own need. This is not a commercial transaction, as though we could buy God's favour with our charitable deeds. It is rather the outworking of a spiritual principle: that the merciful shall obtain mercy, that the man who sows generously shall reap generously, and that the God who sees every act of kindness done in His name will not forget it in the day of trouble.

Thomas Manton expounded this passage with careful attention to the word "considereth," noting that it implies not a hasty or impulsive act of charity but a deliberate and thoughtful engagement with the needs of others. The man who truly considers the poor takes the time to understand their circumstances. He does not assume that poverty is always the result of laziness or vice. He recognises that affliction comes to the godly as well as to the ungodly, that the righteous sometimes suffer want through no fault of their own, and that the obligation to show mercy is not contingent upon the worthiness of the recipient but upon the character of the God who commands it.

"The Lord will strengthen him upon the bed of languishing: thou wilt make all his bed in his sickness." What a beautiful and intimate picture of divine care. When the merciful man himself falls ill, when his strength fails and he lies upon his bed unable to help himself, the Lord draws near and becomes his nurse, his physician, his comforter. "Thou wilt make all his bed in his sickness." This is the language of tender, personal, hands-on care. God does not merely send His healing from a distance; He comes to the bedside. He smooths the pillow. He straightens the covers. He ministers to the suffering body and the anxious soul with a gentleness that surpasses the most devoted human nurse.

John Flavel found in this promise a rich source of comfort for the afflicted saints of his congregation, many of whom were poor and persecuted and had no earthly physician to attend them. He told them that the Great Physician never loses a patient, that His prescriptions never fail, that His presence at the bedside is worth more than all the medicines in the world. And he urged them to show mercy to others even in their own poverty, knowing that the God who commanded them to give would supply them with the means to give, and that every act of mercy would be repaid a thousandfold in the day of their own need.

Richard Baxter, who knew much about sickness from his own lifelong struggle with ill health, wrote with deep personal conviction that the promise of God's presence in illness is the greatest comfort a sufferer can know. The disease may be painful, the nights may be long, the body may waste away, but if God is there -- if God is making the bed, if God is smoothing the pillow, if God is whispering words of comfort in the dark watches of the night -- then the bed of languishing becomes a place of communion, and the chamber of sickness becomes a vestibule of heaven.

Let us then cultivate the grace of considering the poor, not with a casual and indifferent pity that costs us nothing, but with a genuine, thoughtful, self-sacrificing compassion that imitates the compassion of Christ. And let us rest in the promise that the God who commands mercy will also reward it, and that in the day of our own trouble, He will deliver us, preserve us, and strengthen us upon the bed of languishing. Amen.
