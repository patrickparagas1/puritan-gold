PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 15
"Gouge on Mutual Love"
Domestical Duties — Puritan Household Duties


Good evening, dear husband and wife. Welcome to our third week together. This week we sit at the feet of one of the greatest Puritan writers on marriage: William Gouge. His masterwork, Of Domestical Duties, published in 1622, remains one of the most thorough and practical treatments of married life ever written. Let us learn from this wise pastor who spent decades helping couples build godly homes.

Let us read our passage. 1 Corinthians 13:4-7 (KJV):

"Charity suffereth long, and is kind; charity envieth not; charity vaunteth not itself, is not puffed up, Doth not behave itself unseemly, seeketh not her own, is not easily provoked, thinketh no evil; Rejoiceth not in iniquity, but rejoiceth in the truth; Beareth all things, believeth all things, hopeth all things, endureth all things."


William Gouge began his treatment of marriage duties with a principle that surprised many in his congregation: before he spoke of the separate duties of husband and wife, he spoke at length about their common duties, the obligations they share equally before God. Chief among these common duties is the duty of mutual love.

Gouge taught that the love between husband and wife is the foundation upon which all other duties rest. Without love, the husband's authority becomes tyranny. Without love, the wife's submission becomes slavery. Without love, the household becomes a prison rather than a paradise. But where mutual love reigns, every duty is sweetened, every burden is lightened, and every sacrifice becomes a joy.

What did Gouge mean by mutual love? He was careful to distinguish it from the world's understanding of love as mere feeling or attraction. For Gouge, marital love was a settled disposition of the will, a deliberate choice to seek the good of the other person above one's own good. It was rooted not in the worthiness of the beloved but in the command of God and the example of Christ. Gouge argued that this kind of love, far from being cold or dutiful, actually produces deeper and more lasting affection than the most passionate romantic feelings.

Gouge identified several expressions of mutual love. The first was a genuine delight in each other's company. The loving spouse does not merely tolerate the other's presence but takes pleasure in it. Do you look forward to being with your husband or wife, or has their company become a burden? If the latter, Gouge would say that love has grown cold and needs to be rekindled.

The second expression was a tender concern for each other's well-being. This includes physical care, such as attention to health, comfort, and rest. It includes emotional care, such as listening, comforting, and encouraging. And it includes spiritual care, such as praying for each other, reading Scripture together, and lovingly correcting each other when sin creeps in.

The third expression was a readiness to bear with each other's weaknesses. Gouge was a realist. He knew that every husband and wife is a sinner, and that living in close quarters with a sinner means daily exposure to imperfection. The loving spouse does not demand perfection but extends grace. This does not mean ignoring sin. It means addressing it with patience, with hope, and with a deep commitment to restoration rather than condemnation.

Richard Baxter echoed Gouge's teaching, adding that mutual love must be nourished like a garden. It does not sustain itself. It requires daily watering with kindness, regular weeding of bitterness and resentment, and constant exposure to the sunlight of God's Word and Spirit. The couple that takes their love for granted will watch it wither. The couple that tends it deliberately will watch it flourish.

Dear couple, consider the state of mutual love in your marriage tonight. Not the love you felt on your wedding day, but the love you practice today. Is it patient and kind? Does it bear all things, believe all things, hope all things, endure all things? Where it has grown cold, ask God to rekindle it. Where it has been neglected, commit to tending it with fresh intentionality. Gouge would tell you that there is no duty more important and no investment more rewarding than the cultivation of mutual love in your marriage.


Question 1: Do we genuinely delight in each other's company, or have we allowed our love to become routine and unfeeling?

Question 2: How well do we bear with each other's weaknesses? Are we more inclined to correct or to extend grace?

Question 3: What is one practical way we can nourish and tend our mutual love this week?


O God of love, who hast joined us together in the bond of marriage, renew and deepen the love between us. Forgive us for the times we have let our love grow cold through neglect or selfishness. Teach us the mutual love that William Gouge described: a love that delights, that cares, that bears, that endures. May our love for one another be a reflection of Thy love for us in Christ. Water the garden of our marriage with Thy Spirit, and let it bring forth fruit that glorifies Thy name. Through Jesus Christ our Lord, Amen.
