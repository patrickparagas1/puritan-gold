PURITAN GOLD -- Together: Couples Devotional, Day 13
"Patience with Each Other's Battles"
Romans for Marriage — Day 13


Good evening, dear couple. The Puritans believed that marriage was one of the greatest means of grace God provides. Tonight we enter into that grace together. Let us open our hearts and our ears to what the Lord has for us.

As we settle in together, let us hear from Romans 7:15-20:

"For that which I do I allow not: for what I would, that do I not; but what I hate, that do I. If then I do that which I would not, I consent unto the law that it is good. Now then it is no more I that do it, but sin that dwelleth in me. For I know that in me (that is, in my flesh,) dwelleth no good thing: for to will is present with me; but how to perform that which is good I find not. For the good that I would I do not: but the evil which I would not, that I do. Now if I do that I would not, it is no more I that do it, but sin that dwelleth in me."

That is Romans 7:15-20. Take a breath. Let those words settle between the two of you. God is speaking to your marriage through this passage.


One of the gifts of the Christian tradition is that we do not study alone. We have the wisdom of the saints who came before us. Tonight we hear from John Owen.

In Romans 7, Paul opens the door to one of the most honest confessions in Scripture. The great apostle — the man who wrote half the New Testament, who planted churches across the Roman Empire, who endured shipwreck and stoning for Christ — confesses: 'The good that I would I do not: but the evil which I would not, that I do.' Even Paul struggled with indwelling sin.

John Owen, who wrote more extensively on the remaining sin in believers than perhaps any theologian in history, used this passage to teach a crucial truth: sanctification is progressive, not instantaneous. The believer genuinely wants to do good — 'to will is present with me.' But the power to consistently perform that good is not yet perfected — 'how to perform that which is good I find not.' This is the universal experience of every Christian until glory.

For marriage, this truth demands patience. Your spouse genuinely wants to be a better husband or wife. They truly desire to overcome their weaknesses, their bad habits, their recurring sins. But the battle with indwelling sin is fierce, and victory comes slowly. When your spouse fails — again — in an area they have been trying to change, remember Paul's words: the desire is real, but the performance is imperfect.

Owen taught that impatience with another's spiritual growth is itself a form of pride. It assumes that we would do better in their shoes — which Romans 7 proves false. Even the most mature believers struggle with indwelling sin. If Paul battled sin until the end of his life, we should not expect our spouses to achieve perfection during theirs.

Patience does not mean complacency about sin. Owen distinguished clearly between tolerance for the person and tolerance for the sin. We love the sinner and hate the sin — in our spouses and in ourselves. But we recognize that the battle is real, the struggle is genuine, and transformation takes time. Your spouse is a work in progress, just as you are. God is not finished with either of you.


When you see your spouse struggling with a recurring sin or weakness, how do you respond? With frustration? With sighs and eye-rolls? With internal contempt? Or with compassion, patience, and prayer?

Romans 7 reminds you that your spouse's struggle is the same struggle you face — the war between the flesh and the spirit. They want to do better. They hate their failures. They feel the weight of falling short. What they need from you is not condemnation but companionship in the battle.

Tonight, choose patience. Choose to see your spouse not as a hopeless case but as a fellow soldier in a war that is already won — even though the daily battles continue. Fight alongside them, not against them.

The Puritans understood that marriage is not a destination but a journey. Every day presents new opportunities to love, to serve, to forgive, and to grow together. The truths we have studied tonight are not just for tonight. They are for tomorrow morning when the alarm goes off, for the difficult conversation that has been waiting, for the quiet moments when only God sees how you treat each other.


Discussion time. Husband, listen to your wife. Wife, listen to your husband. Let this be a safe and sacred space.

Question 1: What is the recurring struggle your spouse faces that tests your patience the most? How does Romans 7 change your perspective on it?

Question 2: Do you believe your spouse genuinely wants to change? How does that belief (or lack of it) affect your attitude toward them?

Question 3: How can you be a companion in your spouse's battle against sin rather than a critic on the sidelines?

Take a few minutes with each question. Do not rush. The best conversations in marriage happen when both people feel truly heard.


Let us close our eyes and open our hearts to the Lord in prayer. Husband, pray for your wife. Wife, pray for your husband. There is no greater gift you can give each other.

Tonight's prayer focus: Pray for patience with each other's battles. Confess any impatience or contempt you have harbored. Ask God to give you eyes to see your spouse as a fellow soldier and to fight alongside them with grace.

If one of you finds it difficult to pray out loud, that is perfectly fine. Simply holding hands in silence before the Lord is itself a powerful act of worship. Let the Spirit intercede where words fail.


May you walk through the battles of sanctification side by side — patient with each other's weakness, rejoicing in each other's growth, and trusting the God who will complete what He began.

Go in peace, husband and wife. The God who ordained your marriage is faithful to sustain it. Trust Him. Love each other. And come back tomorrow for more. Goodnight.
