PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Doctrine Deep-Dive, Day 12
"The Last Things"
Eschatology Explored Through the Puritans


Good evening. A.W. Tozer said that what comes into our minds when we think about God is the most important thing about us. Tonight we think carefully, biblically, and reverently about a great doctrine of the faith.

Tonight we draw from the work of Richard Baxter, specifically from "The Saints' Everlasting Rest." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Revelation 21:1-4:

"And I saw a new heaven and a new earth: for the first heaven and the first earth were passed away; and there was no more sea. And I John saw the holy city, new Jerusalem, coming down from God out of heaven, prepared as a bride adorned for her husband. And I heard a great voice out of heaven saying, Behold, the tabernacle of God is with men, and he will dwell with them, and they shall be his people, and God himself shall be with them, and be their God. And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


Eschatology — the doctrine of the last things — encompasses death, judgment, heaven, and hell. It is the doctrine that speaks to the ultimate destiny of every human being and of creation itself. The Puritans were deeply eschatological thinkers, not in the sense of plotting end-times timelines, but in the sense of living with eternity constantly in view. They understood that the present age is temporary and that the choices made in this life reverberate forever.

Richard Baxter's 'The Saints' Everlasting Rest' is the greatest Puritan work on heaven and the most sustained meditation on the glory that awaits believers. Baxter wrote it while gravely ill, expecting to die, and the book pulses with the urgency of a man who believed he was about to enter the reality he described. The 'rest' Baxter writes about is not mere cessation from labor; it is the positive enjoyment of God — the fullness of communion with the triune God in a renewed creation.

Baxter begins by defining the saints' rest. It is, he writes, 'the most happy state of a Christian, having obtained the end of his course.' It includes the cessation of all suffering, all sin, all temptation, and all sorrow. But more importantly, it includes the perfection of all joy, all knowledge, all love, and all fellowship. Heaven is not merely the absence of bad things; it is the presence of all good things in their fullest, most perfect expression.

Baxter identifies the central feature of the saints' rest as the vision of God. Drawing on the biblical promise that 'we shall see him as he is' (1 John 3:2), Baxter argues that the direct, unmediated sight of God will be the supreme joy of heaven. In this life, we see God through the mediating lens of Scripture, creation, and providence — 'through a glass, darkly' (1 Corinthians 13:12). In the next life, we shall see Him face to face. Baxter struggles for words adequate to describe this experience, and confesses that no language can capture what the soul will experience when it gazes upon infinite beauty, infinite goodness, and infinite love without any barrier between itself and God.

Baxter also addresses the doctrine of the resurrection of the body. The saints' rest is not a disembodied existence of floating spirits. It is a bodily existence in a renewed creation. The same body that suffered, served, and struggled in this life will be raised, glorified, and fitted for eternal communion with God. Baxter writes that the glorified body will be an instrument of perfect worship — free from pain, disease, fatigue, and every limitation that currently hampers our service of God.

The Puritan Thomas Watson adds to Baxter's vision by emphasizing the perfection of communion with the saints. In heaven, believers will enjoy the company of all the redeemed — the patriarchs, prophets, apostles, martyrs, and every humble believer who has ever lived. Watson writes that the conversations of heaven will be infinitely richer than any we have known on earth, for they will be conversations between perfected minds, free from misunderstanding, selfishness, and sin.

But Baxter does not allow his readers to treat heaven as a mere future curiosity. He insists that meditation on the saints' rest has profound practical effects in the present. The believer who regularly contemplates heaven will find three things happening. First, the things of this world will lose their grip on his heart. The man who has seen the New Jerusalem cares less about advancing in the old one. Second, the sufferings of this life will become more bearable. Paul wrote, 'Our light affliction, which is but for a moment, worketh for us a far more exceeding and eternal weight of glory' (2 Corinthians 4:17). The man who keeps heaven in view can endure anything on earth. Third, holiness will become more attractive. The man who longs for heaven longs for the purity that belongs to heaven, and that longing produces practical godliness in the present.

The Puritan John Owen provides a Christological anchor for eschatology that is essential. Owen argues that the center of the believer's hope is not heaven as a place but Christ as a Person. What makes heaven heavenly is not the golden streets or the crystal sea but the unmediated presence of the glorified Christ. Owen writes that the believer's greatest longing should not be for the end of suffering (though that is a legitimate desire) but for the sight of Christ's glory. 'Father, I will that they also, whom thou hast given me, be with me where I am; that they may behold my glory' (John 17:24). The eschatological hope is ultimately a personal hope — not merely that we will be somewhere better but that we will be with Someone infinitely glorious.

Baxter's closing exhortation is a challenge to every reader: set your affections on things above. Do not be so absorbed in the concerns of this temporary world that you forget the eternal world that is coming. Live as a man who is heading somewhere glorious, and let the anticipation of that glory shape everything you do today.


Let these questions search your heart. Answer honestly before God.

Question 1: How often do I consciously meditate on heaven, and how does that meditation (or its absence) affect my daily priorities?

Question 2: Does the promise of the saints' rest make the sufferings and frustrations of this present life more bearable?

Question 3: Am I living as though this world is my home, or as a pilgrim passing through on the way to something infinitely better?

Question 4: How does the doctrine of bodily resurrection shape my understanding of the physical world and my daily stewardship of my body?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Lord God, Author of the new creation, I thank You that the best is yet to come. Fix my eyes on the saints' everlasting rest — the day when I shall see You face to face, when every tear shall be wiped away, when sin and sorrow shall be no more. Let the hope of heaven loosen my grip on this world and strengthen my grip on You. I long for the day when I shall know You fully, even as I am fully known. Until then, keep me faithful. In Jesus' name, Amen.

Goodnight. Remember: the same God who spoke to the Puritans speaks to you.
