PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 29
"Summer Love: Keeping Romance Alive"
Puritan Marriage Wisdom — Summer Marriage Reflections

---

Good evening, dear husband and wife. As June draws to a close and summer stretches before you, we turn tonight to a subject that the Puritans addressed with surprising warmth and candor: the romantic dimension of marriage. Far from being the cold, stern figures of popular imagination, the Puritans celebrated marital love with a passion that would put many modern couples to shame.

Let us read our passage. Song of Solomon 2:10-13 (KJV):

"My beloved spake, and said unto me, Rise up, my love, my fair one, and come away. For, lo, the winter is past, the rain is over and gone; The flowers appear on the earth; the time of the singing of birds is come, and the voice of the turtle is heard in our land; The fig tree putteth forth her green figs, and the vines with the tender grape give a good smell. Arise, my love, my fair one, and come away."

--- Teaching ---

The Song of Solomon is God's celebration of romantic love between husband and wife. It is not an allegory that merely points to Christ and the church, though the Puritans rightly saw that dimension. It is also, and primarily, a celebration of the delight that a husband and wife are meant to find in each other. The beloved calls his bride to rise up, to come away, to enjoy the beauty of the season together. The winter is past. The flowers have appeared. The birds are singing. It is a time for love to bloom afresh.

The Puritans took this passage, and the entire Song of Solomon, as evidence that God intends marriage to be a source of deep romantic joy. They rejected the notion that physical affection and emotional delight between husband and wife are merely tolerable necessities. They taught that marital love, in all its dimensions, is a gift from God to be enjoyed with thanksgiving and cultivated with intentionality.

Richard Baxter wrote with remarkable directness about the importance of maintaining romantic affection in marriage. He counseled husbands to court their wives throughout their entire married lives, not merely during the season of courtship. The husband who stops pursuing his wife after the wedding has misunderstood the nature of marriage. Marriage is not the destination at which pursuit ends; it is the garden in which pursuit deepens. The wife who was worth pursuing before the wedding is worth pursuing fifty years later.

Baxter also counseled wives to receive and reciprocate their husband's romantic attention with gladness. The wife who makes her husband feel desired, appreciated, and delighted in is giving him one of the greatest gifts a human being can receive. The Puritans understood that romantic love in marriage is not selfish; it is the fuel that powers the engine of daily faithfulness. The couple that delights in each other will find it far easier to serve each other, to forgive each other, and to endure hardship together.

William Gouge addressed a common problem: the erosion of romance over time. He acknowledged that the passionate feelings of early marriage often cool as years pass, responsibilities multiply, and the demands of children, work, and church consume a couple's energy. But Gouge insisted that this cooling is not inevitable. It is the result of neglect, not of nature. The couple that deliberately invests in their romantic life, that sets aside time for each other, that speaks words of affection, that maintains physical tenderness, will find that their love deepens rather than diminishes with the passing years.

Summer is a fitting season for this renewal. The long evenings, the warm nights, the slower pace of life, all of these provide opportunities for a husband and wife to reconnect, to remember why they fell in love, and to fan the flame that first drew them together. Do not let the summer pass without making intentional time for romance. Take a walk together after dinner. Sit on the porch and talk as you did when you were courting. Plan a day or an evening away from the routine. Write your spouse a letter telling them what you love about them. These are not luxuries; they are necessities for a healthy marriage.

Dear couple, do not be embarrassed by romance. God invented it. He placed the Song of Solomon in the very heart of His Word. He designed marriage to be a source of delight, not merely a structure for duty. Let this summer be a season of renewed affection, of rekindled joy, of deepened intimacy. Rise up, my love, my fair one, and come away.

--- For Discussion ---

Question 1: When was the last time we intentionally invested in the romantic dimension of our marriage? What did we do, and how did it make us feel?

Question 2: What has been crowding out romance in our marriage recently? How can we create more space for each other this summer?

Question 3: What is one romantic gesture or activity we can plan together for this coming week?

--- Prayer ---

Lord God, who created the love between man and woman and called it very good, rekindle the flame of romance in this marriage. Forgive us for allowing the demands of life to crowd out the delight we are meant to find in each other. Help us to pursue each other with the same passion and intentionality we had in the days of our courtship. Give us eyes to see each other's beauty, words to express our affection, and time to enjoy each other's company. May this summer be a season of renewed love in our marriage, to Thy glory and our great joy. Through Jesus Christ our Lord, Amen.
