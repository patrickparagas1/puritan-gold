PURITAN GOLD -- My Growth, Day 5
"The Pursuit of Holiness"
Pastoral Day — Practical Counsel on Personal Sanctification

---

Welcome. Today is a Pastoral day — a day for practical spiritual counsel. Our subject is holiness: the daily, deliberate pursuit of Christlikeness that the Puritans considered the most essential work of the Christian life.

Let us read 1 Peter 1:15-16 (KJV):

"But as he which hath called you is holy, so be ye holy in all manner of conversation; Because it is written, Be ye holy; for I am holy."

--- Teaching ---

The Puritans were, above all else, people who pursued holiness. Not the hollow, external holiness of mere moral conformity, but the deep, inward holiness that flows from a heart transformed by grace. Today we consider their practical counsel on how to pursue sanctification in daily life.

First, understand what holiness is and what it is not. Holiness is not perfection in this life. The Puritans were realists about the remaining power of sin in the believer. John Owen, in his masterful treatise The Mortification of Sin, wrote that sin will not die except by daily killing it. The Christian life is a lifelong battle against indwelling sin, and the soldier who expects to win the war in a single engagement will be quickly disillusioned. Holiness is not the absence of struggle; it is faithfulness in the midst of struggle.

Second, the Puritans taught that holiness begins with the mind. What you think about shapes what you become. Thomas Watson wrote that the mind is the pilot of the soul — where the mind goes, the heart follows. If your mind dwells on impurity, your heart will follow. If your mind meditates on truth, beauty, and goodness, your heart will be drawn toward holiness. This is why the Puritans were so devoted to Scripture meditation. They saturated their minds with the Word of God because they knew that transformed thinking produces transformed living.

Third, the Puritans counseled what they called the mortification of sin — the daily, deliberate killing of sinful desires, habits, and patterns. Owen identified several steps in this process. First, know your sin. Identify the specific patterns of sin that most easily beset you. Do not fight vaguely; fight specifically. Second, feel the danger. Sin is not merely unpleasant; it is deadly. The complacent Christian who treats sin casually will be overcome by it. Third, apply the blood of Christ. The power to mortify sin comes not from willpower but from the cross. It is by gazing on the suffering Savior that sin loses its appeal. Fourth, starve the sin. Do not provide opportunities for it. Cut off the avenues of temptation.

Fourth, the Puritans taught the importance of the means of grace — the regular disciplines that God uses to strengthen the soul. These include the reading and hearing of Scripture, prayer, the Lord's Supper, Christian fellowship, and the faithful attendance upon public worship. Jeremiah Burroughs wrote that the Christian who neglects the means of grace and then complains of spiritual weakness is like a farmer who refuses to plow and then wonders why nothing grows.

Fifth, the Puritans emphasized the role of the Holy Spirit in sanctification. Holiness is not a human achievement; it is a divine work. Paul writes in Philippians 2:12-13, "Work out your own salvation with fear and trembling. For it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do of his good pleasure." Both parts are essential. We work — but God is working in us. We strive — but the power comes from the Spirit.

Sixth, the Puritans taught that holiness is communal. You cannot pursue holiness in isolation. You need brothers and sisters who will challenge you, encourage you, hold you accountable, and pray for you. Richard Baxter wrote that the lone Christian is the weak Christian, and the weak Christian is the vulnerable Christian.

Finally, the Puritans reminded believers that holiness is not joyless. Thomas Chalmers called it the expulsive power of a new affection. The way to overcome temptation is not merely to resist the sinful pleasure but to be so captivated by a greater pleasure — the pleasure of God Himself — that sin loses its grip. Holiness is not the removal of pleasure but the redirection of pleasure toward the supreme delight of knowing and loving God.

--- Closing ---

Holiness is not a burden; it is a privilege. To be set apart for God, to walk in His ways, to reflect His character — this is the highest calling. Lord, make me holy as You are holy. Not by my strength but by Your Spirit. Kill the sin that lingers. Fan the flame of desire for You. And let the joy of holiness sustain me on the path. Amen.
