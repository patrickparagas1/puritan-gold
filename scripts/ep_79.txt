PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"Bless the Lord, O My Soul"
May 18, 2026

---

"Bless the Lord, O my soul: and all that is within me, bless his holy name. Bless the Lord, O my soul, and forget not all his benefits: Who forgiveth all thine iniquities; who healeth all thy diseases; Who redeemeth thy life from destruction; who crowneth thee with lovingkindness and tender mercies; Who satisfieth thy mouth with good things; so that thy youth is renewed like the eagle's." -- Psalm 103:1-5

David here speaks not to God but to himself. He summons his own soul to the work of praise, as a man might rouse a sleeping servant to his duties. "Bless the Lord, O my soul." And then, lest any part of his being should remain idle while the rest is engaged in worship, he adds, "And all that is within me, bless his holy name." All that is within me. Every faculty of the mind. Every affection of the heart. Every power of the will. Every capacity of the memory. The whole man, body, soul, and spirit, is to be marshalled into one great army of praise and directed toward the glory of God.

This is worship as God intends it: not a thin trickle of half-hearted sentiment, but a mighty river of whole-hearted adoration. The Puritans understood that the greatest enemy of true worship is not atheism but apathy. A man may believe all the right doctrines and yet worship God with a cold and listless heart. He may attend every service, sing every hymn, pray every prayer, and yet offer to God nothing more than the form of godliness without the power thereof. And God, who sees the heart, is not pleased with such worship. He desires the whole man, all that is within us, engaged and inflamed and overflowing with gratitude for His holy name.

"Forget not all his benefits." Here David identifies one of the chief causes of spiritual coldness: forgetfulness. We are a forgetful people. The Israelites stood on the far side of the Red Sea and sang the praises of God with great enthusiasm. Three days later, they were grumbling about bitter water. We experience a remarkable answer to prayer and our hearts overflow with thanksgiving. Three days later, we are anxious and fretful about the next problem. The benefits of God are heaped upon us day after day, like manna falling from heaven, and yet we forget them almost as quickly as we receive them.

And so David takes himself by the hand and leads himself through a catalogue of divine benefits, that his forgetful soul might be awakened to the magnitude of God's goodness. "Who forgiveth all thine iniquities." This is the first benefit, and it is the greatest. Without this, all the others would be meaningless. For what profit is there in health, in prosperity, in long life, if the soul is still under the condemnation of a holy God? But forgiveness removes the condemnation. It cancels the debt. It tears up the indictment. It sets the prisoner free. And it does so not partially but completely: all thine iniquities. Not some. Not most. All. Every sin of thought, word, and deed. Every transgression of commission and omission. Every failure of the past, the present, and the future. All forgiven. All covered by the blood of the Lamb.

"Who healeth all thy diseases." The God who forgives is the God who heals. And while this certainly includes the healing of the body, which the Lord does according to His sovereign will and pleasure, it extends far beyond the body to the healing of the whole man. The diseases of the soul are far more deadly than the diseases of the flesh: the disease of pride, the disease of envy, the disease of lust, the disease of unbelief. These are the maladies that destroy men eternally, and from all of these the Great Physician offers a complete and effectual cure.

"Who redeemeth thy life from destruction." Redemption is the act of buying back what was lost. The believer's life was lost, forfeited by sin, sold under the dominion of death. But God reached down into the pit of destruction and redeemed it. He paid the price of redemption with the precious blood of His own Son. He brought the soul out of darkness and the shadow of death and set it upon the rock of eternal safety. And this redemption is not a temporary reprieve but a permanent deliverance. The life that God has redeemed shall never again fall under the dominion of destruction.

"Who crowneth thee with lovingkindness and tender mercies." The imagery here is breathtaking. God does not merely give His people lovingkindness and tender mercies as a servant might hand a gift to his master. He crowns them with these blessings. He places them upon the head of the believer as a king places a crown upon the head of a prince. The child of God is royalty, not by birth or by merit, but by the crowning grace of a generous Father. And the crown He wears is not gold or silver or precious stones, but something infinitely more valuable: the lovingkindness and tender mercies of the eternal God.

"Who satisfieth thy mouth with good things; so that thy youth is renewed like the eagle's." The God who crowns also satisfies. He fills the hungry soul with good things. He does not give His children stones when they ask for bread, or serpents when they ask for fish. He knows what they need, and He provides it with a lavish and unstinting hand. And the result of this divine provision is a perpetual renewal of strength. Like the eagle, which according to ancient belief renewed its vigour and soared to the heights with unfailing power, so the believer who feeds upon the good things of God finds his spiritual strength constantly replenished.

The psalm goes on to declare the character of this God whose benefits are so immeasurable. "The Lord is merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and plenteous in mercy. He hath not dealt with us after our sins; nor rewarded us according to our iniquities. For as the heaven is high above the earth, so great is his mercy toward them that fear him. As far as the east is from the west, so far hath he removed our transgressions from us."

Thomas Goodwin once said that if all the mercies of God were written in a book, the world itself could not contain the volumes. And yet David attempts the task, not to exhaust the catalogue, but to stir his own soul and ours to the worship that such mercies deserve. Bless the Lord, O my soul. And all that is within me, bless his holy name. Amen.
