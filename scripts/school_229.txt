SEGMENT 2: MAIN LESSON PART 1 -- BIBLICAL FOUNDATION
----------------------------------------

With our hearts prepared, let us move into the substance of today's lesson.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes in Psalm 119:105; 2 Timothy 3:16-17; Isaiah 40:8.

The Word of God is the supreme treasure of the Christian church, and the right of every believer to read it in their own language is one of the most precious gifts of the Reformation. The Bible is not a book for scholars and priests alone — it is the living voice of God speaking to every human heart, and its truths must be accessible to ploughboys and kings alike. The Scriptures themselves testify to their own power, sufficiency, and eternal permanence.

The Psalmist declares in Psalm 119:105, "Thy word is a lamp unto my feet, and a light unto my path." This metaphor speaks of guidance in darkness. The world is a dark place, morally and spiritually, and without the illumination of God's Word, we stumble and fall. The Bible does not merely inform the intellect — it guides the steps. It does not merely satisfy curiosity — it directs life. Every decision, every relationship, every crisis is illuminated when we bring it under the light of Scripture.

Psalm 119, the longest chapter in the Bible, is entirely devoted to the praise of God's Word. In its 176 verses, nearly every verse contains a reference to Scripture using one of several synonyms: law, testimonies, precepts, statutes, commandments, judgments, word, ordinances. The Psalmist loves God's Word with a passion that is almost overwhelming: "O how love I thy law! it is my meditation all the day" (v. 97). "How sweet are thy words unto my taste! yea, sweeter than honey to my mouth!" (v. 103). "Thy word have I hid in mine heart, that I might not sin against thee" (v. 11). This is not the attitude of a man who views Scripture as a burden or a duty — it is the attitude of a man who has found in God's Word the greatest treasure in the universe.

Paul's declaration in 2 Timothy 3:16-17 is the clearest statement of biblical inspiration in all of Scripture: "All scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness: That the man of God may be perfect, throughly furnished unto all good works." The phrase "given by inspiration of God" translates the Greek word theopneustos, which literally means "God-breathed." Scripture is not merely the product of human religious reflection — it is the very breath of God, His Word spoken through human instruments but carrying divine authority.

The fourfold profitability of Scripture that Paul identifies is comprehensive. It is profitable for doctrine — teaching us what is true. For reproof — showing us what is wrong. For correction — teaching us how to get right. For instruction in righteousness — showing us how to stay right. Together, these four functions make the man of God "perfect, throughly furnished unto all good works." The word "throughly" means completely, lacking nothing. Scripture is sufficient for everything the believer needs for faith and practice. We do not need additional revelations, traditions, or councils to supplement what God has spoken in His Word.

The prophet Isaiah proclaims the eternal permanence of God's Word in Isaiah 40:8: "The grass withereth, the flower fadeth: but the word of our God shall stand for ever." Empires rise and fall. Philosophies come and go. Scientific theories are proposed and overturned. Cultural fashions change from generation to generation. But the Word of God remains unchanged, unchallenged, and undiminished. Every attack against Scripture — from the Roman persecutions that burned Christian books, to the medieval church that locked the Bible away from common people, to the modern critics who deny its authority — has failed. The Word of God endures.

Peter echoes this truth in 1 Peter 1:24-25: "For all flesh is as grass, and all the glory of man as the flower of grass. The grass withereth, and the flower thereof falleth away: But the word of the Lord endureth for ever. And this is the word which by the gospel is preached unto you." The connection Peter draws between the eternal Word and the preached gospel is significant. The same Word that endures forever is the Word that is preached in the churches. When a faithful minister opens the Bible and expounds its truths, the congregation is hearing not merely a human lecture but the eternal, indestructible Word of God.

The Lord Jesus Himself affirmed the supreme authority of Scripture. He said, "The scripture cannot be broken" (John 10:35). He appealed to Scripture as the final authority in every dispute: "Have ye not read?" "It is written." "The scriptures must be fulfilled." He resisted Satan's temptations with the Word: "It is written, Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of God" (Matthew 4:4). He declared that "till heaven and earth pass, one jot or one tittle shall in no wise pass from the law, till all be fulfilled" (Matthew 5:18). If the Son of God treated Scripture with such reverence, how much more should we?

The Reformation was, at its core, a battle for the Bible. The medieval church had effectively removed the Scriptures from the hands of the common people. The Bible was available only in Latin, a language that ordinary people could not read. The church taught that only the clergy could interpret Scripture and that the church's tradition was equal in authority to the written Word. The reformers challenged both of these claims. They insisted that Scripture alone (sola Scriptura) is the supreme authority for faith and practice, and they devoted themselves to translating the Bible into the languages of the people so that every believer could read it, study it, and obey it for themselves.

The right to read the Bible in your own language is a right that was purchased with blood. Men and women were imprisoned, tortured, and burned at the stake for the crime of translating, distributing, or even possessing a Bible in the common tongue. We hold our English Bibles today because of their sacrifice, and we dishonor their memory when we neglect the Book they died to give us.

This biblical foundation is essential. Without it, everything else we study today would be like a house built on sand. But with it, we stand on solid rock.

SEGMENT 3: MAIN LESSON PART 2 -- PURITAN TEACHING
----------------------------------------

Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. William Tyndale wrote extensively on this subject in "The Obedience of a Christian Man; English Translation of the New Testament." The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life.

William Tyndale (c. 1494-1536) was the father of the English Bible and one of the greatest heroes of the English Reformation. His burning passion was to put the Word of God into the hands of every English-speaking person, and he pursued this goal with a brilliance, courage, and tenacity that ultimately cost him his life. Tyndale's famous declaration to a learned clergyman — "If God spare my life, ere many years I will cause a boy that driveth the plough shall know more of the Scripture than thou dost" — became the mission statement of his entire career.

Tyndale was a linguistic genius who mastered not only Latin, Greek, and Hebrew but also French, German, Italian, and Spanish. He was educated at Oxford and possibly Cambridge, and he was ordained as a priest. But his encounter with the Greek New Testament of Erasmus — published in 1516 — transformed his understanding of Christianity. Reading the New Testament in its original language, Tyndale discovered that the teachings of the Roman Church on many points were at odds with what the apostles had actually written. He became convinced that the corruption of the church was rooted in the people's ignorance of Scripture, and that the cure was to give them the Bible in their own tongue.

Tyndale sought permission to translate the New Testament into English but was refused by Cuthbert Tunstall, the Bishop of London. The English church and the English crown had no interest in an English Bible — they rightly perceived that a Bible-reading populace would quickly discover how far the church had strayed from apostolic Christianity. Denied permission in England, Tyndale went into exile on the continent, never to return.

Working in Germany and the Low Countries, often in hiding, frequently moving to evade arrest, Tyndale completed his translation of the New Testament from the original Greek in 1525-1526. Copies were smuggled into England in bales of cloth and flour barrels. The Bishop of London bought up copies to burn them — unwittingly providing Tyndale with funds to print more and better editions. The authorities confiscated and destroyed every copy they could find, but the Word of God could not be chained. Within a few years, Tyndale's New Testament had spread throughout England, transforming the spiritual landscape of the nation.

Tyndale's translation was a masterpiece of the English language. He had an extraordinary gift for finding English phrases that were at once faithful to the original Greek and beautiful in their simplicity. Many of Tyndale's phrases passed directly into the King James Version of 1611 and from there into the permanent vocabulary of English-speaking Christianity. Phrases like "Let there be light," "the powers that be," "my brother's keeper," "the salt of the earth," "the signs of the times," "the spirit is willing but the flesh is weak," and "eat, drink, and be merry" all originated with Tyndale. It has been estimated that approximately 83% of the King James New Testament and 76% of the Old Testament portions that Tyndale translated were carried over into the KJV with little or no change.

In addition to his translation work, Tyndale wrote several theological treatises that were widely read and deeply influential. "The Obedience of a Christian Man" (1528) argued for the supremacy of Scripture over church tradition and for the duty of obedience to civil authority — though always with the caveat that God's authority is supreme. "The Parable of the Wicked Mammon" (1528) expounded the doctrine of justification by faith alone. These works, along with his prologues and marginal notes to the biblical books, show Tyndale to be not merely a translator but a theologian of considerable depth and insight.

Tyndale continued his translation work, completing the Pentateuch (the first five books of the Old Testament) in 1530 and the book of Jonah shortly thereafter. He was working on the historical books of the Old Testament when his life was cut short. In 1535, Tyndale was betrayed by a man he had trusted, arrested in Antwerp, and imprisoned in the castle of Vilvoorde near Brussels. After more than a year of imprisonment, during which he was repeatedly interrogated, he was tried for heresy, condemned, and on October 6, 1536, strangled and then burned at the stake.

Tyndale's last words, according to John Foxe's "Book of Martyrs," were a prayer that echoed with prophetic power: "Lord, open the King of England's eyes." Within four years of Tyndale's death, Henry VIII authorized the Great Bible — which was largely based on Tyndale's translation — to be placed in every parish church in England. The ploughboy of whom Tyndale had spoken could now indeed know more of Scripture than the learned clergy who had opposed him. Tyndale's prayer was answered, though he did not live to see it.

The legacy of William Tyndale is incalculable. Every English Bible owes an enormous debt to his pioneering work. The King James Version, which shaped the English language and English-speaking Christianity for four centuries, is largely Tyndale's translation refined and completed by later scholars. More importantly, Tyndale's conviction that every person should be able to read the Bible in their own language became the driving force behind the worldwide Bible translation movement that continues to this day. Thousands of languages now have at least portions of Scripture translated into them, and the work continues — all of it flowing from the stream that Tyndale, at the cost of his life, set in motion.

Tyndale stands as a permanent rebuke to any complacency about the Bible. We live in an age of unprecedented Bible availability — multiple translations, study Bibles, apps, audio versions — and yet biblical literacy is in steep decline. Tyndale died so that the ploughboy could read God's Word. We owe it to his memory, and far more to the God whose Word it is, to read it, study it, love it, and obey it with the same passion that consumed the man who gave his life to translate it.

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.

SEGMENT 4: DISCUSSION AND APPLICATION
----------------------------------------

Let us take time to discuss and apply what we have studied.

Discussion Question 1: Psalm 119:105 calls God's Word 'a lamp unto my feet.' How does the Bible function as a guide in your daily life? In what areas do you most need its guidance?

Discussion Question 2: Tyndale said he wanted the ploughboy to know more Scripture than learned clergy. Why was it so revolutionary — and so threatening to the authorities — to put the Bible in the hands of common people?

Discussion Question 3: The authorities burned Tyndale's Bibles, yet the Word spread anyway. What does this tell us about the power of God's Word and the futility of trying to suppress it?

Discussion Question 4: How does 2 Timothy 3:16-17 address the claim that the Bible is merely a human book? What does 'God-breathed' mean for how we should treat Scripture?

Discussion Question 5: Tyndale's last prayer was 'Lord, open the King of England's eyes.' How was this prayer answered, and what does it teach us about God's faithfulness even when His servants do not live to see the fruit of their labors?

Take time with each question. The Puritans valued what they called "holy conference," the practice of discussing spiritual truths together. Do not settle for quick, surface answers. Push deeper. Ask follow-up questions. Listen carefully to one another.

Application is where truth becomes life. It is not enough to know what Psalm 119:105; 2 Timothy 3:16-17; Isaiah 40:8 says. We must ask: how does this change the way I live today? How does this shape my thoughts, my words, my actions? The Puritans insisted that a truth that does not transform is a truth that has not been truly understood.

SEGMENT 5: AFTERNOON ACTIVITY
----------------------------------------

Learning by doing was central to Puritan education. Here is today's hands-on activity.

Students will compare Tyndale's translation of selected passages with the King James Version and a modern translation. Using printed parallel columns, students will examine key verses (John 3:16, Romans 8:28, Ephesians 2:8-9, Matthew 6:9-13) in Tyndale's 1526 New Testament, the KJV (1611), and a modern version. Students should note similarities and differences in vocabulary, sentence structure, and meaning. They should identify Tyndale phrases that survived into the KJV. After the comparison exercise, students will write a one-page reflection on why translation matters — how even small word choices can affect the meaning of a passage, and why Tyndale's commitment to accuracy and clarity was so important for the English-speaking church.

Materials needed: Printed parallel text columns of selected passages in Tyndale, KJV, and modern versions, highlighters for marking similarities and differences, lined paper for reflection essay, dictionary of archaic English terms for reference, biographical timeline of Tyndale's life

As you complete this activity, think about how it connects to what you studied in today's lesson. The goal is not just to do something, but to reinforce the truths you have been learning. The Puritans believed that education should engage the whole person: mind, heart, and hands.

Take your time with this activity. Quality matters more than speed. When you are finished, reflect on what you learned through the process of doing. Often we understand something far better after we have tried to put it into practice.

SEGMENT 6: CROSS-SUBJECT CONNECTIONS
----------------------------------------

One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

Tyndale's work was set against the background of Henry VIII's England, the early English Reformation, and the larger European political landscape. Study the complex relationship between the English Reformation and politics — Henry's break with Rome was motivated by his desire for a divorce, not by theological conviction. Yet the political Reformation created space for the theological Reformation that Tyndale and others were pursuing. Examine how Tyndale's New Testament traveled from the continent to England through smuggling networks, and how the printing press made censorship increasingly impossible.

Literature Connection:

Tyndale's contribution to the English language is comparable to Shakespeare's. His translation decisions established the vocabulary, rhythm, and cadence of English biblical prose for generations. Analyze Tyndale's translation principles: his preference for Anglo-Saxon words over Latin ones, his sensitivity to rhythm and memorability, his commitment to literal accuracy while maintaining readability. Compare his prose with that of the later Douay-Rheims Catholic translation (1582), which used heavily Latinate vocabulary. Discuss how translation is an art that requires balancing fidelity to the original with accessibility to the reader.

Science Connection:

The invention of the printing press by Johannes Gutenberg around 1440 was the technological revolution that made the Reformation possible. Study the mechanics of movable-type printing and how it transformed the production and distribution of books. Before the press, a single Bible took months to copy by hand and cost as much as a house. After the press, thousands of copies could be produced in weeks at a fraction of the cost. Discuss how technological innovation can amplify the spread of ideas, for good or ill, and draw parallels with the impact of the internet on information distribution today.

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.

The Puritans memorized vast portions of Scripture, and it was one of the great sources of their spiritual strength. Even one verse, truly hidden in the heart, can sustain you in times of trial.
