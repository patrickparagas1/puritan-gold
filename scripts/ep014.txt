PURITAN GOLD — Episode 14
"Scripture Meditation: The Lost Discipline"
Week 2, Day 7 (Sunday) — Devotional

---

When was the last time you sat with a single verse of Scripture... for an entire hour?

Not skimming through a chapter. Not checking off your reading plan. Not listening to an audio Bible at double speed while you drive to work. I mean sitting. Still. Quiet. With one verse — maybe just a phrase — turning it over in your mind the way you'd turn a jewel in your hand, examining every facet, every angle, every flash of light.

If the answer is "never"... you're not alone. And you're not unusual. This practice — what the Puritans called meditation on Scripture — has become one of the most neglected disciplines in the modern church. We read the Bible. We study the Bible. Some of us even memorize the Bible. But very few of us meditate on it. And in losing this practice, we've lost something precious.

This is Puritan Gold. Today is Sunday — our day of rest and reflection — and I want to spend our time together recovering a discipline that was once central to the Christian life. A discipline that transformed the Puritans, that deepened their relationship with God, that made their theology not just something they knew but something they tasted. Something they felt in their bones.

Let's begin with a text. Proverbs 2, verses 1 through 5:

"My son, if thou wilt receive my words, and hide my commandments with thee; So that thou incline thine ear unto wisdom, and apply thine heart to understanding; Yea, if thou criest after knowledge, and liftest up thy voice for understanding; If thou seekest her as silver, and searchest for her as for hid treasures; Then shalt thou understand the fear of the Lord, and find the knowledge of God."

Read that slowly. Notice the verbs. Receive. Hide. Incline. Apply. Cry after. Lift up your voice. Seek. Search. These are not passive words. This is not a description of casual Bible reading over a cup of coffee. This is a portrait of someone who is digging. Who is hunting. Who is treating Scripture like hidden treasure — and who refuses to stop until they've found it.

That is meditation.

Now, I want to be very clear about what we mean by this word, because it carries baggage in our culture. When most people hear "meditation," they think of Eastern meditation — emptying the mind, achieving stillness, dissolving the self into some kind of cosmic consciousness. Biblical meditation is the exact opposite. It is not emptying the mind. It is filling the mind. Not with nothing, but with the Word of God. Not achieving blankness, but achieving depth. Not dissolving the self, but encountering the living God through His revealed truth.

The Puritans understood this distinction perfectly. For them, meditation was the bridge between reading and application. Reading put the text before your eyes. Meditation put it into your heart. And from the heart, it shaped the life.

Thomas Watson — that great Puritan pastor of the seventeenth century — wrote extensively about the discipline of meditation. He described it as the chewing of the cud. An earthy metaphor, but a perfect one. A cow, Watson noted, doesn't just eat its food once. It chews it, swallows it, brings it back up, and chews it again. And it's in that second chewing — that re-processing — that the nutrients are actually extracted. Reading Scripture is the first chewing. Meditation is the second. It's where the nourishment happens.

Watson laid out what we might call a method of meditation, though he would have bristled at anything too rigid. The basic practice looked something like this:

First, choose a text. Not a chapter. Not a book. A verse. Maybe a phrase. Something small enough to hold in your mind, rich enough to reward sustained attention. A verse like Proverbs 2:5 — "Then shalt thou understand the fear of the Lord, and find the knowledge of God."

Second, read it slowly. Then read it again. And again. Don't rush to commentary. Don't immediately reach for a study Bible or a sermon. Just let the words sit. Let them breathe. What is the verse saying? What does each word mean? Why did the Holy Spirit choose these specific words in this specific order?

Third, ask questions of the text. What does it mean to "understand the fear of the Lord"? What is the difference between understanding and merely knowing? What does it mean to "find" the knowledge of God — as opposed to just learning about Him? What kind of seeking is required? The passage says "as silver" and "as hid treasures" — what does that level of intensity look like in my actual life?

Fourth, apply the text to your heart. This is where meditation becomes personal. Not "what does this verse mean in general" but "what does this verse mean for me, today, in my specific circumstances?" Am I seeking God's knowledge with the intensity of a treasure hunter? Or am I treating it as optional — something nice to have if it happens to come easily? Where is my seeking falling short? Where is God calling me deeper?

Fifth, turn the text into prayer. This is the crown of meditation. The verse that you've been chewing on becomes the language of your conversation with God. "Lord, teach me to understand the fear of You. Help me to seek Your knowledge as treasure. Show me what I've been missing. Deepen my hunger for You."

Watson wrote that meditation without prayer is like a bow without a string — it cannot shoot. And prayer without meditation is like an arrow without aim — it cannot hit the mark. The two belong together. They are the inhale and exhale of the devotional life.

A.W. Pink echoed Watson's emphasis, though in his own distinctive voice. In Studies in the Scriptures, Pink argued that the great weakness of the modern church was superficiality in its treatment of Scripture. We read widely but not deeply. We cover chapters but don't plumb verses. We know the general storyline of the Bible but have never wrestled with a single text until it yielded its treasure.

Pink compared this to a man who owns a gold mine but only ever scratches the surface. There is wealth beyond imagining beneath his feet, but he never digs deep enough to find it. He walks over treasure every day and never bends down to pick it up.

How many of us are walking over treasure? How many times have you read through Proverbs — maybe even this week — and passed over a verse that, if you'd stopped and meditated on it, would have transformed your understanding of God, yourself, and your circumstances?

Now, I want to be practical here, because I know that for many of you, this sounds wonderful in theory but impossible in practice. You have jobs. You have children. You have responsibilities that begin the moment your alarm goes off. When are you supposed to sit with a single verse for an hour?

And here's my answer: Start small. You don't need an hour. Start with ten minutes. Start with five. The Puritans didn't develop their meditative depth overnight. They built it gradually, over years, through daily practice.

Here is what I'd suggest, practically:

Choose a time. Morning is ideal, if you can manage it — before the demands of the day crowd in. But any time is better than no time. Even five minutes before bed, sitting quietly with one verse, is a start.

Choose a place. Somewhere quiet, if possible. Somewhere you can be still. It doesn't have to be a dedicated prayer room. A kitchen table before the family wakes. A car in a parking lot before you walk into work. A bench in a park during your lunch break.

Choose a verse. For this week, I'd suggest Proverbs 2:5: "Then shalt thou understand the fear of the Lord, and find the knowledge of God." Just that one verse. For the whole week.

And then... sit with it. Read it. Reread it. Ask it questions. Let it ask you questions. Don't rush. Don't worry if your mind wanders — gently bring it back. Don't feel pressure to have a profound insight. Some days the treasure is right on the surface. Other days you have to dig. Both are valuable. Both are meditation.

Watson offered one more piece of counsel that I find deeply encouraging. He said that meditation is a skill, not a gift. It can be learned. It can be developed. You are not born a good meditator any more than you are born a good musician. It takes practice. And like any skill, the early days are the hardest. You will feel restless. You will feel like you're doing it wrong. You will be tempted to grab your phone, to check your email, to give up and go read something easier.

Don't give up. Press through. The treasure is there. And the deeper you dig, the richer it becomes.

The Puritans who practiced this discipline were not spiritual supermen. They were ordinary believers — pastors, merchants, farmers, mothers — who made a decision to go deep with God rather than skimming the surface. And the fruit of that decision was extraordinary. Their prayers were rich because their meditation was deep. Their lives were holy because their hearts were saturated with Scripture. Their theology was not cold and abstract but warm and living, because they didn't just read about God — they met Him in His Word.

Pink wrote that the Scriptures are like a mine that can never be exhausted. No matter how long you meditate, no matter how deep you dig, there is always more. Always another facet of the jewel you hadn't noticed. Always another layer of meaning that the Spirit is ready to reveal. The Bible is not a book you master. It is a book that masters you — if you'll let it.

Friends, I believe with all my heart that the recovery of this one discipline — the slow, prayerful, meditative reading of Scripture — could transform your walk with God. Not because the practice is magical, but because the Word is living. It is "quick, and powerful, and sharper than any two-edged sword," as Hebrews tells us. And when you give it time — when you stop rushing through it and start dwelling in it — it does its work. It pierces. It discerns. It comforts. It convicts. It changes you from the inside out.

Psalm 1 says that the blessed man is the one whose "delight is in the law of the Lord; and in His law doth he meditate day and night." Day and night. Not just a morning quiet time. A life saturated in Scripture. A mind that returns to God's Word throughout the day — while driving, while working, while lying awake at night. The verse you meditated on in the morning becomes the companion of your afternoon and the comfort of your evening.

That's the life I want. That's the life the Puritans modeled. And that's the life that's available to every single one of us — not through some advanced spiritual technique, but through the simple, ancient, powerful discipline of sitting with God's Word... and refusing to leave until it has done its work.

So this Sunday, as you rest from your labors and reflect on what God has taught you this week... try it. Take Proverbs 2:5. Sit with it. Turn it over. Chew on it. Pray through it. And see what the Spirit reveals.

I think you'll be surprised. No... I think you'll be changed.

Let us close with a benediction from the book of Numbers, chapter 6:

The Lord bless thee, and keep thee. The Lord make His face shine upon thee, and be gracious unto thee. The Lord lift up His countenance upon thee, and give thee peace. May His Word be your daily bread, your hidden treasure, and your heart's delight — this day and every day. Amen.
