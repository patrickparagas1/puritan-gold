PURITAN GOLD -- My Growth, Day 2
"The Discipline of Daily Prayer"
Pastoral Day — Practical Counsel on the Prayer Life


Welcome. Today is a Pastoral day — a day for practical spiritual counsel that you can apply immediately. Our subject is prayer: the lifeblood of the Christian, the breath of the soul, and the discipline that the Puritans considered more essential than any other.

Let us read Matthew 6:6 (KJV):

"But thou, when thou prayest, enter into thy closet, and when thou hast shut thy door, pray to thy Father which is in secret; and thy Father which seeth in secret shall reward thee openly."


The Puritans were people of prayer. They did not merely believe in prayer as a doctrine; they practiced it as a discipline. Their daily lives were structured around seasons of prayer — morning, midday, evening, and often in the watches of the night. They understood that prayer is not a supplement to the Christian life. It is the Christian life. Without it, everything else withers.

John Bunyan himself wrote that "prayer is a sincere, sensible, affectionate pouring out of the heart or soul to God, through Christ, in the strength and assistance of the Holy Spirit, for such things as God hath promised, or according to the Word of God, for the good of the church, with submission in faith to the will of God." Notice how comprehensive this definition is. Prayer involves the mind (sincere and sensible), the heart (affectionate), the will (pouring out), the mediator (through Christ), the enabler (in the strength of the Holy Spirit), and the standard (according to the Word of God).

The first counsel the Puritans offer is this: establish a fixed time and place for prayer. The "closet" that Jesus mentions in Matthew 6 is a private room — a designated space where you meet with God without distraction. Richard Baxter wrote that the Christian who has no regular place and time for prayer will pray irregularly and eventually not at all. He recommended morning prayer as the foundation of the day: "Give God the firstfruits of your time, and He will bless the remainder."

The second counsel is to pray with Scripture. The Puritans did not separate Bible reading from prayer. They read the Word and then prayed the Word back to God. If you are reading Psalm 23, you pray: "Lord, You are my shepherd. I shall not want. Help me to trust in Your provision today." If you are reading Romans 8, you pray: "Father, there is no condemnation for me in Christ Jesus. Free me from the guilt that still haunts me." Praying with Scripture keeps your prayers grounded in truth rather than floating in emotion.

The third counsel is to pray with honesty. Thomas Goodwin wrote that God is not honored by polished prayers that mask the true state of the heart. He is honored by raw, genuine, broken cries. "Lord, I do not want to pray right now, but I know I need You." "God, I am angry and confused, and I do not understand what You are doing." "Father, my faith is weak. Help my unbelief." The Psalms are full of this kind of prayer — David pouring out complaint, confusion, even accusation before God. And God received every word.

The fourth counsel is to pray with persistence. The Puritans warned against what they called "the sin of easy discouragement in prayer." Many Christians pray once for something and then give up when the answer does not come immediately. But Jesus taught persistence: "Ask, and it shall be given you; seek, and ye shall find; knock, and it shall be opened unto you" (Matthew 7:7). The verbs are continuous — keep asking, keep seeking, keep knocking. William Gurnall, in his massive work The Christian in Complete Armour, wrote that "the prayer closet is a battlefield, and the soldier who retreats at the first volley will never win the victory."

The fifth counsel is to pray with others. While private prayer is the foundation, the Puritans also highly valued corporate prayer — praying with your family, your spouse, your church, your small group. They believed that there is a power in united prayer that surpasses individual prayer. Matthew 18:19-20 was a cornerstone text for them: "If two of you shall agree on earth as touching any thing that they shall ask, it shall be done for them of my Father which is in heaven."

The sixth counsel is to pray with expectation. The Puritans prayed expecting God to answer — not always in the way they hoped, but always in the way that was best. Jeremiah Burroughs wrote that "the prayer of faith is the prayer that leaves the results with God but trusts that He will act." This is not presumption; it is confidence in the character of God. He hears. He cares. He responds. And His response, even when it differs from our request, is always good.

If your prayer life has grown cold, let this day be a fresh beginning. Set a time. Find a place. Open your Bible. Speak honestly. Persist courageously. And expect the God who sees in secret to reward openly.


Prayer is the breath of the soul. When you stop praying, your soul begins to suffocate. Today, take one step to revive your prayer life — even if that step is simply kneeling beside your bed tonight and saying, "Lord, teach me to pray." That is enough. He will meet you there. Father, draw us into the closet of prayer. Teach us to speak to You with honesty, persistence, and faith. In Jesus' name, Amen.
