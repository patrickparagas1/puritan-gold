PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 25
"Make Haste, My Beloved"
Song of Solomon 8:11-14 — The Song's Grand Finale

---

Welcome, beloved couple. Tonight we reach the closing verses of the Song of Solomon — the final words of the greatest love poem ever written. As the curtain falls, we hear one last exchange between the beloved and his bride, and it ends not with a resolution but with a longing. Listen.

Song of Solomon 8:11-14:

"Solomon had a vineyard at Baalhamon; he let out the vineyard unto keepers; every one for the fruit thereof was to bring a thousand pieces of silver. My vineyard, which is mine, is before me: thou, O Solomon, must have a thousand, and those that keep the fruit thereof two hundred. Thou that dwellest in the gardens, the companions hearken to thy voice: cause me to hear it. Make haste, my beloved, and be thou like to a roe or to a young hart upon the mountains of spices."

--- Teaching ---

"Solomon had a vineyard at Baalhamon; he let out the vineyard unto keepers." Solomon owned vast vineyards tended by hired workers. Each keeper owed a thousand pieces of silver — a staggering sum. But the bride draws a sharp distinction: "My vineyard, which is mine, is before me." Solomon may have his commercial vineyards, his empire, his wealth. But the bride has her vineyard — her own person, her own love, her own heart — and it is hers to give.

The Puritans found in this passage a beautiful assertion of personal agency within marriage. Thomas Gataker wrote that when a woman gives herself in marriage, she does not lose herself. She retains her vineyard — her identity, her dignity, her personhood. She gives it freely to her beloved, not under compulsion but from the sovereignty of love. And the same is true for the husband. Marriage is not the annihilation of the self but the free, voluntary gift of the self to another.

"Thou, O Solomon, must have a thousand." The bride acknowledges Solomon's wealth but is unimpressed by it. Her vineyard — her love — is not for sale. It cannot be purchased at any price. This echoes the great declaration of chapter eight, verse seven: "If a man would give all the substance of his house for love, it would utterly be contemned." Love transcends economy. It cannot be bartered, traded, or bought.

Richard Baxter applied this to the temptation that many couples face: treating marriage as a transaction. "I will love you if you love me. I will serve you if you serve me. I will be kind if you are kind." This is the economy of the marketplace, not the economy of the covenant. Covenant love gives freely, without conditions, without keeping accounts. The bride's vineyard is before her, and she gives it away — not for a thousand pieces of silver but for love itself.

"Thou that dwellest in the gardens, the companions hearken to thy voice: cause me to hear it." The bridegroom speaks to his bride: others may listen to your voice in the gardens, but let me hear it. Let your voice come to me. There is a yearning here that is remarkable. After all they have shared — the courtship, the wedding, the consummation, the separation, the reconciliation — the bridegroom still wants to hear her voice. He is not satisfied. He is not finished. He still longs.

William Gouge saw in this the perpetual nature of marital desire. He wrote that in the best marriages, desire does not decrease over time — it deepens. The longing to hear your spouse's voice, to know their thoughts, to be in their presence does not diminish with familiarity. It grows. But only if it is tended. Only if the garden is cultivated.

"Make haste, my beloved, and be thou like to a roe or to a young hart upon the mountains of spices." These are the final words of the Song. And they are words of longing. The bride calls to her beloved: Come quickly. Leap over the mountains. Do not delay. The Song does not end with a settled, satisfied conclusion. It ends with desire — with the bride reaching for her beloved, calling him to come.

The Puritans saw in this ending a profound theological truth: all earthly love, even the most beautiful, points beyond itself. The Song of Solomon, at its deepest level, is about the love between Christ and His church. And that love has not yet reached its consummation. We are still in the longing. We are still crying, "Make haste, my beloved." We are still waiting for the Bridegroom to come over the mountains.

Richard Sibbes wrote that the longing of a bride for her beloved is the nearest analogy in human experience to the church's longing for Christ's return. "Even so, come, Lord Jesus." The Song ends where the Bible ends — with a bride calling for her Bridegroom.

For your marriage, this means two things. First, your love is meant to point beyond itself — to the greater love of Christ for His people. Your marriage is an icon, a window through which the world sees the Gospel. Second, your love will never be fully satisfied in this life. There will always be a holy longing for more — more closeness, more understanding, more intimacy, more of each other. And that longing is not a failure. It is a sign that you were made for an eternal love that only Christ can fully provide.

Tonight, as we close the Song of Solomon, let the last word be a word of longing: Make haste, my beloved. Come quickly. The mountains of spices await.

--- Reflection Prompts ---

Turn to each other and consider these questions.

Question 1: As the Song of Solomon closes, what has been the most meaningful lesson for your marriage this month? Share it with each other.

Question 2: The Song ends with longing, not satisfaction. Where is the holy longing in your marriage — the desire for more closeness, more depth, more of each other? How can you honor that longing rather than suppress it?

Question 3: How does your marriage point to the greater love of Christ for His church? In what ways is your relationship a picture of the Gospel?

--- Prayer Focus ---

Tonight's prayer focus: Pray the final words of the Song over your lives: "Make haste, my beloved." Pray for the return of Christ, the ultimate Bridegroom. And pray that your marriage, until that day, would be a living picture of the love story between heaven and earth.

--- Closing ---

Make haste, my beloved. Be like a roe upon the mountains of spices. The Song is never truly finished — it echoes through every marriage that reflects the love of Christ. May its echo fill your home tonight and always. Goodnight, beloved couple.
