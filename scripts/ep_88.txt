PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"Let Every Thing That Hath Breath"
May 27, 2026

---

"Praise ye the Lord. Praise God in his sanctuary: praise him in the firmament of his power. Praise him for his mighty acts: praise him according to his excellent greatness. Praise him with the sound of the trumpet: praise him with the psaltery and harp. Praise him with the timbrel and dance: praise him with stringed instruments and organs. Praise him upon the loud cymbals: praise him upon the high sounding cymbals. Let every thing that hath breath praise the Lord. Praise ye the Lord." -- Psalm 150:1-6

We have come to the end of the Psalter, and the end is all praise. The Book of Psalms, which began with the quiet meditation of the blessed man under the shade of a tree by the rivers of water, concludes with a thundering, all-encompassing, universe-filling shout of adoration. Every instrument is summoned. Every voice is enlisted. Every creature that draws breath is commanded to join the chorus. The final word of the Book of Psalms is not a sigh but a shout, not a whisper but a roar, not a prayer but a praise. Hallelujah. Praise the Lord.

"Praise God in his sanctuary." The sanctuary is the temple, the place where God has chosen to make His presence known among His people. But for the Christian, the sanctuary is wherever God is worshipped in spirit and in truth. It is the gathered assembly of believers on the Lord's Day. It is the quiet room where the individual saint kneels in private prayer. It is the heart of every believer in which the Holy Spirit has taken up His permanent abode. Wherever God dwells, there is His sanctuary, and there His praise should resound.

"Praise him in the firmament of his power." The firmament, the vast expanse of the heavens, is itself a sanctuary of divine power. The stars praise Him by their brightness. The planets praise Him by their orderly movements. The galaxies praise Him by their inconceivable magnitude. And the believer who lifts his eyes to the heavens and sees in them the fingerprints of God joins his voice to a chorus that is already singing, a cosmic anthem that has been sounding since the morning stars sang together and all the sons of God shouted for joy.

"Praise him for his mighty acts." The mighty acts of God are the great deeds by which He has demonstrated His power and His grace throughout the course of history. The creation of the world. The call of Abraham. The deliverance of Israel from Egypt. The parting of the Red Sea. The giving of the law at Sinai. The conquest of Canaan. The establishment of David's throne. And supremely, above all and encompassing all, the incarnation, the life, the death, the resurrection, and the ascension of the Lord Jesus Christ. These are the mighty acts for which God is to be praised, and each one of them provides enough material for an eternity of adoration.

"Praise him according to his excellent greatness." This is the measure of our praise. Not according to our feeble comprehension of His greatness, not according to the limits of our vocabulary or the range of our emotions, but according to His excellent greatness. And since His greatness is infinite, the praise that is due to Him is infinite also. We shall never reach the end of it. We shall never exhaust the reasons for it. We shall never offer to God a praise that fully corresponds to the magnitude of what He deserves. But we are to aim at that standard, to stretch every faculty of our being toward that goal, to give Him the best and the most and the fullest praise of which we are capable, knowing that even at our best, we fall infinitely short of what is due.

And then the psalmist calls for instruments. Trumpet, psaltery, harp, timbrel, stringed instruments, organs, loud cymbals, high sounding cymbals. Every instrument that human ingenuity has devised is pressed into the service of praise. This is no place for aesthetic snobbery or liturgical minimalism. The praise of God is too great, too glorious, too overwhelming to be confined to a single instrument or a single style. It demands everything that the human race can bring to it, every sound that the human hand can produce, every melody that the human heart can conceive.

The Puritans, though they are sometimes caricatured as dour and joyless, were in fact passionate about the worship of God. They stripped away what they considered to be the empty ceremonies and superstitious accretions of medieval worship, not because they were opposed to beauty, but because they wanted the worship of God to be pure, genuine, and heartfelt. They knew that the most elaborate ritual is worthless without the engagement of the heart, and they also knew that the simplest praise offered from a sincere heart is more precious to God than all the pomp and ceremony of the grandest cathedral.

"Let every thing that hath breath praise the Lord." Here is the final, all-inclusive commandment. Not merely every person, not merely every angel, not merely every saint in glory, but every thing that hath breath. The birds of the air. The beasts of the field. The fish of the sea. The insects of the ground. Every creature that draws breath from the hand of the Almighty is summoned to praise Him. The whole creation, which now groans and travails in pain, waiting for the redemption that is to come, is called to anticipate that redemption with praise.

And let us, who have been given not merely the breath of physical life but the breath of spiritual life, the Holy Spirit Himself dwelling within us, let us lead this universal chorus. Let us praise God with our lips and with our lives. Let us praise Him in our worship and in our work. Let us praise Him in our joys and in our sorrows. Let us praise Him in our health and in our sickness. Let us praise Him in our abundance and in our poverty. Let us praise Him in the morning and in the evening, on the mountaintop and in the valley, in the sanctuary and in the marketplace. For He is worthy. He is worthy of all the praise that every breathing creature can offer, and more. Praise ye the Lord. Hallelujah. Amen.
