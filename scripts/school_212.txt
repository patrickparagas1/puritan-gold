Lesson 2: "Christ as Priest — His Sacrifice for Us"
The Work of Christ


Good morning, students. The fear of the Lord is the beginning of knowledge. That ancient truth is the foundation of everything we study here at the Puritan Academy. Let us begin our day with reverence and expectation.

Dear Lord, we come before You at the start of this school day. We are small and the world of knowledge is vast, but You hold it all in Your hand. Guide our thoughts, sharpen our attention, and give us understanding. Through Christ. Amen.

A moment of quiet meditation will prepare our hearts for deeper study.

Our Scripture for today is Hebrews 7:25-27:

"Wherefore he is able also to save them to the uttermost that come unto God by him, seeing he ever liveth to make intercession for them. For such an high priest became us, who is holy, harmless, undefiled, separate from sinners, and made higher than the heavens; Who needeth not daily, as those high priests, to offer up sacrifice, first for his own sins, and then for the people's: for this he did once, when he offered up himself."

This morning, remember that at this very moment, the Son of God is alive in heaven and praying for you. He knows your name. He knows your struggles. He knows your weaknesses and your fears. And He is presenting His blood before the Father on your behalf, securing your acceptance, your forgiveness, and your eternal salvation. You do not stand before God on the basis of your own merit but on the basis of Christ's perfect sacrifice. He is able to save you to the uttermost. Rest in His finished work and take courage from His living intercession. Your High Priest will never fail you.

As we prepare for today's lesson, remember that everything we study flows from this one fountain: the Word of God. Our lesson today is part of The Work of Christ, and it is titled: Christ as Priest — His Sacrifice for Us. Let us proceed with minds engaged and hearts open.


Now we come to the heart of today's curriculum.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes in Hebrews 7:25-27.

The priestly office of Christ is at the very heart of the gospel. If Christ as Prophet reveals God to us, Christ as Priest reconciles us to God. The book of Hebrews develops the priestly work of Christ more extensively than any other portion of Scripture, and Hebrews 7:25-27 provides one of the richest summaries of what it means that Jesus is our Great High Priest.

"Wherefore he is able also to save them to the uttermost that come unto God by him." The phrase "to the uttermost" translates the Greek "eis to panteles," which means completely, perfectly, entirely, and forever. Christ's saving power has no limit. There is no sinner so far gone that Christ cannot reach him. There is no sin so great that Christ's blood cannot cleanse it. There is no believer so weak that Christ's intercession cannot sustain him. He saves to the uttermost — from the first moment of faith to the last breath of life and beyond, into eternity.

"Seeing he ever liveth to make intercession for them" — this is one of the most comforting truths in all of Scripture. Christ's priestly work did not end at the cross. After His death, resurrection, and ascension, He sat down at the right hand of the Father, where He "ever liveth" — perpetually, without interruption — to intercede for His people. This means that at this very moment, the Son of God is presenting His people before the Father, pleading His blood on their behalf, and securing their acceptance in the courts of heaven. Romans 8:34 confirms this: "Who is he that condemneth? It is Christ that died, yea rather, that is risen again, who is even at the right hand of God, who also maketh intercession for us."

The qualifications of Christ as our High Priest are set forth in verse 26: "holy, harmless, undefiled, separate from sinners, and made higher than the heavens." Each word is significant. "Holy" — He is consecrated to God in every aspect of His being. "Harmless" — the Greek word "akakos" means without guile, free from malice, completely innocent. "Undefiled" — He is without any stain or contamination of sin. "Separate from sinners" — not in the sense that He avoided sinners (He was called "the friend of sinners," Matthew 11:19), but in the sense that He is qualitatively distinct from sinners in His moral purity. "Made higher than the heavens" — He has been exalted to the supreme position of authority and honor.

Hebrews 9:11-14 reveals the superiority of Christ's sacrifice over the Old Testament sacrificial system: "But Christ being come an high priest of good things to come, by a greater and more perfect tabernacle, not made with hands, that is to say, not of this building; Neither by the blood of goats and calves, but by his own blood he entered in once into the holy place, having obtained eternal redemption for us. For if the blood of bulls and of goats, and the ashes of an heifer sprinkling the unclean, sanctifieth to the purifying of the flesh: How much more shall the blood of Christ, who through the eternal Spirit offered himself without spot to God, purge your conscience from dead works to serve the living God?"

The animal sacrifices of the Old Testament were shadows pointing to the reality of Christ's sacrifice. They could purify ceremonially but not actually cleanse the conscience. They had to be repeated daily because they could never truly take away sin (Hebrews 10:4: "For it is not possible that the blood of bulls and of goats should take away sins"). But Christ offered "his own blood" — infinitely more precious than the blood of any animal. He entered not an earthly tabernacle but the heavenly sanctuary itself, the very presence of God. And He offered His sacrifice "once" — once for all, never to be repeated, because His single offering accomplished what millions of animal sacrifices could not: eternal redemption.

Isaiah 53:4-6 prophesied the substitutionary nature of Christ's priestly sacrifice with breathtaking clarity: "Surely he hath borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows: yet we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted. But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed. All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned every one to his own way; and the LORD hath laid on him the iniquity of us all." Every word here speaks of substitution — Christ in our place. Our griefs, He bore. Our sorrows, He carried. Our transgressions wounded Him. Our iniquities bruised Him. Our peace cost Him chastisement. Our healing came through His stripes. Our sins were laid upon Him by the Father's sovereign act.

This is the doctrine of penal substitutionary atonement — the teaching that Christ died in the place of sinners, bearing the penalty that their sins deserved, so that they might be forgiven and accepted by God. This is not a peripheral doctrine but the very center of the Christian faith. Without substitutionary atonement, there is no gospel. Without the cross, there is no salvation. Without the blood of Christ, there is no remission of sins.

Romans 8:34 ties together Christ's death, resurrection, and ongoing intercession: "Who is he that condemneth? It is Christ that died, yea rather, that is risen again, who is even at the right hand of God, who also maketh intercession for us." Paul's logic is devastating to every accusation against the believer. Who can condemn us? Not God — for He justified us. Not Christ — for He died for us, rose for us, and now intercedes for us. Not Satan — for Christ's blood silences every charge. The believer who rests in Christ's priestly work has nothing to fear from any accuser, in this world or the next.

This biblical foundation is essential. Without it, everything else we study today would be like a house built on sand. But with it, we stand on solid rock.


Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. John Flavel wrote extensively on this subject in "The Fountain of Life." The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life.

John Flavel's treatment of Christ's priestly office in "The Fountain of Life" is one of the most moving and theologically rich sections of the entire work. Flavel approached the priesthood of Christ not as a dry doctrinal topic but as a living, vital, soul-nourishing truth. He wrote: "The priestly office of Christ is the very soul of the gospel. Take away the priest and the sacrifice, and you have taken away all."

Flavel explored the meaning of sacrifice with particular depth. He observed that every sacrifice in the Old Testament pointed forward to the one true sacrifice that would be offered on Calvary. The Passover lamb, whose blood protected Israel from the angel of death (Exodus 12), foreshadowed Christ, "our passover," who was "sacrificed for us" (1 Corinthians 5:7). The scapegoat on the Day of Atonement, upon whose head the sins of the people were symbolically placed and which was sent away into the wilderness (Leviticus 16), foreshadowed Christ, who bore our sins "in his own body on the tree" (1 Peter 2:24) and carried them away forever. The daily burnt offerings, offered morning and evening without ceasing, foreshadowed the continual efficacy of Christ's once-for-all sacrifice.

Flavel was particularly eloquent in describing the voluntary nature of Christ's sacrifice. He emphasized that Christ was not a reluctant victim but a willing offering. Christ Himself declared: "Therefore doth my Father love me, because I lay down my life, that I might take it again. No man taketh it from me, but I lay it down of myself. I have power to lay it down, and I have power to take it again" (John 10:17-18). Flavel meditated deeply on what it meant for the eternal Son of God to willingly embrace the cross — the physical agony, the spiritual darkness, the separation from the Father, the bearing of the sins of all His people. He wrote that the willingness of Christ's sacrifice magnified its value infinitely, for a gift that is freely given is far more precious than one that is coerced.

Flavel also devoted extensive attention to Christ's ongoing intercession in heaven. He taught that Christ's intercession is not a mere formality or a general plea but a specific, personal, and effective ministry on behalf of each individual believer. Christ knows every one of His sheep by name (John 10:3). He knows their specific temptations, their particular weaknesses, their individual needs. And He prays for each one with perfect knowledge, perfect love, and perfect effectiveness. Flavel pointed to Christ's prayer for Peter in Luke 22:31-32 as an illustration: "Simon, Simon, behold, Satan hath desired to have you, that he may sift you as wheat: But I have prayed for thee, that thy faith fail not." Christ's intercession did not prevent Peter from falling, but it prevented Peter from falling away permanently. This is the security of every believer — not that they will never stumble, but that Christ's intercession ensures they will never ultimately fall.

Flavel addressed the sufficiency of Christ's sacrifice with great pastoral care. He knew that many sincere believers struggled with doubts about whether Christ's blood was truly enough to cleanse their particular sins. Flavel responded to these doubts by pointing to the infinite dignity of Christ's person. Because Christ is the eternal Son of God — infinite in value, infinite in worth, infinite in dignity — His sacrifice is of infinite merit. One drop of Christ's blood, Flavel argued, is of more value than the blood of all creatures combined, because it is the blood of one who is God as well as man. "Can your sins be greater than the blood of the Son of God?" Flavel asked. "Can your guilt outweigh the merit of His sacrifice? Impossible! The fountain of Christ's blood is deeper than the deepest ocean of human sin."

Thomas Goodwin, another Puritan divine, complemented Flavel's teaching by exploring the heavenly dimension of Christ's priesthood. Goodwin taught that Christ in heaven is not idle but actively engaged in presenting His people before the Father. He compared Christ's intercession to an advocate in a courtroom who presents evidence on behalf of his client. Christ presents His own blood — His own wounds — as evidence that the debt of sin has been fully paid and that the believer has been fully justified. The Father receives this evidence with perfect satisfaction, for Christ's sacrifice perfectly fulfills all the demands of divine justice.

The Puritans saw profound practical implications in Christ's priestly ministry. First, it provides the basis for boldness in prayer. Hebrews 4:16 invites us to "come boldly unto the throne of grace" — not because we are worthy in ourselves but because our great High Priest has opened the way. Second, it provides comfort in times of temptation. Because Christ was "in all points tempted like as we are, yet without sin" (Hebrews 4:15), He understands our struggles and provides grace precisely when we need it. Third, it provides assurance of final salvation. Because Christ "ever liveth to make intercession," no believer will be lost. The same Christ who died for us on the cross now lives for us in heaven, and His intercession is as effective as His sacrifice.

Flavel concluded his treatment of Christ's priestly office with a passionate appeal. He urged believers to find all their peace, all their confidence, and all their hope in the priestly work of Christ. Do not look to your own prayers, your own obedience, or your own spiritual attainments for acceptance before God. Look only to Christ — His perfect sacrifice, His prevailing intercession, His sufficient blood. Flavel wrote: "Christ is the meeting place between God and the soul. In Him, justice is satisfied and mercy flows free. In Him, the guilty are pardoned and the condemned are justified. In Him, and in Him alone, is the fountain of life that never runs dry.'

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.


Let us now explore additional dimensions of Christ's priestly work that will deepen our understanding and strengthen our souls.

The Westminster Larger Catechism, Question 44, asks: "How doth Christ execute the office of a priest?" The answer is: "Christ executeth the office of a priest, in his once offering himself a sacrifice without spot to God, to be a reconciliation for the sins of his people; and in making continual intercession for them." This concise statement captures the two great aspects of Christ's priestly work: His sacrifice and His intercession. Both are essential. The sacrifice without the intercession would leave us with a completed payment but no advocate to apply it. The intercession without the sacrifice would leave us with an advocate who has no basis on which to plead. Together, they constitute the complete priestly ministry that secures our eternal salvation.

Consider the depth of what transpired on the cross from the perspective of Christ's priestly office. In the Old Testament, the high priest was the mediator between God and the people. He stood in the gap, representing sinful humanity before a holy God. He bore the names of the twelve tribes on the stones of his breastplate, carrying them symbolically upon his heart into the presence of God. He sprinkled the blood of the sacrifice upon the mercy seat, making atonement for the sins of the people. Every detail of this elaborate ceremony pointed forward to what Christ would accomplish on Calvary.

But consider the astonishing uniqueness of Christ's priesthood. In the Old Testament system, the priest and the sacrifice were two different things — the priest was a man, and the sacrifice was an animal. But in Christ, the priest and the sacrifice are one and the same person. Hebrews 9:14 declares that Christ "through the eternal Spirit offered himself without spot to God." He is simultaneously the one who offers and the one who is offered. He is the priest at the altar and the lamb upon it. This is what gives His sacrifice its infinite value — it is not merely the blood of a spotless animal but the blood of the eternal, sinless Son of God, offered voluntarily in an act of perfect love and perfect obedience.

Hugh Martin, a Scottish theologian deeply influenced by the Puritan tradition, wrote extensively on the priestly work of Christ in his work "The Atonement." Martin explored the concept of Christ as both priest and sacrifice with remarkable theological precision. He argued that Christ's death was not a tragedy that happened to Him but a priestly act that He performed. When Christ went to the cross, He was not merely a victim of Roman cruelty or Jewish jealousy — He was a priest performing the supreme act of His priestly ministry. The cross was His altar. His body was His offering. His blood was the atoning sacrifice. And the cry "It is finished" (John 19:30) was the priest's declaration that the sacrifice had been accepted and the work of atonement was complete.

Thomas Goodwin, one of the most profound of the Puritan theologians, who served as Oliver Cromwell's chaplain and as president of Magdalen College, Oxford, devoted extensive attention to Christ's heavenly intercession in his work "The Heart of Christ in Heaven towards Sinners on Earth." Born in 1600, Goodwin studied at Christ's College, Cambridge, where he was converted under the preaching of Thomas Bainbridge. His writing is characterized by extraordinary theological depth combined with a tender pastoral heart. Goodwin taught that Christ's intercession in heaven is not merely a legal formality but an expression of His deepest affections toward His people. The same Christ who wept at the grave of Lazarus, who had compassion on the multitudes, who tenderly restored the fallen Peter — this same Christ, with the same heart of compassion, now intercedes for His people in the courts of heaven. Goodwin wrote that when a believer sins and the devil rushes to accuse him before God, Christ rises to his defense with the same urgency and tenderness with which a mother rushes to protect her child. His intercession is not cold or formal but passionate, personal, and effective.

Consider the remarkable passage in Hebrews 5:7-8: "Who in the days of his flesh, when he had offered up prayers and supplications with strong crying and tears unto him that was able to save him from death, and was heard in that he feared; Though he were a Son, yet learned he obedience by the things which he suffered." This passage reveals that Christ's priestly ministry involved real suffering, real tears, and real agony. His prayer in the Garden of Gethsemane — "O my Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me: nevertheless not as I will, but as thou wilt" (Matthew 26:39) — was the prayer of a priest who understood the full horror of what He was about to endure, yet submitted Himself willingly to the Father's will. The tears of Gethsemane sanctified the blood of Calvary. The agony of the garden prepared the way for the triumph of the cross.

John Owen, perhaps the greatest of all Puritan theologians, addressed the priestly work of Christ with unmatched theological rigor in his monumental work "The Death of Death in the Death of Christ." Owen, who was born in 1616 and served as vice-chancellor of Oxford University under Cromwell, argued with devastating logical precision that Christ's priestly sacrifice was not a general, undirected offering but a specific, intentional act performed on behalf of particular individuals chosen by the Father from before the foundation of the world. Owen's argument proceeds as follows: If Christ, as priest, offered His sacrifice for specific persons, and if that sacrifice actually accomplishes what it was intended to accomplish (namely, the full redemption of those for whom it was offered), then all those for whom Christ died will certainly be saved. Owen wrote: "The Father imposed His wrath due unto, and the Son underwent punishment for, either all the sins of all men, or all the sins of some men, or some sins of all men. If the last, then all men have some sins to answer for, and so none are saved. If the first, then why are not all men freed from the punishment due unto their sins? You answer, because of unbelief. I ask, is this unbelief a sin, or is it not? If it be, then Christ suffered the punishment due unto it, or He did not. If He did, why must that hinder them more than their other sins for which He died? If He did not, then He did not die for all their sins." This rigorous reasoning demonstrates the Puritan commitment to thinking carefully and precisely about the priestly work of Christ.

The practical comfort that flows from Christ's priestly work is inexhaustible. Consider the believer who has fallen into sin and is overwhelmed with guilt. What is his remedy? Not despair, but the priestly blood of Christ. "If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness" (1 John 1:9). Consider the believer who faces a powerful temptation and feels his strength failing. What is his resource? Not his own willpower, but the priestly intercession of Christ. "He is able also to save them to the uttermost that come unto God by him, seeing he ever liveth to make intercession for them" (Hebrews 7:25). Consider the believer who fears death and the judgment to come. What is his confidence? Not his own righteousness, but the priestly sacrifice that has fully satisfied the demands of divine justice. "There is therefore now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1).


Let us now draw out the practical implications of Christ's priestly ministry for our daily lives.

First, Christ's priesthood teaches us to pray with boldness. Hebrews 4:16 urges: "Let us therefore come boldly unto the throne of grace, that we may obtain mercy, and find grace to help in time of need." Because our great High Priest has opened the way into the very presence of God, we need not approach God with timidity or terror. We come as children who know they are accepted because of the work of their Elder Brother. We come as sinners who know they are forgiven because the blood has been shed. We come as needy souls who know that the throne before which they stand is a throne of grace, not a throne of judgment. Do you struggle with prayer? Do you feel unworthy to approach God? Remember your High Priest. He has gone before you into the heavenly sanctuary. He has opened the door. He is inviting you in. Come boldly.

Second, Christ's priesthood teaches us to find comfort in our weakness. Hebrews 4:15 assures us: "For we have not an high priest which cannot be touched with the feeling of our infirmities; but was in all points tempted like as we are, yet without sin." Jesus knows what it is to be tired, hungry, thirsty, lonely, misunderstood, rejected, and grieved. He experienced every category of human suffering during His earthly life. He is not a distant deity who observes our struggles from an infinite remove. He is a sympathetic priest who has walked the path before us and who understands, from personal experience, the full weight of what we endure. When you are hurting, He is not indifferent. When you are struggling, He is not impatient. When you fail, He is not surprised. He understands, and He intercedes.

Third, Christ's priesthood teaches us to rest in the finished work of the cross. The phrase "It is finished" (John 19:30) — in Greek, a single word, "tetelestai" — is the priest's declaration that the sacrifice has been completed and accepted. There is nothing to add to it. There is no further payment required. The debt has been paid in full. This truth should deliver us from the exhausting treadmill of trying to earn God's favor through our own efforts. We do not work for acceptance; we work from acceptance. We do not obey in order to be loved; we obey because we are loved. The finished work of Christ the Priest is the foundation on which the entire Christian life is built.

Fourth, Christ's priesthood teaches us to persevere through trials. Because Christ ever lives to intercede for us, we can be confident that no trial will ultimately destroy us. Peter denied Christ three times, yet Christ's intercession ensured that his faith did not permanently fail (Luke 22:32). We too will stumble and fall, but the intercession of our great High Priest guarantees that we will rise again. The Puritan Richard Sibbes wrote: "Christ's intercession in heaven is a continuation of His love on earth. He who loved us enough to die for us surely loves us enough to pray for us. And His prayers, unlike ours, are never weak, never misdirected, and never unheard."


Now we move from hearing to discussing. This is where knowledge becomes understanding.

Discussion Question 1: What does it mean that Christ saves 'to the uttermost' (Hebrews 7:25)? How does this truth address the fears of believers who doubt whether their salvation is secure?

Discussion Question 2: How does Christ's ongoing intercession in heaven differ from His once-for-all sacrifice on the cross? Why are both necessary?

Discussion Question 3: Isaiah 53 describes Christ as bearing 'our griefs,' 'our sorrows,' 'our transgressions,' and 'our iniquities.' What does this repetition of 'our' emphasize about the nature of Christ's sacrifice?

Discussion Question 4: Flavel said that Christ's intercession is personal and specific, not general and vague. How does knowing that Christ prays for you by name affect your confidence before God?

Discussion Question 5: How did the Old Testament sacrificial system prepare Israel to understand the meaning of Christ's death? What are the main points of comparison and contrast?

Take time with each question. The Puritans valued what they called "holy conference," the practice of discussing spiritual truths together. Do not settle for quick, surface answers. Push deeper. Ask follow-up questions. Listen carefully to one another.

Application is where truth becomes life. It is not enough to know what Hebrews 7:25-27 says. We must ask: how does this change the way I live today? How does this shape my thoughts, my words, my actions? The Puritans insisted that a truth that does not transform is a truth that has not been truly understood.


Now for the practical portion of our day. Here is today's activity.

Students will create a detailed comparison chart showing the parallels between the Old Testament sacrificial system and the sacrifice of Christ. Using a large sheet of paper divided into columns, students will compare: (1) the Passover Lamb with Christ as 'our Passover' (1 Corinthians 5:7), (2) the Day of Atonement offerings with Christ's once-for-all sacrifice (Hebrews 9-10), (3) the daily burnt offerings with the continual efficacy of Christ's blood, (4) the qualifications of Old Testament priests with the qualifications of Christ as High Priest (Hebrews 7:26), and (5) the earthly tabernacle with the heavenly sanctuary Christ entered. For each comparison, students should note both the similarities and the ways in which Christ's priesthood far surpasses the Old Testament system.

Materials needed: Large poster paper, rulers, colored markers, Bibles, printed copies of Hebrews 7-10 and Leviticus 16 for reference

As a supplementary exercise, students should write a reflective essay of at least three paragraphs answering the following question: "If I could sit in the heavenly courtroom and watch Christ intercede for me before the Father, what would I see and hear?" Use the biblical descriptions of Christ's heavenly ministry (Hebrews 7:25, Romans 8:34, 1 John 2:1) to inform your answer. Consider: What accusations might Satan bring against you? How would Christ respond? What evidence would He present? What would the Father's verdict be? This imaginative exercise, grounded in scriptural truth, will help you grasp the personal reality of Christ's ongoing priestly intercession. The Puritans frequently used such exercises of sanctified imagination to make abstract doctrines vivid and personal. John Bunyan, in "The Pilgrim's Progress," depicted Christian's visit to the Interpreter's House, where spiritual truths were presented through vivid visual scenes. In a similar manner, this exercise invites you to see with the eyes of faith the priestly work that Christ is performing for you at this very moment.

Additionally, students should study the detailed instructions for the Day of Atonement in Leviticus 16. Note the two goats: one was sacrificed, its blood sprinkled upon the mercy seat; the other — the scapegoat — had the sins of the people symbolically laid upon it by the high priest's hands and was then sent away into the wilderness, never to return. Both goats together picture the work of Christ: He both bears the punishment for our sins (the sacrificed goat) and carries our sins away from us forever (the scapegoat). Psalm 103:12 celebrates this removal: "As far as the east is from the west, so far hath he removed our transgressions from us." This is the double blessing of Christ's priestly work — not only is the penalty paid, but the guilt is removed. The believer stands before God not merely pardoned but cleansed, not merely acquitted but accepted, not merely forgiven but beloved.

As you complete this activity, think about how it connects to what you studied in today's lesson. The goal is not just to do something, but to reinforce the truths you have been learning. The Puritans believed that education should engage the whole person: mind, heart, and hands.

Take your time with this activity. Quality matters more than speed. When you are finished, reflect on what you learned through the process of doing. Often we understand something far better after we have tried to put it into practice.


One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

The sacrificial system was central to ancient Israelite worship and to the worship practices of many ancient cultures. Study how sacrifice functioned in ancient Near Eastern religions — the Mesopotamian, Egyptian, and Canaanite systems — and compare them with the Israelite system ordained by God. Discuss what was unique about the biblical sacrificial system, particularly the emphasis on substitutionary atonement and the prophetic anticipation of a perfect, final sacrifice. Study also the destruction of the Jerusalem temple in AD 70 by the Roman armies under Titus. This event, which occurred approximately forty years after Christ's death and resurrection, brought an end to the entire Old Testament sacrificial system. The temple was razed to the ground, the altar was destroyed, and the Levitical priesthood was scattered. From the perspective of the New Testament, this was not a tragedy but a divine confirmation that the shadows had given way to the substance. The Old Testament sacrificial system had served its purpose — it had pointed forward to Christ, the Lamb of God. Now that the true sacrifice had been offered, the shadows were no longer needed. The writer of Hebrews had anticipated this very thing when he wrote, "In that he saith, A new covenant, he hath made the first old. Now that which decayeth and waxeth old is ready to vanish away" (Hebrews 8:13). The fall of Jerusalem was God's stamp of finality upon the sufficiency of Christ's once-for-all priestly sacrifice.

Literature Connection:

The theme of sacrificial love — someone giving their life for another — is one of the most powerful and recurring themes in world literature. From the tale of Alcestis in Greek mythology to Sydney Carton in Dickens' "A Tale of Two Cities" to Aslan in Lewis's "The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe," the willing sacrifice of the innocent for the guilty resonates deeply with the human heart. Discuss why this theme is so universal and how it points to the supreme sacrifice of Christ on the cross. Consider also how John Milton, in his great Puritan epic "Paradise Lost," depicts the Son of God volunteering to take upon Himself the punishment due to fallen humanity. In Book Three, when the Father declares that justice must be satisfied and that mankind must die unless a substitute is found, the Son steps forward and offers Himself: the divine Priest volunteering to be the divine sacrifice. Milton's portrayal draws directly on the biblical account and powerfully illustrates the voluntary nature of Christ's priesthood. No one compelled Him. No one forced Him. He chose the cross out of love — love for the Father whose justice must be satisfied, and love for the sinners whose debt must be paid.

Science Connection:

The concept of substitution has applications in chemistry. In substitution reactions, one atom or group replaces another in a molecule, fundamentally changing the molecule's properties. In blood transfusions, healthy blood replaces diseased or lost blood, restoring health to the recipient. These scientific examples of substitution provide helpful analogies for understanding the theological concept of Christ's substitutionary atonement — His righteous life and sacrificial death taking the place of our sinful lives and deserved death. Consider also the medical concept of an organ transplant. A failing heart can be replaced with a healthy one, giving the recipient new life. But the healthy heart must come from a donor who has died. In a profound sense, this is what Christ has done: He has given us His righteous heart — His perfect obedience, His spotless character — at the cost of His own life. We live because He died. We are healthy because He bore our disease. We are whole because He was broken. The science of transplantation, which has saved countless lives through the sacrifice of donors, provides a powerful window into the gospel truth that life comes through death, and health comes through another's suffering. As the great hymn writer Isaac Watts wrote, meditating on the cross: "When I survey the wondrous cross / On which the Prince of glory died, / My richest gain I count but loss, / And pour contempt on all my pride." The cross is the operating table of heaven, where the Great Physician performed the surgery that saves sinners from eternal death.

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.


We have reached the final segment of today's lesson. The Puritans placed great value on review and memorization. They knew that what is not reviewed is soon forgotten. Let us take time now to consolidate what we have learned.

Review Questions:

Review Question 1: What does the phrase 'to the uttermost' in Hebrews 7:25 mean?

Review Question 2: What five qualities describe Christ as High Priest in Hebrews 7:26?

Review Question 3: How does Hebrews 9:11-14 contrast Christ's sacrifice with the Old Testament animal sacrifices?

"Wherefore he is able also to save them to the uttermost that come unto God by him, seeing he ever liveth to make intercession for them." -- Hebrews 7:25

Read this verse aloud five times. Then close your eyes and try to recite it from memory. Write it out by hand on a card or in your journal. Recite it to a family member or fellow student. The Puritans memorized vast portions of Scripture, and it was one of the great sources of their spiritual strength. They did not merely store verses in their memory as intellectual exercises but treasured them as weapons for spiritual warfare, as balm for wounded consciences, and as fuel for the fire of devotion. Even one verse, truly hidden in the heart, can sustain you in the darkest valley and give you confidence before the highest throne.

Closing Reflection:

As we close this lesson on Christ as Priest, let us consider the words of Samuel Rutherford, the beloved Scottish Puritan who endured persecution and exile for the sake of the gospel. Writing from his prison cell in Aberdeen, Rutherford penned these words to a friend: "I find it my duty to bear witness to the sufficiency of Christ's blood for the chief of sinners. He is a Priest whose sacrifice cannot fail, whose intercession cannot be denied, and whose love cannot be exhausted. I have tested Him in the furnace of affliction and found Him altogether faithful. He is the same yesterday, today, and forever."

Let us carry this confidence with us as we go. Christ the Priest has done everything necessary for our salvation. His sacrifice is complete. His intercession is ongoing. His blood is sufficient. We rest not on our own merit but on His finished work. May this truth be the anchor of your soul in every storm, the rock beneath your feet in every earthquake, and the light before your eyes in every darkness. Christ the Priest will never fail you. His sacrifice is eternally valid. His intercession is perpetually effective. His blood speaks better things than the blood of Abel. Rest in Him, and find peace.
