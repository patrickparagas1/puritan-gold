PURITAN GOLD -- My Growth, Day 17
"The Art of Meditation"
Pastoral Day — The Lost Discipline of Biblical Meditation

---

Welcome. Today is a Pastoral day. Our subject is meditation — not the emptying of the mind that Eastern religions promote, but the filling of the mind with God's truth that the Puritans practiced as a daily discipline.

Let us read Psalm 1:1-2 (KJV):

"Blessed is the man that walketh not in the counsel of the ungodly, nor standeth in the way of sinners, nor sitteth in the seat of the scornful. But his delight is in the law of the Lord; and in his law doth he meditate day and night."

--- Teaching ---

The Puritans were master meditators. They distinguished sharply between reading and meditating. Reading is like swallowing food whole; meditating is like chewing it slowly until the nutrients are absorbed. Thomas Watson wrote that a Christian may read much and yet be no better for it — like a man who takes medicine but does not digest it. Meditation is the soul's digestion.

Thomas Manton defined meditation as a serious intention of the mind whereby we come to search out the truth and settle it effectually upon the heart. Notice the two movements: searching out the truth (intellectual engagement) and settling it upon the heart (affectional application). Meditation begins with the mind but it must end with the heart. If your meditation leaves your heart unmoved, you have stopped too soon.

The Puritans recommended several kinds of meditation. First, deliberate meditation — setting aside a specific time to think deeply about a passage of Scripture, a doctrine, or an attribute of God. This is structured, intentional, and requires solitude. Baxter recommended at least thirty minutes daily for this practice, though he acknowledged that busy schedules might require flexibility.

Second, occasional meditation — using the events, objects, and circumstances of daily life as springboards for spiritual reflection. A sunset prompts meditation on God's beauty. A storm prompts meditation on God's power. A child's laughter prompts meditation on God's gift of joy. The Puritans trained themselves to see the spiritual in the ordinary — to find sermons in stones and books in running brooks, as it were. This transforms every moment into an opportunity for communion with God.

Third, ejaculatory prayer — brief, spontaneous prayers that arise from meditation throughout the day. "Lord, help me." "Father, I trust You." "Spirit, fill me." These arrow prayers, as the Puritans called them, keep the soul in constant connection with God even when sustained meditation is not possible.

The method of meditation the Puritans recommended involved several steps. First, choose your subject — a verse, a doctrine, a divine attribute, a spiritual duty. Second, read it carefully, noting every word and phrase. Third, consider it from multiple angles — What does it mean? Why does it matter? How does it apply to me? What does it reveal about God? Fourth, engage the affections — let the truth move you to love, gratitude, awe, repentance, or joy. Fifth, make specific applications — what will I do differently today because of this truth?

Edmund Calamy warned against two dangers in meditation. The first is wandering — letting the mind drift from the subject into random thoughts, worries, or plans. The remedy is gentle persistence: when you notice that your mind has wandered, simply bring it back without self-condemnation. The second danger is formalism — going through the motions of meditation without genuine engagement. The remedy is prayer: ask the Holy Spirit to open your eyes and warm your heart before you begin.

The fruit of regular meditation is transformation. The person who meditates daily on God's Word becomes, over time, a different person — more patient, more joyful, more discerning, more resilient, more Christ-like. Joshua 1:8 promises that the one who meditates on God's law day and night will be prosperous and successful — not in the world's terms, but in God's.

--- Closing ---

Meditation is the lost discipline of the modern church. Recover it today. Choose one verse. Sit with it. Chew it slowly. Let it nourish your soul. Lord, slow me down. Teach me to meditate — to chew Your Word until its sweetness fills me. Deliver me from the habit of skimming and the sin of rushing past Your voice. Speak, Lord, for Your servant is listening. Amen.
