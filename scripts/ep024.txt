PURITAN GOLD — Episode 24
"Wisdom Builds Her House"
Week 4, Day 3 (Wednesday) — Proverbs 14

---

There are two women in the book of Proverbs. You've met them before, perhaps without realizing it. One is Lady Wisdom — she stands at the crossroads, she calls out in the public square, she sets a feast and invites the simple to come and eat. The other is Lady Folly — she's loud, she's seductive, she promises pleasure, but her house leads down to the chambers of death.

And today, in Proverbs 14, Solomon opens with a line that draws the contrast between these two as sharply as a blade: "The wisest of women builds her house, but folly with her own hands tears it down." That's Proverbs 14, verse 1.

This is Puritan Gold, and we are in the middle of our Week Four journey through Proverbs 13 through 16. Today we turn to chapter 14 — a chapter rich with contrasts, warnings, and invitations. It's a chapter about building and tearing down, about the fear of the Lord and the deception of the human heart.

Let's walk through it together. Slowly. Carefully. The way you'd walk through a house that someone wise has built.

---

So... Proverbs 14:1. "The wisest of women builds her house, but folly with her own hands tears it down."

Now, while Solomon speaks of women here, the principle extends to every human being. Every one of us is either building or tearing down. Every day. With every choice, every word, every habit — we're either constructing something that will stand, or we're dismantling the very thing we claim to care about.

And notice the details. Wisdom builds. That takes time. That takes patience. That takes a thousand small, faithful decisions that nobody applauds. You build a house one brick at a time. You build a family one conversation at a time. You build a life of integrity one quiet obedience at a time.

But folly tears down — and she does it "with her own hands." That's the tragedy. Nobody forces the fool to destroy his life. He does it himself. She does it herself. With their own hands. Through laziness, through recklessness, through the slow erosion of careless living.

Matthew Henry, the great Puritan commentator, wrote on this verse that "a godly woman is the crown of her household, not by ambition, but by wisdom; not by demanding honor, but by deserving it through faithful, quiet industry." And I think that captures it perfectly. Building a house — a life, a family, a community of faith — is not glamorous work. But it is the most important work any of us will ever do.

---

Let's move to verse 12, because here Solomon issues one of the most sobering warnings in all of Proverbs: "There is a way that seems right to a man, but its end is the way to death."

Let that settle in for a moment.

There is a way that seems right. It feels right. It looks reasonable. It makes sense to the natural mind. And yet... its end is death.

This is the great deception of the human heart. Jeremiah 17:9 tells us, "The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately sick; who can understand it?" And Proverbs 14:12 illustrates that deception in vivid terms. You can be walking a path with absolute confidence — certain that you're heading in the right direction — and all the while, you're walking toward a cliff.

This is why we need something outside of ourselves to guide us. We need a lamp for our feet and a light for our path — and that lamp is the Word of God, not our own intuition.

The Puritans understood this deeply. John Owen, perhaps the greatest Puritan theologian who ever lived, wrote extensively about the deceitfulness of sin. He said that sin always presents itself in disguise. It comes to you dressed as freedom, as happiness, as fulfillment. It whispers, "This is the way. Walk in it." And if you listen to your heart instead of God's Word, you will follow it... right over the edge.

This is why Solomon keeps coming back to the fear of the Lord. Because the fear of the Lord is the only reliable compass in a world full of false paths.

---

And that brings us to verse 27 — one of the most magnificent verses in Proverbs 14: "The fear of the Lord is a fountain of life, that one may turn away from the snares of death."

A fountain of life. Not a trickle. Not a drip. A fountain. Overflowing. Abundant. Refreshing.

When you fear the Lord — when you hold Him in reverent awe, when you take His Word seriously, when you tremble at the thought of offending Him — something opens up inside you. A spring. A source of life that doesn't dry up when circumstances turn hard. The fear of the Lord gives you discernment to see the snares before you step in them. It gives you strength to say no when temptation whispers yes. It gives you peace in the midst of confusion, because you know that the One you fear is also the One who loves you most.

Spurgeon preached on this verse and said: "The fear of God is the beginning of wisdom, but it is also the middle and the end of it. It is not a stage you pass through — it is the atmosphere in which all true wisdom breathes."

And I love that image. The fear of the Lord isn't a phase. It's the air you breathe. It's the constant, underlying posture of the soul that knows it stands before a holy God. And from that posture, everything else flows — wisdom, courage, love, patience, humility.

---

Now, I want to draw your attention to verse 10, because it's one of the more poignant verses in this chapter: "The heart knows its own bitterness, and no stranger shares its joy."

There is a loneliness to the human experience that Solomon acknowledges here with extraordinary tenderness. You carry things inside you that no one else fully knows. There are sorrows in your heart that you've never been able to put into words. And there are joys — quiet, private joys — that no one else can fully enter into.

This isn't pessimism. This is realism. And it's a realism that should drive us to God — the only One who truly knows the heart. Psalm 139 says, "O Lord, you have searched me and known me... you discern my thoughts from afar." God knows your bitterness. He knows your joy. He doesn't need you to explain it perfectly. He already understands.

Richard Sibbes, the Puritan known as "the heavenly doctor," wrote beautifully about the compassion of Christ for bruised souls. He said that Christ came not to break the bruised reed or to quench the smoking flax. And that means this: whatever you carry in the hidden rooms of your heart, Christ is gentle enough to enter, wise enough to understand, and powerful enough to heal.

---

Let's look at a few more verses that round out the portrait of Proverbs 14.

Verse 29: "Whoever is slow to anger has great understanding, but he who has a hasty temper exalts folly."

Slowness to anger. This is not indifference. It's not passivity. It's strength under control. It's the kind of patient, measured response that comes from a soul grounded in the fear of the Lord. James 1:19 echoes this: "Let every person be quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger." And the reason is simple: anger, unchecked, makes fools of us all. It causes us to say things we can't unsay, to do things we can't undo. But the person who is slow to anger... that person has great understanding.

Verse 31: "Whoever oppresses a poor man insults his Maker, but he who is generous to the needy honors him."

This is striking. When you mistreat the poor, you're not just sinning against them — you're insulting God. Because God made them. They bear His image. And how you treat the vulnerable reveals what you truly believe about their Creator.

The Puritans took this seriously. Despite our caricature of them as dour and inward-looking, many Puritans were deeply involved in works of mercy. Cotton Mather, for all his complexity, wrote extensively about the duty of Christians to care for the poor, the sick, and the marginalized. He saw it as an essential expression of genuine faith.

And verse 34: "Righteousness exalts a nation, but sin is a reproach to any people."

This verse has echoed through centuries of political and social thought. But notice — Solomon isn't talking about military power, economic strength, or cultural influence. He's talking about righteousness. The thing that truly exalts a nation — a community, a family, a church — is not wealth or fame. It's righteousness. Right living before God.

---

So what do we take away from Proverbs 14? Let me offer three things.

First, build wisely. You are constructing something with your life — whether you realize it or not. Every decision is a brick. Every habit is a beam. Build with wisdom, with patience, with the fear of the Lord. And don't be deceived by paths that seem right but lead to death.

Second, fear the Lord. Let it be the fountain from which everything else flows. Let it be the compass that guides you when your heart is confused. Let it be the atmosphere in which you live and move and have your being.

And third, be honest about what's in your heart. Don't hide from God. He already knows your bitterness. He already knows your joy. Bring it all to Him — the things you can name and the things you can't. He is gentle. He is wise. And He is building something in you that will last forever.

Let's pray.

Gracious God, we confess that we are often foolish builders. We tear down with our own hands the very things You've called us to construct. Forgive us, Lord. Give us the wisdom of Proverbs 14 — the wisdom that builds, that fears You, that sees through the deceptions of our own hearts. Thank You that You know us fully — our bitterness and our joy — and that You love us still. Make us slow to anger, generous to the needy, and faithful in the quiet, unseen work of building lives that honor You. We pray in Christ's name, the Wisdom of God and the Power of God. Amen.
