PURITAN GOLD -- Study Topic: Female Piety, Part 2 of 10
"The Importance of Female Piety — Why Women's Spiritual Life Matters to All of Society"
John Angell James — Female Piety

---

Welcome back, dear listener, to Puritan Gold. We continue our study of Female Piety by John Angell James. In our first episode, we introduced the author and his pastoral vision. Today, we turn to one of his most urgent themes: the importance of female piety — not merely as a private virtue, but as a force that shapes families, churches, and entire nations. Let us attend carefully.

Let us read from Proverbs 14:1, in the King James Version:

"Every wise woman buildeth her house: but the foolish plucketh it down with her hands."

And from Titus 2:3-5:

"The aged women likewise, that they be in behaviour as becometh holiness, not false accusers, not given to much wine, teachers of good things; That they may teach the young women to be sober, to love their husbands, to love their children, To be discreet, chaste, keepers at home, good, obedient to their own husbands, that the word of God be not blasphemed."

--- Teaching ---

John Angell James understood that religion is not a private affair locked away in the closet of individual conscience. True piety, wherever it takes root, sends forth branches that shade and shelter all who stand nearby. And James was convinced that the piety of women, in particular, carries an influence out of all proportion to its visibility. The godly woman may never stand in a pulpit, may never write a theological treatise, may never hold public office — and yet her influence upon the course of human history, through her home, her children, and her quiet faithfulness, may exceed that of many who occupy the most prominent stations in life.

James opens his discussion of this theme by observing that women constitute one half of the human race, and that their spiritual condition therefore affects, directly or indirectly, every family, every congregation, and every community on the face of the earth. If the women of a nation are godly, James argues, the whole nation feels the effect. If they are worldly and irreligious, the whole nation suffers. This is not mere rhetoric; it is a sober assessment of the way God has ordered human society.

The Puritans before James had grasped this truth with equal clarity. Richard Baxter, in his Reformed Pastor, while addressing the duties of ministers, acknowledged that the godly wife and mother is often the most effective evangelist in the household. He observed that many a man has been brought to Christ not by the sermons of his minister but by the patient, prayerful example of his wife. Thomas Watson likewise wrote that the Christian home is a little church, and that the mother is in many respects the chief minister of that domestic congregation. She teaches by word and example, by prayer and presence, by the tone of her voice and the pattern of her daily life.

James carries this argument further. He contends that female piety is important for at least four reasons, and we shall examine each in turn.

First, female piety is important because of the formative influence women exert upon children. James writes with great feeling about the impressions made upon the young mind by a mother's faith. The child's earliest ideas of God, of prayer, of right and wrong, are shaped not in the schoolroom but at the mother's knee. Augustine's mother Monica prayed for him for decades before he was converted. The mother of John and Charles Wesley — Susanna Wesley — governed her household with such spiritual discipline and tenderness that her sons carried her influence with them into a revival that shook the English-speaking world. James believed that the conversion of a single mother might, through her children and her children's children, affect the destiny of generations yet unborn.

Second, female piety is important because of the sanctifying influence women exert upon their husbands. The apostle Peter, in the passage we read, speaks of wives whose chaste conversation, coupled with fear, might win their unbelieving husbands without a word. James comments upon this text at length, noting that the quiet, consistent godliness of a wife often accomplishes what the most powerful arguments cannot. A man may resist a sermon, but he cannot easily resist the daily witness of a life transformed by grace. The Puritan John Flavel once observed that the strongest arguments for religion are not those made in books but those lived out in homes. James would heartily agree.

Third, female piety is important because of the role women play in the life of the church. James was not an advocate of women preaching in public worship — he held to the apostolic order as he understood it — but he insisted that the contribution of godly women to the church is incalculable. They teach other women and children. They visit the sick and comfort the afflicted. They give generously to the poor. They sustain the prayer life of the congregation in ways that are often invisible to the world but visible to God. James observed that in nearly every revival of religion he had witnessed, the piety of women had been a leading instrument.

Fourth, female piety is important because of its influence upon the broader culture. James was writing at a time when the moral tone of English society was being shaped, for good and ill, by the domestic habits of its people. He believed that the godly woman, by ordering her household according to the Word of God, was contributing to the moral health of the nation. When homes are governed by faith, temperance, and love, the effects extend outward into neighborhoods, towns, and cities. When homes are governed by worldliness, selfishness, and irreligion, the effects are equally far-reaching, but in the opposite direction.

This is why James regarded the spiritual indifference of women as so dangerous. If the devil can capture the women of a generation, he has captured the nurseries, the kitchens, the bedsides, and the prayer closets — and with them, the future. James writes with an urgency born of pastoral experience: he had seen families disintegrate when mothers abandoned the faith, and he had seen families flourish when mothers held fast to Christ through every trial.

The prophet Isaiah, lamenting the spiritual decline of Judah, addressed the complacent women of his day with these searching words: "Rise up, ye women that are at ease; hear my voice, ye careless daughters; give ear unto my speech" (Isaiah 32:9). James echoes this prophetic call. He summons the women of his generation — and through them, the women of every generation — to recognize the weight of their spiritual responsibility and to embrace it not as a burden but as a sacred privilege.

Let us be clear: James is not placing upon women a load that God has not placed upon them. He is simply pointing out what Scripture plainly teaches — that the spiritual life of women matters enormously, that it has consequences far beyond what the eye can see, and that the cultivation of true piety in the female heart is a work of the highest importance for the glory of God and the good of mankind.

--- Application ---

1. Reflect upon the influence you exercise, even without intending it. Your words, your habits, your prayers, your attitudes — all of these shape the people around you. Ask the Lord to make you more conscious of the weight of your daily example.

2. Read Isaiah 32:9-20 and consider the prophet's call to the women of Judah. In what ways might you be "at ease" or "careless" in your spiritual life? Ask God to stir you to greater seriousness and devotion.

3. If you are a mother, consider the formative influence you have upon your children's souls. What habits of prayer, Scripture reading, and worship are you modeling for them? Identify one area where you can be more intentional this week.

4. Pray for the women in your church. Ask God to raise up a generation of women marked by deep, genuine piety — women whose faith strengthens families, sustains congregations, and blesses communities.

Stir our hearts to earnest piety, that our homes may be sanctuaries of Thy grace, our families schools of Thy truth, and our lives instruments of Thy glory.
