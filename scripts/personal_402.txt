PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Pastoral Encouragement, Day 2
"Leading Your Family in Worship"
Richard Baxter — Pastoral Counsel for Fathers


Welcome, friend. There are nights when what we need most is not more information but more encouragement. Tonight is one of those nights. Let the Lord speak directly to your heart.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Joshua 24:15:

"And if it seem evil unto you to serve the LORD, choose you this day whom ye will serve; whether the gods which your fathers served that were on the other side of the flood, or the gods of the Amorites, in whose land ye dwell: but as for me and my house, we will serve the LORD."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


Joshua's declaration is one of the most stirring statements of spiritual leadership in all of Scripture. He does not merely announce his personal commitment; he speaks for his entire household: 'As for me and my house, we will serve the LORD.' This is the calling of every Christian father: to lead his family in the worship and service of God.

Richard Baxter, writing in his practical masterpiece 'A Christian Directory,' addresses the duty of family worship with characteristic directness. Baxter argues that the father who neglects family worship is guilty of a grave sin, for he has been entrusted by God with the spiritual oversight of his household. Baxter writes that a family without worship is like a house without a roof: exposed to every spiritual danger and offering no shelter to those within.

Baxter outlines the basic elements of family worship. First, there should be the reading of Scripture. Not a hasty glance at a devotional book, but the systematic, reverent reading of God's Word. Baxter recommends reading through the Bible in order, so that the family is exposed to the whole counsel of God. He advises the father to read a portion and then briefly explain it in simple terms that even the youngest can grasp.

Second, there should be prayer. Baxter is practical: he acknowledges that many men feel inadequate to pray aloud before their families. His counsel is to begin simply. Pray the Lord's Prayer. Pray through the petitions of Scripture. Confess specific sins, give thanks for specific mercies, and make specific requests. Do not aim for eloquence; aim for sincerity. Baxter writes that God is more pleased with a stammering prayer from an honest heart than with polished phrases from a careless one.

Third, Baxter commends the singing of psalms and hymns. Music fixes truth in the memory and engages the affections in a way that mere reading cannot. Even families that are not musically gifted can sing simple melodies together. The psalms, Baxter notes, were given by God for precisely this purpose: to put His truth on the lips and in the hearts of His people.

Baxter also addresses the practical objections. 'I do not have time.' Baxter responds that the man who has time for meals but not for worship has disordered priorities. 'My children are too young.' Baxter responds that children are never too young to begin learning the sound of Scripture and prayer; the habits formed in infancy shape the soul for life. 'I do not know enough.' Baxter responds that the father does not need to be a theologian; he needs to be a faithful man who opens God's Word and speaks to God in prayer.

Baxter also warns against two extremes. The first is formalism: going through the motions of family worship without genuine engagement. The father who reads Scripture in a bored monotone and prays the same lifeless prayer every night is not truly leading worship; he is merely performing a ritual. The second extreme is perfectionism: waiting until conditions are ideal before beginning. The children are restless, the evening is busy, the father feels unprepared — there will always be reasons to postpone. Baxter's counsel is blunt: begin today, however imperfectly, and trust God to bless your obedience.

The Puritan Thomas Watson echoes Baxter's urgency: 'The family is the seminary of the church and the state. If family religion is neglected, all other religion will be superficial.' Watson argues that the man who leads his family in worship is doing more for the kingdom of God than many who are busy with public ministry but neglect their own households.

The challenge before you today is simple but weighty: are you leading your family in worship? If you are, take encouragement and press on. If you are not, there is no better day to begin than today. Start small. Read a chapter. Pray a prayer. Sing a psalm. Trust God with the results. The father who faithfully leads his family to the throne of grace is investing in eternity.


Before we close, take a few moments with these reflection questions.

Question 1: Am I consistently leading my family in worship, or have I allowed busyness and excuses to crowd out this sacred duty?

Question 2: What is the biggest practical obstacle to family worship in my home, and how can I address it this week?

Question 3: What would it look like for me to grow in this area — not to be perfect, but to be faithful and improving?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Father, forgive me for the times I have neglected the spiritual leadership of my home. Give me the courage to lead my family in worship, however imperfectly. Make our home a place where Your Word is read, Your praises are sung, and Your throne is approached in prayer. Amen.

Until next time. May the Lord deepen your faith with every passing day.
