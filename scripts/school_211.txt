SEGMENT 1: MORNING DEVOTION
----------------------------------------

Welcome, young scholars. Today we gather in the tradition of the great Puritan academies, where every subject was studied under the lordship of Christ. No knowledge is secular when it is received from the hand of God.

Our Father, the Puritans prayed before every lesson, and so do we. We ask for Your blessing on today's studies. Let the truths we learn take root in our hearts and produce fruit in our lives. We study not for our own glory, but for Yours. Amen.

Let us still our hearts with the Word of God before we proceed.

Our Scripture for today is Deuteronomy 18:15:

"The LORD thy God will raise up unto thee a Prophet from the midst of thee, of thy brethren, like unto me; unto him ye shall hearken."

This morning, open your Bible with fresh expectation. The same Christ who astonished the crowds with His teaching two thousand years ago desires to speak to you today through His Word. He is not a distant, silent God but a Prophet who actively reveals Himself to those who seek Him. Ask the Holy Spirit to open the eyes of your understanding as you read. Ask Christ the Prophet to teach you what you need to know for this day. He will not disappoint you. He who declared the invisible God to the world is ready to declare the will of God to your heart. Listen. He is speaking.

As we prepare for today's lesson, remember that everything we study flows from this one fountain: the Word of God. Our lesson today is part of The Work of Christ, and it is titled: Christ as Prophet — Learning from Him. Let us proceed with minds engaged and hearts open.

SEGMENT 2: MAIN LESSON PART 1 -- BIBLICAL FOUNDATION
----------------------------------------

Having laid the foundation in prayer and Scripture, let us build upon it.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes in Deuteronomy 18:15.

The Lord Jesus Christ fulfills three great offices that were established in the Old Testament: Prophet, Priest, and King. Understanding these three offices — what theologians call the "munus triplex" or threefold office — is essential for grasping the fullness of who Christ is and what He does for His people. We begin with Christ's prophetic office, which concerns His work of revealing God to us and teaching us the truth.

The foundational prophecy of Christ's prophetic office is found in Deuteronomy 18:15, where Moses declares, "The LORD thy God will raise up unto thee a Prophet from the midst of thee, of thy brethren, like unto me; unto him ye shall hearken." Moses was the greatest prophet of the Old Testament — the one to whom God spoke "face to face, as a man speaketh unto his friend" (Exodus 33:11) and through whom God gave the Law. Yet Moses himself prophesied that a greater Prophet was coming. This Prophet would be "from the midst of thee, of thy brethren" — that is, He would be a true Israelite, fully human, born among His people. And the command was absolute: "unto him ye shall hearken." To refuse to listen to this Prophet would be to refuse God Himself.

Peter identified Jesus as the fulfillment of this prophecy in Acts 3:22-23: "For Moses truly said unto the fathers, A prophet shall the Lord your God raise up unto you of your brethren, like unto me; him shall ye hear in all things whatsoever he shall say unto you. And it shall come to pass, that every soul, which will not hear that prophet, shall be destroyed from among the people." The stakes could not be higher. To reject Christ's prophetic teaching is not merely an intellectual error but a spiritual catastrophe.

John 1:14-18 reveals the unique qualification that makes Christ the supreme Prophet: "And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us, (and we beheld his glory, the glory as of the only begotten of the Father,) full of grace and truth... No man hath seen God at any time; the only begotten Son, which is in the bosom of the Father, he hath declared him." The Old Testament prophets spoke God's Word as it was revealed to them — they were messengers who delivered messages. But Christ did not merely deliver God's Word; He is God's Word. He did not merely speak about God; He is God. He did not merely point to the truth; He is the truth (John 14:6). The incarnation — God becoming man — is the ultimate prophetic act: God revealing Himself to humanity by becoming human.

The phrase "in the bosom of the Father" is extraordinarily intimate. It describes the eternal, personal, face-to-face communion between the Son and the Father. No created being has ever enjoyed this relationship. No prophet, no angel, no seraphim has ever been "in the bosom of the Father." Only the eternal Son, who is one with the Father in the unity of the Godhead, possesses this infinite, personal knowledge of God. And it is this Son who "hath declared him" — the Greek word is "exegesato," from which we get the word "exegesis." Christ is the exegesis of God — the one who interprets, explains, and makes known the invisible God to finite creatures.

Matthew 7:28-29 records the response of the crowds to Jesus' teaching: "And it came to pass, when Jesus had ended these sayings, the people were astonished at his doctrine: For he taught them as one having authority, and not as the scribes." The scribes and Pharisees taught by citing other authorities — "Rabbi so-and-so says this, and Rabbi such-and-such says that." But Jesus taught with direct, personal authority: "Ye have heard that it was said... but I say unto you" (Matthew 5:21-22). No prophet before or since has ever spoken with such authority. Jesus did not say "Thus saith the Lord" as the Old Testament prophets did. He said "I say unto you" — because He Himself is the Lord.

Hebrews 1:1-2 provides the definitive statement of Christ's prophetic supremacy: "God, who at sundry times and in divers manners spake in time past unto the fathers by the prophets, Hath in these last days spoken unto us by his Son, whom he hath appointed heir of all things, by whom also he made the worlds." The contrast is between partial and complete revelation. In the past, God spoke through many prophets at various times and in various ways — through visions, dreams, signs, and spoken words. But in these last days, He has spoken through His Son — the final, complete, and perfect revelation of God. The Son is not merely a messenger but the heir of all things and the Creator of the worlds. His prophetic word carries the full weight of divine authority.

Christ's prophetic ministry encompasses three dimensions. First, He reveals the character of God. In seeing Christ, we see the Father (John 14:9). His compassion, His holiness, His love, His justice, His mercy — all are visible in the life and ministry of Jesus. Second, He reveals the will of God. Through His teaching — the Sermon on the Mount, His parables, His direct instructions — Christ shows us how God wants His people to live. Third, He reveals the way of salvation. Christ is both the revealer and the content of the revelation. He does not merely tell us about salvation; He is salvation. "I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man cometh unto the Father, but by me" (John 14:6).

Christ's prophetic ministry continues today through His Word (the Scriptures) and His Spirit (the Holy Spirit). John 16:13 records Jesus' promise: "Howbeit when he, the Spirit of truth, is come, he will guide you into all truth." The Holy Spirit, working through the inspired Scriptures, continues to teach, illuminate, and guide God's people into the truth that Christ revealed. Every time we open the Bible and read it with understanding, we are benefiting from Christ's ongoing prophetic ministry. Every time a faithful pastor preaches the Word, Christ the Prophet is speaking through him to His people.

This biblical foundation is essential. Without it, everything else we study today would be like a house built on sand. But with it, we stand on solid rock.

SEGMENT 3: MAIN LESSON PART 2 -- PURITAN TEACHING
----------------------------------------

Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. John Flavel wrote extensively on this subject in "The Fountain of Life." The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life.

John Flavel (c. 1627-1691) was one of the most devoted and eloquent Puritan writers, and his masterwork "The Fountain of Life" is a rich meditation on the person and work of Jesus Christ. Flavel served as a minister in Dartmouth, England, until he was ejected from his pulpit in 1662 under the Act of Uniformity. He continued to preach in secret, often at great personal risk, holding clandestine services in the woods and homes of his congregation. His personal experience of suffering for the faith deepened his appreciation for Christ and gave his writing an urgency and warmth that has endured for centuries.

Flavel's treatment of Christ's prophetic office in "The Fountain of Life" begins with the observation that humanity's greatest need is not merely forgiveness of sin but knowledge of God. Sin has not only made us guilty; it has made us ignorant. The fall did not merely corrupt our moral character; it darkened our understanding. We are, by nature, blind to spiritual truth. Flavel wrote that fallen man is like a person locked in a dark room — even though the sun shines outside, he cannot see it because his eyes are closed and the shutters are barred. Christ comes as the Prophet who opens the shutters (by revealing truth externally through His Word) and opens the eyes (by illuminating the mind internally through His Spirit).

Flavel was careful to distinguish between Christ's prophetic work and the work of all other prophets. The Old Testament prophets were servants in God's house; Christ is the Son over God's house (Hebrews 3:5-6). The prophets received revelation from God and communicated it to the people; Christ is Himself the revelation. The prophets could speak God's Word to the ears; Christ, by His Spirit, can speak God's Word to the heart. Flavel emphasized that external teaching alone is insufficient for salvation. A person may hear the gospel preached a thousand times and remain unmoved. It is only when Christ the Prophet speaks internally — when the Holy Spirit illuminates the mind and opens the heart — that the Word takes root and produces faith. This is what happened on the day of Pentecost: the same Peter who had denied Christ was now preaching Christ, and three thousand were converted — not by Peter's eloquence but by the prophetic power of Christ working through the Spirit.

Flavel explored the manner of Christ's prophetic teaching with great insight. He observed that Christ teaches with absolute clarity. Whereas human teachers are often confused, contradictory, or obscure, Christ's teaching is perfectly clear and perfectly consistent. He never needs to correct Himself, retract a statement, or revise His theology. He observed that Christ teaches with infinite patience. He does not give up on slow learners or reject those who struggle to understand. The disciples were frequently confused and slow to comprehend, yet Christ continued to teach them, explain to them, and bear with them until understanding came. He observed that Christ teaches with personal application. His teaching is never merely theoretical or abstract — it always aims at the transformation of the heart and the reformation of the life.

Flavel also addressed Christ's prophetic teaching through types and shadows in the Old Testament. He argued that the entire Old Testament is a prophetic testimony to Christ. The sacrificial system points to Christ's atoning death. The priesthood points to Christ's intercessory ministry. The tabernacle and temple point to God's dwelling with His people through Christ. The feasts and festivals point to the great events of salvation history that Christ would accomplish. Flavel delighted in tracing these typological connections, showing how every thread of Old Testament revelation leads ultimately to Christ.

The practical implications of Christ's prophetic office, as Flavel developed them, are profound. First, believers must receive Christ's teaching with humble submission. Since He is the Prophet whom God has sent, and since God has commanded us to "hear him" (Matthew 17:5), we have no right to reject, modify, or ignore any of His teaching. Flavel was deeply concerned about the tendency of Christians to be selective in their obedience — accepting the parts of Christ's teaching that are comfortable and ignoring the parts that are demanding. He insisted that true faith receives the whole of Christ's revelation, not just the parts we prefer.

Second, believers must depend on Christ's illuminating work through the Holy Spirit. Reading the Bible without prayer for the Spirit's illumination is like reading in the dark. The same Christ who inspired the Scriptures must illuminate the reader's mind if the Scriptures are to be understood and applied. Flavel urged his hearers never to open the Bible without first asking Christ to open their understanding. He wrote: "The Word without the Spirit is but a dead letter; the Spirit without the Word leads to dangerous enthusiasm. But the Word and the Spirit together — this is the appointed means by which Christ the Prophet teaches His people."

Third, believers must share Christ's teaching with others. The light that Christ gives is not to be hidden under a bushel but set on a candlestick to give light to all who are in the house (Matthew 5:15). Flavel encouraged parents to teach their children, masters to teach their servants, and every Christian to be a witness to the truth of Christ in their daily interactions. The prophetic light of Christ is meant to spread — from generation to generation, from family to family, from nation to nation — until the knowledge of the Lord covers the earth as the waters cover the sea (Habakkuk 2:14).

Flavel's devotion to Christ the Prophet was not merely academic but intensely personal. He wrote of his own experience of Christ's teaching with passionate gratitude: "He found me in darkness and brought me into His marvelous light. He opened my blind eyes and caused me to see wonders in His Word. He spoke to my deaf ears and caused me to hear the voice of the Good Shepherd. All that I know of God, I owe to this blessed Prophet." This personal warmth, combined with theological depth and practical application, makes Flavel's treatment of Christ's prophetic office one of the finest in all of Puritan literature.

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.

SEGMENT 4: DISCUSSION AND APPLICATION
----------------------------------------

True learning requires reflection. Let us discuss today's key ideas.

Discussion Question 1: How is Christ's prophetic ministry different from and superior to the ministry of all the Old Testament prophets?

Discussion Question 2: John 1:18 says Christ 'hath declared' the Father. What does it mean that Christ is the 'exegesis' of God? How does the incarnation itself serve as revelation?

Discussion Question 3: Flavel said that Christ teaches both externally (through the Word) and internally (through the Spirit). Why are both necessary? What happens when we have one without the other?

Discussion Question 4: How does Christ continue to exercise His prophetic ministry today, even though He is no longer physically present on earth?

Discussion Question 5: Flavel urged believers to receive the 'whole of Christ's revelation, not just the parts we prefer.' In what areas are Christians today most tempted to be selective in their obedience to Christ's teaching?

Take time with each question. The Puritans valued what they called "holy conference," the practice of discussing spiritual truths together. Do not settle for quick, surface answers. Push deeper. Ask follow-up questions. Listen carefully to one another.

Application is where truth becomes life. It is not enough to know what Deuteronomy 18:15 says. We must ask: how does this change the way I live today? How does this shape my thoughts, my words, my actions? The Puritans insisted that a truth that does not transform is a truth that has not been truly understood.

SEGMENT 5: AFTERNOON ACTIVITY
----------------------------------------

The afternoon brings us to our activity time. Let us engage our hands as well as our minds.

Students will conduct a comparative study of Christ's prophetic teaching in the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5-7). Working in small groups, each group will be assigned one section of the Sermon: the Beatitudes (5:1-12), salt and light (5:13-16), the law fulfilled (5:17-48), prayer and fasting (6:1-18), treasures and anxiety (6:19-34), or judging and the two ways (7:1-29). Each group will prepare a presentation that includes: (1) a summary of what Christ teaches in their section, (2) how this teaching demonstrates His prophetic authority (noting where He says 'I say unto you'), (3) how this teaching reveals the character and will of God, and (4) a practical application for daily life. Groups will present their findings to the class, building a comprehensive picture of Christ the Prophet at work.

Materials needed: Bibles, poster paper for each group, markers, printed copies of Matthew 5-7 for marking up, notebooks for research notes

As you complete this activity, think about how it connects to what you studied in today's lesson. The goal is not just to do something, but to reinforce the truths you have been learning. The Puritans believed that education should engage the whole person: mind, heart, and hands.

Take your time with this activity. Quality matters more than speed. When you are finished, reflect on what you learned through the process of doing. Often we understand something far better after we have tried to put it into practice.

SEGMENT 6: CROSS-SUBJECT CONNECTIONS
----------------------------------------

One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

The prophetic tradition in Israel spanned over a thousand years, from Moses (c. 1400 BC) to Malachi (c. 430 BC). Study the role of prophets in Israelite society — they were not merely predictors of the future but forth-tellers of God's truth, often confronting kings and nations with God's demands for justice and righteousness. Compare the prophetic role in Israel with similar roles in other ancient cultures (oracles in Greece, seers in Mesopotamia) and discuss what made the biblical prophetic tradition unique.

Literature Connection:

The teaching of Jesus, particularly the parables, represents some of the most brilliant and influential literature in human history. The parable of the Good Samaritan, the Prodigal Son, the Sower and the Seed — these stories have shaped Western literature, art, and moral thinking for two thousand years. Study the literary techniques Jesus used: metaphor, hyperbole, irony, surprise endings, and vivid concrete imagery. Discuss why stories and images are often more powerful tools of communication than abstract argument.

Science Connection:

The concept of revelation — making hidden things known — has parallels in scientific discovery. Just as Christ reveals the hidden things of God, scientists reveal the hidden laws of nature through observation, experimentation, and theorization. Discuss the relationship between divine revelation (God making Himself known through Christ and Scripture) and natural revelation (God making Himself known through creation). Consider how the scientific enterprise itself depends on the assumption that the universe is intelligible — an assumption that is grounded in the biblical teaching that the world was created by a rational God.

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.

SEGMENT 7: EVENING REVIEW AND MEMORY WORK
----------------------------------------

We have reached the final segment of today's lesson. The Puritans placed great value on review and memorization. They knew that what is not reviewed is soon forgotten. Let us take time now to consolidate what we have learned.

Review Questions:

Review Question 1: What prophecy in Deuteronomy 18:15 did Christ fulfill as the great Prophet?

Review Question 2: According to John 1:14-18, what unique qualification does Christ have as the revealer of God?

Review Question 3: How did the crowds respond to Jesus' teaching in Matthew 7:28-29, and why?

Review Question 4: What three dimensions of Christ's prophetic ministry were identified in the lesson?

Review Question 5: How did Flavel explain the difference between Christ's external teaching through the Word and His internal teaching through the Spirit?

Write out your answers to these questions. Use complete sentences and support your answers with what you learned in today's lesson. If you are unsure of an answer, go back and review that section of the lesson.

Memory Verse:

"God, who at sundry times and in divers manners spake in time past unto the fathers by the prophets, Hath in these last days spoken unto us by his Son." -- Hebrews 1:1-2

Read this verse aloud five times. Then close your eyes and try to recite it from memory. Write it out by hand on a card or in your journal. The Puritans memorized vast portions of Scripture, and it was one of the great sources of their spiritual strength. Even one verse, truly hidden in the heart, can sustain you in times of trial.

That brings us to the end of today's study. The Puritans would close their school day with a prayer of thanksgiving. Let us do the same. Thank You, Lord, for the gift of learning. May we be faithful stewards of what we have received. Amen.
