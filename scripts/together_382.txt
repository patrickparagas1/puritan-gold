PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 21
"I Am My Beloved's"
Song of Solomon 7:10-13 — Mutual Desire and New Adventures

---

Welcome, beloved couple. We close our third week with a passage that breathes with desire, invitation, and the promise of new experiences shared together. This is love in its most outward-looking, adventurous form.

Song of Solomon 7:10-13:

"I am my beloved's, and his desire is toward me. Come, my beloved, let us go forth into the field; let us lodge in the villages. Let us get up early to the vineyards; let us see if the vine flourish, whether the tender grape appear, and the pomegranates bud forth: there will I give thee my loves. The mandrakes give a smell, and at our gates are all manner of pleasant fruits, new and old, which I have laid up for thee, O my beloved."

--- Teaching ---

"I am my beloved's, and his desire is toward me." This is the third and final iteration of the great declaration of mutual belonging that threads through the Song. In chapter two, the bride said, "My beloved is mine, and I am his." In chapter six, she reversed it: "I am my beloved's, and my beloved is mine." Now, in its final form, something new appears: desire. "His desire is toward me." She does not merely belong to him; she is desired by him. She is wanted. She is pursued.

The Hebrew word for "desire" here is the same word used in Genesis 3:16, where God says to the woman, "Thy desire shall be to thy husband." What was given as part of the curse — a longing that would be marked by pain — is here redeemed and restored. In the Song, desire is not a source of suffering but of delight. The bride does not ache with unrequited longing; she celebrates the reality that her beloved's desire flows toward her. The Puritans saw in this the redemptive power of the Gospel within marriage. Christ takes what sin has broken and makes it beautiful again.

William Gouge wrote that one of the deepest needs in marriage is to be desired — not merely tolerated, not merely accepted, but actively wanted. When your spouse knows that your desire is toward them — that you choose them not out of obligation but out of genuine longing — it meets a need that nothing else in the world can satisfy.

Then the bride issues an invitation: "Come, my beloved, let us go forth into the field; let us lodge in the villages." This is an invitation to leave the familiar and venture out together. The field, the villages, the vineyards — these represent new territory, shared adventure, the willingness to explore life together beyond the walls of home.

The Puritans valued shared experiences between husband and wife. Thomas Gataker wrote that couples who only occupy the same house but never go out together will eventually become strangers sharing a roof. He commended the practice of walking together, traveling together, worshipping together, and serving together. The marriage that stays in motion stays alive.

"Let us get up early to the vineyards." There is an urgency here. Not "let us sleep in" or "let us get to it eventually." The bride wants to rise early, to seize the day, to make the most of the time they have together. Richard Baxter wrote that time is marriage's most precious and most wasted resource. Many couples live as if they have forever, postponing connection, delaying adventure, putting off the things that matter most. The bride knows better. She says: let us get up early. Life is short. The vineyards will not wait.

"Let us see if the vine flourish, whether the tender grape appear, and the pomegranates bud forth." Again, the image of inspecting the garden — checking on the growth, looking for signs of new life. The healthiest marriages are those where both partners are actively looking for new fruit. "Did you notice how well we handled that disagreement?" "Did you see the way we served together this weekend?" "I think our prayer life is deeper than it was a year ago." These observations are the tender grapes and budding pomegranates of a growing marriage.

"There will I give thee my loves." In the fields, in the vineyards, in the fresh air of new adventures — that is where she will give him her love. Not just in the bedroom. Not just in the house. But in the wide world of shared life, she will love him. The Puritans understood that physical intimacy is enhanced by emotional, spiritual, and experiential intimacy. The couple that explores life together brings more passion home at night.

"At our gates are all manner of pleasant fruits, new and old, which I have laid up for thee." This is the bride's treasury — a collection of both old and new delights. The "old" fruits are the established joys of the marriage: the shared history, the inside jokes, the familiar rhythms of comfort. The "new" fruits are fresh discoveries, new experiences, ongoing surprises. A great marriage has both. It treasures the old while always adding the new.

Jeremiah Burroughs wrote that the Christian life should be marked by "new mercies every morning." The same applies to marriage. Do not settle for yesterday's manna. Look for today's fresh provision. Bring something new to your spouse — a new compliment, a new gesture, a new adventure, a new prayer. Keep the treasury stocked with both old and new.

--- Reflection Prompts ---

Turn to each other and consider these questions.

Question 1: Does your spouse know that your desire is toward them — not out of habit, but out of genuine, current longing? Find a way to express that tonight.

Question 2: When was the last time you went on an adventure together — even a small one? Plan one now. It does not have to be extravagant. A walk, a drive, a visit to a new place, an early morning coffee in the garden.

Question 3: What are the "old fruits" of your marriage that you treasure, and what "new fruits" would you like to add? Share with each other.

--- Prayer Focus ---

Tonight's prayer focus: Pray for desire and adventure in your marriage. Ask God to keep your longing for each other fresh and strong. Pray for opportunities to explore life together, to rise early, to find new vineyards and new fruit. Pray that your treasury would always be full — old and new, laid up for each other.

--- Closing ---

I am my beloved's, and his desire is toward me. What a gift to be desired. What a privilege to desire. Go forth into the field together, beloved couple, and discover what God has waiting for you there. Goodnight.
