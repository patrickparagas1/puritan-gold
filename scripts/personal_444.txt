PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Guided Puritan Reading, Day 13
"Christ's Heart Toward Backsliders"
Thomas Goodwin — The Heart of Christ, Chapter 4


Good evening. We are continuing our journey through one of the treasures of the Puritan library. These are not dusty old words. They are living truths that have the power to transform your walk with God.

Tonight we draw from the work of Thomas Goodwin, specifically from "The Heart of Christ in Heaven Towards Sinners on Earth." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Jeremiah 3:12-14:

"Go and proclaim these words toward the north, and say, Return, thou backsliding Israel, saith the LORD; and I will not cause mine anger to fall upon you: for I am merciful, saith the LORD, and I will not keep anger for ever. Only acknowledge thine iniquity, that thou hast transgressed against the LORD thy God, and hast scattered thy ways to the strangers under every green tree, and ye have not obeyed my voice, saith the LORD. Turn, O backsliding children, saith the LORD; for I am married unto you."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


We come now to what is perhaps the most pastorally significant section of Goodwin's treatise: his exploration of Christ's heart toward backsliders. This is the believer who has not merely stumbled once but has wandered — whose heart has grown cold, whose prayers have become infrequent, whose love for the things of God has diminished, who has perhaps fallen into a pattern of sin that seemed unthinkable in the fervor of earlier days. What does Christ feel toward such a one?

Goodwin's answer is breathtaking in its grace: Christ's heart toward the backslider is more tender, not less, than toward the believer who is walking faithfully. This seems paradoxical, even scandalous. Should not Christ be more displeased with the one who has wandered? Goodwin acknowledges the objection and responds with careful theological reasoning.

He begins with the nature of Christ's love. Christ's love for His people is not conditioned upon their performance. It is rooted in His eternal choice of them before the foundation of the world. When a believer backslides, Christ does not stop loving him; rather, His love takes on a particular quality — the quality of yearning, of pursuit, of heartbroken longing for the return of the wanderer. It is the love of the shepherd who leaves the ninety-nine to pursue the one lost sheep, not because the one is more valuable than the ninety-nine but because the one is in more danger.

Goodwin illustrates this from the prophecy of Hosea, where God pursues faithless Israel with the language of a wounded lover: 'How shall I give thee up, Ephraim? How shall I deliver thee, Israel? ... Mine heart is turned within me, my repentings are kindled together' (Hosea 11:8). Goodwin argues that this is not merely an Old Testament sentiment but a revelation of the very heart of God as it is expressed through Christ. The glorified Christ, looking down upon a backsliding believer, feels within Himself that same divine reluctance to give up, that same burning desire to reclaim and restore.

Goodwin then addresses the process by which Christ restores the backslider. It begins with conviction — the Spirit's work of making the wanderer aware of how far he has strayed. This conviction is not vindictive; it is the first expression of restoring love. Goodwin writes that the pain of conviction, though it feels like judgment, is actually the evidence that Christ has not abandoned the sinner. If Christ had given up, the conviction would cease. The very fact that your conscience is troubled is proof that Christ is still pursuing you.

After conviction comes the invitation to return. Goodwin dwells at length on the extraordinary tenderness of the divine invitation. 'Return, thou backsliding Israel ... I will not keep anger for ever.' Christ does not stand at a distance with arms crossed, waiting for the backslider to grovel. He goes out to meet him. He speaks first. He assures the wanderer that the way back is open, that forgiveness is available, that the relationship can be restored.

Goodwin makes the profound observation that Christ's argument for the backslider's return is not the backslider's merit but Christ's own character: 'for I am merciful.' The reason for returning is not that you deserve to come back but that He is the kind of Savior who receives wanderers. Your merit is not the ground of restoration; His mercy is.

Finally, Goodwin describes the joy of restoration. He points to the parable of the prodigal son, where the father does not merely accept the returning son but runs to him, embraces him, clothes him in the best robe, puts a ring on his finger, and throws a feast. Christ's response to the returning backslider is not grudging acceptance but lavish, joyful, celebratory welcome. There is joy in heaven over one sinner who repents — and Goodwin wants us to know that the joy is in Christ's own heart.

Goodwin also makes the striking observation that Christ's welcome of the returning backslider is often more lavish than His initial reception of the new convert. This is not because backsliding earns greater blessing — God forbid — but because the restoration reveals the depth of grace more vividly than the first conversion. The restored backslider knows, in a way that the new convert cannot, just how far grace can reach, just how deep mercy can go, just how wide the arms of Christ can stretch. Peter, after his denial and restoration, loved Christ with a fire that he had never known before, precisely because he had experienced the forgiveness of a sin he thought was beyond forgiveness.

If you are reading this as one who has wandered — whose heart has grown cold, whose walk has faltered, whose sin has become a pattern — hear the voice of Christ through Goodwin's pen: Return. The way is open. His anger will not fall upon you. His heart is turned within Him. He is married unto you, and the covenant stands. Only acknowledge your iniquity and come home.


Reflection is where truth becomes personal. Consider these questions carefully.

Question 1: Have I experienced seasons of spiritual backsliding, and what drew me back — was it a sense of Christ's pursuing love or merely fear of consequences?

Question 2: Is there an area of my life right now where I am drifting from Christ, and am I willing to acknowledge it honestly before Him?

Question 3: How does the truth that Christ's heart is more tender toward backsliders than toward the faithful challenge my understanding of His love?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Lord Jesus, I confess that my heart is prone to wander. There are times when my love grows cold, my prayers become hollow, and my walk becomes careless. Thank You that even in my wandering, Your heart pursues me with tender, relentless love. I return to You today, not because I deserve to come back, but because You are merciful. Receive me with the welcome of the prodigal's Father. Restore the joy of my salvation. Amen.

May the Lord guard your mind and your heart tonight. Until we meet again.
