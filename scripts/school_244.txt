"God's Outlaw — William Tyndale"
The Reformation — Lesson 4


Welcome back, students. Today we meet a man who gave his life so that you could read the Bible in English — William Tyndale, the father of the English Bible and one of the greatest martyrs of the Reformation. In our first lesson, we learned about John Wycliffe, who produced the first complete English Bible in the fourteenth century. But Wycliffe's Bible was translated from the Latin Vulgate, not from the original Hebrew and Greek. Moreover, it existed only in handwritten manuscript copies, each one painstakingly produced by a scribe over the course of many months. The invention of the printing press by Johannes Gutenberg around 1440 changed everything. For the first time in human history, it was possible to produce books in large quantities, quickly and inexpensively. And the man whom God raised up to place the printed English Bible into the hands of the common people was William Tyndale.


THE ENGLAND OF TYNDALE'S YOUTH

To understand Tyndale and the magnitude of his achievement, we must first understand the England into which he was born. The England of the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries was a deeply Catholic country. The church of Rome dominated every aspect of English life — religious, political, educational, and cultural. The archbishops of Canterbury and York were among the most powerful figures in the realm, second only to the king himself. Monasteries and abbeys owned vast tracts of land. Parish churches dotted the landscape, and the rhythms of medieval Catholic worship — the Mass, the saints' days, the cycle of confession and penance — structured the daily lives of the English people.

The Bible, in this world, was a closed book. The Latin Vulgate was read in churches, but in a language that the vast majority of the population could not understand. The Constitutions of Oxford, issued by Archbishop Thomas Arundel in 1407-1409 in response to the Lollard movement, had effectively banned the translation of the Bible into English without the approval of a bishop. Anyone found possessing an unauthorized English translation could be arrested, tried for heresy, and burned at the stake. The suppression of the Lollards in the fifteenth century had driven underground the desire for an English Bible, but it had not extinguished it. Lollard communities survived in secret in the towns and villages of the Midlands and southern England, passing worn copies of the Wycliffe Bible from hand to hand and keeping alive the flame of biblical Christianity until the day when a new translator would arise.

The prophetic words of Jesus Himself anticipated the situation perfectly: "Search the scriptures; for in them ye think ye have eternal life: and they are they which testify of me" (John 5:39). The people of England could not search the Scriptures because the Scriptures were hidden from them. They could not come to Christ through His Word because that Word was locked away in a language they could not read. The institutional church, which was charged with the care of souls, had become the jailer of the very truth that could set those souls free.

That translator was William Tyndale, and his work would transform not only the English church but the English language itself.

TYNDALE'S EARLY LIFE AND EDUCATION

William Tyndale was born around the year 1494 in or near the town of Dursley in Gloucestershire, in the west of England, near the Welsh border. His family — originally named Hychyns — had connections to the Tyndale family of Northumberland, a family of some local prominence. Little is known of his childhood, but his intellectual gifts were evident from an early age.

Tyndale entered Magdalen Hall at the University of Oxford around 1506, at approximately twelve years of age — not unusual for the period. He studied at Oxford for nearly a decade, earning his Bachelor of Arts degree in 1512 and his Master of Arts in 1515. Oxford in this period was beginning to feel the influence of the Renaissance humanism that was transforming European scholarship. The study of Greek and Hebrew — the original languages of the Old and New Testaments — was gaining ground, though it was still viewed with suspicion by the conservative theological faculty, who feared that access to the original texts would undermine the authority of the Latin Vulgate and, by extension, the authority of the church that had authorized it.

Tyndale's years at Oxford were formative. The university was beginning to be touched by the new learning — the humanist emphasis on returning to the original sources of classical and biblical literature. The cry of the humanists was ad fontes — "to the sources!" — meaning that scholars should study the great texts of antiquity in their original languages rather than through the often distorted lens of medieval commentaries and translations. For biblical studies, this meant going beyond the Latin Vulgate to study the Old Testament in Hebrew and the New Testament in Greek. This principle — which seems obvious to us today — was revolutionary in the early sixteenth century and would prove to be the intellectual foundation of Tyndale's life's work.

The great humanist scholar Erasmus of Rotterdam visited Oxford during this period and lectured on Greek, inspiring a new generation of scholars to take up the study of the original languages of Scripture. Erasmus's Greek New Testament, published in 1516, would become the textual foundation for Tyndale's English translation. Though Tyndale may not have met Erasmus in person at Oxford, the Dutch scholar's influence was pervasive, and Tyndale certainly absorbed the humanist conviction that the Bible must be read — and translated — from its original languages.

After completing his Master's degree at Oxford, Tyndale moved to the University of Cambridge, probably sometime around 1517-1521. Cambridge in this period was a hotbed of evangelical ferment. A group of scholars who had been influenced by the writings of Erasmus and Luther met regularly at the White Horse Tavern — a gathering place that was nicknamed "Little Germany" for its association with Lutheran ideas. Although Tyndale's presence at these meetings cannot be definitively confirmed, it is highly probable that he was among the Cambridge scholars who were reading Luther's writings and discussing the implications of the Reformation for England.

Tyndale was a linguist of extraordinary ability. He was fluent in at least eight languages: English, Latin, Greek, Hebrew, German, French, Italian, and Spanish. John Foxe, in his Acts and Monuments, noted that Tyndale was "so skilled in eight languages — Hebrew, Greek, Latin, Italian, Spanish, English, French, and German — that whichever he spoke, you would suppose it his native tongue." This remarkable linguistic gift was the indispensable tool that God would use to accomplish the great work of Tyndale's life.

After leaving Cambridge, Tyndale served as a tutor in the household of Sir John Walsh at Little Sodbury Manor in Gloucestershire, probably from 1521 to 1523. It was during this period that Tyndale's evangelical convictions fully crystallized. He preached in the villages around Little Sodbury, teaching the Scriptures in English to the common people. He engaged in heated disputes with the local clergy, whose ignorance of the Bible appalled him. It was in one of these disputes that Tyndale made the statement that would define his life's mission.

THE YEARS AT LITTLE SODBURY AND THE CALL TO TRANSLATE

The years at Little Sodbury Manor were a turning point in Tyndale's life. Sir John Walsh was a generous host who frequently entertained the local clergy at his table. Tyndale participated in these conversations and was repeatedly shocked by the ignorance of the priests and monks who claimed spiritual authority over the people. He later wrote of these encounters: "I began to smell the Word of God, and marked how Scripture was abused." The clergy quoted church tradition, papal decrees, and the writings of the schoolmen — the medieval scholastic theologians — but they showed little knowledge of the Bible itself. When Tyndale pointed to what the Scriptures actually said, the clergy either could not answer or dismissed the Bible as irrelevant compared to the pronouncements of the church.

It was in this context that Tyndale made the vow that would define his life's work. The medieval church had created a system in which the people were entirely dependent on the clergy for their understanding of God, salvation, and the Christian life. The Latin Bible was inaccessible to the laity, and any attempt to translate it into English was forbidden by law. The result was a population that was spiritually enslaved — bound not by the truth of God's Word but by the traditions, superstitions, and self-serving teachings of an often corrupt and ignorant clergy. The words of the prophet Hosea applied with devastating accuracy: "My people are destroyed for lack of knowledge" (Hosea 4:6). Tyndale was determined to remedy that lack of knowledge by placing the Scriptures directly into the hands of the people.

Foxe records that during a heated argument with a clergyman who asserted the supremacy of papal authority, Tyndale declared: "If God spare my life, ere many years I will cause a boy that driveth the plough shall know more of the Scripture than thou doest." This was not idle boasting. It was a sacred vow — a declaration of intent to place the Word of God into the hands of the humblest people in the land, regardless of the cost. Tyndale was echoing the vision of Erasmus, who had written in the preface to his Greek New Testament in 1516: "I wish that every woman might read the Gospel and the epistles of Paul. I wish they were translated into all languages of all people, so that not only the Scots and Irish, but also the Turk and the Saracen might read and understand them. I wish the farmer might sing parts of them at his plow."

But Tyndale was not merely wishing. He was committing himself to the work of translation — a work that would consume the rest of his life and ultimately cost him his life.

The first step was to secure official support. In 1523, Tyndale traveled to London to seek the patronage of Cuthbert Tunstall, the Bishop of London. Tunstall was a distinguished humanist scholar who had been a friend of Erasmus and who Tyndale hoped would be sympathetic to the cause of an English Bible. But Tunstall refused. He told Tyndale that there was no room in his household for such a project. The English ecclesiastical establishment, fearful of the Lollard precedent and alarmed by the spread of Lutheran ideas, was implacably opposed to any vernacular translation of the Scriptures.

Tyndale spent nearly a year in London, lodging with a wealthy cloth merchant named Humphrey Monmouth, who became his patron and protector. During this period, Tyndale came to a momentous realization. He later wrote: "I perceived that it was impossible to establish the lay people in any truth, except the Scripture were plainly laid before their eyes in their mother tongue." And he concluded: "There was no place to do it in all England."

THE EXILE AND THE TRANSLATION

The decision to leave England was a momentous one. Tyndale was leaving behind everything familiar — his country, his family, his friends, his career — and entering a life of exile, danger, and uncertainty. He would spend the next twelve years as a fugitive, hunted by the agents of the English crown and the ecclesiastical authorities, moving from city to city, working in secret, and trusting in the providence of God to protect him and his work. The parallels with the experience of the apostle Paul are striking. Paul wrote to the Corinthians: "In journeyings often, in perils of waters, in perils of robbers, in perils by mine own countrymen, in perils by the heathen, in perils in the city, in perils in the wilderness, in perils in the sea, in perils among false brethren; In weariness and painfulness, in watchings often, in hunger and thirst, in fastings often, in cold and nakedness" (2 Corinthians 11:26-27). Tyndale endured every one of these perils in the service of his calling.

In 1524, Tyndale left England for the Continent — never to return. He sailed to Hamburg, Germany, and from there made his way to Wittenberg, the epicenter of the Reformation. Although there is no conclusive documentary evidence that Tyndale met Martin Luther in person, it is highly probable that the two men had some contact during Tyndale's months in Wittenberg. Tyndale certainly knew Luther's writings intimately, and his own theological positions — especially on justification by faith and the authority of Scripture — were deeply influenced by the German reformer. The University of Wittenberg's matriculation records include the name "Guillelmus Daltici ex Anglia" in 1524, which many scholars believe is an alias used by Tyndale to conceal his identity.

From Wittenberg, Tyndale moved to Cologne, where he began the work of printing his English New Testament in 1525. He hired a printer named Peter Quentel and the work progressed to the printing of the first ten sheets of the Gospel of Matthew. But the project was betrayed. Johann Cochlaeus, an opponent of the Reformation, discovered what Tyndale was doing and alerted the authorities. Tyndale was forced to flee with the printed sheets to the city of Worms, where he found a more sympathetic printer, Peter Schoeffer, and completed the printing of his New Testament in early 1526.

The escape from Cologne to Worms is one of the most dramatic episodes in the history of Bible translation. Tyndale and his assistant William Roye fled up the Rhine River with the printed sheets they had managed to save, traveling by boat through some of the most beautiful — and most dangerous — territory in Europe. At any point, they could have been apprehended by imperial authorities sympathetic to Rome. But God preserved them, and they reached Worms safely. Worms was a fitting location for the completion of Tyndale's work, for it was in this very city, just five years earlier, that Martin Luther had made his famous stand before the Imperial Diet, declaring: "Here I stand. I can do no other." The city that had witnessed Luther's defiance of Rome would now witness another act of defiance — the publication of the Word of God in the English tongue.

In Worms, Tyndale found a sympathetic printer in Peter Schoeffer, the son of the famous Schoeffer who had been a partner of Gutenberg himself. The printing was completed in early 1526 — a small, portable volume, roughly the size of a modern paperback, designed to be easily concealed and transported. It was a masterpiece not only of translation but of practical design, intended for the hands of ordinary people who would need to hide it from the authorities.

The first complete printed English New Testament — translated directly from the original Greek by William Tyndale — was one of the most significant publishing events in the history of the English-speaking world. Approximately three thousand copies were printed in the first edition at Worms, and they were smuggled into England by sympathetic merchants. The books were hidden in bales of cloth, in sacks of flour, in barrels of merchandise, and in the false bottoms of shipping containers. The network of merchants and sympathizers who carried these Bibles across the English Channel was one of the earliest and most effective underground distribution systems in the history of publishing.

THE PERSECUTION AND THE BURNING OF BIBLES

The reaction of the English authorities was swift and furious. Cardinal Thomas Wolsey, the Lord Chancellor and the most powerful churchman in England, organized a campaign to suppress Tyndale's translation. Cuthbert Tunstall, the Bishop of London — the same man who had refused to support Tyndale's work — ordered copies of the New Testament to be purchased and publicly burned at Paul's Cross, the outdoor preaching station near St. Paul's Cathedral in London. The great bonfire of English Bibles at Paul's Cross in October 1526 was one of the most dramatic and symbolic acts of the English Reformation — a public declaration by the institutional church that the Word of God in the language of the people was an intolerable threat to its authority.

Sir Thomas More, the brilliant humanist scholar and Lord Chancellor who succeeded Wolsey in 1529, launched a sustained literary and legal campaign against Tyndale and his translation. More wrote thousands of pages of polemical prose attacking Tyndale's theology and his translation, accusing him of deliberately distorting the text to promote heresy. More was particularly incensed by Tyndale's translation of key ecclesiastical terms: Tyndale translated the Greek word ekklesia as "congregation" rather than "church," and the Greek word presbyteros as "elder" rather than "priest." These were not innocent lexical choices; they struck at the institutional authority of the medieval church by suggesting that the New Testament knows nothing of the hierarchical church structure that Rome claimed was divinely ordained.

The controversy between More and Tyndale produced one of the most remarkable literary exchanges of the sixteenth century. More's Dialogue Concerning Heresies (1529) attacked Tyndale's translation in detail, arguing that Tyndale's word choices were deliberately heretical. Tyndale replied with his Answer to Sir Thomas More's Dialogue (1531), defending his translation choices with meticulous scholarship and devastating logic. The exchange reveals Tyndale as a formidable controversialist — sharp, witty, learned, and utterly committed to the principle that the Word of God must be available to the people in a language they can understand. The Puritan John Owen, writing more than a century later, would describe the debate between More and Tyndale as one of the pivotal intellectual contests of the Reformation.

More also targeted those who possessed or distributed Tyndale's translation. Several of Tyndale's associates were arrested, imprisoned, and in some cases burned at the stake. Thomas Hitton, a young priest who was caught carrying copies of Tyndale's New Testament, was burned at Maidstone in February 1530 — the first person to be martyred specifically for distributing Tyndale's translation. More called Hitton "the devil's stinking martyr." Tyndale, from across the Channel, called him a saint.

But the campaign of suppression had an unintended consequence. When Tunstall bought up copies of Tyndale's New Testament to burn them, the money paid for the confiscated copies went into Tyndale's pockets — and Tyndale used the funds to print revised and improved editions. As Tyndale reportedly observed with grim humor, the Bishop was paying for the very translations he sought to destroy.

THE GENIUS OF TYNDALE'S TRANSLATION

Before we examine Tyndale's translation in detail, let us pause to appreciate the magnitude of what he was attempting. Tyndale was not merely translating words from one language to another. He was creating a new idiom — a way of expressing the deepest truths of the Christian faith in a language that had never before been used for this purpose at this level of precision and beauty. The English language in the 1520s was in a state of rapid evolution. Middle English was giving way to Early Modern English, and the language had not yet developed the rich theological vocabulary that it would later possess — largely because of Tyndale's own contributions. He had to invent new words, coin new phrases, and find new ways of expressing concepts that had been locked in Latin and Greek for centuries.

The challenge was immense. A translator must be faithful to the original text — he must accurately convey the meaning of the Hebrew or Greek words. But he must also produce a translation that is natural, clear, and beautiful in the target language. Tyndale's genius lay in his ability to do both simultaneously. He was faithful to the original text — his knowledge of Greek and Hebrew was unsurpassed among English scholars of his day — and he was a master of English prose whose ear for rhythm, cadence, and the music of spoken language has rarely been equaled.

Tyndale's translation was not merely competent — it was a work of literary genius. Working directly from the Greek text published by Erasmus in 1516 (and revised in subsequent editions), Tyndale produced an English Bible of extraordinary clarity, beauty, and power. He had an unparalleled gift for finding the English words and phrases that conveyed the meaning, the rhythm, and the emotional force of the original Greek and Hebrew texts.

His phrases have become so deeply embedded in the English language that most English speakers use them without knowing their origin. Expressions such as "let there be light," "the salt of the earth," "the spirit is willing but the flesh is weak," "fight the good fight," "the powers that be," "eat, drink, and be merry," "a law unto themselves," "the signs of the times," "my brother's keeper," "the apple of his eye," and "filthy lucre" all come from Tyndale's pen. He coined words and phrases that did not exist in English before he created them, drawing on the full resources of the language — its Anglo-Saxon roots, its Latin borrowings, its native rhythms and cadences — to produce a translation that was both faithful to the original text and beautiful in its English expression.

The impact of Tyndale's translation on the King James Version of 1611 cannot be overstated. Scholars estimate that approximately eighty to ninety percent of the King James New Testament and a significant portion of the King James Old Testament are drawn directly from Tyndale's work. The translators of the King James Version did not start from scratch; they built upon the foundation that Tyndale had laid nearly a century earlier. In a very real sense, when you read the King James Bible, you are reading William Tyndale. His voice speaks through every page, every chapter, every verse. The beauty, the clarity, the majesty of the King James Bible is, in large measure, the beauty, the clarity, and the majesty of William Tyndale's English prose.

TYNDALE'S THEOLOGICAL WRITINGS

Tyndale was not only a translator but a theologian of considerable depth and originality. His most important theological works include The Obedience of a Christian Man (1528) and The Parable of the Wicked Mammon (1528).

In The Obedience of a Christian Man, Tyndale articulated a comprehensive vision of the relationship between the Christian and civil authority, grounded in the Reformed doctrine of sola Scriptura. He argued that the Bible is the supreme authority in all matters of faith and life, and that both church and state are subject to its teaching. He attacked the papal claim to authority over secular rulers and argued that the king — not the pope — is the rightful head of the English church in temporal matters. This argument would later be taken up by King Henry VIII, who used it to justify his break with Rome. Indeed, when Anne Boleyn showed Henry a copy of The Obedience of a Christian Man, the king reportedly declared: "This is a book for me and all kings to read."

The Obedience of a Christian Man was one of the most widely read works of the English Reformation. Its central argument — that Christians owe obedience to the civil magistrate in all temporal matters, but that both magistrate and church are subject to the authority of Scripture — became a foundational principle of English Protestant political thought. Tyndale insisted that the pope had no rightful authority over the English king and that the Roman hierarchy had usurped powers that properly belonged to the secular government. This argument struck a powerful chord with Henry VIII and with many English statesmen who resented papal interference in English affairs.

In The Parable of the Wicked Mammon, Tyndale expounded the doctrine of justification by faith alone with a clarity and force that rivaled Luther himself. He argued from the Scriptures that salvation is entirely the work of God's grace, received through faith in Christ, and that human works — however good and necessary in their proper place — contribute nothing to our standing before God. He quoted Paul's words to the Romans: "Therefore being justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ" (Romans 5:1). And he explained: "Faith is the gift of God, and the gift of God is given freely, not for our deservings."

Tyndale also wrote a prologue to each book of the New Testament, offering the reader guidance on how to understand and apply the text. His prologue to the book of Romans is one of the finest pieces of Reformed theological writing in the English language — a lucid exposition of the doctrines of sin, grace, faith, and justification that anticipates the great Puritan expositors of the seventeenth century.

TYNDALE AND THE OLD TESTAMENT

After completing the New Testament, Tyndale turned his formidable linguistic gifts to the Old Testament. He learned Hebrew — a language far more difficult than Greek and far less commonly studied in sixteenth-century Europe — and undertook to translate the Hebrew Scriptures into English. He completed the Pentateuch (Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, and Deuteronomy) and published it in 1530. He also translated and published the book of Jonah separately. At the time of his arrest, he was working on a translation of the historical books of the Old Testament — from Joshua through 2 Chronicles — and this unfinished work was later completed by his friend and associate Miles Coverdale, who incorporated it into the Matthew's Bible of 1537.

Tyndale's translation of the Pentateuch is a remarkable achievement. Working from the Hebrew text, he produced an English version of the opening books of the Bible that is characterized by the same clarity, vigor, and beauty that mark his New Testament. His rendering of the creation narrative in Genesis 1 — "In the beginning God created heaven and earth. The earth was void and empty, and darkness was upon the deep, and the Spirit of God moved upon the water" — established the cadences that the King James translators would later refine into their immortal version. Tyndale's prologues to each book of the Pentateuch are also important theological documents, guiding the reader to understand the books in the light of Christ and the doctrine of grace.

Tyndale believed passionately that the Old Testament was essential to the Christian life and that the covenant promises of God to Abraham, Moses, and David found their fulfillment in Christ. He wrote: "The Old Testament is a book wherein is written the law and commandments of God, together with the deeds of those who observe or observe them not. As the New Testament is the book of the covenant which God made with us, the fulfillment and restitution of all that was promised in the Old." This understanding of the unity of the Old and New Testaments was characteristic of the Reformed tradition and would be developed further by Calvin, the Puritans, and the great Reformed theologians of the seventeenth century.

THE BETRAYAL AND ARREST

For over a decade, Tyndale lived as a fugitive on the Continent, moving from city to city, always one step ahead of the English agents sent to capture him. He lived in Antwerp, Belgium, for much of this period, enjoying the protection of the English merchant community and the relative tolerance of the local authorities. He continued to translate and to write, completing the Pentateuch (the five books of Moses — Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, and Deuteronomy) from the original Hebrew, as well as the book of Jonah. He was also working on a translation of the historical books of the Old Testament at the time of his arrest.

In 1535, Tyndale was betrayed by Henry Phillips, a young Englishman of uncertain motives. Phillips was a well-educated, charming man who insinuated himself into Tyndale's confidence over a period of months. He dined with Tyndale, walked with him, and won his trust. Then, on a spring day in 1535, Phillips lured Tyndale out of the safety of the English merchant house in Antwerp and into a narrow alleyway, where agents of the Imperial authorities were waiting. Tyndale was seized, arrested, and taken to the castle of Vilvoorde, about six miles north of Brussels, where he was imprisoned.

The betrayal by Phillips is one of the most despicable acts in the history of the English Reformation. Tyndale, who was a trusting and generous man by nature, had been deceived by a man he considered a friend. The words of the psalmist apply with terrible force: "Yea, mine own familiar friend, in whom I trusted, which did eat of my bread, hath lifted up his heel against me" (Psalm 41:9). Like his Lord before him, Tyndale was betrayed by a kiss — or its equivalent.

THE IMPRISONMENT AND MARTYRDOM

THE IMPRISONMENT AND MARTYRDOM

Tyndale was held in the castle of Vilvoorde for approximately eighteen months — from May 1535 to October 1536. The conditions of his imprisonment were harsh. The castle was cold, damp, and dark. In a letter written during his imprisonment — the only surviving letter from his hand — Tyndale asked for warmer clothing, a candle, and, above all, his Hebrew Bible, his Hebrew grammar, and his Hebrew dictionary, so that he could continue his translation work even in prison. The letter reveals a man whose consuming passion, even in the face of death, was to complete his work of giving the Word of God to the English people.

During his imprisonment, Tyndale was subjected to interrogation and debate by theologians appointed by the authorities. Pierre Dufief, the procurator general of the Low Countries, conducted the investigation, and a commission of theologians from the University of Leuven examined Tyndale's writings and testimony. But Tyndale defended himself with the same mastery of Scripture and theological argument that had characterized his entire career. Even his captors seem to have been impressed by his learning and his character. Tradition records that the jailer, the jailer's daughter, and other members of the household were converted to the evangelical faith through Tyndale's personal witness during his imprisonment — a testimony to the power of the gospel even in chains. As Paul had written from his own imprisonment: "But I would ye should understand, brethren, that the things which happened unto me have fallen out rather unto the furtherance of the gospel" (Philippians 1:12).

Tyndale was tried for heresy in August 1536. He was formally degraded from the priesthood — stripped of his ecclesiastical vestments and status — and condemned to death. On the morning of October 6, 1536, William Tyndale was led from his cell to the place of execution outside the walls of Vilvoorde. According to John Foxe's account, he was tied to the stake, and, before the fire was lit, he cried out with a loud voice his final prayer: "Lord, open the King of England's eyes!" He was then strangled by the executioner and his body burned.

Tyndale's dying prayer was one of the most poignant and prophetic utterances in the history of the English Reformation. "Lord, open the King of England's eyes!" Within three years of Tyndale's death, that prayer was answered. In 1537, the Matthew's Bible — a translation compiled by John Rogers, a friend and associate of Tyndale, and based largely on Tyndale's own work — was published with the official authorization of King Henry VIII. In 1538, Henry issued a royal injunction ordering that a copy of the English Bible be placed in every parish church in England. And in 1539, the Great Bible — also based substantially on Tyndale's translation — was published and distributed throughout the land.

The plowboy could now read the Scriptures for himself. The sacred vow that Tyndale had made in Gloucestershire — that the boy who drives the plough should know more of the Scripture than the learned clergy — had become reality. The dream for which Tyndale had labored, suffered, and died had been fulfilled by the sovereign hand of God. The Word of God in the English language, purchased at the price of his blood, was freely available to every man, woman, and child in England. As the prophet Isaiah had declared: "So shall my word be that goeth forth out of my mouth: it shall not return unto me void, but it shall accomplish that which I please, and it shall prosper in the thing whereto I sent it" (Isaiah 55:11).

THE PROVIDENCE OF GOD IN TYNDALE'S WORK

Looking back on the life and death of William Tyndale, we can see the sovereign hand of God at work in every detail. God prepared Tyndale with extraordinary linguistic gifts — giving him mastery of eight languages, including the Greek and Hebrew that were essential for translating the Scriptures from their original texts. God brought Tyndale into contact with the ideas of Erasmus and Luther at precisely the moment when those ideas were transforming European thought. God provided protectors and patrons — Humphrey Monmouth in London, the English merchants of Antwerp — who sheltered Tyndale and enabled his work. God even used the hostility of Tyndale's enemies to advance His purposes: when Bishop Tunstall burned Tyndale's Bibles, the money paid for the confiscated copies funded the printing of new and better editions.

And God answered Tyndale's dying prayer with breathtaking speed. "Lord, open the King of England's eyes!" Within three years, King Henry VIII authorized the distribution of the English Bible throughout his realm. Within seventy-five years, Tyndale's translation had been refined, polished, and published as the King James Version — the most influential book in the history of the English language. Within four centuries, the Bible had been translated into thousands of languages and carried to every nation on earth. The seed that Tyndale planted at the cost of his life has borne fruit beyond anything he could have imagined.

The words of the apostle Paul ring true: "For I reckon that the sufferings of this present time are not worthy to be compared with the glory which shall be revealed in us" (Romans 8:18). Tyndale suffered imprisonment, betrayal, and death. But the glory that has been revealed through his work — the millions of souls who have read the Word of God in English and found salvation through its pages — is a glory that far outweighs the suffering. The Puritan Thomas Watson wrote: "God's providences are like Hebrew letters: they must be read backward." Read backward, the life of William Tyndale is a story not of defeat but of triumph — a triumph of the Word of God over every power that sought to suppress it.

THE LEGACY OF WILLIAM TYNDALE

Tyndale's legacy is incalculable. He is the true father of the English Bible. His translation shaped the language of English-speaking Christianity for five centuries and counting. Through the King James Version — which is, in its core, Tyndale's translation refined and polished — Tyndale's words have been read, memorized, preached, and sung by hundreds of millions of people around the world. He gave the English language some of its most beautiful and enduring phrases, and he made the Word of God accessible to a people who had been kept in ignorance for centuries.

The Puritan Richard Baxter, writing in the seventeenth century, reflected on the privilege of having the Scriptures in English: "The Word of God is the charter of all our privileges, the foundation of all our hopes, the rule of all our duties, and the judge of all controversies." Every word of that privilege, for English-speaking Christians, rests upon the foundation laid by William Tyndale. The Puritan John Flavel likewise observed that the providence of God employs the labors and sufferings of His servants to accomplish purposes far beyond what they themselves could envision — a truth magnificently illustrated in the life and death of William Tyndale, whose faithful obedience bore fruit in ways he never lived to see.

It is worth noting how Tyndale's work relates to the broader story of the English Bible. After Tyndale's death, the torch of English Bible translation was carried forward by Miles Coverdale, who published the first complete printed English Bible in 1535 — using Tyndale's translations supplemented by material from Luther's German Bible and the Latin Vulgate for the portions that Tyndale had not completed. The Matthew's Bible of 1537, compiled by John Rogers using Tyndale's unpublished manuscripts, represented the most complete edition of Tyndale's work. The Great Bible of 1539, authorized by Henry VIII, was essentially a revision of the Matthew's Bible. The Geneva Bible of 1560, produced by English Protestants in exile during the reign of Mary Tudor, became the household Bible of Elizabethan England and was the Bible used by Shakespeare and the Puritans. And the King James Version of 1611, produced by forty-seven scholars working under the authorization of King James I, drew upon all of these earlier translations — but at its foundation, beneath every subsequent revision and refinement, lay the work of William Tyndale.

Tyndale's martyrdom also stands as a permanent reminder that the freedom to read the Bible — which we take for granted today — was purchased at an enormous price. Men and women were burned at the stake, imprisoned, exiled, and hunted like criminals for the simple act of reading or possessing an English Bible. The Word of God, which we can now carry in our pockets on our phones, was once a book that cost people their lives. As the writer of Hebrews declared of the heroes of faith: "They were stoned, they were sawn asunder, were tempted, were slain with the sword: they wandered about in sheepskins and goatskins; being destitute, afflicted, tormented; (Of whom the world was not worthy)" (Hebrews 11:37-38). William Tyndale was a man of whom the world was not worthy, and his sacrifice demands our gratitude, our reverence, and our faithful use of the Bible he died to give us.

Every time you open an English Bible, you hold in your hands the fruit of William Tyndale's sacrifice. The words you read were shaped by his genius, purchased by his blood, and preserved by the sovereign hand of God. Read them with gratitude. Read them with reverence. And read them with the same passion that drove Tyndale to give his life so that you could have them. As the psalmist declared: "The law of the Lord is perfect, converting the soul: the testimony of the Lord is sure, making wise the simple. The statutes of the Lord are right, rejoicing the heart: the commandment of the Lord is pure, enlightening the eyes" (Psalm 19:7-8). This was the treasure for which Tyndale gave everything.
