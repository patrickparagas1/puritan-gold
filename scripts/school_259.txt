"Honest Living"
Christian Ethics & Moral Philosophy — Lesson 19

Welcome back, students, to our continuing study of the Ten Commandments in the Puritan Gold series. Today we examine the eighth commandment — the commandment that governs our relationship to property, labor, and the material possessions that God entrusts to our care. "Thou shalt not steal" (Exodus 20:15). Like the commandments we have already studied, the eighth commandment appears deceptively simple on the surface. Three words. A clear prohibition. But the Puritans, following the Reformed tradition of careful exposition, unpacked this commandment into a comprehensive ethic of property, labor, stewardship, generosity, and economic justice that reaches into every corner of daily life. This is a lesson about honest living — about what it means to earn, to spend, to save, and to give in a manner that honors God and serves our neighbor.

PART ONE: THE THEOLOGICAL FOUNDATION — DIVINE OWNERSHIP AND HUMAN STEWARDSHIP

To understand the eighth commandment properly, we must begin with a foundational theological truth that the Puritans never tired of emphasizing: God is the ultimate owner of everything. "The earth is the LORD'S, and the fulness thereof; the world, and they that dwell therein" (Psalm 24:1). "The silver is mine, and the gold is mine, saith the LORD of hosts" (Haggai 2:8). "For every beast of the forest is mine, and the cattle upon a thousand hills" (Psalm 50:10). These passages establish with unmistakable clarity that God, as the Creator and Sustainer of all things, possesses ultimate ownership over every material thing in the universe. Nothing that exists belongs to human beings in an absolute sense. We may use the language of "my house," "my money," "my possessions," but these are expressions of convenience, not of ultimate reality. In truth, we are stewards — trustees, managers, caretakers — appointed by God to manage His property on His behalf, according to His purposes, and in accountability to His judgment.

This doctrine of divine ownership and human stewardship is the theological bedrock upon which the entire Puritan economic ethic was built. Thomas Watson, in his exposition of the eighth commandment, wrote that "we are but tenants at will, and hold all upon the courtesy of God." Watson's point is that our possessions are not truly ours — they are entrusted to us for a time, and we will be required to give an account of how we have managed them. The parable of the talents (Matthew 25:14-30) was a favorite text of the Puritans in this regard. In that parable, a master entrusts his servants with different amounts of money and then departs on a journey. When he returns, he demands an accounting. The servants who invested wisely and produced a return were commended: "Well done, thou good and faithful servant: thou hast been faithful over a few things, I will make thee ruler over many things: enter thou into the joy of thy lord" (Matthew 25:21). The servant who buried his talent out of fear was condemned: "Thou wicked and slothful servant" (Matthew 25:26). The lesson is clear: God expects us to manage His resources diligently, productively, and faithfully — and He will hold us accountable for how we have done so.

William Perkins, the Cambridge theologian who lived from 1558 to 1602 and who is often called the father of Puritanism, developed the doctrine of stewardship in his influential work "A Treatise of the Vocations or Callings of Men," published in 1603. Perkins argued that every Christian has a twofold calling: a general calling to salvation and sanctification through faith in Christ, and a particular calling to a specific vocation or trade through which he serves God and contributes to the welfare of society. These two callings are inseparable. The Christian farmer is not merely growing crops — he is serving God through the faithful exercise of his vocation. The Christian merchant is not merely conducting business — he is fulfilling a divine assignment, managing God's resources, and contributing to the common good. This understanding of work as a sacred calling transformed the Puritan approach to economic life and gave the eighth commandment a depth and richness that it had rarely possessed in previous ages of Christian thought.

PART TWO: THE LEGITIMACY OF PRIVATE PROPERTY

The very fact that stealing is forbidden presupposes that people have a right to possess property that others may not take from them. The eighth commandment, therefore, implicitly establishes the legitimacy of private property. This is a point of enormous theological and political significance.

The Puritans defended the right of private property against two opposing threats. On one side stood the various Anabaptist and radical Protestant groups of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, some of which advocated communal ownership of property. The most famous — or infamous — experiment in Christian communism took place in the German city of Munster in 1534-1535, where radical Anabaptists seized control of the city, abolished private property, and established a theocratic commune that quickly descended into tyranny, violence, and chaos. The Puritans pointed to the Munster catastrophe as evidence that the abolition of private property is both unscriptural and destructive.

On the other side stood the feudal system that had dominated European society for centuries, concentrating vast wealth and land ownership in the hands of a small aristocracy while the majority of the population labored in poverty. The Puritans, while defending private property, also insisted that the right of property is not absolute — it is limited by the duties of charity, the claims of justice, and the ultimate ownership of God. Wealth is not given for hoarding or for selfish indulgence; it is given for the glory of God and the benefit of others. The Puritan economic vision was neither socialism nor unrestrained capitalism — it was a stewardship economy, in which private property is respected but its use is governed by moral law and directed toward the common good.

John Calvin had laid the groundwork for this teaching in his Institutes, and the Puritans built upon it with characteristic thoroughness. Calvin wrote that God "has appointed all things for our use, but with the condition that we should be satisfied with a frugal and moderate use of them." The Puritans embraced this principle wholeheartedly. They were not opposed to wealth — many Puritans were successful merchants, traders, and landowners. But they insisted that wealth carries moral obligation, that prosperity must be accompanied by generosity, and that the accumulation of riches must never become an end in itself.

PART THREE: THE SINS FORBIDDEN BY THE EIGHTH COMMANDMENT

The Westminster Larger Catechism, in Question 142, provides an extraordinarily detailed catalogue of the sins forbidden by the eighth commandment. The list is so comprehensive that it addresses virtually every form of economic wrongdoing known to human civilization.

The most obvious sins are theft in its various forms — robbery, burglary, pickpocketing, pilfering, and the forcible seizure of property that belongs to another. These are the crimes that most people think of when they hear "Thou shalt not steal," and they are indeed grievous violations of the commandment. But the Westminster divines went far beyond these obvious forms of theft to identify a host of more subtle violations.

Fraud and dishonesty in business are explicitly condemned. This includes the use of false weights and measures, the misrepresentation of goods, the concealment of defects, the charging of unjust prices, the breaking of contracts, and every form of deception designed to gain an economic advantage at the expense of another person. The prophet Amos thundered against merchants who could not wait for the Sabbath to end so they could resume their dishonest practices: "Hear this, O ye that swallow up the needy, even to make the poor of the land to fail, Saying, When will the new moon be gone, that we may sell corn? and the sabbath, that we may set forth wheat, making the ephah small, and the shekel great, and falsifying the balances by deceit? That we may buy the poor for silver, and the needy for a pair of shoes; yea, and sell the refuse of the wheat?" (Amos 8:4-6). The Puritans applied these prophetic denunciations directly to the merchants and tradespeople of their own day, insisting that every business transaction must be conducted with scrupulous honesty.

Oppressive lending and exploitative financial practices are also condemned. The Mosaic law prohibited the charging of interest to fellow Israelites who were in need: "If thou lend money to any of my people that is poor by thee, thou shalt not be to him as an usurer, neither shalt thou lay upon him usury" (Exodus 22:25). While the Puritans debated the precise application of this law to their own commercial economy — they generally permitted the charging of reasonable interest on commercial loans — they were unanimous in condemning lending practices that exploited the poor, trapped people in cycles of inescapable debt, or took advantage of the desperate circumstances of borrowers to extract exorbitant returns.

The withholding of wages from laborers is particularly condemned by Scripture. "Thou shalt not oppress an hired servant that is poor and needy, whether he be of thy brethren, or of thy strangers that are in thy land within thy gates: At his day thou shalt give him his hire, neither shall the sun go down upon it; for he is poor, and setteth his heart upon it: lest he cry against thee unto the LORD, and it be sin unto thee" (Deuteronomy 24:14-15). James echoed this condemnation with fierce intensity: "Behold, the hire of the labourers who have reaped down your fields, which is of you kept back by fraud, crieth: and the cries of them which have reaped are entered into the ears of the Lord of sabaoth" (James 5:4). The Puritans took these passages with utmost seriousness and insisted that the prompt and fair payment of wages was a binding moral obligation under the eighth commandment.

The Puritans also identified laziness and idleness as forms of theft — a teaching that may surprise some students. Their reasoning was straightforward: the person who refuses to work and instead lives off the labor of others is effectively stealing — consuming resources that he has not earned and taking from others what they have produced. Paul's instruction was unambiguous: "For even when we were with you, this we commanded you, that if any would not work, neither should he eat. For we hear that there are some which walk among you disorderly, working not at all, but are busybodies. Now them that are such we command and exhort by our Lord Jesus Christ, that with quietness they work, and eat their own bread" (2 Thessalonians 3:10-12).

The Puritans extended this principle to condemn gambling, which they saw as an attempt to gain wealth without honest labor. Gambling, they argued, produces nothing — it merely redistributes wealth from losers to winners through a process based on chance rather than on productive effort. It violates the eighth commandment because it seeks to acquire what belongs to others without providing anything of value in return.

PART FOUR: THE DUTY OF HONEST LABOR

On the positive side, the eighth commandment requires honest, diligent labor as the God-appointed means of acquiring the necessities of life. The duty to work is woven throughout the fabric of Scripture and was one of the cornerstones of the Puritan moral vision.

Work is not a curse — it is a creation ordinance, established by God before the Fall. In the Garden of Eden, before sin entered the world, God placed Adam in the garden "to dress it and to keep it" (Genesis 2:15). Work became burdensome and toilsome after the Fall — "In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread" (Genesis 3:19) — but it did not originate with the Fall. It is part of God's original design for human life, a reflection of the fact that God Himself is a worker: "My Father worketh hitherto, and I work," Jesus declared (John 5:17).

The Puritans elevated the concept of work to the status of a divine calling. William Perkins, in his "Treatise of the Vocations," argued that every lawful occupation is a calling from God — not merely the work of pastors and missionaries, but the work of farmers, merchants, craftsmen, teachers, soldiers, physicians, and servants. When a Christian farmer plows his field with diligence and integrity, he is serving God just as surely as the minister who preaches from his pulpit. When a Christian merchant conducts his business with honesty and fairness, he is glorifying God just as truly as the evangelist who proclaims the gospel. This doctrine of vocation — the belief that all honest work is sacred when done for the glory of God — was one of the most revolutionary and far-reaching contributions of the Puritan movement to Western civilization.

Richard Baxter, in his "Christian Directory," outlined the characteristics of faithful labor with his customary thoroughness. First, work must be diligent — performed with energy, attention, and a commitment to excellence. The Puritans despised slothfulness and half-hearted effort. "Whatsoever thy hand findeth to do, do it with thy might" (Ecclesiastes 9:10). Second, work must be honest — conducted with integrity, truthfulness, and fairness toward every person involved. There are no shortcuts in the Puritan ethic; there is no room for cutting corners, deceiving customers, or exploiting workers. Third, work must be useful — directed toward the production of genuine value, whether in goods, services, knowledge, or care. The Puritans regarded work that was merely parasitic — that consumed resources without producing benefit — as a violation of the eighth commandment's spirit.

Cotton Mather, in his essay "A Christian at His Calling," published in 1701, made a distinction between a person's general calling (the call to serve God through faith in Christ) and his particular calling (the specific occupation through which he serves God and society). Mather urged every Christian to pursue both callings with equal devotion: "A Christian at his two callings is a man in a boat, rowing toward heaven. If he pull one oar only, he will go in circles; he must pull both oars equally to make progress toward his destination." In other words, the Christian who neglects his work in order to pursue only spiritual activities is as unbalanced as the Christian who neglects his spiritual life in order to pursue only worldly success. The eighth commandment requires both — faithful work in one's earthly vocation and faithful devotion to one's heavenly calling.

PART FIVE: FAIR DEALING IN BUSINESS — THE PURITAN COMMERCIAL ETHIC

The Puritans were not anti-business. Many of the most prominent Puritans were successful merchants, traders, and entrepreneurs. But they insisted that the marketplace must be governed by the same moral law that governs the home and the church. Business, for the Puritans, was not a moral-free zone where the only rule was profit — it was a sphere of human activity in which the eighth commandment applied with full force.

The Puritan commercial ethic rested on several key principles. First, honesty in all transactions. A merchant's word must be as good as his bond. Goods must be accurately described. Defects must not be concealed. Prices must be fair and transparent. The practice of selling one thing while representing it as another was condemned as a form of theft. Leviticus 19:35-36 was frequently cited: "Ye shall do no unrighteousness in judgment, in meteyard, in weight, or in measure. Just balances, just weights, a just ephah, and a just hin, shall ye have: I am the LORD your God, which brought you out of the land of Egypt."

Second, fair wages for workers. As we have already noted, the eighth commandment requires that laborers be paid promptly, fairly, and in full for their work. The employer who withholds wages, who pays less than what was agreed, or who exploits the vulnerability of workers who have no other options is guilty of theft — a theft that the Scriptures describe as crying out to God for justice.

Third, concern for the poor and vulnerable. The Puritans recognized that economic life always involves unequal distributions of wealth and power, and they insisted that those who possess more have a moral obligation to those who possess less. The eighth commandment does not merely forbid taking from others — it requires giving to others. "He that hath two coats, let him impart to him that hath none; and he that hath meat, let him do likewise" (Luke 3:11).

Robert Bolton, the Puritan minister who served at Broughton in Northamptonshire from 1610 until his death in 1631, wrote powerfully about the duty of the wealthy to care for the poor. Bolton argued that the rich man who hoards his wealth while his neighbors starve is guilty of a form of theft — he is withholding from others what God intended for their relief. Bolton drew from the teaching of the early church father Basil of Caesarea, who wrote in the fourth century: "The bread you store up belongs to the hungry; the coat that lies in your trunk belongs to the naked; the gold you have hidden in the ground belongs to the poor." While the Puritans did not endorse forced redistribution of wealth, they insisted that voluntary, generous, and systematic giving to the poor was a binding moral obligation under the eighth commandment.

PART SIX: THE PURITAN ETHIC OF STEWARDSHIP — GAIN, SAVE, GIVE

The Puritan approach to wealth and possessions can be summarized in three principles that were later articulated with memorable clarity by John Wesley, who owed much to the Puritan tradition: "Gain all you can. Save all you can. Give all you can."

The first principle — gain all you can — reflects the Puritan conviction that diligent labor and productive enterprise are moral goods. The eighth commandment does not condemn the acquisition of wealth — it condemns the dishonest, lazy, or exploitative acquisition of wealth. Wealth that is earned through honest labor, fair dealing, and wise investment is a blessing from God and should be received with gratitude. The Puritans encouraged their people to be industrious, enterprising, and skilled in their vocations. They saw economic success as a potential sign of God's blessing — though they were careful to note that it is not always so, and that God sometimes allows the righteous to suffer poverty and the wicked to prosper.

The second principle — save all you can — reflects the Puritan emphasis on frugality and moderation. The Puritans were deeply suspicious of luxury, extravagance, and conspicuous consumption. They did not believe that wealth was given for personal indulgence; it was given for the glory of God and the benefit of others. Saving — living below one's means, avoiding waste, and building reserves for future needs and opportunities — was seen as both a prudential virtue and a moral duty. The Puritans distinguished between legitimate enjoyment of God's gifts and sinful indulgence that squanders resources that could be used for higher purposes.

Baxter counseled: "If God show you a way in which you may lawfully get more than in another way, if you refuse this, and choose the less gainful way, you cross one of the ends of your calling, and you refuse to be God's steward." At the same time, Baxter warned against the spiritual danger of accumulating wealth as an end in itself: "If it be possible that you may labor to be rich for God, though it is not possible that you should labor to be rich for the world." The Puritan lived in the tension between these two truths — diligently seeking to produce wealth while carefully guarding against the love of wealth.

The third principle — give all you can — is the crown of the Puritan economic ethic. The Puritans believed that the ultimate purpose of wealth is not personal enrichment but the glory of God and the welfare of others. They practiced and preached systematic, sacrificial, joyful giving. They supported their churches, their pastors, their schools, their colleges, and their poor with extraordinary liberality. They established charitable institutions, endowed scholarships, and organized poor relief with a thoroughness and generosity that was remarkable for their age.

Paul's instruction to the Corinthians captured the spirit of Puritan giving: "But this I say, He which soweth sparingly shall reap also sparingly; and he which soweth bountifully shall reap also bountifully. Every man according as he purposeth in his heart, so let him give; not grudgingly, or of necessity: for God loveth a cheerful giver" (2 Corinthians 9:6-7). The Puritans insisted that giving must be planned, proportional, and cheerful — not reluctant, sporadic, or grudging. They saw generosity as one of the surest signs of genuine faith and as one of the most effective means of combating the sin of covetousness.

PART SEVEN: THE EIGHTH COMMANDMENT AND ECONOMIC JUSTICE

The Puritans did not limit the eighth commandment to individual transactions — they applied it to the structures and systems of economic life as well. The prophets of Israel had thundered against rulers and merchants who exploited the poor, who rigged the scales of justice, and who built their fortunes on the backs of the vulnerable. The Puritans carried this prophetic tradition forward with conviction and courage.

Isaiah denounced the rulers of Israel who devoured the poor: "The LORD will enter into judgment with the ancients of his people, and the princes thereof: for ye have eaten up the vineyard; the spoil of the poor is in your houses. What mean ye that ye beat my people to pieces, and grind the faces of the poor? saith the Lord GOD of hosts" (Isaiah 3:14-15). Micah condemned those who "covet fields, and take them by violence; and houses, and take them away: so they oppress a man and his house, even a man and his heritage" (Micah 2:2). Jeremiah pronounced woe upon those who built their houses through injustice: "Woe unto him that buildeth his house by unrighteousness, and his chambers by wrong; that useth his neighbour's service without wages, and giveth him not for his work" (Jeremiah 22:13).

The Puritans applied these prophetic denunciations to the economic abuses of their own day. They condemned landlords who charged excessive rents, merchants who cornered markets to drive up prices, employers who exploited workers, and government officials who used their power to enrich themselves at the expense of the public. They insisted that economic systems — no less than individual transactions — must be measured against the standard of divine justice.

In Puritan New England, communities enacted regulations designed to enforce fair dealing in the marketplace. Price controls were sometimes imposed to prevent gouging during shortages. Merchants who were found to have used false weights or engaged in deceptive practices were publicly disciplined. The famous case of Robert Keayne, a Boston merchant who was censured by both the civil court and the church in 1639 for overcharging his customers, illustrates the seriousness with which the Puritans took the eighth commandment's requirements of fair dealing. Keayne was fined by the court and admonished by the church — not for any spectacular crime, but for the seemingly minor offense of charging more than was considered fair for his goods. The incident reveals that the Puritans saw commercial ethics not as a matter of personal preference but as a matter of divine law.

PART EIGHT: THE EIGHTH COMMANDMENT AND THE SABBATH

The Puritans also recognized a connection between the eighth commandment and the fourth commandment — the commandment to keep the Sabbath. The Sabbath, they taught, is God's weekly reminder that our lives do not belong to us and that our work does not ultimately depend on our own effort but on God's blessing. The person who refuses to rest on the Sabbath — who works seven days a week in the relentless pursuit of wealth — is guilty of a form of idolatry. He has made work his god and wealth his salvation. The Sabbath breaks the cycle of ceaseless labor and directs our attention upward, reminding us that "except the LORD build the house, they labour in vain that build it" (Psalm 127:1).

The Puritans also saw the Sabbath as a protection for workers. In a world where employers could demand unlimited labor from their workers, the Sabbath established a divinely mandated day of rest that no human authority could override. The Sabbath protected the laborer from exploitation by guaranteeing him at least one day in seven for rest, worship, and the refreshment of body and soul. In this sense, the fourth and eighth commandments work together to create a humane and balanced approach to economic life — one that values productive labor but also protects the dignity and well-being of the laborer.

PART NINE: THE EIGHTH COMMANDMENT AND THE GOSPEL

As always, the Puritans pointed their readers from the law to the gospel. The eighth commandment, they taught, serves as a mirror that reveals our sinfulness and drives us to Christ.

Who among us has perfectly kept the eighth commandment? Who has never taken what does not belong to him — whether through outright theft, or through dishonesty, laziness, exploitation, stinginess, or the failure to give generously to those in need? The eighth commandment, when applied in all its fullness, convicts every person of sin. We have all fallen short of God's standard of perfect justice, honesty, and generosity. We all stand in need of the forgiveness that is found only in Christ.

And Christ Himself is the supreme example of the eighth commandment's positive requirements. Though He was rich, "yet for your sakes he became poor, that ye through his poverty might be rich" (2 Corinthians 8:9). Jesus gave everything — His comfort, His reputation, His very life — for the welfare of others. He is the ultimate model of the generous, self-sacrificing love that the eighth commandment requires.

The gospel also provides the power to keep the eighth commandment. The person who has experienced the grace of God in Christ — who knows that he has received a gift of infinite value that he could never have earned — is freed from the anxious, grasping, covetous spirit that drives so much of human economic behavior. He can be generous because he knows that God is generous. He can be content with what he has because he knows that God has given him the greatest treasure of all — eternal life in Christ. He can work diligently and give freely because he knows that his ultimate security does not rest in his bank account but in the faithfulness of God, who has promised: "I will never leave thee, nor forsake thee" (Hebrews 13:5).

Paul captured this transformation beautifully in his letter to the Ephesians: "Let him that stole steal no more: but rather let him labour, working with his hands the thing which is good, that he may have to give to him that needeth" (Ephesians 4:28). Notice the complete reversal that the gospel produces. The thief does not merely stop stealing — he starts working. And he does not merely work to acquire — he works to give. The gospel transforms takers into givers, parasites into producers, hoarders into stewards. This is the eighth commandment fulfilled through the power of grace.

PART TEN: BIBLICAL EXAMPLES OF THE EIGHTH COMMANDMENT

The Scriptures provide us with numerous examples that illustrate both the violations and the faithful keeping of the eighth commandment. The Puritans drew upon these examples frequently in their teaching, using them to bring the abstract principles of the commandment into vivid, concrete reality.

The story of Achan in Joshua 7 provides one of the most dramatic examples of the consequences of theft. After the fall of Jericho, God had commanded the Israelites that all the spoil of the city was to be devoted to Him — it was not to be taken for personal use. But Achan, of the tribe of Judah, violated this command: "When I saw among the spoils a goodly Babylonish garment, and two hundred shekels of silver, and a wedge of gold of fifty shekels weight, then I coveted them, and took them" (Joshua 7:21). Notice the progression that Achan describes: he saw, he coveted, and he took. The sin began in the eye, moved to the heart, and finally expressed itself in the hand. And the consequences were devastating: Israel's army was defeated at the city of Ai, thirty-six men were killed, and Achan himself was executed along with his entire family. The Puritans used this narrative as a solemn warning that theft is never a private sin — its consequences always extend beyond the individual to affect the entire community.

By contrast, the story of Zacchaeus in Luke 19 provides a beautiful example of the eighth commandment fulfilled through the transforming power of the gospel. Zacchaeus was a chief tax collector — a man who had built his fortune through the systematic exploitation of his fellow citizens, using his position to collect more than was required and pocketing the excess. Tax collectors in Roman-occupied Judea were despised as both thieves and traitors — collaborators with the occupying power who enriched themselves at the expense of their own people.

When Jesus called Zacchaeus down from the sycamore tree and announced that He would dine at his house, the immediate result was a radical transformation of Zacchaeus's relationship to money and possessions: "And Zacchaeus stood, and said unto the Lord; Behold, Lord, the half of my goods I give to the poor; and if I have taken any thing from any man by false accusation, I restore him fourfold" (Luke 19:8). Jesus responded: "This day is salvation come to this house" (Luke 19:9). The encounter with Christ transformed a thief into a generous giver, an exploiter into a restorer. This is the gospel at work — not merely changing outward behavior but transforming the heart from which behavior flows.

The story of the early church in Acts 2 and 4 provides yet another example. After the outpouring of the Holy Spirit at Pentecost, the believers in Jerusalem demonstrated a remarkable spirit of generosity: "And all that believed were together, and had all things common; And sold their possessions and goods, and parted them to all men, as every man had need" (Acts 2:44-45). "Neither was there any among them that lacked: for as many as were possessors of lands or houses sold them, and brought the prices of the things that were sold, And laid them down at the apostles' feet: and distribution was made unto every man according as he had need" (Acts 4:34-35).

The Puritans were careful to note that this generosity was voluntary, not compulsory. When Ananias and Sapphira sold their property and brought only part of the proceeds while pretending to give all, Peter rebuked them — not for keeping back part of the money, but for lying about what they had done: "Whiles it remained, was it not thine own? and after it was sold, was it not in thine own power?" (Acts 5:4). Peter's words confirm the legitimacy of private property — the money was theirs to keep or to give as they chose. But the dishonesty of pretending to give all while secretly keeping back a portion was condemned as a lie to the Holy Spirit. The early church's generosity, the Puritans argued, was not a model of communism but of Spirit-filled, voluntary, joyful giving — the eighth commandment lived out in its most beautiful expression.

PART ELEVEN: THE EIGHTH COMMANDMENT AND THE PURITAN WORK ETHIC

The Puritan work ethic, which has shaped the economic culture of the English-speaking world for centuries, was rooted directly in the eighth commandment. The German sociologist Max Weber, in his famous 1905 work "The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism," identified the Puritan emphasis on hard work, frugality, and vocational faithfulness as a major contributing factor to the development of modern capitalism. While Weber's thesis has been debated and refined by subsequent scholars, the basic observation is sound: the Puritans' theological convictions about work, stewardship, and economic responsibility produced a distinctive economic culture that valued productivity, honesty, and disciplined effort.

The Puritans did not view economic success as a guarantee of God's favor — they were too well-versed in the book of Job and the Psalms to make that simplistic equation. But they did view faithful, diligent labor as a form of obedience to God, and they expected that such obedience would generally be accompanied by God's providential blessing. The key qualification is "generally" — the Puritans fully recognized that godly people sometimes suffer poverty, and that wicked people sometimes prosper. But they insisted that, as a general rule, honest labor and wise stewardship tend to produce material prosperity, while laziness and dishonesty tend to produce poverty. Proverbs 10:4 captures this principle: "He becometh poor that dealeth with a slack hand: but the hand of the diligent maketh rich."

The practical fruit of the Puritan work ethic was visible throughout the Puritan world. In England, Puritan merchants and manufacturers built some of the most successful businesses of their era. In New England, Puritan settlers transformed a wilderness into thriving communities within a single generation, establishing farms, mills, shipyards, fisheries, and trading networks that produced remarkable prosperity. Harvard College, the first institution of higher education in North America, was founded in 1636 — just six years after the founding of the Massachusetts Bay Colony. The Puritans' investment in education was itself an expression of the eighth commandment's positive requirements: they believed that an educated population was essential to economic productivity, social order, and spiritual health.

The legacy of the Puritan work ethic continues to shape our world today. The values of hard work, honest dealing, financial responsibility, and generous giving that the Puritans championed remain the foundation of a healthy economy and a just society. The eighth commandment, faithfully applied, produces not only individual prosperity but social flourishing — communities where trust is maintained, where labor is valued, where the poor are cared for, and where the glory of God is reflected in the responsible management of the material world He has created.

PART TWELVE: THE EIGHTH COMMANDMENT AND CONTENTMENT

Before we close, we must note the intimate connection between the eighth commandment and the tenth commandment — the prohibition against covetousness. Theft, in its deepest root, springs from discontent — the restless feeling that we do not have enough, that others have more than their share, that God has not provided adequately for our needs. The cure for theft, therefore, is not merely the fear of punishment but the cultivation of contentment — the deep, settled satisfaction that comes from trusting in God's sufficiency.

Paul wrote from prison: "Not that I speak in respect of want: for I have learned, in whatsoever state I am, therewith to be content. I know both how to be abased, and I know how to abound: every where and in all things I am instructed both to be full and to be hungry, both to abound and to suffer need. I can do all things through Christ which strengtheneth me" (Philippians 4:11-13). The contented Christian is the honest Christian. The person who trusts God to provide for his needs has no reason to steal, to defraud, or to exploit others. He can work diligently, save wisely, and give generously because his security rests not in his possessions but in the promises of God.

Jeremiah Burroughs, in his beloved work "The Rare Jewel of Christian Contentment" published in 1648, defined contentment as "that sweet, inward, quiet, gracious frame of spirit, which freely submits to and delights in God's wise and fatherly disposal in every condition." Burroughs argued that contentment is not a natural human quality — it is a supernatural grace, produced by the Holy Spirit and cultivated through the disciplines of faith, prayer, and meditation on God's promises. The person who possesses this contentment is inoculated against the temptations that the eighth commandment addresses. He does not steal because he does not need to. He does not defraud because he does not want to. He does not hoard because he knows that his true treasure is laid up in heaven. And he gives freely because he has experienced the generosity of God and desires to reflect that generosity to others.

PART THIRTEEN: APPLICATION FOR OUR TIME

The eighth commandment speaks with extraordinary relevance to our own age. We live in a culture marked by materialism, consumer debt, financial dishonesty, and a pervasive sense of entitlement. The Puritan economic ethic — with its emphasis on honest labor, frugal living, generous giving, and faithful stewardship — offers a compelling alternative to the restless, acquisitive spirit of our time.

As homeschool students, you are in a unique position to develop habits of honest living that will serve you throughout your lives. Begin now to practice the principles of the eighth commandment: work diligently at your studies, fulfilling your responsibilities with excellence and integrity. Be scrupulously honest in all your dealings with others. Avoid the temptation to take shortcuts, to cut corners, or to acquire things through dishonest means. Practice generosity — even in small ways — giving to those in need, supporting the work of the church, and sharing your resources with others. And above all, cultivate the attitude of stewardship — recognizing that everything you possess belongs to God and that you are accountable to Him for how you use it.

Identify and explain at least four of these less obvious violations. Why did the Puritans insist that laziness, gambling, and oppressive lending practices are forms of stealing?

Review Question 3: Explain the Puritan doctrine of vocation as developed by William Perkins. How does this doctrine elevate ordinary work to the status of a sacred calling, and how does it relate to the eighth commandment's requirement of honest labor?

Review Question 4: Summarize the three principles of the Puritan economic ethic — gain all you can, save all you can, give all you can — and explain how each principle relates to the eighth commandment. What is the relationship between these three principles, and why did the Puritans insist that all three are necessary?

Review Question 5: How does the gospel transform our relationship to money and possessions? Using Ephesians 4:28 and 2 Corinthians 8:9, explain how the grace of Christ turns takers into givers and hoarders into stewards.

Write a paragraph explaining the reasoning behind your allocations and how the eighth commandment's principles of stewardship and generosity shaped your decisions.

--- Closing ---

The eighth commandment calls us to a life of integrity, industry, and generosity — to earn honestly, spend wisely, save prudently, and give freely. When we do, we honor the God who owns everything and entrusts us with the privilege of managing a small portion for His glory. The Puritan vision of honest living is not a burden but a liberation — for the person who lives by these principles is free from the anxiety of dishonesty, the guilt of exploitation, and the emptiness of selfishness. He works with joy, gives with gladness, and rests in the assurance that his true treasure is laid up in heaven, where neither moth nor rust can corrupt, and where thieves cannot break through and steal. Until next time, students.
