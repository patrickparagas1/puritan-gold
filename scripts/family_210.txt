PURITAN GOLD -- Family Devotional, Day 18
"A Tender Conscience in the Home"
Jonathan Edwards — Religious Affections Applied to the Family

---

Good evening, dear family. Welcome back to Puritan Gold. Tonight Edwards teaches us about the tender conscience — a heart that is sensitive to sin and quick to repent — and why this is essential for a healthy family. Let us begin with prayer.

Let us pray together. Lord, give us tender hearts and sensitive consciences. Help us to hate sin not merely because of its consequences but because it grieves You. And when we stumble, give us the grace to confess quickly and the humility to receive forgiveness. In Christ's name, Amen.

Our Scripture reading this evening comes from Psalm 51:10, 17. Let us hear the Word of the Lord:

"Create in me a clean heart, O God; and renew a right spirit within me. The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit: a broken and a contrite heart, O God, thou wilt not despise."

That is Psalm 51:10, 17. Let those words settle in your heart for a moment before we continue.

--- Teaching ---

Jonathan Edwards listed a tender conscience as one of the distinguishing marks of genuine religious affection. A person whose heart has truly been touched by God develops an acute sensitivity to sin — not the anxious, scrupulous kind that sees sin where there is none, but the healthy, responsive kind that recognizes genuine offenses against God and grieves over them.

Edwards observed that counterfeit religion often produces the opposite effect. People who have had intense emotional experiences but no genuine work of the Spirit frequently become harder, not softer. They become proud of their experiences, dismissive of others' concerns, and increasingly comfortable with sin in their own lives. True grace, by contrast, produces a softening of the heart — a tenderness that responds to sin the way a healthy nerve responds to pain.

For families, a tender conscience is an indispensable virtue. Consider what happens in a home where consciences are dull. Offenses go unacknowledged. Hurts accumulate without apology. Patterns of sin become entrenched because no one confronts them. The atmosphere of the home gradually becomes thick with unresolved grievances, and relationships deteriorate under the weight of accumulated bitterness.

Now consider what happens in a home where consciences are tender. When a parent speaks too harshly, the conviction comes quickly, and the apology follows: "I'm sorry. That was wrong. Will you forgive me?" When a child disobeys, the internal awareness of wrongdoing leads to confession rather than excuse-making. When siblings hurt one another, the discomfort of a guilty conscience motivates reconciliation rather than avoidance. In such a home, sin is addressed promptly, forgiveness flows freely, and the air is kept clear.

Edwards would point out that a tender conscience is not naturally produced. It is a work of the Holy Spirit. Our natural tendency is to excuse our sin, minimize it, or blame others for it. It takes supernatural grace to look honestly at our own failures and to say, with David, "I have sinned against the Lord." This is why family devotion is so important. As the family regularly encounters God's Word, the Spirit uses that Word to sensitize their consciences, to illuminate areas of sin they had not noticed, and to move them toward repentance and renewal.

There is a particular application here for parents. Children learn how to handle sin by watching their parents. If parents never apologize, children learn that admitting fault is a sign of weakness. If parents blame others for their mistakes, children learn to do the same. But if parents model repentance — if they are willing to say to their children, "I was wrong, and I'm sorry" — children learn that confession is not shameful but courageous, and that a tender conscience is not a burden but a gift.

Edwards also taught that repentance is not a one-time event but a lifelong habit. Families should cultivate what the Puritans called "daily repentance" — the practice of regularly examining the conscience, confessing known sins to God and to one another, and receiving the fresh assurance of God's forgiveness through Christ. This practice keeps the family's spiritual life healthy, just as regular hygiene keeps the body healthy.

David's prayer in Psalm 51 should be the prayer of every family: "Create in me a clean heart, O God." Not just once, but daily. Not just for me, but for us — for our whole household. A clean heart. A tender conscience. A readiness to repent. This is the soil in which healthy family relationships grow.

--- Discussion Questions ---

Let us pause and talk as a family. Here are tonight's discussion questions. Everyone gets a turn to share.

Question 1: What is a "tender conscience"? Why is it a sign of genuine faith rather than a sign of weakness?

Question 2: Edwards observed that parents model repentance for their children. How does seeing a parent apologize affect a child's understanding of confession and forgiveness?

Question 3: Is there anything on anyone's conscience tonight that needs to be confessed and forgiven within our family? Let us practice the grace of reconciliation right now.

Take a few minutes to talk about these questions together. Let everyone share, and listen to one another with patience and love.

--- Memory Verse ---

Now, here is our memory verse for today. Let us say it together:

"Create in me a clean heart, O God; and renew a right spirit within me." -- Psalm 51:10

Try to say it out loud three times right now. Then, before bed tonight, see if you can say it from memory.

--- Closing ---

Let us pray together. Father, create in us clean hearts and tender consciences. Help us to be quick to confess and quick to forgive. May our home be a place where sin is addressed honestly and grace is offered freely. Renew a right spirit within every member of this family. In Jesus' name, Amen.

Sleep well tonight, with a clean heart and a clear conscience before God.
