PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Doctrine Deep-Dive, Day 24
"The Court of Conscience"
The Doctrine of Conscience Explored Through the Puritans


Good evening. The Puritans were theologians of the highest order, but they were also the most practical of people. They knew that theology untethered from life is useless, and that life untethered from theology is dangerous. Tonight, we hold the two together.

Tonight we draw from the work of William Fenner, specifically from "The Sacred Doctrine of Divinitie / A Treatise of Conscience." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Romans 2:14-15:

"For when the Gentiles, which have not the law, do by nature the things contained in the law, these, having not the law, are a law unto themselves: Which shew the work of the law written in their hearts, their conscience also bearing witness, and their thoughts the mean while accusing or else excusing one another."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


The Puritans were the great theologians of conscience. No tradition in Christian history has given more careful, sustained, and practical attention to the nature, functions, and care of the conscience than the Puritan movement. For the Puritans, conscience was not merely a feeling of guilt or approval; it was a God-given faculty of the soul, a court within, appointed by the Creator to apply His law to the specifics of human life.

William Fenner, a lesser-known but profoundly insightful Puritan minister, wrote with particular clarity on the doctrine of conscience. Fenner defines conscience as that faculty of the soul by which a person knows himself and judges his own actions, thoughts, and motives in light of the moral law of God. Conscience is not the moral law itself; it is the application of the moral law to particular cases. It functions, as the Puritans were fond of saying, as God's deputy within the soul — a judge whom God has appointed to sit in the courtroom of the heart.

Fenner identifies three functions of conscience. First, it bears witness. Conscience testifies to what we have done, thought, and intended. It keeps a record — not infallible, but substantial — of our moral history. Paul speaks of conscience 'bearing witness' and 'thoughts accusing or excusing.' Conscience is the soul's historian, and its records will be opened on the day of judgment.

Second, conscience judges. It does not merely record; it evaluates. It pronounces actions right or wrong, motives pure or impure, intentions good or evil. This judicial function is what produces the experience of guilt (when conscience condemns) or peace (when conscience approves). Fenner writes that a good conscience is one of the greatest treasures a believer can possess: it is the foundation of spiritual peace, the source of boldness in prayer, and the shield against despair.

Third, conscience executes. It does not merely judge; it acts upon its judgment. A condemning conscience produces fear, shame, and restlessness. An approving conscience produces peace, joy, and confidence. Fenner writes that the terrors of a guilty conscience are among the most severe sufferings a human being can experience — more painful than physical illness, more oppressive than external persecution. The man whose conscience is at war with him has no peace, even if all his outward circumstances are favorable.

The Puritan William Ames, in his influential work 'Conscience with the Power and Cases Thereof,' adds further depth. Ames develops what became known as 'casuistry' or 'case divinity' — the art of applying moral principles to specific, complex situations. The Puritans did not believe that conscience operates in a vacuum; it must be informed by Scripture and guided by sound reasoning. An uninformed conscience may approve what God condemns or condemn what God approves. Therefore, the education of the conscience through the study of God's Word is one of the most important duties of the Christian life.

Richard Baxter further distinguishes between different states of conscience. A tender conscience is one that is sensitive to sin and quick to respond to the Spirit's conviction. A hardened conscience is one that has been repeatedly overruled until it no longer functions properly — seared, as Paul says, 'with a hot iron' (1 Timothy 4:2). A weak conscience is one that is overly scrupulous, condemning what God has not condemned and producing unnecessary guilt. A well-informed and tender conscience, Baxter argues, is the goal: one that is sensitive enough to detect real sin, sturdy enough to resist false guilt, and educated enough to apply God's Word accurately.

The Puritans also addressed the care of the conscience. Fenner counsels believers to keep short accounts with God — to confess sin promptly rather than allowing it to accumulate and burden the conscience. He also counsels the distinction between the accusations of conscience (which may be the Spirit's conviction) and the accusations of Satan (which aim at despair rather than repentance). The Spirit convicts with the aim of restoration; Satan accuses with the aim of destruction.

The Puritan John Owen adds a vital Christological dimension to the doctrine of conscience. Owen writes that the blood of Christ is the ultimate answer to the accusing conscience. When conscience condemns — and condemns rightly, for we are indeed sinners — the believer must point his conscience to the cross. 'The blood of Jesus Christ his Son cleanseth us from all sin' (1 John 1:7). Owen argues that the conscience can only be truly pacified by the objective reality of Christ's atonement. Subjective feelings of remorse are insufficient; only the blood of Christ can silence the legitimate accusations of a rightly-functioning conscience. This is why the doctrine of the atonement is not merely academic theology but the most practical truth in the believer's daily experience.

Patrick, how is the court of your conscience functioning? Is it tender and well-informed, quick to detect real sin and wise enough to distinguish it from false guilt? Or has it been neglected, either hardened by repeated overruling or confused by lack of biblical instruction? The care of the conscience is one of the most practical and important aspects of the Christian life. Tend it well.


Here are some questions to carry with you into the coming days.

Question 1: Is my conscience tender and responsive to the Spirit's conviction, or has it been dulled by repeated compromise?

Question 2: Can I distinguish between the Spirit's conviction (which leads to repentance and restoration) and Satan's accusation (which leads to despair)?

Question 3: Am I educating my conscience through regular, careful study of God's Word so that it judges accurately?

Question 4: Do I keep short accounts with God, confessing sin promptly, or do I allow guilt to accumulate and burden my soul?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Lord God, Searcher of hearts, I ask You to tend the court of my conscience. Where it has been hardened, soften it. Where it has been confused, inform it by Your Word. Where it has been burdened by false guilt, relieve it with the truth of the gospel. Help me to keep short accounts with You, confessing sin promptly and receiving forgiveness gratefully. Give me a conscience that is tender enough to hear Your voice and sturdy enough to resist the accuser. Through Christ my Advocate, Amen.

Rest in His grace tonight. Tomorrow is a new day of growth.
