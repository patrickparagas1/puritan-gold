PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Guided Puritan Reading, Day 2
"The Mortification of Sin, Part 2"
John Owen — The Means of Killing Sin

---

Good evening, friend. Last time we laid the foundation with John Owen by understanding what indwelling sin is and why mortification is necessary. Tonight we press further into Owen's treatise to ask the practical question: how does a believer actually mortify sin? Owen's answer is both deeply theological and intensely practical.

--- Scripture ---

Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Galatians 5:16-17:

"This I say then, Walk in the Spirit, and ye shall not fulfil the lust of the flesh. For the flesh lusteth against the Spirit, and the Spirit against the flesh: and these are contrary the one to the other: so that ye cannot do the things that ye would."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.

--- Guided Reading ---

Having established the necessity of mortification in the first portion of his treatise, Owen now turns to the methods. And here he surprises many readers. Owen does not begin with practical techniques or behavioral strategies. He begins with the gospel.

Owen's first direction for the mortification of sin is this: get a clear and abiding sense of the guilt, danger, and evil of the sin you are fighting. This is not merely intellectual acknowledgment. Owen means a deep, heart-felt conviction wrought by the Spirit. The believer must see his sin as God sees it: as a violation of divine holiness, an act of rebellion against infinite love, and a grieving of the Spirit who dwells within. Owen argues that many believers fail in mortification because they fight sin without truly hating it. They want to be free from its consequences but not from its nature. They want relief but not repentance.

Owen's second direction is to load the conscience with the guilt of sin by a clear application of the law. The law of God is a mirror that shows us the true ugliness of our transgression. Owen counsels the believer to take his particular sin and hold it up against the commands of God, asking: what does this sin say about my love for God? What does it say about my gratitude for the cross? What does it say about my regard for the Spirit who indwells me? This is not legalism; it is the proper use of the law in the life of the justified man.

Third, Owen instructs the believer to cultivate a constant longing for deliverance from the power of indwelling sin. This is not mere frustration with failure. It is a holy desire, born of the Spirit, for complete freedom from sin's remaining influence. Owen points to Paul's cry in Romans 7:24: "O wretched man that I am! who shall deliver me from the body of this death?" This is the cry of a man who knows both the reality of his corruption and the certainty of his deliverance. The believer who has lost this longing has grown dangerously comfortable with his sin.

Fourth, Owen addresses what he considers the most important direction of all: fix your heart upon the cross of Christ. Owen argues that the death of Christ is not merely the ground of justification but the power of sanctification. When the believer contemplates the cross, he sees what sin cost the Son of God. He sees the infinite love that bore that cost. And in seeing these things, the power of sin is broken. Owen writes that a man who walks in the light of the cross will find sin increasingly distasteful. The lusts that once seemed attractive will appear in their true character: as the very things that nailed the Savior to the tree.

Owen develops this point extensively. He argues that there is a mortifying virtue in the cross itself. The believer who by faith beholds Christ crucified for his sins receives a power that no mere resolution or moral effort can supply. The cross humbles pride, for it shows us that we could not save ourselves. It weakens covetousness, for it displays a treasure infinitely more valuable than anything this world offers. It subdues sensuality, for it reveals a pleasure — the pleasure of communion with God — that far exceeds any fleshly indulgence.

Fifth, Owen counsels vigorous and diligent resistance at the first motions of sin. Do not parley with temptation. Do not entertain sinful thoughts. Do not reason with lust. Owen compares the first motions of sin to sparks: extinguished quickly, they do no damage; left unattended, they become a conflagration. The believer who waits until sin has taken hold before he begins to fight has already lost significant ground.

Sixth, Owen emphasizes the necessity of prayer. The believer must pray specifically against his particular sins. General prayers for holiness are good but insufficient. Owen counsels the believer to name his sin before God, to confess its power over him, to plead the blood of Christ, and to ask for the specific grace needed to overcome this specific enemy.

Finally, Owen reminds the believer that mortification is a lifelong work. There is no point in this earthly pilgrimage where the believer can say, "I have finished the fight." New temptations arise. Old sins resurface in new forms. The flesh never fully surrenders. But the Spirit is sufficient. The grace of God is inexhaustible. And the promise of final victory is certain: one day, in glory, sin will be no more. Until that day, we fight. And in the fighting, we live.

--- Reflection ---

Carry these questions with you into the quiet of the evening.

Question 1: Am I fighting sin at the level of behavior only, or am I addressing the heart by fixing my gaze upon the cross of Christ?

Question 2: Do I pray specifically against my besetting sins, or do I offer only general prayers for holiness?

Question 3: Owen says to resist sin at its first motions. Where have I been entertaining sinful thoughts rather than cutting them off immediately?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.

--- Closing Prayer ---

Lord Jesus, let the power of Thy cross break the power of sin in my life. Teach me to hate what is evil and to cling to what is good. Give me the grace to fight at the first motion of temptation and to rest in Thy sufficient strength. Amen.

Rest well tonight. The battle is the Lord's, and He has already won.
