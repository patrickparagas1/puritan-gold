SEGMENT 1: MORNING DEVOTION
----------------------------------------

Good morning, students. Welcome to the Puritan Academy. Let us begin this day as the Puritans did, by acknowledging God as the source of all knowledge and wisdom. Before we open our textbooks, let us open our hearts.

Almighty God, the fountain of all wisdom, we ask You to bless our studies today. Open our minds to understand, our hearts to receive, and our wills to obey the truth. Grant us diligence, humility, and a spirit of inquiry that honors You. Through Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.

Let us meditate on a portion of Scripture before we begin our main lesson.

Our Scripture for today is Isaiah 6:1-7:

"In the year that king Uzziah died I saw also the Lord sitting upon a throne, high and lifted up, and his train filled the temple. Above it stood the seraphims: each one had six wings; with twain he covered his face, and with twain he covered his feet, and with twain he did fly. And one cried unto another, and said, Holy, holy, holy, is the LORD of hosts: the whole earth is full of his glory. And the posts of the door moved at the voice of him that cried, and the house was filled with smoke. Then said I, Woe is me! for I am undone; because I am a man of unclean lips, and I dwell in the midst of a people of unclean lips: for mine eyes have seen the King, the LORD of hosts. Then flew one of the seraphims unto me, having a live coal in his hand, which he had taken with the tongs from off the altar: And he laid it upon my mouth, and said, Lo, this hath touched thy lips; and thine iniquity is taken away, and thy sin purged."

As you begin this day, quiet your heart and consider: the God you worship is holy beyond all comprehension. The angels who have never sinned still cover their faces before Him. The foundations of heaven tremble at the sound of His holiness. Yet this same God, in His unfathomable mercy, has reached down to cleanse you through the blood of His Son. You are not worthy to stand in His presence — and yet He invites you to come boldly to the throne of grace. Let this paradox fill you with humble wonder. Let the holiness of God drive away all casual, careless thoughts and replace them with deep reverence and grateful worship. Be ye holy, for He is holy.

As we prepare for today's lesson, remember that everything we study flows from this one fountain: the Word of God. Our lesson today is part of Knowing God — His Attributes, and it is titled: God's Holiness & Our Reverence. Let us proceed with minds engaged and hearts open.

SEGMENT 2: MAIN LESSON PART 1 -- BIBLICAL FOUNDATION
----------------------------------------

Now let us turn to our main lesson for today.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes in Isaiah 6:1-7.

The holiness of God is the foundation upon which all other divine attributes rest. When the prophet Isaiah beheld the Lord in the year that King Uzziah died, he witnessed something that would transform his entire ministry and understanding of who God is. The text tells us in Isaiah 6:1, "I saw also the Lord sitting upon a throne, high and lifted up, and his train filled the temple." This vision was not merely a visual spectacle but a profound theological revelation. The death of Uzziah, a king who had been struck with leprosy for presuming to enter the temple and burn incense (2 Chronicles 26:16-21), provided the historical backdrop for understanding holiness. The earthly king who violated God's holiness was dead; the heavenly King whose holiness endures forever was revealed.

The seraphims — angelic beings whose very name means "burning ones" — stood above the throne of God. Their posture is deeply instructive. With two wings they covered their faces, signifying that even sinless, glorified creatures cannot gaze fully upon the radiance of divine holiness. With two wings they covered their feet, an act of humility and reverence before the Most High. With two wings they flew, carrying out the service of God. Here we see that worship and reverence must always precede and surround our service. The proportion is significant: four wings devoted to worship, two to service. The priorities of heaven teach us the priorities of the Christian life.

The cry of the seraphims is the only attribute of God repeated three times in succession in all of Scripture: "Holy, holy, holy, is the LORD of hosts." God is not merely called "love, love, love" or "power, power, power" — though He is infinite in both. The threefold repetition emphasizes holiness as the supreme attribute, the attribute that qualifies all others. God's love is a holy love. God's justice is a holy justice. God's power is a holy power. The Hebrew language uses repetition for emphasis; a threefold repetition signifies the superlative degree. God is not simply holy — He is infinitely, perfectly, and eternally holy.

The physical effects of this declaration are staggering: "the posts of the door moved at the voice of him that cried, and the house was filled with smoke." The very foundations of the temple shook. The smoke recalls the cloud of glory that filled the tabernacle in Exodus 40:34-35, when "Moses was not able to enter into the tent of the congregation, because the cloud abode thereon, and the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle." God's holiness is not a passive quality but an active, overwhelming reality that shakes creation itself.

Isaiah's response reveals what happens when a sinful creature encounters the holy God: "Woe is me! for I am undone; because I am a man of unclean lips, and I dwell in the midst of a people of unclean lips: for mine eyes have seen the King, the LORD of hosts." The word "undone" in Hebrew carries the sense of being ruined, destroyed, cut to pieces. Isaiah did not merely feel uncomfortable — he felt annihilated. The holiness of God is like a blazing furnace before which all human pretension of goodness melts away. Isaiah, one of the godliest men in Israel, did not say "Woe to the pagans" or "Woe to the idolaters." He said "Woe is me." The closer one draws to God's holiness, the more one perceives one's own sinfulness.

This principle is confirmed throughout Scripture. When Job finally encountered God, he declared, "I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear: but now mine eye seeth thee. Wherefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes" (Job 42:5-6). When Peter witnessed the miraculous catch of fish and perceived the holiness of Christ, he "fell down at Jesus' knees, saying, Depart from me; for I am a sinful man, O Lord" (Luke 5:8). When the apostle John saw the risen Christ in glory, "I fell at his feet as dead" (Revelation 1:17). The pattern is unmistakable: true encounters with God's holiness produce not self-confidence but self-abasement.

Yet the passage does not end in despair. The seraphim took a live coal from the altar — the altar of atonement — and touched Isaiah's lips, saying, "Lo, this hath touched thy lips; and thine iniquity is taken away, and thy sin purged." The coal from the altar speaks of sacrifice, of blood shed for the remission of sins. Before Isaiah could serve God, he needed to be cleansed by God. This prefigures the work of Christ, the Lamb of God, whose blood alone purifies sinners and makes them fit to stand before a holy God.

Leviticus 11:44-45 establishes God's holiness as the basis for the holiness He requires of His people: "For I am the LORD your God: ye shall therefore sanctify yourselves, and ye shall be holy; for I am holy." This command is not optional — it flows from God's very nature. Because God is holy, those who belong to Him must be holy. Peter quotes this passage in 1 Peter 1:15-16: "But as he which hath called you is holy, so be ye holy in all manner of conversation; Because it is written, Be ye holy; for I am holy." The word "conversation" in the King James Version means conduct, manner of life. Holiness is not confined to religious exercises; it must pervade every aspect of our lives.

Revelation 4:8 takes us into the heavenly throne room where the four living creatures repeat the same eternal refrain: "Holy, holy, holy, Lord God Almighty, which was, and is, and is to come." From Isaiah's temple vision to John's apocalyptic vision, across the span of biblical revelation, the holiness of God remains the centerpiece of heavenly worship. This is not a truth for theologians alone but for every believer who would know God as He truly is.

The practical implications of God's holiness are vast. First, it defines true worship. Worship that does not begin with a sense of God's holiness is not truly Christian worship — it is merely entertainment or self-expression. Second, it produces genuine repentance. We cannot truly repent until we see our sin against the backdrop of God's perfect purity. Third, it motivates our pursuit of holiness. As Hebrews 12:14 warns, "Follow peace with all men, and holiness, without which no man shall see the Lord." Fourth, it magnifies the cross of Christ. Only when we understand how holy God is can we begin to appreciate what it cost for Him to save unholy sinners. The cross is where God's holiness and God's love meet — where the just demands of a holy God are satisfied by the loving sacrifice of His Son.

This biblical foundation is essential. Without it, everything else we study today would be like a house built on sand. But with it, we stand on solid rock.

SEGMENT 3: MAIN LESSON PART 2 -- PURITAN TEACHING
----------------------------------------

Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. Stephen Charnock wrote extensively on this subject in "Discourses upon the Existence and Attributes of God." The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life.

Stephen Charnock (1628-1680) devoted some of the most profound theological writing in the English language to the subject of God's holiness in his monumental work, "Discourses upon the Existence and Attributes of God." For Charnock, the holiness of God was not one attribute among many but the crown and glory of all the divine perfections. He wrote that holiness is "the most sparkling jewel in the crown of God" and that it is "the attribute of attributes," the one quality that adorns and beautifies all the rest. Without holiness, Charnock argued, God's power would be tyranny, His wisdom would be cunning, and His sovereignty would be despotism. It is holiness that makes God altogether lovely and worthy of infinite adoration.

Charnock carefully distinguished between God's holiness and human holiness. Human holiness, he observed, is derived — it comes from God and depends upon God for its continuance. But God's holiness is original, underived, and self-existent. God is not holy because He conforms to some external standard of goodness; rather, He Himself is the standard. Charnock wrote: "He is not only holy, but holiness itself, the spring and fountain of all holiness." This insight is crucial because it means that holiness is not arbitrary — it is rooted in the very nature and character of the eternal God. What God commands is holy because God Himself is holy, and He cannot act contrary to His own nature.

Charnock explored the implications of divine holiness for God's hatred of sin with penetrating clarity. He argued that God's opposition to sin is not merely a decision God makes but an expression of who God is. Just as fire by its very nature opposes water, so holiness by its very nature opposes all that is unholy. Charnock observed that God "cannot look upon sin without abhorrence; He cannot look upon it as a mote, but a beam; He cannot look upon it as a small thing." This understanding helps explain the severity of God's judgments throughout Scripture — from the flood, to the destruction of Sodom, to the plagues of Egypt, to the exile of Israel. These are not overreactions by a temperamental deity but the necessary responses of infinite holiness to the offense of sin.

The Puritan pastor also taught that a right understanding of God's holiness is the foundation of genuine spiritual experience. He observed that many professing Christians have a superficial view of God that produces a superficial faith. They think of God as merely powerful, or merely loving, without grasping that His power and love are exercised in perfect holiness. Charnock wrote that to know God's holiness rightly is to know the terror of sin and the preciousness of Christ's sacrifice. When a soul truly apprehends the holiness of God, it can no longer be content with a casual, half-hearted religion. It must either flee from God in terror or flee to Christ in faith.

Thomas Watson, another great Puritan divine, complemented Charnock's teaching on holiness. In his work "A Body of Divinity," Watson described God's holiness as "the enamel of all His attributes" — the quality that gives beauty and luster to everything God is and does. Watson argued that God's holiness is so central to His nature that it was the first thing proclaimed by the angels in Isaiah's vision, and it will be the eternal theme of heavenly worship. Watson wrote: "God is the exemplar and pattern of holiness; He is not only holy but the Holy One."

Watson was particularly effective in applying the doctrine of God's holiness to the daily life of the believer. He taught that meditation on God's holiness should produce three effects in the Christian. First, it should produce humility. When we see how infinitely pure God is, all pride in our own goodness is destroyed. Watson observed that even our best works are tainted with sin and cannot stand before God's perfect standard. The holiest saint on earth, if measured against the holiness of God, would find himself as far short of the mark as the worst sinner. This is why salvation must be by grace alone through faith alone — because no human righteousness can satisfy the demands of divine holiness.

Second, Watson taught that meditation on God's holiness should produce a love for holiness in the believer's own life. If God is holy, and we are called to be like God, then we must love what God loves and hate what God hates. Watson cautioned against a mere outward conformity to rules and insisted on inward transformation. True holiness, he argued, begins in the heart. A person may refrain from outward sins while harboring pride, envy, and malice within. Such a person is like a tomb that is whitewashed on the outside but full of corruption within — the very comparison our Lord made in Matthew 23:27. Watson urged believers to examine not merely their actions but their affections, their desires, and their secret thoughts.

Third, Watson taught that meditation on God's holiness should produce a grateful dependence upon Christ. Since we can never achieve holiness sufficient to satisfy God's standard, we must rest entirely upon the holiness of Christ imputed to us by faith. Watson frequently pointed believers to 1 Corinthians 1:30, where Paul declares that Christ "is made unto us wisdom, and righteousness, and sanctification, and redemption." Christ is our holiness. His perfect life is credited to our account. His spotless righteousness is the garment in which we stand before a holy God. Watson wrote that the believer should put on Christ's righteousness as a man puts on a warm garment against the cold — wrapping himself entirely in it, finding all his comfort and protection in it.

Charnock and Watson together provide a comprehensive Puritan understanding of divine holiness that is deeply biblical and practically transformative. Charnock gives us the theological depth — the careful, systematic exposition of what it means for God to be holy. Watson gives us the devotional warmth — the application that reaches the heart and changes the life. Together, they teach us that the holiness of God is not a terrifying abstraction but a living truth that should drive us to worship, repentance, faith, and the daily pursuit of godliness.

The Puritan emphasis on God's holiness stands as a corrective to much modern Christianity that emphasizes God's love and kindness while neglecting His purity and moral perfection. The Puritans understood that you cannot truly know God's love until you know His holiness, for only then can you appreciate the staggering cost of that love. A God who is not holy has nothing from which to save us. A God who is holy but not loving would consume us in judgment. But the God of Scripture — the God whom Charnock and Watson devoted their lives to studying — is both infinitely holy and infinitely loving, and in the cross of Christ these attributes are perfectly harmonized for the salvation of sinners.

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.

SEGMENT 4: DISCUSSION AND APPLICATION
----------------------------------------

Now let us pause and consider what we have learned. Discussion is where understanding deepens.

Discussion Question 1: Why do you think holiness is the only attribute of God repeated three times by the seraphims? What does this tell us about its importance compared to God's other attributes?

Discussion Question 2: When Isaiah saw God's holiness, he immediately became aware of his own sinfulness. Why does a true vision of God always produce this effect? Can you think of other biblical examples?

Discussion Question 3: Charnock called holiness 'the attribute of attributes.' What did he mean by this? How does holiness affect all of God's other qualities like power, love, and justice?

Discussion Question 4: How should the doctrine of God's holiness affect the way we approach worship? What would change in our churches if we truly grasped this truth?

Discussion Question 5: Watson taught that meditation on God's holiness should produce humility, love for holiness, and dependence on Christ. Which of these three do you most need to grow in, and why?

Take time with each question. The Puritans valued what they called "holy conference," the practice of discussing spiritual truths together. Do not settle for quick, surface answers. Push deeper. Ask follow-up questions. Listen carefully to one another.

Application is where truth becomes life. It is not enough to know what Isaiah 6:1-7 says. We must ask: how does this change the way I live today? How does this shape my thoughts, my words, my actions? The Puritans insisted that a truth that does not transform is a truth that has not been truly understood.

SEGMENT 4B: DEEPER THEOLOGICAL ANALYSIS
----------------------------------------

Let us now press further into the depths of this doctrine, examining additional Scripture passages and theological insights that will enrich our understanding of God's holiness.

Consider the tabernacle that God commanded Moses to build in the wilderness. The entire structure was designed to teach Israel about the holiness of God. Exodus 26:33 records God's instruction: "And thou shalt hang up the vail under the taches, that thou mayest bring in thither within the vail the ark of the testimony: and the vail shall divide unto you between the holy place and the most holy." The veil was a thick curtain of blue, purple, and scarlet, embroidered with cherubim, and it served as a barrier between sinful man and the holy presence of God. No one could pass through that veil except the High Priest, and he only once per year on the Day of Atonement, and never without the blood of sacrifice. The message was unmistakable: God is holy, and sinful man cannot approach Him except on His terms and through His provision.

When Christ died upon the cross, Matthew 27:51 records a breathtaking event: "And, behold, the veil of the temple was rent in twain from the top to the bottom; and the earth did quake, and the rocks rent." The veil was torn not from the bottom upward, as though by human hands, but from the top downward, as though by the hand of God Himself. This signified that through the sacrifice of Christ, the barrier between God and man was removed. Hebrews 10:19-22 draws out the application: "Having therefore, brethren, boldness to enter into the holiest by the blood of Jesus, By a new and living way, which he hath consecrated for us, through the veil, that is to say, his flesh; And having an high priest over the house of God; Let us draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith, having our hearts sprinkled from an evil conscience, and our bodies washed with pure water." The holiness of God has not diminished, but the blood of Christ has opened the way for sinners to approach the Holy One with confidence.

Exodus 3:1-6 records another powerful encounter with divine holiness — Moses at the burning bush. "And the angel of the LORD appeared unto him in a flame of fire out of the midst of a bush: and he looked, and, behold, the bush burned with fire, and the bush was not consumed. And Moses said, I will now turn aside, and see this great sight, why the bush is not burnt. And when the LORD saw that he turned aside to see, God called unto him out of the midst of the bush, and said, Moses, Moses. And he said, Here am I. And he said, Draw not nigh hither: put off thy shoes from off thy feet, for the place whereon thou standest is holy ground. Moreover he said, I am the God of thy father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob. And Moses hid his face; for he was afraid to look upon God." The ground was not inherently holy — it was ordinary desert sand. But the presence of God made it holy. Wherever God is, holiness is, and wherever holiness is, reverence is required. Moses removed his sandals and hid his face. This is the posture of a man who understands that he stands before the thrice-holy God.

The Puritan divine John Owen (1616-1683), who served as Vice-Chancellor of Oxford University and chaplain to Oliver Cromwell, wrote extensively on the holiness of God in connection with the believer's sanctification. Owen taught that the holiness of God is both the standard and the source of the believer's holiness. In his treatise "On the Mortification of Sin," Owen argued that believers must wage a constant battle against sin precisely because God is holy. He wrote that a Christian who tolerates sin in his life is like a man who keeps a serpent in his bosom — it may seem dormant, but it will eventually strike. Owen insisted that the only proper response to the holiness of God is the diligent, daily mortification of sin through the power of the Holy Spirit.

Owen also taught that the Holy Spirit is the agent of sanctification in the believer's life. Romans 8:13 declares, "For if ye live after the flesh, ye shall die: but if ye through the Spirit do mortify the deeds of the body, ye shall live." Owen emphasized that believers cannot make themselves holy by sheer willpower or moral effort. Holiness is the work of the Spirit, who applies the holiness of Christ to the hearts and lives of those who belong to Him. The believer's responsibility is to cooperate with the Spirit — to use the means of grace, to resist temptation, to confess sin, and to pursue righteousness — while relying entirely upon the Spirit's power to accomplish what human effort alone never could.

Richard Sibbes (1577-1635), another eminent Puritan pastor known as "the heavenly Doctor Sibbes," offered a complementary perspective on God's holiness in his famous work "The Bruised Reed." While Owen emphasized the severity of God's holiness in demanding the mortification of sin, Sibbes emphasized the tenderness of God's holiness in dealing with weak and struggling believers. Sibbes pointed to Isaiah 42:3, which prophesied of Christ: "A bruised reed shall he not break, and the smoking flax shall he not quench." Sibbes taught that God's holiness does not crush those who are humbly seeking Him. The bruised reed — the believer whose faith is weak and battered — will not be broken by God's holy hand but will be gently supported and restored. The smoking flax — the believer whose spiritual fire has dimmed to a mere spark — will not be extinguished but will be carefully tended until it burns brightly again. This is the compassionate side of God's holiness: it demands perfection, yet it patiently nurtures those who are on the way to perfection through the grace of Christ.

Habakkuk 1:13 adds another dimension to our understanding: "Thou art of purer eyes than to behold evil, and canst not look on iniquity." God's holiness is so absolute that He cannot look upon evil with any degree of tolerance or approval. This does not mean that God is unaware of evil — He is omniscient and sees all things. Rather, it means that God cannot regard evil with complacency or indifference. Every sin, no matter how small it appears to human eyes, is an affront to His infinite purity. This truth should produce in us what the Puritans called "a holy trembling" — not the trembling of a slave before a cruel master, but the trembling of a child who loves his father too much to grieve him.

The prophet Ezekiel's vision in Ezekiel 1:26-28 provides yet another glimpse of divine holiness: "And above the firmament that was over their heads was the likeness of a throne, as the appearance of a sapphire stone: and upon the likeness of the throne was the likeness as the appearance of a man above upon it. And I saw as the colour of amber, as the appearance of fire round about within it, from the appearance of his loins even upward, and from the appearance of his loins even downward, I saw as it were the appearance of fire, and it had brightness round about. As the appearance of the bow that is in the cloud in the day of rain, so was the appearance of the brightness round about. This was the appearance of the likeness of the glory of the LORD. And when I saw it, I fell upon my face." Like Isaiah, like Moses, like Job, Ezekiel fell upon his face before the majesty of God. The consistent testimony of Scripture is that every genuine encounter with God's holiness produces prostration, humility, and awe.

SEGMENT 4C: PRACTICAL APPLICATION
----------------------------------------

Before we move to our afternoon activity, let us consider specific ways to apply the doctrine of God's holiness to our daily lives. The Puritans were never content with mere intellectual knowledge — they insisted that every doctrine must bear fruit in practice.

First, let the holiness of God transform your private worship. When you come before God in prayer, do not rush into His presence with a casual, distracted mind. Pause. Remember who He is. The seraphim cover their faces; shall you not at least still your heart? Begin your prayers with adoration — acknowledging God's holiness, majesty, and glory before presenting your petitions. Ecclesiastes 5:2 counsels, "Be not rash with thy mouth, and let not thine heart be hasty to utter any thing before God: for God is in heaven, and thou upon earth: therefore let thy words be few." Reverence should mark every approach to the throne of grace.

Second, let the holiness of God govern your speech. Exodus 20:7 commands, "Thou shalt not take the name of the LORD thy God in vain; for the LORD will not hold him guiltless that taketh his name in vain." If God is holy, then His name is holy, and it must never be used carelessly, flippantly, or as a mere exclamation. The Puritans were scrupulous in their use of God's name, and they would be grieved to hear how casually it is spoken in our day. But this principle extends beyond merely avoiding profanity. It includes speaking of God with accuracy, humility, and reverence in all our conversations.

Third, let the holiness of God shape your entertainment and your habits. What you read, what you watch, what you listen to, and what you allow into your mind all either reflect or contradict your belief in a holy God. Paul exhorts in Philippians 4:8, "Finally, brethren, whatsoever things are true, whatsoever things are honest, whatsoever things are just, whatsoever things are pure, whatsoever things are lovely, whatsoever things are of good report; if there be any virtue, and if there be any praise, think on these things." A person who truly believes that God is holy will guard the gates of his mind with diligence, refusing to dwell upon things that offend the purity of God.

Fourth, let the holiness of God deepen your gratitude for the gospel. The holier you understand God to be, the more astonishing the gospel becomes. That the infinitely holy God would send His only Son to bear the punishment for your sins — that He would justify the ungodly, adopt rebels as sons, and welcome sinners into His eternal glory — this is grace beyond all comprehension. Let the holiness of God drive you not to despair but to the cross, where mercy and truth have met together, where righteousness and peace have kissed each other (Psalm 85:10).

Fifth, let the holiness of God motivate your pursuit of personal holiness. First Peter 1:14-16 instructs, "As obedient children, not fashioning yourselves according to the former lusts in your ignorance: But as he which hath called you is holy, so be ye holy in all manner of conversation; Because it is written, Be ye holy; for I am holy." Holiness is not optional for the believer — it is the calling of every child of God. This does not mean perfectionism, which is the proud attempt to achieve sinlessness by human effort. It means a humble, daily, Spirit-empowered pursuit of Christlikeness in thought, word, and deed. The Puritans called this "the practice of piety," and they regarded it as the highest occupation of the Christian life.

SEGMENT 5: AFTERNOON ACTIVITY
----------------------------------------

Now we move to our afternoon activity, where we put truth into practice.

Students will create an illuminated manuscript page of Isaiah 6:1-7. Using calligraphy pens or fine-tipped markers on parchment-style paper, students will carefully copy the full text of Isaiah 6:1-7 in their best handwriting. Around the margins, they will illustrate the key elements of the vision: the throne, the seraphims with six wings, the live coal, and the smoke filling the temple. Each illustration should include a brief annotation explaining its theological significance (e.g., 'Wings covering face = even sinless angels cannot gaze upon God's full holiness'). Students should use gold or yellow coloring for the throne and divine glory, red for the coal from the altar, and leave white space around the threefold 'Holy' to emphasize its centrality. This exercise combines artistic expression with careful meditation on the text.

Materials needed: Parchment-style paper or heavy cardstock, calligraphy pens or fine-tipped markers, colored pencils (especially gold, red, blue), ruler for margin lines, reference images of medieval illuminated manuscripts

As you complete this activity, think about how it connects to what you studied in today's lesson. The goal is not just to do something, but to reinforce the truths you have been learning. The Puritans believed that education should engage the whole person: mind, heart, and hands.

Take your time with this activity. Quality matters more than speed. When you are finished, reflect on what you learned through the process of doing. Often we understand something far better after we have tried to put it into practice.

SEGMENT 6: CROSS-SUBJECT CONNECTIONS
----------------------------------------

One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

The Temple in Jerusalem was the center of Israelite worship for nearly a thousand years. Isaiah's vision occurred around 740 BC, during a period of political upheaval as Assyria threatened the ancient Near East. King Uzziah's death marked the end of a prosperous era and the beginning of uncertainty. Study how ancient civilizations understood the concept of sacred space and how the design of the Temple reflected God's holiness — with increasing levels of restriction from the outer courts to the Holy of Holies, where only the High Priest could enter once per year.

Literature Connection:

Isaiah 6 is one of the greatest examples of theophany literature — writing that describes a direct encounter with God. Compare Isaiah's vision with other biblical theophanies such as Moses at the burning bush (Exodus 3) and Ezekiel's vision of the wheel (Ezekiel 1). Notice how each writer uses vivid sensory language — sight, sound, smell, physical sensation — to convey an experience that ultimately transcends human description. Discuss how the limitations of human language shape the way Scripture communicates divine realities.

Science Connection:

The concept of holiness — absolute purity and separation from contamination — has parallels in the scientific world. In laboratory settings, 'clean rooms' are designed to be completely free from contaminants, with multiple barriers separating the pure environment from the outside world. Discuss how the principle of separation and purity applies in chemistry (pure elements vs. compounds), biology (sterile environments for surgery), and physics (the concept of absolute zero as an ideal state). These physical analogies help illustrate the theological concept of God's absolute moral purity.

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.

SEGMENT 4D: THE HOLINESS OF GOD IN REDEMPTIVE HISTORY
----------------------------------------

Let us now trace the theme of God's holiness through the broader narrative of redemptive history, drawing upon additional Scripture passages and the wisdom of the Puritan divines who spent their lives meditating upon this glorious truth.

The holiness of God is not a static concept confined to a single vision or a single book of the Bible. It is a living theme that pulses through the entire story of redemption from Genesis to Revelation. In the garden of Eden, we see holiness in the command God gave to Adam: "Of every tree of the garden thou mayest freely eat: But of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, thou shalt not eat of it: for in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die" (Genesis 2:16-17). The prohibition was not arbitrary; it was the expression of a holy God who had established a moral boundary that reflected His own pure nature. When Adam and Eve transgressed that boundary, the consequences were immediate and devastating. Genesis 3:8 records that they "heard the voice of the LORD God walking in the garden in the cool of the day: and Adam and his wife hid themselves from the presence of the LORD God amongst the trees of the garden." Sin had shattered the fellowship between man and his holy Creator. The hiding of Adam and Eve is the first evidence in Scripture of what the holiness of God produces in guilty sinners: shame, fear, and the desire to flee from His presence.

Yet even in judgment, God's holiness is mingled with mercy. In Genesis 3:21, we read that "unto Adam also and to his wife did the LORD God make coats of skins, and clothed them." The skins required the death of an animal — the first blood sacrifice in Scripture — pointing forward to the ultimate sacrifice of the Lamb of God who would take away the sin of the world. God's holiness demanded that sin be addressed; God's mercy provided the covering. This pattern of holiness and mercy intertwined runs like a golden thread through the entire biblical narrative.

The giving of the law at Mount Sinai further reveals the holiness of God in breathtaking terms. Exodus 19:16-18 describes the scene: "And it came to pass on the third day in the morning, that there were thunders and lightnings, and a thick cloud upon the mount, and the voice of the trumpet exceeding loud; so that all the people that was in the camp trembled. And Moses brought forth the people out of the camp to meet with God; and they stood at the nether part of the mount. And mount Sinai was altogether on a smoke, because the LORD descended upon it in fire: and the smoke thereof ascended as the smoke of a furnace, and the whole mount quaked greatly." The mountain trembled. The people trembled. Fire, smoke, thunder, and the blast of a heavenly trumpet — all of these were manifestations of divine holiness descending upon the earth. God established strict boundaries around the mountain, commanding that neither man nor beast should touch it on pain of death (Exodus 19:12-13). The message was unmistakable: the holy God is to be approached only on His terms, with the utmost reverence and awe.

The Puritan theologian Thomas Manton (1620-1677), who served as one of the scribes at the Westminster Assembly and later as rector of St. Paul's, Covent Garden, wrote with great penetration on the practical implications of divine holiness for the believer's daily walk. Manton taught that a proper understanding of God's holiness should produce what he called "evangelical holiness" in the life of every Christian — a holiness that flows not from slavish fear but from grateful love. Manton distinguished this evangelical holiness from mere legal obedience. The legalist obeys because he fears punishment; the evangelical Christian obeys because he loves the holy God who has redeemed him. Manton wrote that "holiness is not the way to Christ, but Christ is the way to holiness." We do not make ourselves holy in order to earn God's favor; rather, we receive God's favor in Christ and then pursue holiness out of love and gratitude.

Manton also emphasized that the pursuit of holiness is a lifelong endeavor that requires both divine grace and human diligence. He pointed to 2 Peter 1:5-7: "And beside this, giving all diligence, add to your faith virtue; and to virtue knowledge; And to knowledge temperance; and to temperance patience; and to patience godliness; And to godliness brotherly kindness; and to brotherly kindness charity." Peter calls believers to "give all diligence" — this is not passive waiting but active striving. Yet Manton was careful to insist that this diligence is empowered by the Holy Spirit. The believer works because God works in him: "For it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do of his good pleasure" (Philippians 2:13). The pursuit of holiness is thus a cooperative effort — God supplies the grace, and the believer supplies the willing obedience.

The prophet Micah summarizes the practical demands of holiness with memorable brevity in Micah 6:8: "He hath shewed thee, O man, what is good; and what doth the LORD require of thee, but to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God?" Three requirements flow from the holiness of God. First, do justly — let your dealings with others reflect the justice of a holy God. Second, love mercy — let your heart overflow with the compassion that flows from a holy God who is also merciful. Third, walk humbly with thy God — let your entire life be characterized by the reverent, dependent posture of a creature who stands before the infinite holiness of the Creator.

The Puritan pastor William Gurnall (1617-1679), author of the massive three-volume work "The Christian in Complete Armour," applied the holiness of God to the believer's spiritual warfare. Gurnall taught that holiness is the believer's greatest weapon against the attacks of Satan. He wrote that "the devil trembles when he sees the weakest saint upon his knees," because prayer offered in holiness connects the finite believer to the infinite power of the holy God. Gurnall also taught that personal holiness provides a kind of spiritual armor that protects the believer from temptation. The heart that is filled with love for God's holiness has little room for the allurements of sin. Gurnall urged believers to "put on the whole armour of God" (Ephesians 6:11), understanding that each piece of armor — truth, righteousness, the gospel of peace, faith, salvation, and the Word of God — is an expression of the holiness that God provides for His people's protection.

Consider also the vision of the prophet Zechariah, who saw Joshua the high priest standing before the angel of the LORD "clothed with filthy garments" (Zechariah 3:3). The filthy garments represent the sinfulness of God's people — their inability to stand before a holy God in their own righteousness. But the angel commanded that the filthy garments be removed and said to Joshua, "Behold, I have caused thine iniquity to pass from thee, and I will clothe thee with change of raiment" (Zechariah 3:4). Joshua was given clean garments and a fair mitre — symbols of the imputed righteousness that God provides for His people. This vision beautifully illustrates the relationship between God's holiness and the gospel. God's holiness condemns our filthy garments of self-righteousness. But God's grace provides the clean garments of Christ's righteousness. We stand before the holy God not in our own purity but in the purity of Christ, freely given and freely received.

Practical Application — The Pursuit of Holiness in Daily Life:

1. Begin each day by consciously placing yourself in the presence of the holy God. Before you speak to anyone else, speak to Him. Before you engage with the demands of the world, engage with the demands of His holiness. Let the first moments of your day be consecrated to the One who is "glorious in holiness, fearful in praises, doing wonders" (Exodus 15:11).

2. Examine your conscience daily in the light of God's holiness. The Puritans practiced what they called "the evening examination" — a nightly review of the day's thoughts, words, and actions in the presence of God. Ask the Holy Spirit to reveal any unconfessed sin, any compromise with the world, any failure to love God and neighbor as you ought. Confess what He reveals and receive His cleansing through the blood of Christ (1 John 1:9).

3. Cultivate a hatred of sin that is proportional to your love of God's holiness. The Puritans taught that we cannot truly love holiness without hating sin, and we cannot truly hate sin without loving holiness. These two dispositions are inseparable. As you grow in your understanding of how holy God is, let that understanding produce in you an increasing sensitivity to sin — not the sins of others, but your own.

4. Surround yourself with influences that promote holiness and remove influences that promote sin. Psalm 101:3 declares, "I will set no wicked thing before mine eyes." Guard your eyes, your ears, and your heart from the pollution of a world that mocks the holiness of God. Choose companions who challenge you toward holiness. Read books that elevate your vision of God. Listen to preaching that exalts the holy character of the Almighty.

5. Remember that your ultimate calling is to reflect the holiness of God before a watching world. As the moon reflects the light of the sun, so believers are called to reflect the holiness of God in a dark world. Matthew 5:16 instructs, "Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven." Every act of kindness, every word of truth, every deed of justice, every expression of love performed in the power of the Holy Spirit is a reflection of the holiness of the God you serve. Let your life be a living testimony that the God you worship is holy, and that His holiness transforms everything it touches.
