PURITAN GOLD -- Study Devotional, Day 18
Series: Romans
"Vessels of Mercy"
The potter and the clay, and God's patience with vessels of wrath

---

Welcome to our study devotional. The Apostle Paul told Timothy to study to show himself approved unto God, a workman that needeth not to be ashamed. That is our aim tonight: to study with diligence and devotion.

We are studying the book of Romans together. Tonight's lesson is titled "Vessels of Mercy." Each session builds on what came before, so if you have been following along, you will see how tonight's passage connects to the larger argument of the book.

--- Scripture Reading ---

Let us read our passage for tonight. It comes from Romans 9:19-33:

"Thou wilt say then unto me, Why doth he yet find fault? For who hath resisted his will? Nay but, O man, who art thou that repliest against God? Shall the thing formed say to him that formed it, Why hast thou made me thus? Hath not the potter power over the clay, of the same lump to make one vessel unto honour, and another unto dishonour? What if God, willing to shew his wrath, and to make his power known, endured with much longsuffering the vessels of wrath fitted to destruction: And that he might make known the riches of his glory on the vessels of mercy, which he had afore prepared unto glory, Even us, whom he hath called, not of the Jews only, but also of the Gentiles?... What shall we say then? That the Gentiles, which followed not after righteousness, have attained to righteousness, even the righteousness which is of faith. But Israel, which followed after the law of righteousness, hath not attained to the law of righteousness. Wherefore? Because they sought it not by faith, but as it were by the works of the law. For they stumbled at that stumblingstone."

Read that passage again to yourself. Mark any words or phrases that stand out. The Puritans read with a pen in hand, always ready to note what God was showing them.

--- Key Verse ---

Let us zoom in on a verse that is central to everything we will study tonight.

"What if God, willing to shew his wrath, and to make his power known, endured with much longsuffering the vessels of wrath fitted to destruction: And that he might make known the riches of his glory on the vessels of mercy, which he had afore prepared unto glory." -- Romans 9:22-23

This verse is the lens through which we will examine the rest of tonight's passage. Keep it in the front of your mind as we proceed.

--- Exposition ---

Now we turn to the substance of tonight's study.

John Owen offers valuable commentary on this passage. The Puritans were expositors of the highest order, and their insights remain remarkably fresh.

John Owen addressed the potter-and-clay analogy of Romans 9 with both theological rigor and pastoral tenderness. Owen insisted that the analogy establishes God's absolute right over His creation without reducing human beings to mere automatons. The potter has the right to make vessels for different purposes from the same lump — this is not injustice but creative sovereignty. Owen argued that the analogy does not teach that God creates some people for the express purpose of damning them but rather that God has the right to deal with sinful humanity as He sees fit, showing mercy to some and passing over others according to His own wise purposes.

Owen gave particular attention to verse 22, noting the phrase 'endured with much longsuffering the vessels of wrath fitted to destruction.' He observed that the vessels of wrath are described as 'fitted to destruction' — and the Greek verb katartizo is in the middle or passive voice, suggesting that these vessels fitted themselves for destruction through their own sin, while the vessels of mercy are described as having been 'afore prepared unto glory' by God's active choice. Owen saw in this an important asymmetry: God is the active cause of mercy but not the active cause of destruction. He prepares vessels of mercy for glory; vessels of wrath prepare themselves for judgment.

Owen also found great encouragement in verse 24: 'Even us, whom he hath called, not of the Jews only, but also of the Gentiles.' The vessels of mercy are drawn from both Jews and Gentiles — the very composition of the church in every age. Owen marveled that sovereign election, far from narrowing God's grace, actually broadens it beyond Israel's borders to encompass the whole world. The God who chooses is the God who surprises — calling those who were not His people to be His people, and finding sons and daughters in the most unlikely places.

Romans 9:19-33 continues and completes Paul's argument about God's sovereignty in election. Having established that God has the right to show mercy to whom He wills (verses 1-18), Paul now addresses the most forceful objection and then demonstrates from Scripture that God's plan always included bringing Gentiles into His family.

Verse 19 voices the objection that every reader is thinking: 'Thou wilt say then unto me, Why doth he yet find fault? For who hath resisted his will?' If God's sovereign will determines everything, how can He blame anyone? If no one can resist His will, how can anyone be held responsible? This is the age-old tension between divine sovereignty and human responsibility.

Paul's initial response in verses 20-21 is not philosophical but relational: 'Nay but, O man, who art thou that repliest against God? Shall the thing formed say to him that formed it, Why hast thou made me thus? Hath not the potter power over the clay, of the same lump to make one vessel unto honour, and another unto dishonour?' This is not a dismissive answer; it is a corrective one. The questioner's fundamental problem is not intellectual but attitudinal — they are positioning themselves as God's judge rather than acknowledging themselves as God's creature. The clay does not have the standing to interrogate the potter.

The potter-clay imagery is drawn from Isaiah 29:16, Isaiah 45:9, and Jeremiah 18:1-6. It does not mean that human beings are as lifeless as clay or that they have no will. It means that God has the sovereign right to deal with His creation as He sees fit, and that we are in no position to question His wisdom or justice. A child may not understand why a parent makes certain decisions, but that does not make the parent wrong — it means the child's understanding is limited.

Verses 22-23 present the deepest explanation Paul offers. God has two purposes that He is simultaneously accomplishing. First, He is 'willing to shew his wrath, and to make his power known' — He endures 'with much longsuffering the vessels of wrath fitted to destruction.' Notice: God endures them with patience. He does not rush to judgment but bears with them, giving time for repentance (as in Romans 2:4), demonstrating His forbearance, and ultimately revealing His justice when they persist in rebellion.

Second, and this is Paul's main emphasis, God acts 'that he might make known the riches of his glory on the vessels of mercy, which he had afore prepared unto glory.' The vessels of mercy are 'afore prepared' — prepared beforehand by God's sovereign choice. And the purpose is to display 'the riches of his glory.' Grace is most glorious against the backdrop of judgment. Mercy shines brightest when we understand what we deserved. The vessels of mercy understand the riches of their salvation precisely because they know what the alternative would have been.

Verse 24 identifies the vessels of mercy: 'Even us, whom he hath called, not of the Jews only, but also of the Gentiles.' This is the breakthrough revelation. God's sovereign election has always included Gentiles. The church — composed of believing Jews and believing Gentiles — is the community of the vessels of mercy.

Verses 25-26 confirm this from the prophet Hosea (Hosea 2:23; 1:10): 'I will call them my people, which were not my people; and her beloved, which was not beloved. And it shall come to pass, that in the place where it was said unto them, Ye are not my people; there shall they be called the children of the living God.' The Gentiles, who were not God's people, have become God's people through sovereign grace.

Verses 27-29 confirm from Isaiah (Isaiah 10:22-23; 1:9) that only a remnant of Israel will be saved. This is not a failure of God's promises but their precise fulfillment. God never promised to save every Israelite unconditionally; He promised to save a remnant according to grace.

Verses 30-33 state the paradoxical conclusion. The Gentiles, who were not pursuing righteousness, obtained it — through faith. Israel, who was pursuing righteousness through the law, did not obtain it. Why? 'Because they sought it not by faith, but as it were by the works of the law. For they stumbled at that stumblingstone.' Christ is the stumbling stone. To the self-righteous, He is an offense — a declaration that all their religious efforts are insufficient. To the believing, He is the foundation — 'whosoever believeth on him shall not be ashamed' (Isaiah 28:16).

This passage reveals that the great reversal of the gospel — Gentiles included, many Jews excluded — is not a contradiction of God's purposes but their fulfillment. God has always worked by grace through faith, and He has always preserved a remnant by His sovereign choice. The inclusion of Gentiles is not plan B but plan A, predicted by the prophets and accomplished by sovereign mercy.

The ultimate lesson of Romans 9 is not primarily about who is saved and who is not. It is about who God is — sovereign, just, patient, merciful, and gloriously free. He does not owe salvation to anyone, yet He freely gives it to multitudes from every nation. The proper response is not resentment but reverence, not argument but adoration.

--- Application ---

The Puritans asked three questions of every passage: What does it teach? What does it rebuke? What does it require? Let us ask those questions now.

Romans 9:19-33 confronts our desire to be autonomous and calls us to humble submission before God. Here are specific applications.

First, surrender your right to understand everything. The potter-clay analogy teaches that some of God's ways are beyond our comprehension. You will not always understand why God does what He does. But you can trust that the Potter is good, wise, and just. When life does not make sense, when suffering seems pointless, when God's ways are mysterious — bow before His sovereignty and trust His character. 'Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?' (Genesis 18:25).

Second, marvel at your inclusion among the vessels of mercy. If you believe in Christ, you have been 'afore prepared unto glory.' You are a vessel of mercy. You were not God's people, and now you are God's people. You were not beloved, and now you are beloved. Let the sheer wonder of this truth produce ongoing, overflowing gratitude. You could have been passed over. Instead, you were chosen. Never get over that.

Third, do not stumble at the stumbling stone. Israel's great error was seeking righteousness through works rather than faith. Are you making the same mistake? Are you trying to earn God's favor through your performance, your religious activity, your moral record? Stop. Christ is the end of the law for righteousness to everyone who believes (Romans 10:4). Stop stumbling over the stone and start building your life upon it.

Fourth, trust God's patience with the world. Verse 22 says God endures the vessels of wrath with much longsuffering. When you see evil prospering and wonder why God does not act immediately, remember: He is patient. He is giving time for repentance. He is simultaneously displaying His forbearance and preparing His mercy. Trust His timing.

Write down one concrete action you will take this week in response to tonight's study. Do not let this truth remain theoretical. The Puritans measured the success of a sermon not by how much the hearer learned but by how much the hearer changed.

--- Closing ---

Sovereign Potter, we are the clay. We have no right to question Your purposes or demand explanations for Your decisions. Yet You have treated us not merely as vessels for common use but as vessels of mercy, prepared beforehand for glory. We stand amazed that You called us — not because of anything in us, but because of the riches of Your grace. Forgive us for stumbling at the stumbling stone. Help us to receive Christ by faith, abandoning all self-righteousness. Make us patient as You are patient, and humble as the clay ought to be before its Maker. In Jesus' name. Amen.

Go in peace, student of the Word. God rewards those who diligently seek Him.
