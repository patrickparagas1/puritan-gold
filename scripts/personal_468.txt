PURITAN GOLD -- My Growth, Day 7
"All Things for Good — Thomas Watson"
Reading Day — Watson on Romans 8:28


Welcome. Today is a Reading day. We turn to Thomas Watson's beloved treatise All Things for Good, a sustained meditation on Romans 8:28 that has comforted afflicted Christians for nearly four centuries.

Let us read Romans 8:28 (KJV):

"And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose."


Thomas Watson (c. 1620-1686) was one of the most gifted popular theologians among the Puritans. His treatise All Things for Good unpacks Romans 8:28 with a depth that transforms a familiar verse into a fortress of faith.

Watson begins by insisting that this promise is not universal. It does not say all things work together for good to everyone. It says to them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose. The promise is for believers — those who have been called by sovereign grace and who respond with love for God.

But for the believer, the promise is staggering in scope. Not some things, but all things. Watson catalogues the all things that work for the Christian's good.

First, the best things work for our good: Scripture, prayer, the sacraments, Christian fellowship, the promises of God. These are the obvious blessings.

Second — and here Watson becomes truly remarkable — the worst things work for our good. He argues that afflictions, losses, disappointments, sickness, poverty, and persecution are all included in the all things that God orchestrates for blessing. Watson writes that affliction is God's medicine — bitter to the taste but healing in its effect. He compared afflictions to the pruning of a vine: the gardener cuts the branches not to destroy the plant but to make it more fruitful.

Watson provides several reasons why afflictions work for the believer's good. They purge sin — removing the dross from the gold of character. They prevent sin — keeping us humble and dependent on God. They test faith — proving whether our confidence in God is genuine or theoretical. They increase compassion — the person who has suffered is better equipped to comfort others. They wean us from the world — loosening our grip on earthly comforts and tightening our hold on heavenly ones. And they prepare us for glory — for our light affliction, which is but for a moment, worketh for us a far more exceeding and eternal weight of glory.

Third — and this is Watson's most daring claim — even the Christian's own sins work for good. Watson is careful here. He does not mean that sin is good, or that God approves of sin. What he means is that God, in His sovereign wisdom, uses even the Christian's failures as instruments of spiritual growth. Sin humbles us — reminding us of our desperate need for grace. Sin drives us to Christ — the person who has never fallen deeply has never clung desperately to the cross. Sin makes us watchful — the burned Christian becomes more vigilant.

Watson illustrates this with Peter. Peter's denial was a grievous sin. But God used it to break Peter's self-confidence, deepen his dependence on grace, and make him a more compassionate shepherd. The Peter who wept bitterly was a better apostle than the Peter who boasted.

Watson closes with a meditation on the phrase work together. The key word is together. Individual ingredients may be bitter or harmful on their own. But when combined by the hand of a sovereign God, they produce something good. The same chemicals that are poisonous in isolation become medicine when compounded by a skilled physician. God is the great Physician. He knows exactly which ingredients your life needs, in what proportions, and in what order. Trust His prescription, even when the individual doses are bitter.

This is a book for the suffering. If you are going through a season of loss, pain, confusion, or disappointment, Watson speaks directly to your condition. All things — even this — are working together for your good. Not because the suffering is pleasant, but because the Physician is wise.


All things. Even this. Even the hardship you face right now. God is working. Trust the Physician. Lord, I do not understand all that You are doing. But I trust that You are working all things together for my good. Help me to see Your hand even in the bitter ingredients. Give me patience to wait for the medicine to do its work. Amen.
