PURITAN GOLD -- Exposition of 1 John by Arthur W. Pink: Full Audiobook, Summary
Exposition of 1 John -- Overview and Key Themes

---

This is a summary of Arthur W. Pink's Exposition of First John, covering chapters one through thirty-nine, spanning First John chapter one, verse one through chapter three, verse one.

Arthur W. Pink composed this exposition as a series of monthly articles in his periodical Studies in the Scriptures during the final years of his life, from 1951 to 1953. He passed away in 1952 before completing his treatment of the entire epistle, leaving us with a rich and searching exposition that covers the first two chapters and the opening verse of chapter three. Despite its incomplete scope, the work stands as one of the most thorough devotional and doctrinal treatments of this portion of Scripture from the Reformed tradition.

THE GRAND THEME: FELLOWSHIP WITH GOD

Pink identifies the overarching theme of First John as fellowship with God in and through Christ. He draws attention to chapter one, verse three, where the apostle declares his purpose: that believers may have fellowship with the apostles, and that this fellowship is with the Father and with His Son Jesus Christ. Pink insists that this fellowship is not a mere theological abstraction but the living reality of the Christian life. It is communion with the Triune God, made possible through the incarnation, atoning work, and ongoing advocacy of Christ, and sustained by the indwelling of the Holy Spirit.

This fellowship, Pink argues, is exclusively the possession of the regenerate. It takes place in the light, with the Holy One, and is therefore impossible for those who remain dead in trespasses and sins. Yet Pink is careful to emphasize that the believer's infirmities are not to be regarded as permanent hindrances to this communion, since full provision has been made through the blood of Christ and His intercession at the right hand of the Father.

THE INCARNATION AND PERSON OF CHRIST

The exposition opens with a careful treatment of the humanity of Christ as set forth in the first verse of the epistle. Pink underscores the significance of the apostolic testimony: the apostles heard, saw, gazed upon, and handled the Word of Life. This was no phantom or docetic Christ, but the eternal Son of God who truly took upon Himself human nature. Pink then moves to the manifestation of the life in chapter one, verse two, showing that the eternal life which was with the Father was made visible and tangible in the Person of Christ. These opening chapters establish the doctrinal foundation upon which all that follows is built.

FELLOWSHIP AND JOY

Chapters three through six address the nature and fruits of fellowship with God. Pink treats fellowship in two parts, showing both its basis and its character. True fellowship involves a shared life, shared interests, and shared communion between the believer and God. It is not a sporadic emotional experience but a settled relationship that shapes the whole of the Christian's existence.

The apostle's declaration that these things are written so that the believers' joy may be full receives extended treatment. Pink shows that fullness of joy is inseparable from fellowship with God and is the natural fruit of walking in the light. He warns against seeking joy through carnal means or worldly pleasures, insisting that the deepest and most enduring satisfaction of the human soul is found only in communion with the Father and the Son.

LIGHT AND DARKNESS

A major section of the exposition deals with the apostle's great declaration that God is light and in Him is no darkness at all. Pink treats this theme across multiple chapters, examining the implications of this truth for doctrine and practice. If God is light, then those who claim fellowship with Him while walking in darkness are liars. The contrast between light and darkness runs throughout the epistle, and Pink draws it out with characteristic sharpness.

Walking in the light, Pink explains, does not mean sinless perfection but rather transparency before God, honesty about one's condition, and a habitual orientation toward holiness. The blood of Jesus Christ, God's Son, cleanses from all sin those who walk in this light. Pink is careful to show that the cleansing is not automatic or unconditional but is connected to the believer's posture of honest, humble dependence upon God.

SIN AND ITS REMEDY

Several chapters address the apostle's teaching on sin. Pink treats the denial of sin, the confession of sin, and the prohibition of sin as three distinct but related themes. He shows that John combats both the error of those who deny the presence of indwelling sin and the error of those who use grace as a license for ungodliness.

The remedy for sin is presented in the advocacy of Christ and the propitiation He has made. Pink gives careful attention to the distinction between Christ's work as propitiation, which deals with the judicial aspect of sin before God's throne, and His work as advocate, which deals with the restorative aspect of the believer's communion with God when that communion has been disrupted by transgression. He insists that these provisions are not designed to make believers comfortable in sin but to restore them to fellowship and to encourage them in the pursuit of holiness.

OBEDIENCE AND ASSURANCE

A substantial portion of the exposition is devoted to the relationship between obedience and assurance. Pink expounds the apostle's teaching that the one who claims to know God must keep His commandments. This is not legalism, Pink argues, but the necessary evidence of a living faith. Obedience is the fruit of the new birth, and where it is absent, no amount of doctrinal profession can supply its place.

Pink treats Christ as the exemplar of the obedient life. The believer is called to walk as Christ walked, regulated by the same principles, actuated by the same motives, and directed toward the same ends. This is a high standard, and Pink does not soften it. He insists that while perfection is not attainable in this life, the habitual direction of the regenerate soul is toward conformity to Christ.

THE COMMANDMENT OF LOVE

The new-old commandment of love receives extended treatment. Pink shows that the commandment to love one another is both ancient in its origin and new in its manifestation through Christ. Love for the brethren is one of the distinguishing marks of the genuine believer, and hatred of the brethren is one of the surest signs of an unregenerate heart. Pink draws the contrast between love and hatred with the sharpness that characterizes the apostle's own style, refusing to allow any middle ground.

He connects love with light and hatred with darkness. The one who loves his brother walks in the light; the one who hates his brother is in darkness and does not know where he is going. Pink applies this searching test with pastoral seriousness, warning both against the sentimental dilution of Christian love and against the cold formalism that professes orthodoxy while lacking genuine affection for the people of God.

THE WORLD AND ITS DANGERS

Multiple chapters address the apostle's warning against love of the world. Pink exposits the prohibition, the description, and the doom of the world with thoroughness. The world, in the Johannine sense, is the organized system of human society as it exists apart from God and in opposition to His purposes. It encompasses not merely gross immorality but the lust of the flesh, the lust of the eyes, and the pride of life in all their varied expressions.

Pink is especially searching in his treatment of worldliness within the professing church. He warns that the most dangerous form of worldliness is not open vice but the subtle adoption of the world's values, methods, and spirit by those who bear the Christian name. The world is passing away, together with its lusts, but the one who does the will of God abides forever.

ANTICHRISTS AND APOSTATES

The closing portion of the exposition deals with the apostle's warnings about antichrists, apostates, and seducers. Pink shows that the last time is characterized not primarily by external persecution but by internal corruption. False teachers arise from within the professing church, denying fundamental truths about the Person and work of Christ. Their departure from the fellowship of believers reveals that they were never truly of the company of the redeemed.

Pink treats the anointing of the Holy Spirit as the believer's safeguard against deception. Those who have received this anointing possess an instinctive discernment of truth and error. They do not need any man to teach them, not in the sense that they are independent of the ministry of the Word, but in the sense that the Spirit within them confirms the truth and exposes the lie.

THE ABIDING LIFE AND DIVINE SONSHIP

The final chapters address abiding in Christ, the nature of true righteousness, and the amazing grace of divine sonship. Pink shows that abiding in Christ is not a mystical experience reserved for an elite few but the normal posture of every genuine believer. It involves continued dependence upon Christ, continued obedience to His Word, and continued communion with His Person.

The exposition reaches its climax with the apostle's exclamation in chapter three, verse one: Behold, what manner of love the Father hath bestowed upon us, that we should be called the sons of God. Pink treats this as the summit of the apostle's teaching, showing that the new birth, with all its evidences in righteousness and love, flows from the sovereign and electing love of the Father. The world does not recognize the children of God, just as it did not recognize Christ Himself. But this misunderstanding by the world is no cause for dismay; it is rather a mark of conformity to the Master.

SELF-EXAMINATION AND THE MARKS OF THE REGENERATE

One of the most distinctive features of Pink's exposition is his relentless emphasis on self-examination. Following the apostle's own method, Pink sets forth a series of tests by which the professing Christian may determine whether his faith is genuine or counterfeit. These tests are not designed to torment sensitive consciences but to expose empty profession and to confirm the true believer in his assurance.

The marks of the regenerate, as Pink draws them from the epistle, include walking in the light, keeping the commandments of God, loving the brethren, overcoming the world, and confessing the truth concerning the Person of Christ. Where these marks are present, even in imperfect measure, they constitute evidence of the new birth. Where they are altogether absent, no amount of doctrinal knowledge or religious activity can compensate for their lack. Pink is unsparing in his application of these tests, yet he is always careful to distinguish between the habitual direction of a life and isolated failures or momentary lapses.

THE NATURE OF ASSURANCE

Closely connected with his treatment of self-examination is Pink's handling of Christian assurance. He shows that the epistle was written so that believers might know that they have eternal life. This assurance is not presumption; it is the settled confidence that arises from the witness of the Spirit and the evidence of a transformed life. Pink warns against both the presumption that rests upon a bare profession without corresponding fruit, and the unbelief that refuses to take God at His word when the evidences of grace are genuinely present.

PINK'S METHOD AND CONTRIBUTION

Throughout the exposition, Pink combines doctrinal precision with searching practical application. He writes as a pastor-theologian who is deeply concerned not merely with correct understanding but with holy living. His treatment is marked by frequent warnings against self-deception, repeated calls to honest self-examination, and a consistent insistence that genuine faith always produces visible fruit.

Pink's style reflects the influence of the Puritans whom he so deeply admired. His sentences are often long and carefully constructed. His arguments proceed with logical rigor. His illustrations are drawn primarily from Scripture itself rather than from contemporary life. He quotes extensively from earlier Reformed and Puritan writers, weaving their insights into his own exposition in a way that demonstrates the continuity of evangelical truth across the centuries.

It is worth noting that Pink composed this work during the final period of his life, when he and his wife were living in relative obscurity on the Isle of Lewis in Scotland. The exposition bears the marks of a man who had spent decades meditating upon the Scriptures and who wrote with the urgency of one who knew his earthly course was nearing its end. There is a warmth and a weightiness in these chapters that distinguishes them from mere academic commentary.

This exposition of First John remains a valuable resource for any believer who desires to understand the apostle's teaching on fellowship with God, the evidences of the new birth, and the marks that distinguish genuine Christianity from its many counterfeits. Pink's work is demanding reading, but it repays careful study with a deeper understanding of what it means to walk in the light, to love the brethren, and to abide in Christ.
