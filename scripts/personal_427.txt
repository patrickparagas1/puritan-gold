PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Doctrine Deep-Dive, Day 27
"The Glory of God as the Chief End"
Living for the One Thing That Matters Most


Welcome, friend. The Puritans called doctrine the bones of the body of divinity. Without strong bones, the body collapses. Tonight we strengthen those bones as we study a vital truth together.

Tonight we draw from the work of Multiple (Edwards, Charnock, Watson), specifically from "Various works on God's glory." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is 1 Corinthians 10:31:

"Whether therefore ye eat, or drink, or whatsoever ye do, do all to the glory of God."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


The Westminster Shorter Catechism opens with the most famous question and answer in the history of Christian catechesis: 'What is the chief end of man? Man's chief end is to glorify God, and to enjoy Him for ever.' This answer is the lens through which the Puritans viewed every aspect of human existence. Understanding it deeply will reorder your entire life.

Jonathan Edwards devoted more attention to the glory of God than perhaps any other Puritan. In 'The End for Which God Created the World,' Edwards argues that God's own glory is the ultimate purpose of all that exists. God did not create the world because He was lonely or incomplete; He created it to display and communicate His own infinite perfections. The beauty of creation, the drama of redemption, the consummation of all things in Christ — all exist to magnify the glory of God.

Edwards defines God's glory as 'the emanation and true external expression of God's internal glory and fullness.' God's glory is not something added to Him from the outside; it is the outshining of who He already is. When we glorify God, we do not make Him more glorious (that is impossible); we recognize, reflect, and rejoice in the glory that is eternally His.

Edwards further argues that glorifying God and enjoying God are not two separate activities but one. God is most glorified in us when we are most satisfied in Him. The man who delights in God glorifies God, because his delight testifies to the supreme worth of what he delights in. A man who praises a meal glorifies the cook; a man who delights in God glorifies the Creator.

Stephen Charnock, in 'The Existence and Attributes of God,' explores the glory of God through His attributes. Charnock argues that every attribute of God is glorious, and that understanding each attribute deepens our apprehension of His glory. God's power is glorious because it is infinite and unfailing. God's wisdom is glorious because it is perfect and all-encompassing. God's love is glorious because it is unmerited and inexhaustible. God's justice is glorious because it is perfectly righteous and utterly impartial.

Charnock writes that meditation on God's attributes is one of the primary means by which the soul grows in its capacity to glorify God. The man who does not know God's attributes cannot truly glorify Him. He may offer vague praise, but he cannot offer the specific, informed worship that God's perfections deserve. Charnock commends the regular, sustained study of God's character as one of the most important spiritual disciplines.

Thomas Watson, in 'A Body of Divinity' (his exposition of the Westminster Shorter Catechism), provides the most practical treatment. Watson explains that glorifying God involves four things: appreciation (valuing God above all else), adoration (worshipping God in spirit and truth), affection (loving God with all the heart), and subjection (obeying God in all things). Watson writes that a life lived for the glory of God is not a life of grim duty but a life of supreme satisfaction. 'He who aims at God's glory will find his own happiness wrapped up in it.'

Watson also addresses the comprehensive scope of glorifying God. Paul writes: 'Whether therefore ye eat, or drink, or whatsoever ye do, do all to the glory of God.' This means that the glory of God is not limited to religious activities. You glorify God in your work when you do it with excellence and integrity. You glorify God in your marriage when you love sacrificially. You glorify God in your rest when you receive it as a gift. You glorify God in your suffering when you trust Him through it. There is no moment of your life that falls outside the scope of this calling.

The challenge for the Christian man is to make this vision the organizing principle of his entire life. Not merely to know that the chief end of man is to glorify God, but to live as though it were true — at the breakfast table, in the office, at the gym, in the quiet hours of the night when no one is watching. The man who lives for the glory of God has found the one thing that gives meaning to everything else.


Reflection is where truth becomes personal. Consider these questions carefully.

Question 1: Is the glory of God truly the organizing principle of my life, or do I functionally live for something else — comfort, approval, success?

Question 2: Which of God's attributes am I least acquainted with, and how might deeper knowledge of that attribute reshape my worship?

Question 3: What would change in my daily routine if I genuinely asked, at every decision point, 'How can I glorify God in this?'

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


O God of glory, forgive me for the smallness of my vision. I was made to glorify You and enjoy You forever, yet I so often live for lesser things. Enlarge my knowledge of Your perfections. Deepen my delight in who You are. Make Your glory the consuming passion of my life. Amen.

May the Lord guard your mind and your heart tonight. Until we meet again.
