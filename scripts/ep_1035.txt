PURITAN GOLD -- Melchizedek: A Complete Biblical Study, Part 5 of 8
Hebrews 7:4-17 — Greater Than Abraham and Levi

---

Welcome back to Puritan Gold and our complete study of Melchizedek. We have traced this figure from his sudden appearance in Genesis 14, through the prophetic declaration of Psalm 110, through the groundwork laid in Hebrews 5, and into the opening exposition of Hebrews 6:13-7:3. Now we enter one of the most tightly reasoned theological arguments in the entire Bible — Hebrews 7:4-17, where the writer proves beyond all question that the priesthood of Melchizedek is superior to the priesthood of Levi, and that this superiority demands a fundamental change in the entire priestly and legal system of the old covenant.

Let us read the full text. Hebrews 7, verses 4 through 17:

"Now consider how great this man was, unto whom even the patriarch Abraham gave the tenth of the spoils. And verily they that are of the sons of Levi, who receive the office of the priesthood, have a commandment to take tithes of the people according to the law, that is, of their brethren, though they come out of the loins of Abraham: but he whose descent is not counted from them received tithes of Abraham, and blessed him that had the promises. And without all contradiction the less is blessed of the better. And here men that die receive tithes; but there he receiveth them, of whom it is witnessed that he liveth. And as I may so say, Levi also, who receiveth tithes, payed tithes in Abraham. For he was yet in the loins of his father, when Melchisedec met him."

"If therefore perfection were by the Levitical priesthood, (for under it the people received the law,) what further need was there that another priest should rise after the order of Melchisedec, and not be called after the order of Aaron? For the priesthood being changed, there is made of necessity a change also of the law. For he of whom these things are spoken pertaineth to another tribe, of which no man gave attendance at the altar. For it is evident that our Lord sprang out of Juda; of which tribe Moses spake nothing concerning priesthood. And it is yet far more evident: for that after the similitude of Melchisedec there ariseth another priest, who is made, not after the law of a carnal commandment, but after the power of an endless life. For he testifieth, Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchisedec."

--- Teaching ---

The writer begins with a command: "Now consider how great this man was." He is asking his readers to stop and think — to weigh the evidence carefully. The argument he is about to make is logical, precise, and devastating to any notion that the Levitical priesthood is the final or highest form of priestly ministry.

The first argument, in verses 4 through 6, is from the tithe. Abraham gave a tenth of the spoils to Melchizedek. Now, the Levitical priests received tithes from the people according to the law. But they received tithes from their brethren — from fellow Israelites, fellow descendants of Abraham. There was a kind of equality between the Levitical priests and the people: both were sons of Abraham, both were under the same covenant. But Melchizedek was not a descendant of Abraham. He was outside the system entirely. His descent is not counted from them. And yet Abraham — the great patriarch, the father of the faithful, the man who had received the promises of God — paid tithes to him. If Abraham acknowledged Melchizedek's superiority by tithing to him, then Melchizedek is greater than Abraham.

John Owen notes the subtlety of this argument. The tithe was not commanded by any law at this point. Abraham tithed voluntarily. This makes the act even more significant, for it reveals Abraham's own recognition of Melchizedek's superior dignity. He was not compelled by statute. He was moved by spiritual discernment to acknowledge that this priest-king stood above him in the order of divine appointment.

The second argument, in verse 7, is from the blessing: "And without all contradiction the less is blessed of the better." This is stated as a universal principle. When a priest blesses a people, he acts from a position of superiority. The lesser receives the blessing; the greater bestows it. Melchizedek blessed Abraham. Therefore Melchizedek was greater than Abraham. The writer presents this as beyond dispute — without all contradiction.

Matthew Henry observes that this argument would have been particularly forceful for the original Jewish readers. Abraham was the most revered figure in their national history. To hear that someone was greater than Abraham — and that this someone operated outside the Levitical system — would have shaken the foundations of their religious assumptions. And that was precisely the writer's intent. He was dismantling the Jewish confidence in the sufficiency of the Levitical system, piece by piece.

The third argument, in verses 8 through 10, is perhaps the most remarkable. The writer argues that Levi himself — the progenitor of the entire priestly tribe — paid tithes to Melchizedek through Abraham. "And as I may so say, Levi also, who receiveth tithes, payed tithes in Abraham. For he was yet in the loins of his father, when Melchisedec met him."

This is the principle of seminal presence. Levi was in Abraham's body — in his loins — when Abraham met Melchizedek. Therefore, when Abraham paid tithes, Levi paid tithes. The entire Levitical priesthood, through its progenitor, acknowledged the superiority of Melchizedek. The tithe-receiving priests were themselves tithe-payers to a higher priest.

Thomas Goodwin finds in this argument a profound illustration of federal headship — the principle that a representative's actions are counted as the actions of all whom he represents. Just as Levi's tithing through Abraham demonstrates the Levitical priesthood's subordination to Melchizedek, so Adam's sin is counted as the sin of all his descendants, and Christ's obedience is counted as the obedience of all His people. The principle runs throughout Scripture: the head acts, and the members participate in that action.

Now the writer draws his great conclusion, beginning in verse 11. "If therefore perfection were by the Levitical priesthood, (for under it the people received the law,) what further need was there that another priest should rise after the order of Melchisedec, and not be called after the order of Aaron?"

The logic is inescapable. If the Levitical system could bring perfection — that is, complete and permanent access to God, full cleansing of conscience, and final reconciliation — then why would God promise another priest of a different order? The very fact that Psalm 110 prophesied a priest after the order of Melchizedek proves that the Levitical system was insufficient. If Aaron's priesthood was enough, there would be no need for Melchizedek's.

Owen writes that this is the deathblow to all confidence in the ceremonial law. The ceremonies of the old covenant were never designed to save. They were shadows pointing to the substance. They were temporary provisions awaiting the permanent reality. The very existence of Psalm 110:4 — uttered centuries after the Levitical system was established — demonstrates that the old system was always meant to be superseded.

Verse 12 states the revolutionary implication: "For the priesthood being changed, there is made of necessity a change also of the law." This is one of the most consequential statements in the New Testament. The priesthood and the law were bound together. The entire Mosaic legal system was built around the Levitical priesthood — the sacrifices, the rituals, the regulations of purity, the temple service. If the priesthood changes, the entire legal framework must change with it. You cannot have a Melchizedekian priest operating under Levitical regulations. A new priesthood demands a new covenant.

The Puritans understood this verse as the foundation for the doctrine of the abrogation of the ceremonial law. The moral law, summarized in the Ten Commandments, remains as the eternal expression of God's character. But the ceremonial law — the sacrificial system, the dietary regulations, the ritual calendar — was tied to the Levitical priesthood and was set aside when that priesthood was superseded by Christ's.

Verses 13 and 14 drive the point home with historical fact: "For he of whom these things are spoken pertaineth to another tribe, of which no man gave attendance at the altar. For it is evident that our Lord sprang out of Juda; of which tribe Moses spake nothing concerning priesthood."

Jesus was from the tribe of Judah — David's tribe, the royal tribe. No one from Judah ever served as a Levitical priest. Moses said nothing about Judah and the priesthood. The very tribal identity of Christ proves that His priesthood is of an entirely different order. He is not a Levitical priest who happens to also be a king. He is a Melchizedekian priest — one whose priesthood operates on completely different principles.

Verses 15 through 17 state those principles with magnificent clarity: "And it is yet far more evident: for that after the similitude of Melchisedec there ariseth another priest, who is made, not after the law of a carnal commandment, but after the power of an endless life. For he testifieth, Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchisedec."

The Levitical priests were made priests by the law of a carnal commandment — by physical descent, by genealogical qualification, by the regulations of the Mosaic code. Their priesthood was external, bodily, temporary. But Christ is made priest after the power of an endless life. His priesthood rests not on genealogy but on the inherent, indestructible power of His own eternal existence. He lives forever. Therefore He is priest forever.

Owen writes that the phrase "the power of an endless life" is one of the most glorious descriptions of Christ in all of Scripture. It speaks not merely of immortality but of the active, dynamic, life-giving power of the risen and exalted Son of God. His priesthood is sustained not by human succession but by divine vitality. It cannot be interrupted by death, for He has conquered death. It cannot be ended by time, for He dwells in eternity. It cannot be weakened by age, for His youth is renewed like the eagle's.

And the final appeal is again to Psalm 110:4 — "For he testifieth, Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchisedec." This is the testimony of God Himself, spoken under oath, concerning His own Son. It is the final word. There is no higher authority, no greater proof, no stronger ground for confidence.

Beloved, the argument of Hebrews 7:4-17 is now complete in its first movement. Melchizedek is greater than Abraham. Levi paid tithes through Abraham to Melchizedek. The Levitical system could not bring perfection. A change of priesthood demands a change of law. Christ's priesthood rests not on carnal commandment but on the power of an endless life.

In our next episode, we shall complete Hebrews 7, reading verses 18 through 28, where the writer gives us the unchangeable priesthood, the better covenant, and the magnificent declaration that Christ is able to save to the uttermost. Until then, consider how great this Man is. Amen.
