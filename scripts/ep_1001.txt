PURITAN GOLD -- Study Topic: The Book of Sirach, Part 1 of 10
"The Wisdom of Ben Sira"
Sirach Introduction — Who Was Ben Sira and Why Study This Book?

---

Good evening, dear friend, and welcome to Puritan Gold. Tonight we begin a new series of studies that will carry us through one of the most fascinating and edifying books of ancient wisdom literature — the Book of Sirach, also known as Ecclesiasticus. Over the next ten sessions, we will walk together through this remarkable collection of counsel, prayer, and practical godliness. I pray that the Lord will open our eyes to behold wondrous things as we explore these pages.

Before we read from Sirach itself, let me set the stage with a word from canonical Scripture. Turn with me to Proverbs 4:7:

"Wisdom is the principal thing; therefore get wisdom: and with all thy getting get understanding."

And now, from the prologue to Sirach, written by the author's grandson:

"My grandfather Jesus, when he had much given himself to the reading of the law, and the prophets, and other books of our fathers, and had gotten therein good judgment, was drawn on also himself to write something pertaining to learning and wisdom; to the intent that those which are desirous to learn, and are addicted to these things, might profit much more in living according to the law."

--- Teaching ---

Who was Ben Sira, and why should we give our time to studying his book? These are the questions we must answer before we venture further into this series, and I believe the answers will stir your heart.

The author of this book was a man named Jesus son of Sirach — Yeshua ben Sira in Hebrew — a Jewish sage and schoolmaster who lived and taught in Jerusalem around 180 to 175 years before the birth of our Lord Jesus Christ. He was not a prophet in the formal sense. He was a wisdom teacher, a man who had devoted his life to the study of the Torah, the Prophets, and the collected wisdom of Israel's sages. He ran what we might call an academy of godly learning, a beth midrash, a house of study, where young men came to be formed in the fear of God and the practice of righteous living.

Ben Sira wrote in Hebrew, and his grandson later translated the work into Greek around 132 BC, providing the prologue we just read. That grandson tells us something vital: his grandfather had steeped himself in "the law, and the prophets, and other books of our fathers." Ben Sira was a man saturated in Scripture. He did not write from his own cleverness but from a lifetime of meditating on the Word of God, much as the Puritans would later commend what they called "the digestion of Scripture" — not merely reading it, but chewing upon it, turning it over in the mind and heart until it became the very substance of one's thought.

Now, a word about the book's place in the tradition. Sirach is part of what Protestants call the Apocrypha and what Roman Catholics and Eastern Orthodox call the Deuterocanonical books. It was not included in the Hebrew canon as finalized at the end of the first century. However, it was widely read and deeply respected in the early church. The great church fathers — Clement of Alexandria, Origen, Cyprian, Augustine — quoted from it freely. It was read in churches for instruction and edification. The sixth Article of the Church of England, reflecting a position shared by many Reformed divines, states that these books may be read "for example of life and instruction of manners," though not to establish doctrine.

The Puritans themselves, while careful to distinguish canonical from non-canonical Scripture, did not ignore the wisdom found in Sirach. Richard Baxter, in his Christian Directory, drew upon principles remarkably similar to Sirach's counsel on friendship, speech, and household management. Thomas Adams, sometimes called "the prose Shakespeare of the Puritans," echoed Sirach's warnings about the tongue and the dangers of sloth. They understood that wisdom, wherever it is found, reflects the character of the God who is its ultimate source.

What, then, will we find in the Book of Sirach? We will find a treasury of practical godliness. Sirach covers an astonishing range of topics: the fear of the Lord, the honoring of parents, humility, patience, friendship, the governance of the tongue, the use of wealth, the discipline of the appetites, the right conduct of worship, the duties of rulers, the training of children, the care of the sick, the mourning of the dead, and the praise of God's faithful servants throughout history. It is, in many ways, the most comprehensive guide to daily godly living produced in the period between the Old and New Testaments.

The style of Sirach will feel familiar to anyone who loves the Book of Proverbs. Like Solomon, Ben Sira writes in short, memorable maxims grouped by theme. But Sirach also contains longer passages of sustained reflection — hymns of praise to God's creation, extended meditations on the nature of wisdom, and that magnificent section near the end known as the Praise of the Fathers, a sweeping review of Israel's heroes from Enoch to the high priest Simon, which reads like a forerunner to the great roll call of faith in Hebrews chapter 11.

There is a warmth and humanity to Sirach that is deeply endearing. Ben Sira writes as a man who has lived long and observed much. He has known sorrow and joy, betrayal and loyal friendship, the temptations of wealth and the dignity of honest labor. He writes not as one lecturing from above but as a father counseling a beloved son, a pastor tending his flock. You will hear in his voice something of what the Puritan William Gurnall called "the experienced Christian" — one who speaks not merely from books but from the proving ground of daily life under the eye of God.

Let me highlight several great themes that will occupy us over the coming sessions. First and foremost is the fear of the Lord. Sirach opens with a magnificent hymn declaring that all wisdom begins with God and that the fear of the Lord is wisdom's root, fullness, and crown. This is the same foundation we find in Proverbs 1:7 and 9:10, and it pervades every chapter of the book.

Second, Sirach is deeply concerned with the ordering of relationships. How should a son treat his aging father? What does true friendship look like? How should a host govern his table? What does the wise man owe to the poor? Ben Sira addresses these questions with a specificity and tenderness that is rare in ancient literature.

Third, Sirach takes up the great theme of the tongue. Chapters 18 and 19 in particular contain searching counsel on the power of speech to build up or destroy, counsel that echoes powerfully in the Epistle of James. The Puritans were deeply attentive to this theme. Thomas Watson wrote that "the tongue is a little member but it does great exploits, either for God or the devil," and Ben Sira would have agreed heartily.

Fourth, Sirach contains a robust theology of worship and prayer. Chapters 34 and 35 teach that God desires not mere ritual but the sacrifice of a righteous heart — a theme that resonates with the prophets Isaiah, Amos, and Micah, and with our Lord's own teaching.

Finally, the great Praise of the Fathers in chapters 44 through 50 gives us a panoramic view of God's faithfulness through the generations, culminating in a breathtaking description of the high priest Simon son of Onias ministering in the temple. It is one of the most moving passages in all of ancient literature.

As we begin this journey, I want to encourage you to approach this book with the same spirit the Puritans brought to all their reading — what John Owen called "a humble, dependent frame of spirit." We are not reading Sirach as we read Isaiah or Romans. We read it as wisdom literature from a godly sage, tested against the touchstone of canonical Scripture. Where it agrees with the Bible — and it very often does — we receive it gladly. Where it adds practical counsel that does not contradict the Word of God, we weigh it carefully and gratefully.

The Puritan method of reading was always to read with discernment, with prayer, and with the aim of application. Let us do the same with Sirach.

--- Application ---

1. Begin with prayer. Before each session in this series, ask the Lord to give you a discerning heart — to receive what is profitable and to hold all things up to the light of canonical Scripture. As the Bereans searched the Scriptures daily, so should we.

2. Read Sirach alongside your Bible. Keep your Bible open as we study. Notice where Ben Sira echoes Proverbs, the Psalms, Deuteronomy, and the prophets. You will find the connections rich and frequent.

3. Adopt the Puritan posture of "reading for practice." The Puritans never read merely for information. They read to be changed. As you hear Ben Sira's counsel, ask yourself: Where does this apply to my life today? What habit of speech, what relationship, what attitude needs reforming?

4. Share what you learn. Wisdom was never meant to be hoarded. As Ben Sira taught in his house of learning, so you can bring these ancient insights into your conversations, your family devotions, and your fellowship with other believers.

--- Closing ---

Let us pray. Grant us, we pray, humble and teachable hearts as we explore this ancient book. Let Thy Holy Spirit guide our reading, that we may receive what is profitable and hold fast to what is true. May this study draw us nearer to Thee and deepen our love for Thy Word. Through Jesus Christ our Lord, who is the Wisdom of God made flesh. Amen.
