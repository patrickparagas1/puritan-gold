Lesson 5: "The Helmet of Salvation — Confident Hope"
Spiritual Armor


Welcome to today's lesson. The Puritans were some of the most learned men and women of their age, and yet they counted all their learning as nothing compared to knowing Christ. Let us follow their example today.

God of all knowledge, we begin with You. Without Your light, even the brightest minds walk in darkness. Shine Your truth into our studies today. Help us to love learning and to learn in love. For the glory of Your name. Amen.

Let us read a passage slowly, letting each word settle into our minds.

Our Scripture for today is Ephesians 6:17a:

"And take the helmet of salvation."

As you put on the helmet of salvation this morning, let your mind be filled with the confident hope of all that God has done, is doing, and will do for you. You have been saved from the penalty of sin — justified freely by His grace. You are being saved from the power of sin — sanctified daily by His Spirit. You will be saved from the very presence of sin — glorified eternally in His kingdom. This hope is your helmet. It protects your mind from despair, your thoughts from confusion, and your heart from the paralyzing fear that the enemy would inflict upon you. Put it on. Wear it with confidence. And look forward with joy to the day when you will exchange this helmet for a crown of glory that will never fade away.

As we prepare for today's lesson, remember that everything we study flows from this one fountain: the Word of God. Our lesson today is part of Spiritual Armor, and it is titled: The Helmet of Salvation — Confident Hope. Let us proceed with minds engaged and hearts open.


Let us proceed to the main body of today's teaching.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes in Ephesians 6:17a.

The helmet of salvation is the final piece of defensive armor in Paul's description of the Christian's spiritual equipment. In Roman warfare, the helmet protected the soldier's head — the seat of his thinking, his reasoning, and his decision-making. A blow to the head could disorient, confuse, or kill. The helmet was therefore essential not only for physical survival but for maintaining the mental clarity needed to fight effectively. Paul's spiritual application is precise: the helmet of salvation protects the believer's mind from the attacks of the enemy and preserves the clarity of thought needed for spiritual warfare.

The apostle Paul provides an important parallel passage in 1 Thessalonians 5:8: "But let us, who are of the day, be sober, putting on the breastplate of faith and love; and for an helmet, the hope of salvation." Here the helmet is identified specifically as "the hope of salvation." This clarifies the meaning of Ephesians 6:17. The helmet of salvation is the confident hope that flows from knowing we are saved — the assurance of past forgiveness, the reality of present grace, and the certainty of future glory. This hope is the helmet that protects the Christian's mind from despair, confusion, and the paralyzing effects of the enemy's attacks.

Salvation in Scripture has three tenses. Past: "By grace ye are saved through faith" (Ephesians 2:8) — the believer has been saved from the penalty of sin through justification. Present: "Work out your own salvation with fear and trembling" (Philippians 2:12) — the believer is being saved from the power of sin through sanctification. Future: "We shall be saved from wrath through him" (Romans 5:9) — the believer will be saved from the presence of sin through glorification. The helmet of salvation encompasses all three tenses. We are protected by the assurance of what God has done, what God is doing, and what God will do.

Romans 8:28-30 provides the golden chain of salvation that undergirds the helmet: "And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose. For whom he did foreknow, he also did predestinate to be conformed to the image of his Son, that he might be the firstborn among many brethren. Moreover whom he did predestinate, them he also called: and whom he called, them he also justified: and whom he justified, them he also glorified." Every link in this chain is forged by God — foreknowledge, predestination, calling, justification, glorification. Not one link depends on human merit or effort. Not one link can break. The chain holds from eternity past to eternity future, and the believer who grasps this truth has a helmet on his head that no enemy can dislodge.

First Peter 1:3-5 describes the living hope that constitutes the helmet: "Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, which according to his abundant mercy hath begotten us again unto a lively hope by the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead, To an inheritance incorruptible, and undefiled, and that fadeth not away, reserved in heaven for you, Who are kept by the power of God through faith unto salvation ready to be revealed in the last time." The hope of salvation is described as "lively" — living, active, dynamic. It is grounded in the resurrection of Christ, which guarantees the believer's own future resurrection. And the inheritance it promises is "incorruptible" (it cannot decay), "undefiled" (it cannot be contaminated), and "fadeth not away" (it cannot diminish). It is reserved in heaven, where nothing can threaten it, and the believer is "kept by the power of God" until the day it is revealed.

The mind is one of the primary battlefields of spiritual warfare. Romans 12:2 commands the believer to be "transformed by the renewing of your mind." Second Corinthians 10:5 speaks of "casting down imaginations, and every high thing that exalteth itself against the knowledge of God, and bringing into captivity every thought to the obedience of Christ." Satan attacks the mind with lies, distortions, fears, and false philosophies. The helmet of salvation protects the mind by filling it with the truth of God's saving work — past, present, and future.

The practical effect of the helmet is confidence. When the believer knows that he is saved — truly, permanently, irrevocably saved by the grace of God — he can face any battle without fear. He can endure any suffering without despair. He can confront any enemy without trembling. Not because he is strong in himself, but because the salvation that protects his mind was accomplished by an omnipotent Savior and is guaranteed by an unbreakable covenant. As Paul concludes in Romans 8:31, "If God be for us, who can be against us?'

This biblical foundation is essential. Without it, everything else we study today would be like a house built on sand. But with it, we stand on solid rock.


Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. William Gurnall wrote extensively on this subject in "The Christian in Complete Armour." The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life.

William Gurnall's exposition of the helmet of salvation brings "The Christian in Complete Armour" to its magnificent conclusion. For Gurnall, the helmet represents the comprehensive salvation that God has accomplished, is accomplishing, and will accomplish for His people — and the confident hope that this salvation produces in the believer's mind and heart.

Gurnall began by observing that the head is the most vital part of the body and therefore the most important to protect. A soldier can survive wounds to his arms or legs, but a mortal blow to the head ends the fight immediately. In the same way, if Satan can wound the believer's mind — by destroying his assurance of salvation, by confusing his thinking about God, or by filling his thoughts with despair — the spiritual battle is effectively over. The helmet of salvation is therefore not an optional accessory but an absolute necessity.

Gurnall identified the helmet of salvation specifically with the assurance of salvation — the believer's confidence that he is truly saved, that his sins are truly forgiven, that he is truly accepted by God in Christ, and that he will truly be brought safely home to glory. This assurance is not presumption. It is not a vague hope or a wishful feeling. It is a confident conviction grounded in the promises of God, confirmed by the witness of the Holy Spirit, and evidenced by the fruits of genuine faith in the believer's life.

Gurnall distinguished between three grounds of assurance. First, the objective ground: the promises of God in Scripture. God has promised that "whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life" (John 3:16). If I believe in Christ, then God's promise applies to me, and I can be assured of my salvation on the basis of His infallible Word. Second, the subjective ground: the internal witness of the Holy Spirit. Romans 8:16 declares, "The Spirit itself beareth witness with our spirit, that we are the children of God." The Holy Spirit produces an inner conviction — a deep, settled sense of belonging to God — that confirms the truth of the gospel in the believer's experience. Third, the evidential ground: the fruit of genuine faith in the believer's life. "By their fruits ye shall know them" (Matthew 7:20). A life that displays the fruits of the Spirit — love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control — provides evidence that the Spirit is present and active, confirming the reality of salvation.

Gurnall was deeply concerned about believers who lacked assurance of salvation. He observed that many sincere Christians live in a state of perpetual doubt, constantly questioning whether they are truly saved. He identified several causes of this lack of assurance. Unconfessed sin obscures assurance, because a guilty conscience cannot rest peacefully before God. Ignorance of God's promises weakens assurance, because the believer does not know the grounds on which assurance rests. The attacks of Satan undermine assurance, because the devil is constantly whispering accusations and raising doubts. Spiritual neglect diminishes assurance, because the believer who does not use the means of grace (prayer, Bible reading, fellowship, the Lord's Supper) grows cold and uncertain.

Gurnall's remedy for a lack of assurance was thoroughly practical. First, confess all known sin and seek God's forgiveness. Second, study the promises of God and learn the grounds of assurance. Third, resist the devil's accusations with the truth of Scripture. Fourth, faithfully use the means of grace. Fifth, ask the Holy Spirit to renew the witness of assurance in your heart. Gurnall was patient and compassionate with doubting believers, but he was also firm: "Do not be content with a life of doubt and uncertainty. God wants you to know that you are His child. He has provided everything you need for this knowledge. Seek it. Pursue it. And you will find it."

Gurnall concluded "The Christian in Complete Armour" with a magnificent vision of the final salvation that awaits the fully armored believer. He described the day when every piece of armor will be laid aside because the battle will be over. The belt of truth will give way to the full, unmediated vision of God's truth. The breastplate of righteousness will give way to the perfected righteousness of the glorified saint. The shield of faith will give way to sight. The sword of the Spirit will give way to the eternal song of praise. And the helmet of salvation will give way to the crown of life that the Lord has promised to those who love Him (James 1:12).

Gurnall wrote with passionate anticipation: "The day is coming when you will put off this armor and put on a crown. The fight will be over. The victory will be won. And you will stand before your King, not as a weary soldier but as a triumphant conqueror, clothed in the white robes of eternal righteousness, bearing the palm branch of victory, and singing the song of the redeemed through all eternity. Press on, Christian soldier. The end is near. The crown is certain. And the King who called you to the battle will welcome you to the feast."

This is the hope that the helmet of salvation protects — the hope of final, complete, eternal salvation. And this hope, as the Puritans never tired of proclaiming, does not disappoint, because it is grounded not in our faithfulness but in the faithfulness of God, who has promised and who will perform.

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.


Let us consider together what these truths mean for our lives.

Discussion Question 1: Why is the mind such an important battlefield in spiritual warfare? How does Satan attack the believer's thinking?

Discussion Question 2: First Thessalonians 5:8 identifies the helmet as 'the hope of salvation.' How does hope function as protection for the mind?

Discussion Question 3: Gurnall identified three grounds of assurance: the promises of God, the witness of the Spirit, and the evidence of fruit. Which of these do you find most helpful for strengthening your own assurance?

Discussion Question 4: Romans 8:28-30 presents a 'golden chain' of salvation from foreknowledge to glorification. How does this chain provide security for the believer?

Discussion Question 5: Gurnall concluded his work with a vision of the day when armor will be exchanged for a crown. How does the hope of final glory motivate faithfulness in present battles?

Take time with each question. The Puritans valued what they called "holy conference," the practice of discussing spiritual truths together. Do not settle for quick, surface answers. Push deeper. Ask follow-up questions. Listen carefully to one another.

Application is where truth becomes life. It is not enough to know what Ephesians 6:17a says. We must ask: how does this change the way I live today? How does this shape my thoughts, my words, my actions? The Puritans insisted that a truth that does not transform is a truth that has not been truly understood.

One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

The concept of salvation — deliverance from danger, oppression, or destruction — is central to human history. Study major historical deliverances: the Exodus from Egypt, the deliverance of Europe from the Black Plague, the liberation of slaves in the 19th century, the defeat of tyranny in the 20th century. Discuss how each of these historical deliverances reflects, however imperfectly, the ultimate deliverance that God accomplishes through Christ. The Puritans saw their own history — their escape from persecution, their settlement in New England — as a type of divine salvation.

Literature Connection:

The concept of salvation — rescue from peril and restoration to safety — is one of the most fundamental narrative patterns in world literature. From the fairy tale rescue of the princess from the dragon to the epic rescue of the hostages from the villain, stories of salvation resonate deeply with the human heart. Study how the Christian story of salvation — creation, fall, redemption, and restoration — provides the master narrative that all other salvation stories dimly reflect. C.S. Lewis argued that all great stories are ultimately echoes of this one Great Story.

Science Connection:

The human brain is one of the most complex structures in the known universe, containing approximately 86 billion neurons forming trillions of connections. Study the basic neuroscience of how the brain processes information, forms beliefs, and generates emotions. Discuss how the biblical command to 'renew your mind' (Romans 12:2) and to 'take every thought captive' (2 Corinthians 10:5) relates to what we know about neuroplasticity — the brain's ability to form new neural pathways and change patterns of thinking. The helmet of salvation protects this remarkably complex organ and the mind it produces.

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.


Let us now press deeper into the doctrine of the helmet of salvation, exploring additional Scripture and Puritan teaching that will fortify our minds and strengthen our hope.

The doctrine of the perseverance of the saints — the teaching that those whom God has truly saved will be preserved by His power to the end — is the theological foundation upon which the helmet of salvation rests. If the believer could lose his salvation, the helmet would be a fragile, unreliable piece of armor that might shatter at any moment. But the Puritans, grounding themselves firmly in Scripture, taught that genuine salvation is eternal and irrevocable. Jesus Himself declared in John 10:27-29: "My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me: And I give unto them eternal life; and they shall never perish, neither shall any man pluck them out of my hand. My Father, which gave them me, is greater than all; and no man is able to pluck them out of my Father's hand." The believer is held in a double grip — the hand of Christ and the hand of the Father — and no power in heaven, earth, or hell can pry those hands open.

John Owen explored the perseverance of the saints with his customary theological rigor in "The Doctrine of the Saints' Perseverance Explained and Confirmed." Owen marshaled an overwhelming array of scriptural evidence to demonstrate that genuine believers cannot finally and totally fall away from grace. He pointed to Philippians 1:6: "Being confident of this very thing, that he which hath begun a good work in you will perform it until the day of Jesus Christ." He cited Jude 24: "Now unto him that is able to keep you from falling, and to present you faultless before the presence of his glory with exceeding joy." He referenced John 6:39: "And this is the Father's will which hath sent me, that of all which he hath given me I should lose nothing, but should raise it up again at the last day." Owen concluded: "The salvation of the believer does not depend upon the believer's ability to hold on to God, but upon God's ability to hold on to the believer. And this ability is infinite, unchangeable, and unfailing. The helmet of salvation is forged from the eternal purposes of an omnipotent God, and no creature in the universe can dent it."

Thomas Watson, in "A Body of Divinity," explored the doctrine of glorification — the final stage of salvation when the believer is made perfectly holy and brought into the immediate presence of God forever. Watson taught that glorification is the consummation of everything God has been doing in the believer's life from the moment of regeneration. It is the completion of sanctification, the fullness of adoption, the climax of the entire redemptive process. Watson wrote: "Glorification is the last link in the golden chain of salvation, and it is as certain as the first. If God has justified you, He will glorify you. If He has begun the work of salvation in your soul, He will finish it in your glorification. This is the hope that the helmet protects — not a mere wish, not a vague aspiration, but a divine certainty grounded in the immutable decree of Almighty God."

Watson described the glories of the final state with exquisite pastoral warmth. He spoke of the glorified body — free from disease, pain, weakness, and death. He spoke of the glorified mind — free from error, confusion, doubt, and ignorance. He spoke of the glorified will — free from temptation, rebellion, inconsistency, and the pull of indwelling sin. He spoke of the glorified affections — free from sinful desires, disordered loves, unholy attachments, and worldly passions. And he spoke of the glorified relationships — perfect communion with God, perfect fellowship with the saints, and perfect harmony in the new creation. Watson wrote: "In glory, we shall know as we are known. We shall love as we are loved. We shall be as holy as He who called us is holy. We shall see His face and bear His name. And there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain, for the former things shall have passed away (Revelation 21:4). This is the hope that the helmet of salvation protects. This is the future that no fiery dart can destroy."

Richard Sibbes, in "The Returning Backslider," addressed the experience of believers who have temporarily lost their sense of assurance through sin and disobedience. Sibbes taught that the loss of assurance is not the same as the loss of salvation. A believer may lose the comfortable sense of God's love while retaining God's love itself — just as a child may temporarily lose the sense of his father's favor while remaining his father's child. Sibbes counseled backsliders to return to Christ through confession and repentance, assuring them that the Savior who received them at first will receive them again. Sibbes wrote: "Christ does not cast out those who come to Him, even if they come the second time, or the third, or the hundredth. His patience is inexhaustible. His mercy is without limit. His love endures all things. Return to Him, bruised and broken believer. Put the helmet back on. And know that the salvation it represents has not been lost — only the sense of it. And that sense will return, as surely as the morning follows the night."

Jonathan Edwards explored the relationship between the hope of salvation and present spiritual motivation in his treatise "Religious Affections." Edwards argued that genuine hope of future glory does not make the believer passive or complacent but rather energizes him for present obedience. The person who truly believes that he will one day stand before the throne of God in perfect holiness is motivated to pursue holiness now. The person who truly believes that he will one day see Christ face to face is motivated to seek Christ's face in prayer and worship today. Edwards wrote: "The hope of glory is the most powerful motivator of present godliness. The man who truly expects to spend eternity in the presence of a holy God will not be content to spend his remaining days in the service of sin. The hope of the helmet drives the warrior forward — it does not lull him to sleep."

Edwards also addressed the relationship between hope and suffering, drawing upon Romans 5:3-5: "And not only so, but we glory in tribulations also: knowing that tribulation worketh patience; And patience, experience; and experience, hope: And hope maketh not ashamed; because the love of God is shed abroad in our hearts by the Holy Ghost which is given unto us." Edwards observed that hope is actually strengthened, not weakened, by suffering. The chain of spiritual development runs from tribulation through patience and experience to hope — meaning that the believer who endures suffering with faith and patience arrives at a deeper, stronger, more tested hope than the one who has never suffered. The helmet of salvation, like the shield of faith, is actually forged stronger in the fires of affliction.

Thomas Brooks, in "Heaven on Earth," devoted an entire work to the doctrine of assurance and the practical means of obtaining it. Brooks identified several marks of genuine salvation that can strengthen the believer's confidence. First, a love for holiness — the genuine believer loves what God loves and hates what God hates. Second, a sensitivity to sin — the genuine believer is grieved by his own sin, not comfortable with it. Third, a desire for God's glory — the genuine believer wants God to be honored above all things. Fourth, a love for God's people — the genuine believer delights in the fellowship of the saints. Fifth, a hunger for God's Word — the genuine believer finds Scripture nourishing and satisfying. Brooks wrote: "If you find these marks in your soul, take comfort. They are the fingerprints of the Holy Spirit, and they prove that He dwells within you. Put on the helmet of salvation with confidence. Your salvation is real. Your hope is not in vain. And the God who began this work in you will complete it on the day of Jesus Christ."

Consider the magnificent vision of the New Jerusalem in Revelation 21:1-4: "And I saw a new heaven and a new earth: for the first heaven and the first earth were passed away; and there was no more sea. And I John saw the holy city, new Jerusalem, coming down from God out of heaven, prepared as a bride adorned for her husband. And I heard a great voice out of heaven saying, Behold, the tabernacle of God is with men, and he will dwell with them, and they shall be his people, and God himself shall be with them, and be their God. And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away." This is the ultimate fulfillment of the salvation that the helmet protects. The New Jerusalem is not a metaphor; it is the eternal home of the redeemed, the place where God dwells with His people in unmediated, uninterrupted, unending communion. Every tear will be wiped away. Every sorrow will be healed. Every pain will cease. Every death will be swallowed up in victory. This is the hope that sustains the believer through every battle, every trial, and every dark night of the soul. This is the city toward which we march. This is the crown for which we fight. And this is the future that no fiery dart can ever take from us.

Jeremiah Burroughs, in "The Rare Jewel of Christian Contentment," connected the helmet of salvation to the believer's experience of peace in the midst of adversity. Burroughs taught that the hope of eternal glory produces a contentment that cannot be shaken by any temporal loss. The believer who truly grasps the magnitude of what awaits him in glory can bear the deprivations of this present life with patience and even with joy. Burroughs wrote: "If I have an inheritance incorruptible, undefiled, and that fadeth not away, reserved in heaven for me, what is the loss of a few earthly comforts? If I am destined for eternal glory, what is the sting of a few years of suffering? The helmet of salvation gives me an eternal perspective that transforms every present sorrow into a light and momentary affliction, not worthy to be compared with the weight of glory that is to come."

Richard Baxter, in "The Saints' Everlasting Rest," wrote with passionate urgency about the importance of meditating frequently on the hope of heaven. Baxter argued that most Christians live far below their spiritual privileges because they rarely think about the glories that await them. They are so occupied with the concerns of this present world that the eternal world fades into a dim, distant abstraction. Baxter urged believers to set aside time each day for deliberate meditation on heaven — to think concretely and specifically about the presence of God, the fellowship of the saints, the cessation of all suffering, and the endless delight of the eternal state. Baxter wrote: "If you would maintain a lively hope, you must maintain a lively meditation upon the things hoped for. The helmet of salvation protects the mind, but the mind must be filled with the things of salvation in order for the helmet to do its work. A mind filled with earthly anxieties and worldly distractions is a mind that has taken off the helmet and left itself exposed to every dark thought the enemy can devise."

The Puritan pastor Samuel Bolton, in "The True Bounds of Christian Freedom," connected the helmet of salvation to the believer's experience of spiritual liberty. Bolton taught that the assurance of salvation produces a freedom that is unlike any other freedom on earth. It is freedom from the fear of death, freedom from the fear of judgment, freedom from the tyranny of guilt, and freedom from the paralyzing anxiety that grips the soul of one who does not know where he stands with God. Bolton wrote: "The believer who wears the helmet of salvation walks through this world as a free man — free from the fear that makes cowards of the ungodly, free from the dread that haunts the conscience of the impenitent, free from the bondage that chains the soul of the unbeliever. He knows where he came from (the electing love of God), he knows where he is (the sanctifying grace of God), and he knows where he is going (the glorifying purpose of God). And this knowledge makes him free — free to love, free to serve, free to suffer, and free to die with confident hope."


Let us now consider the daily, practical implications of the helmet of salvation for our lives.

First, the helmet of salvation calls us to cultivate the habit of heavenly-mindedness. Colossians 3:1-2 commands: "If ye then be risen with Christ, seek those things which are above, where Christ sitteth on the right hand of God. Set your affection on things above, not on things on the earth." The believer who fills his mind with thoughts of heaven, of Christ's glory, and of the eternal inheritance that awaits him is a believer whose helmet is firmly in place. The world competes for our attention with a thousand distractions. The enemy seeks to fill our minds with earthly anxieties and temporal concerns. But the helmet of salvation redirects our gaze upward, reminding us that this world is not our home and that the best is yet to come.

Second, the helmet of salvation calls us to resist despair in times of suffering. The Puritans suffered greatly — persecution, loss, illness, exile — and they found in the hope of salvation the strength to endure. Romans 8:18 declares: "For I reckon that the sufferings of this present time are not worthy to be compared with the glory which shall be revealed in us." When suffering comes — and it will come — the helmet of salvation protects the mind from the lie that God has abandoned us, that the pain is pointless, or that the future is hopeless. The hope of glory is the anchor of the soul in the storms of life.

Third, the helmet of salvation calls us to live with eternal perspective. The decisions we make today have eternal consequences. The way we use our time, our talents, and our resources either advances or hinders the kingdom of God. The helmet of salvation reminds us that we are citizens of an eternal kingdom and that the choices we make in this brief life echo throughout eternity. The Puritans lived with this awareness, and it gave their lives a gravity, a purpose, and a direction that is sorely lacking in our age of distraction and triviality. Live for eternity. Think about the long view. And let the hope of salvation shape every decision you make.

Fourth, the helmet of salvation calls us to encourage one another with the promises of God. First Thessalonians 4:18 commands: "Wherefore comfort one another with these words." The hope of salvation is not a private possession to be hoarded but a public treasure to be shared. When a fellow believer is downcast, discouraged, or overwhelmed, bring the promises of salvation to bear upon his situation. Remind him of what God has done, what God is doing, and what God will do. The helmet of salvation is strengthened when it is shared — when believers remind one another of the certainties that undergird their hope.

Additional Memory Verse:

"Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, which according to his abundant mercy hath begotten us again unto a lively hope by the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead, To an inheritance incorruptible, and undefiled, and that fadeth not away, reserved in heaven for you." -- 1 Peter 1:3-4

Closing Reflection:

As we conclude this final lesson on the armor of God, let us step back and see the whole picture. Over the course of this unit, we have studied each piece of the spiritual armor: the belt of truth that holds everything together, the breastplate of righteousness that protects our vital organs, the shield of faith that quenches every fiery dart, the sword of the Spirit that gives us offensive power, and now the helmet of salvation that protects our minds with confident hope. Each piece is essential. Each piece is provided by God. And together, they constitute a complete defense against every weapon the enemy can deploy.

William Gurnall, whose "The Christian in Complete Armour" has been our primary Puritan guide through this unit, concluded his magnificent work with words that are fitting for us as well. Gurnall wrote: "Christian, be of good comfort. Your armor is of God's own making. Your sword is of God's own forging. Your shield is of God's own tempering. Your helmet is of God's own fashioning. And the God who armed you will fight beside you, beneath you, above you, and within you, until the last enemy is destroyed and the last battle is won. Press on. Fight well. And look forward to the day when every piece of armor will be laid aside and every soldier of the cross will receive the crown of life that fadeth not away."

The Puritans did not merely teach about the armor of God. They wore it. They fought in it. They lived and died in it. And they proved, through centuries of faithful obedience, that the armor God provides is sufficient for every battle. May we follow their example. May we put on the whole armor of God and stand firm against every strategy of the enemy. And may we fight with the confident hope that the salvation which crowns our heads is as certain as the God who provides it — eternal, unshakeable, and gloriously, magnificently sufficient.

Paul's final word on the armor of God is a word about prayer: "Praying always with all prayer and supplication in the Spirit, and watching thereunto with all perseverance and supplication for all saints" (Ephesians 6:18). Prayer is the breath of the armored warrior. It is the means by which every piece of armor is put on, maintained, and made effective. Without prayer, the armor sits unused in the closet. With prayer, it is buckled on, polished bright, and wielded in the power of the Holy Spirit. Let us therefore arm ourselves daily with the whole armor of God, bathe every piece in prayer, and march forward in the confident hope that the Captain of our salvation has already won the victory, and we march not toward uncertain conflict but toward certain triumph.

We have covered much ground today and throughout this entire unit. Take time this evening to reflect on what stood out to you most. Talk about it with your family. Write it in your journal. Let these truths take root. Until next time, students. May the Lord Himself be your shield and your exceedingly great reward.
