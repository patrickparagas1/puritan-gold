PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Pastoral Encouragement, Day 8
"The Battle Against Lust"
Thomas Watson & John Owen — Counsel for Purity


Good evening, dear brother or sister. Tonight I speak to you not as a lecturer but as a pastor, as a fellow traveler on the road of faith. Wherever you are tonight, whatever burden you carry, you are not alone. God is near, and He has a word for you.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Matthew 5:28:

"But I say unto you, That whosoever looketh on a woman to lust after her hath committed adultery with her already in his heart."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


No area of personal holiness is more contested in the life of a Christian man than the battle against lust. Jesus' words in the Sermon on the Mount leave no room for evasion: the sin of adultery is not merely an action of the body but a condition of the heart. The man who entertains lustful thoughts has already transgressed the seventh commandment.

Thomas Watson, in his exposition of the Ten Commandments, addresses this with both severity and pastoral tenderness. Watson writes that the seventh commandment requires not only the absence of the outward act but the purity of the inward man. This commandment, he says, 'goes beyond the outward act and reaches to the heart.' Christ's teaching in Matthew 5 is not an innovation but an exposition of what the commandment always meant.

Watson identifies several causes of lust that the godly man must understand and address. The first is idleness. Watson observes that the man who has too much leisure is particularly vulnerable to temptation. He notes the example of David, who fell into adultery at a time when 'kings go forth to battle' but David 'tarried still at Jerusalem.' When the hands are idle, the mind wanders, and when the mind wanders, it tends to wander toward sin.

The second cause Watson identifies is the eye. 'The eye is the window through which sin enters,' Watson writes. Job understood this when he said, 'I made a covenant with mine eyes' (Job 31:1). The undisciplined eye feeds the lustful heart. This is especially urgent in our age, where images of temptation are available at every turn. Watson's counsel to make a covenant with the eyes remains as relevant today as it was in the seventeenth century.

The third cause is the company we keep. Watson warns that association with those who speak casually about sexual sin, who joke about unfaithfulness, or who normalize impurity will gradually erode the defenses of even a godly man. The Puritans understood what modern psychology confirms: we absorb the moral standards of those around us.

Watson then provides counsel for the fight. First, he commends the cultivation of a lively awareness of God's presence. The man who truly believes that God sees his every thought will be more careful about what he allows into his mind. Watson writes that 'the consideration of God's omnipresence is a powerful preservative against sin.' You would not entertain lustful thoughts if Christ were visibly sitting in the room with you; but He is present, though invisible.

Second, Watson commends meditation on the consequences of lust. Not merely the earthly consequences — broken trust, shattered families, public shame — but the spiritual consequences: grieving the Holy Spirit, wounding the conscience, weakening the soul, dishonoring Christ. Watson writes that the man who truly counts the cost of lust will find it far less attractive.

Third, Watson commends fervent prayer. The man who is tempted must pray immediately, not waiting until the temptation has passed (or conquered him). Watson advises the simple cry: 'Lord, deliver me from this evil.' Such prayer, offered sincerely, brings the power of God into the moment of temptation.

John Owen adds, in his 'Mortification of Sin,' that the key to victory over lust is not merely fleeing from temptation (though that is important) but filling the heart with superior affections. Owen writes that the heart that is full of love for Christ has less room for lust. The man who is daily contemplating the beauty and glory of his Savior will find the allurements of the flesh growing dim by comparison.

This is not a battle you will win once and for all. It is a daily fight. But it is a fight you can win, not by your own strength, but by the Spirit's power, through the means of grace, in the fellowship of the saints, and by the blood of Christ that cleanses every sin.


Let us pause and reflect. Consider these questions in the quiet of your heart.

Question 1: Am I being honest with myself and with God about the state of my thought life, or am I hiding behind a veneer of outward respectability?

Question 2: What practical measures am I taking to guard my eyes, my mind, and my heart from the sources of lustful temptation?

Question 3: Am I filling my heart with love for Christ as the primary weapon against the allure of sin?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Lord, I confess that this battle is real and often fierce. Search my heart and cleanse me from secret faults. Help me to make a covenant with my eyes. Fill me with such love for Christ that the attractions of sin grow pale. Keep me pure, for Your name's sake. Amen.

Goodnight, friend. Walk with God tomorrow.
