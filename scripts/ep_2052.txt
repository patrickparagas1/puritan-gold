PURITAN GOLD -- The Book of Sirach: Complete Summary
A 15-Minute Overview of All 51 Chapters

---

The Book of Sirach — also known as Ecclesiasticus, or the Wisdom of Jesus the Son of Sirach — is one of the great wisdom books of the ancient world. Written around 180 to 175 BC by a Jewish scribe named Jesus Ben Sira in Jerusalem, it was later translated into Greek by his grandson around 132 BC for the benefit of Jews living in Egypt. The grandson's prologue, which still survives, explains that his grandfather had devoted himself to reading the Law, the Prophets, and the other writings, and felt compelled to compose a work of instruction and wisdom for those who wished to live according to the Law of God.

Sirach holds a unique place in sacred literature. It is the only book of its kind where we know the author by name. It stands in the tradition of Proverbs and Ecclesiastes, offering practical guidance for daily life, but it is far longer and more comprehensive than either. Across its 51 chapters, Ben Sira addresses nearly every dimension of human experience — from the fear of God to table manners, from choosing friends to burying the dead, from the duty of physicians to the glory of the starry heavens. It is at once a book of theology, a book of ethics, and a book of common sense.

The book is regarded as canonical Scripture by the Catholic and Orthodox churches, and it was widely read and quoted by early Christians and the Church Fathers. Protestant traditions classify it among the Apocrypha, or deuterocanonical books, while still acknowledging its value for instruction in godliness.

THE FEAR OF THE LORD AND THE NATURE OF WISDOM

The book opens with a grand declaration that all wisdom cometh from the Lord, and that the fear of the Lord is the beginning, the fullness, the crown, and the root of wisdom. This is the foundation upon which everything else in Sirach is built. Wisdom is not merely intellectual cleverness; it is a gift of God, poured out upon all His works and given generously to those who love Him. To fear the Lord is to hate evil, to keep His commandments, and to walk humbly before Him. This theme returns again and again throughout the book — in the opening chapters, in the great hymns of praise near the end, and in Ben Sira's own closing prayer.

Ben Sira teaches that wisdom is tested through trial and suffering. He counsels the reader to prepare his soul for temptation, to be patient in adversity, and to trust that God refines the faithful as gold is refined in the fire. Wisdom is personified as a noble woman who calls out in the streets and invites the young to her table — echoing the imagery of the Book of Proverbs. She prepares a feast for those who hunger for understanding, and she offers rest to those who take up her yoke.

SPEECH, THE TONGUE, AND THE POWER OF WORDS

Few ancient writers are as thorough as Ben Sira on the subject of speech. He devotes extensive passages to the power and danger of the tongue. A backbiting tongue, he warns, hath disquieted many and driven them from nation to nation. The stroke of the whip maketh marks in the flesh, but the stroke of the tongue breaketh the bones. Many have fallen by the edge of the sword, but not so many as have fallen by the tongue.

He counsels the reader to weigh his words in a balance and to make a door and bar for his mouth. He distinguishes between the speech of the wise and the babbling of fools. He warns against gossip, slander, oath-breaking, and the betrayal of secrets. He teaches that a man's conversation reveals his character — as the furnace trieth the potter's vessels, so the trial of man is in his reasoning. And he warns that whoso discovereth secrets loseth his credit, and shall never find a friend to his mind.

FRIENDSHIP AND HUMAN RELATIONSHIPS

Ben Sira is one of the ancient world's most perceptive writers on friendship. He distinguishes between true friends and false ones, between the companion who stands by you in adversity and the fair-weather friend who vanishes at the first sign of trouble. A faithful friend, he says, is a strong defense, and he that hath found such a one hath found a treasure. A faithful friend is the medicine of life, and they that fear the Lord shall find him.

He also warns against the dangers of bad company, of the flatterer who speaks sweetly to your face but slanders you behind your back, and of the counselor who gives advice for his own advantage rather than yours. He urges the reader to test his friends before trusting them fully, and to value faithfulness above all other qualities in human relationships.

FAMILY DUTIES: PARENTS, CHILDREN, AND MARRIAGE

The book contains extensive teaching on family life. Ben Sira commands children to honor their fathers and mothers, promising that he who honoreth his father shall have joy in his own children, and when he prayeth he shall be heard. He warns that he who forsaketh his father is as a blasphemer, and he that angereth his mother is cursed of God.

On the discipline of children, he is direct and forceful: he that loveth his son causeth him oft to feel the rod, that he may have joy of him in the end. A horse not broken becometh headstrong, and a child left to himself becometh willful. He counsels fathers to bow down the neck of a son while he is young, lest he wax stubborn.

On marriage, Ben Sira praises the virtuous wife in glowing terms: blessed is the husband of a good wife, for the number of his years shall be doubled. A worthy woman rejoiceth her husband, and he shall fulfill the years of his life in peace. He also speaks frankly about the grief that comes from an unfaithful or quarrelsome wife, and about the anxieties of raising daughters in a dangerous world.

WEALTH, POVERTY, AND GENEROSITY

Ben Sira is remarkably balanced on the subject of wealth. He does not condemn riches outright, but he warns repeatedly against the love of money and the spiritual dangers that attend the pursuit of gain. Watching for riches consumeth the flesh, he writes, and the care thereof driveth away sleep. He that loveth gold shall not be justified. Gold hath been the ruin of many.

He praises the rare rich man who is found without blemish and hath not gone after gold, calling him blessed. He counsels generosity toward the poor, urging the reader to help the needy for the commandment's sake and not to turn them away. Shut up alms in thy storehouses, he teaches, and it shall deliver thee from all affliction. He also gives practical advice about lending and borrowing, warning of the resentments that arise when debts go unpaid and the dangers of becoming surety for another man's obligations.

He teaches contentment: the chief thing for life is water and bread and clothing, and a house to cover shame. Better is the life of a poor man in a mean cottage than delicate fare in another man's house.

WORSHIP, SACRIFICE, AND PRAYER

Ben Sira has a profound theology of worship. He teaches that true sacrifice begins not at the altar but in the heart. He that keepeth the law bringeth offerings enough; to depart from wickedness is a thing pleasing to the Lord. He warns sharply against the hypocrisy of offering sacrifices obtained through injustice — whoso bringeth an offering of the goods of the poor doeth as one that killeth the son before his father's eyes.

He affirms that God is no respecter of persons and will hear the prayer of the oppressed. The prayer of the humble pierceth the clouds, and till it come nigh, the petitioner will not be comforted. He includes a great national prayer in chapter 36, calling upon God to have mercy on Israel, to gather the scattered tribes, to fill Zion with His glory, and to vindicate His people before the nations.

SICKNESS, DEATH, AND THE PHYSICIAN

Ben Sira gives the physician a dignity rarely found in ancient literature. Honor a physician with the honor due unto him, he writes, for the Lord hath created him. The Lord hath created medicines out of the earth, and he that is wise will not abhor them. He counsels the sick man first to pray and to cleanse his heart from sin, and then to give place to the physician, for the Lord hath created him also.

On death, he is realistic and pastoral. He teaches that death is the common lot of all flesh, and counsels the reader not to fear it: remember them that have been before thee and that come after, for this is the sentence of the Lord over all flesh. He gives practical instructions for mourning — weep bitterly, make great moan, but then comfort thyself and move forward, for sorrow hath killed many and there is no profit therein.

THE GLORY OF GOD IN CREATION

In chapter 43, Ben Sira rises to some of the most magnificent nature poetry in all of Scripture. He praises the sun, the moon, the stars, the rainbow, the snow, the frost, the thunder, the whirlwind, and the creatures of the deep sea. The eye marvelleth at the beauty of the whiteness of the snow, he writes, and the heart is astonished at the raining of it. He sees the entire natural world as a testimony to the wisdom and power of the Creator, and concludes: We may speak much, and yet come short; wherefore in sum, He is all.

THE PRAISE OF FAMOUS MEN

The final major section of Sirach, beginning in chapter 44, is the celebrated hymn that opens with the words: Let us now praise famous men, and our fathers that begat us. Ben Sira traces the history of Israel through its great heroes of faith. He begins with Enoch, who pleased the Lord and was translated. He honors Noah, who was found perfect and righteous, and through whom an everlasting covenant was established. He celebrates Abraham, the great father of many people, who kept the law of the Most High and was found faithful when he was proved.

He then turns to Moses, beloved of God and men, through whom the Lord gave the law of life and knowledge. He describes the priestly garments of Aaron in lavish detail, and honors Phinehas for his zeal. He praises Joshua, who stretched out his sword against the cities and called upon the Most High in battle. He remembers Caleb, Samuel, David — who played with lions as with kids and slew the giant Goliath — and Solomon, whose wisdom was as a flood, but who also brought shame upon himself through his unfaithfulness.

He praises the prophet Elijah, who stood up as fire and whose word burned like a lamp, and Elisha, who was filled with the spirit of his master. He remembers the faithful kings — Hezekiah, who fortified Jerusalem and was delivered from Sennacherib, and Josiah, whose remembrance is like the composition of perfume. He honors the prophets Isaiah, Jeremiah, and Ezekiel, the twelve minor prophets, and the builders of the second temple: Zerubbabel, Joshua the high priest, and Nehemiah.

The climax of this section is the portrait of Simon the high priest, son of Onias, whom Ben Sira appears to have seen with his own eyes. He describes Simon coming forth from the sanctuary in his priestly robes — as the morning star in the midst of a cloud, as the sun shining upon the temple, as the rainbow giving light in the bright clouds. It is one of the most vivid liturgical portraits in all of ancient literature.

BEN SIRA'S CLOSING PRAYER

The book concludes with a deeply personal chapter. Ben Sira gives thanks to God for delivering him from danger and from the snares of his enemies. He then recounts how he sought wisdom from his youth, praying for her before the temple and pursuing her to the end. He invites others to join him: Draw near unto me, ye unlearned, and dwell in the house of learning. Put your neck under the yoke, and let your soul receive instruction. Work your work betimes, and in His time He will give you your reward.

WHY THIS BOOK MATTERS

The Book of Sirach matters because it bridges the gap between the older wisdom tradition of Proverbs and the world of the New Testament. Its teachings on forgiveness, on the dangers of the tongue, on generosity to the poor, and on the fear of God as the foundation of all virtue find clear echoes in the teaching of Christ and the apostles. Its great hymn of praise for the heroes of faith anticipates the roll call of faith in the eleventh chapter of Hebrews. Its counsel is timeless — as relevant in our own age as it was in the streets of Jerusalem two centuries before the birth of Christ.

Ben Sira wrote for those who wished to live a godly, ordered, and fruitful life in a world full of temptation and folly. His book remains one of the richest treasuries of practical wisdom ever committed to writing. Blessed is he that shall be exercised in these things, and he that layeth them up in his heart shall become wise.
