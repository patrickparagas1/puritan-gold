PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"The Mighty God, Even the Lord, Hath Spoken"
June 26, 2026

---

"The mighty God, even the Lord, hath spoken, and called the earth from the rising of the sun unto the going down thereof. Out of Zion, the perfection of beauty, God hath shined. Our God shall come, and shall not keep silence: a fire shall devour before him, and it shall be very tempestuous round about him. He shall call to the heavens from above, and to the earth, that he may judge his people." -- Psalm 50:1-4

There is a psalm of comfort and a psalm of terror, and this is both at once. It is comfort to the righteous, for their God is coming and He will vindicate their cause. And it is terror to the hypocrite and the formalist, for the same God who comes to deliver His faithful people comes also to expose and to judge those who have honoured Him with their lips while their hearts have been far from Him. The mighty God is speaking, and when God speaks, the earth itself must listen.

"The mighty God, even the Lord, hath spoken." Three names for God are used in this single verse -- El, Elohim, and Jehovah -- as though the psalmist wished to pile up every divine title to convey the overwhelming majesty of the One who is about to speak. This is not the voice of a creature. This is not the opinion of a man. This is the utterance of the Almighty, the declaration of the sovereign Lord of the universe, and it carries an authority that no earthly power can resist or revoke.

Spurgeon observed that the silence of God is often misinterpreted by the wicked as indifference or absence. Because God does not immediately punish every transgression, men conclude that He does not see, or that He does not care, or that He will not act. But this psalm shatters that illusion. "Our God shall come, and shall not keep silence." The day of reckoning is coming. The Judge is approaching. And when He arrives, He will not arrive quietly. A fire shall devour before Him. A tempest shall rage around Him. The whole creation shall tremble at His presence.

Thomas Manton preached a series of sermons upon this psalm and devoted particular attention to the searching rebuke that God delivers against merely outward religion. "I will not reprove thee for thy sacrifices," God says. "Will I eat the flesh of bulls, or drink the blood of goats?" The hypocrite imagines that God is satisfied with rituals, that the performance of religious duties is sufficient to discharge his obligations, that he can live as he pleases so long as he shows up at the altar with the prescribed offering. But God declares that He has no need of bulls and goats. The cattle on a thousand hills are His. The world and the fulness thereof belong to Him. What He demands is not the outward form of worship but the inward reality: a heart broken for sin, a spirit contrite before Him, a life of genuine thanksgiving and faithful obedience.

John Owen applied this principle with relentless logic to the worship of the New Testament church. If God rejected the elaborate and divinely appointed sacrifices of the Old Testament when they were offered without heart, how much more will He reject the worship of those who come to church on Sunday with outward propriety while their hearts are full of covetousness, their lips are stained with gossip, and their lives contradict everything they profess to believe? The form of worship may change from age to age, but the requirement of sincerity never changes. God has always demanded the heart, and He will always demand the heart, and no amount of external religion can substitute for it.

"Offer unto God thanksgiving; and pay thy vows unto the most High: and call upon me in the day of trouble: I will deliver thee, and thou shalt glorify me." Here is the true worship that God requires. Not the blood of animals, but the gratitude of the heart. Not elaborate ceremonies, but honest prayer. Not religious performances designed to impress, but simple, humble dependence upon the living God. "Call upon me in the day of trouble: I will deliver thee." What a gracious invitation from the lips of the Almighty. He does not say, "Bring me a thousand rams and ten thousand rivers of oil, and then perhaps I will consider your case." He says, "Call upon me." Just call. Just pray. Just come to Me with your trouble, your burden, your need, and I will deliver you, and you shall glorify Me.

William Gurnall found in this invitation the very essence of the gospel. The God who judges hypocrisy is the same God who invites sincerity. The God who will not be mocked by outward religion is the same God who opens His arms to the penitent and promises deliverance to all who call upon His name. The fire that devours the hypocrite warms the heart of the humble. The tempest that terrifies the formalist is the wind of the Spirit that refreshes the contrite soul.

Let us therefore examine our worship in the light of this searching psalm. Are we offering to God the sacrifice of a thankful heart, or merely the outward form of religious observance? Are we calling upon Him in truth, or merely going through the motions of prayer? The mighty God has spoken, and He will not keep silence. Let us make sure that when He comes, He finds in us not the pretence of godliness but the reality. Amen.
