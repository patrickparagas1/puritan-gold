PURITAN GOLD -- Study Topic: The Book of Sirach, Part 6 of 10
"Bridling the Tongue and the Passions"
Sirach Chapters 18–19 — Mastering Self-Control Through Wisdom

---

Good evening, and welcome back to Puritan Gold. Tonight we turn to one of the most searching and practical sections of the Book of Sirach — chapters 18 and 19, where Ben Sira addresses the disciplines of self-governance. Here the ancient sage speaks with penetrating insight about the tongue, the appetites, and the destructive power of gossip. If the Book of Proverbs and the Epistle of James had a companion volume, it would sound remarkably like these chapters.

Let us read from Sirach chapter 18, verses 15 through 18, and then chapter 19, verses 6 through 12:

"My son, blemish not thy good deeds, neither use uncomfortable words when thou givest any thing. Shall not the dew assuage the heat? so is a word better than a gift. Lo, is not a word better than a gift? but both are with a gracious man. A fool will upbraid churlishly, and a gift of the envious consumeth the eyes."

And from chapter 19: "He that can rule his tongue shall live without strife; and he that hateth babbling shall have less evil. Rehearse not unto another that which is told unto thee, and thou shalt fare never the worse. Whether it be to friend or foe, talk not of other men's lives; and if thou canst without offence, reveal them not. For he heard and observed thee, and when time cometh he will hate thee. If thou hast heard a word, let it die with thee; and be bold, it will not burst thee."

--- Teaching ---

Ben Sira understood that the tongue is the most powerful instrument given to man. It can heal or destroy, build or demolish, bless or curse — all in a single sentence. James, writing centuries later, would echo this truth with unforgettable vividness: "The tongue is a fire, a world of iniquity... it defileth the whole body, and setteth on fire the course of nature" (James 3:6). But Ben Sira arrived at the same conclusion through careful observation of human society in Jerusalem.

The first lesson of these chapters concerns the manner of giving. It is not enough to do a good deed; one must do it graciously. "Blemish not thy good deeds, neither use uncomfortable words when thou givest any thing." How many acts of kindness have been spoiled by a grudging word, a sigh of inconvenience, or a look that communicates reluctance! The Puritans called this "giving with an evil eye" — the outward act of generosity poisoned by the inward attitude of resentment. Richard Baxter wrote that "a gift given grudgingly wounds both the giver and the receiver." Ben Sira goes further: "Is not a word better than a gift?" Sometimes the kind word, the word of encouragement, the word of compassion is worth more than gold. The gracious person gives both.

But the great theme of chapter 19 is the governance of speech — particularly the deadly sin of gossip. "Rehearse not unto another that which is told unto thee." This is one of the clearest commands in all of wisdom literature, and one of the most widely disobeyed. Gossip is the currency of social life in every age. It flatters the teller, entertains the hearer, and destroys the subject. Ben Sira cuts to the heart of it: "If thou hast heard a word, let it die with thee." What a magnificent image! Let the rumor, the secret, the whispered report die inside you. Do not give it breath. Do not give it wings. Let it perish in silence.

The Apostle Paul lists gossip among the gravest sins: "Being filled with all unrighteousness... whisperers, backbiters" (Romans 1:29-30). Notice that whisperers and backbiters appear alongside murder and wickedness. God takes the sin of the tongue with terrible seriousness. Proverbs 18:8 observes: "The words of a talebearer are as wounds, and they go down into the innermost parts of the belly." Gossip wounds deeply because it strikes at reputation, which is harder to repair than a broken bone.

Ben Sira also addresses the broader discipline of self-control over the passions. Chapter 18:30-31 counsels: "Go not after thy lusts, but refrain thyself from thine appetites. If thou givest thy soul the desires that please her, she will make thee a laughingstock to thine enemies." The sage understood that unchecked desire leads not to freedom but to bondage. The man who cannot say no to his appetites becomes a slave to them. The Puritans spoke often of the "mortification of sin" — the daily, deliberate putting to death of sinful desires. John Owen's classic treatise on this subject begins with the principle that "sin will not die unless it is constantly weakened and mortified." Ben Sira would have agreed wholeheartedly.

The connection between the tongue and the passions is not accidental. They share the same root: a heart that has not learned submission to God. Jesus taught that "out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaketh" (Matthew 12:34). The undisciplined tongue reveals the undisciplined heart. When we gossip, we reveal our pride — the desire to appear knowledgeable at another's expense. When we speak harshly, we reveal our anger. When we flatter, we reveal our manipulation. The tongue is a window into the soul.

Thomas Watson wrote that "the heart is the mint where words are coined; if the heart be holy, the words will be gracious." This is the positive vision that Ben Sira holds before us. The person who fears God will govern the tongue, not out of grim duty, but out of love for God and neighbor. Self-control is not a burden but a gift — the gift of inner freedom. The person who can hold the tongue when provoked, who can keep a confidence when tempted to share it, who can speak kindly when irritated, possesses a strength that no army can match.

Consider also Ben Sira's practical wisdom about confrontation. Chapter 19:13-17 counsels: "Admonish a friend: it may be he hath not done it; and if he have done it, that he do it no more. Admonish thy friend: it may be he hath not said it; and if he have, that he speak it not again." Here is the alternative to gossip: direct, loving confrontation. Instead of talking about your friend behind his back, talk to him. Instead of whispering to others about what he may have done, ask him directly. This is precisely the pattern Jesus establishes in Matthew 18:15: "If thy brother shall trespass against thee, go and tell him his fault between thee and him alone."

The Puritans practiced this with remarkable faithfulness. Cotton Mather spoke of the importance of having a "faithful monitor" — a friend brave enough to tell you the truth about your failings. Jonathan Edwards and David Brainerd served this role for one another. This requires courage, humility, and love — but it is infinitely better than the alternative of gossip, which poisons relationships and destroys trust.

--- Application ---

1. Examine your speech this week. Do you gossip? Do you repeat what others have told you in confidence? Ben Sira's counsel is clear: "If thou hast heard a word, let it die with thee." Practice letting rumors and secrets die with you.

2. When you give — whether money, time, or help — do you give graciously? Or do you blemish your good deeds with sighs, complaints, or uncomfortable words? Ask God to make you a cheerful giver in word as well as deed.

3. If you have a grievance with a friend or family member, go to them directly rather than talking about them to others. Follow the Matthew 18 pattern: speak privately, speak lovingly, speak honestly.

4. Practice the discipline of silence. Not every thought needs to be spoken. Not every opinion needs to be shared. The wise person speaks less and listens more. As Proverbs 10:19 teaches: "In the multitude of words there wanteth not sin: but he that refraineth his lips is wise."

--- Closing ---

Let us pray. O Lord, who hast given us the gift of speech, forgive us for the countless times we have misused it. Forgive our gossip, our harsh words, our careless speech. Set a watch, O Lord, before our mouths; keep the door of our lips. Teach us by Thy Spirit to speak only what is true, what is kind, what is necessary, and what brings glory to Thy name. And grant us the inner discipline to master not only the tongue but every desire that wars against the soul. Through Jesus Christ our Lord, who spoke only truth and grace. Amen.

Go in peace, dear friend. In our next session, we come to one of the most delightful passages in Sirach — chapters 31 and 32 on the grace of the table, moderation, and the art of hospitality. Until then, may the Lord give you a tongue seasoned with grace.
