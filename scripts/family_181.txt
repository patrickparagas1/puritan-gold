"Teaching the Catechism at Home"
Matthew Henry — Family Worship and Bible Study

Good evening, dear family. Welcome back to Puritan Gold. Tonight we explore one of the Puritans' most powerful educational tools — the catechism. Matthew Henry was a passionate advocate for catechetical instruction in the home, and his counsel is as relevant today as it was three centuries ago. Let us draw near to God.

Let us pray. Lord God, we thank You for the systematic teaching of Your truth that has been passed down through the generations. Help us to value this tradition and to make use of it in our own home, so that our children — and we ourselves — may be grounded in the faith once delivered to the saints. In Christ's name, Amen.

Our Scripture reading this evening comes from Deuteronomy 6:6-7 and 1 Peter 3:15. Let us hear the Word of the Lord:

"And these words, which I command thee this day, shall be in thine heart: And thou shalt teach them diligently unto thy children, and shalt talk of them when thou sittest in thine house, and when thou walkest by the way, and when thou liest down, and when thou risest up. But sanctify the Lord God in your hearts: and be ready always to give an answer to every man that asketh you a reason of the hope that is in you with meekness and fear."

That is Deuteronomy 6:6-7 and 1 Peter 3:15. Let those words settle in your heart for a moment before we continue.

--- Teaching ---

The word "catechism" simply means instruction by question and answer. It is one of the oldest and most effective teaching methods in the history of the church, and the Puritans employed it with extraordinary dedication. Every major Puritan writer produced or commended a catechism for family use. The Westminster Shorter Catechism, composed in 1647, became the standard catechetical tool in Reformed and Presbyterian households for generations. Its opening question and answer are among the most famous sentences in the English language: "What is the chief end of man? Man's chief end is to glorify God, and to enjoy him for ever."

Matthew Henry was catechized as a child by his father Philip, and he carried the practice into his own home and commended it passionately to his congregation. He saw the catechism not as a dry academic exercise but as a lifeline — a way of transmitting the essential truths of the Christian faith from one generation to the next in a form that could be understood, memorized, and retained for a lifetime.

Henry identified several advantages of catechetical instruction. The first is comprehensiveness. A good catechism covers the whole range of Christian doctrine — the nature of God, the creation and fall of man, the person and work of Christ, the way of salvation, the Ten Commandments, the Lord's Prayer, and the sacraments. By working through a catechism together, a family ensures that their children receive a complete education in the faith, not just a collection of favorite passages and popular topics.

The second advantage is clarity. The question-and-answer format forces both the teacher and the learner to be precise. Vague, muddled thinking cannot survive the catechetical method. When a child is asked, "What is God?" and must answer, "God is a Spirit, infinite, eternal, and unchangeable, in his being, wisdom, power, holiness, justice, goodness, and truth," that child is learning to think about God with accuracy and reverence. The catechism is not a straitjacket for the mind but a trellis on which sound thinking can grow.

The third advantage is memorability. Children are natural memorizers. They can commit astonishing amounts of material to memory when it is presented in a structured, rhythmic, question-and-answer format. And what they memorize in childhood stays with them for life. Henry often told stories of elderly men and women who, on their deathbeds, recited catechism answers they had learned as children of six or seven. These truths, planted early and deep, became the pillars that upheld their faith through decades of life.

The fourth advantage is accountability. When a family works through a catechism together, parents and children are held to the same standard. Parents are forced to study the material themselves — and many parents have discovered, to their surprise, that the catechism taught them things they had never clearly understood before. The catechism creates a common language of faith within the household, so that when doctrinal questions arise, the family has a shared framework for discussing them.

Henry recommended a simple method for using the catechism at home. Choose one question per week. Read it together on the Lord's Day. Discuss the meaning of the answer. Have each family member memorize the answer during the week. Review it together the following Lord's Day before moving on to the next question. Over the course of a year or two, the family will have worked through the entire catechism — and every member will carry the essentials of the faith in their heart for the rest of their life.

This practice may seem old-fashioned, but it addresses a pressing modern need. Many Christian families today have a faith that is sincere but shallow — strong in feeling but weak in understanding. The catechism provides the depth that feeling alone cannot. It gives substance to our devotion, structure to our thinking, and words to our faith. Let us consider taking up this ancient practice in our homes.

--- Discussion Questions ---

Let us pause and talk as a family. Here are tonight's discussion questions. Everyone gets a turn to share.

Question 1: The Westminster Catechism says, "Man's chief end is to glorify God, and to enjoy him for ever." What does this mean in practical, everyday terms for our family?

Question 2: Henry says the catechism forces us to think precisely about God. Why is clear thinking about God important? What happens when our understanding of God is vague?

Question 3: Would our family be willing to try working through a catechism together — one question per week? What might that look like for us?

Take a few minutes to talk about these questions together. Let everyone share, and listen to one another with patience and love.

--- Memory Verse ---

Now, here is our memory verse for today. Let us say it together:

"Be ready always to give an answer to every man that asketh you a reason of the hope that is in you with meekness and fear." -- 1 Peter 3:15

Try to say it out loud three times right now. Then, before bed tonight, see if you can say it from memory.

--- Closing ---

Let us pray together. Lord God, we thank You for the rich tradition of catechetical instruction that has preserved Your truth across the centuries. Give us a desire to learn the fundamentals of our faith clearly, deeply, and memorably. Help us to teach our children so that they are ready to give an answer for the hope that is in them. In Jesus' name, Amen.

Sleep well tonight, knowing that God watches over your family.
