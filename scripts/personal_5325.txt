PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Pastoral Encouragement, Day 25
"Generosity and Stewardship"
Biblical Principles of Giving — Managing God's Gifts for His Glory


Good evening, friend. Tonight we consider a subject that touches every part of our lives: how we handle the resources God has entrusted to us. The Puritans had a remarkably balanced and deeply biblical understanding of money, possessions, and generosity. They were neither ascetics who despised material blessings nor materialists who pursued them for their own sake. They were stewards — and they invite us to be the same.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is 2 Corinthians 9:6-7:

"But this I say, He which soweth sparingly shall reap also sparingly; and he which soweth bountifully shall reap also bountifully. Every man according as he purposeth in his heart, so let him give; not grudgingly, or of necessity: for God loveth a cheerful giver."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


The foundation of Christian stewardship is the recognition that everything we possess belongs to God. The Puritans understood this with a clarity that we often lack. Thomas Watson wrote that we are not owners but stewards. The money in our accounts, the houses we inhabit, the clothes we wear, the food we eat — all of it comes from the hand of God and remains under His sovereign authority. We are managers, not proprietors. And one day, we will give an account of our management.

This understanding transforms the entire question of giving. If everything belongs to God, then generosity is not a matter of giving away what is ours. It is a matter of returning to God what was always His. The tithe — the giving of a tenth — was practiced throughout the history of Israel and affirmed by the early church. The Puritans considered it a baseline, not a ceiling. Richard Baxter argued that those whom God has blessed with abundance have a corresponding obligation to share that abundance with those in need. To hoard what God has given for the benefit of others is not prudence; it is theft.

But the Puritans were careful to insist that the spirit of giving matters as much as the act. Paul writes that God loves a cheerful giver. The word "cheerful" in the Greek carries the sense of hilarity — a joyful, eager, glad-hearted willingness to part with what one has for the sake of others. Giving that is done out of guilt, obligation, or social pressure is not truly Christian giving. True generosity flows from a heart that has been so overwhelmed by the generosity of God in the gospel that it cannot help but overflow toward others.

John Flavel counseled believers to think of their possessions as tools rather than treasures. A tool is something you use for a purpose beyond itself. A treasure is something you cling to for its own sake. When we treat our money, our time, and our gifts as tools in the hand of God, we hold them loosely and deploy them freely. When we treat them as treasures, we grip them tightly and guard them jealously. The gospel teaches us that our true treasure is in heaven, where neither moth nor rust doth corrupt, and where thieves do not break through nor steal.

The Puritans also addressed the relationship between generosity and trust. One of the primary reasons we struggle to give is that we are afraid. We fear that if we give too much, we will not have enough. We fear that our generosity will leave us vulnerable. The Puritans answered this fear with the promises of God. "Give, and it shall be given unto you," Jesus said (Luke 6:38). This is not a prosperity gospel that promises material wealth in exchange for donations. It is a covenant promise that God will provide for those who trust Him enough to obey Him. Matthew Henry wrote that the generous man does not impoverish himself by giving; he lays up treasure with the most reliable banker in the universe.

Stewardship extends beyond money to every resource God has given us. Our time, our talents, our influence, our relationships — all of these are gifts to be managed for the glory of God and the good of others. The Puritans were remarkably industrious people, not because they worshiped productivity but because they believed that every hour was a trust from God. Jonathan Edwards resolved to live so as to waste no moment and to use every faculty of his being for the purposes for which God had given it.


First, examine your giving honestly. Are you giving regularly, proportionally, and cheerfully to the work of God? If you have not established a practice of consistent giving, begin this week. Start where you are, and ask God to grow your generosity over time.

Second, identify one area beyond financial giving where you can be more generous — with your time, your attention, your encouragement, or your hospitality. Generosity is a way of life, not merely a line item on a budget.

Third, confess any fear that is holding you back from greater generosity. Bring that fear to God in prayer and ask Him to replace it with trust in His faithful provision.


Gracious Father, everything we have comes from Thy hand. Forgive us for clinging to Thy gifts as though they were our own. Teach us to hold loosely what Thou hast given and to give freely as Thou hast commanded. Replace our fear with faith, our stinginess with generosity, and our love of money with love for Thee. Through Christ, who though He was rich, for our sakes became poor, that we through His poverty might be rich, Amen.

Rest well tonight, friend. Your Father knows what you need, and He is faithful to provide.
