PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"Rejoice in the Lord, O Ye Righteous"
June 16, 2026

---

"Rejoice in the Lord, O ye righteous: for praise is comely for the upright. Praise the Lord with harp: sing unto him with the psaltery and an instrument of ten strings. Sing unto him a new song; play skilfully with a loud noise. For the word of the Lord is right; and all his works are done in truth. He loveth righteousness and judgment: the earth is full of the goodness of the Lord." -- Psalm 33:1-5

There is a joy that belongs exclusively to the righteous, and it is a joy that the world can neither give nor take away. The wicked may have their pleasures, but they cannot rejoice in the Lord, for they have no relationship with Him and no part in His promises. But the righteous -- those who have been justified by faith and clothed in the righteousness of Christ -- these have an inheritance of joy that is as deep as the ocean and as vast as the heavens. "Rejoice in the Lord, O ye righteous: for praise is comely for the upright."

Spurgeon remarked that praise is the dress of the righteous, the garment that fits them best, the ornament that becomes them above all others. When a holy man praises God, there is a fitness, a suitability, a comeliness about it that is beautiful to behold. It is as natural for the righteous to praise God as it is for birds to sing or for the sun to shine. And conversely, when the righteous fail to praise, something is wrong. It is as though a lamp had been placed under a bushel, or a fountain had been stopped up, or a bird had been caged. The righteous were made for praise, and praise was made for the righteous.

"Sing unto him a new song." Not the same old tired words repeated mechanically out of habit, but a fresh expression of living gratitude that springs from the heart's present experience of God's goodness. Every new mercy deserves a new song. Every fresh deliverance calls for a fresh outpouring of praise. The God who makes all things new desires worship that is new -- not novel for the sake of novelty, but new in the sense of fresh, vital, alive with the energy of present faith and present love.

"For the word of the Lord is right; and all his works are done in truth." Here is the foundation upon which all praise rests. We do not praise God blindly or sentimentally, hoping that He is good without any evidence to support our hope. We praise Him because His word is right and His works are true. Every promise He has made has been or will be fulfilled. Every command He has given reflects perfect wisdom and perfect goodness. Every judgment He has executed has been perfectly just. And every act of mercy He has performed has been perfectly gracious. There is no error in God, no inconsistency, no shadow of turning. His word is right, always and everywhere, in every circumstance and in every age.

Stephen Charnock, in his great discourse upon the attributes of God, demonstrated that the truthfulness of God is the pillar upon which every other attribute rests. If God were not true, His mercy would be unreliable, His justice would be uncertain, His promises would be worthless, and His threatenings would be empty. But because God is true, absolutely and invariably true, we may stake our lives and our eternal destinies upon every word that proceeds from His mouth. The man who stands upon the truth of God stands upon an immovable foundation, and though the winds blow and the floods come and the storms beat upon him, he shall not fall.

"He loveth righteousness and judgment: the earth is full of the goodness of the Lord." Here the psalmist lifts our eyes from the word of God to the works of God, and he bids us see in the whole creation a display of divine goodness. The earth is full of it. Not partly full, not nearly full, but full to overflowing. The goodness of God is visible in the sunrise that gilds the morning sky, in the rain that waters the thirsty ground, in the harvest that fills the barns of the farmer, in the breath that fills our lungs, in the beating of our hearts, in the love of our families, in the companionship of our friends. Every good gift and every perfect gift comes down from the Father of lights, and the man who has eyes to see will find the goodness of God confronting him at every turn.

Richard Sibbes taught that the chief hindrance to praise is not the absence of blessings but the blindness of the soul. We are surrounded by the goodness of God on every side, but we fail to recognise it because our hearts are preoccupied with our troubles and our eyes are fixed upon our wants. If we would learn to look upward instead of inward, to count our blessings instead of our burdens, to meditate upon the goodness of God instead of the hardness of our circumstances, we would find that our hearts are overflowing with praise and our lips are singing new songs to the Lord.

John Flavel urged his congregation to cultivate the habit of daily thanksgiving, and he suggested that every evening, before retiring to rest, the believer should review the mercies of the day and give thanks for each one specifically. Not a general, vague thanksgiving that costs the heart nothing, but a particular, detailed acknowledgment of each kindness that the Lord has shown. The man who practices this discipline will find that his life is richer than he knew, his blessings are more numerous than he imagined, and his God is more generous than he ever dared to believe.

Let us therefore obey the command of this psalm and rejoice in the Lord. Let us praise Him with new songs and skilful music. Let us rest upon the truth of His word and wonder at the goodness that fills the earth. For the Lord loveth righteousness and judgment, and His goodness endures forever. Amen.
