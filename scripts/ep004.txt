PURITAN GOLD — Episode 4
"The Soul Winner's Prayer Life"
Week 1, Day 4 (Thursday) — Spurgeon's The Soul Winner & Proverbs 3:5-6

---

"Trust in the Lord with all thine heart; and lean not unto thine own understanding. In all thy ways acknowledge him, and he shall direct thy paths."

Proverbs 3, verses 5 and 6. You probably know those words. You may have had them on a bookmark or a refrigerator magnet at some point. They are among the most quoted, most memorized, most beloved verses in all of Scripture.

But today I want to come at them from a direction you may not expect. I want to talk about those verses... through the lens of evangelism. Through the lens of what Spurgeon called "soul winning." Because I believe there is a connection here — a vital, overlooked connection — between trusting God with all your heart... and the way we share the gospel with others.

This is Puritan Gold. And today we're opening Spurgeon's remarkable book, The Soul Winner, and letting the Prince of Preachers teach us about the most essential ingredient in evangelism. It's not a technique. It's not a strategy. It's not a method or a program.

It's prayer.

Spurgeon was, by any measure, one of the most effective evangelists in the history of the church. Thousands came to faith under his ministry. The Metropolitan Tabernacle in London was filled to capacity — six thousand people — every Sunday, twice a day, for nearly forty years. New converts were being baptized and added to the church constantly.

And when people asked Spurgeon the secret of his success — which they did, often — his answer was always the same. It wasn't his oratory. It wasn't his preparation. It wasn't his command of Scripture, though all of those things were remarkable. The secret, Spurgeon said, was prayer. Specifically, the prayers of his people.

Beneath the platform at the Metropolitan Tabernacle, there was a room. And in that room, during every service, a group of faithful believers would gather to pray. While Spurgeon was preaching above them, they were on their knees below, pleading with God for the conversion of souls. Spurgeon called that room "the powerhouse" of his church. And he meant it literally. He believed — and he saw confirmed week after week, year after year — that prayer was the power behind every conversion.

Not his preaching. Prayer.

In The Soul Winner, Spurgeon laid out his convictions about this with characteristic clarity and force. He taught that the work of converting a soul is entirely the work of the Holy Spirit. No amount of human eloquence, no cleverness of argument, no emotional appeal — none of it can open a blind eye or soften a hard heart. Only God can do that. And God does it in response to prayer.

This is where Proverbs 3:5-6 comes in. Because what does it mean to "trust in the Lord with all thine heart" when it comes to sharing the gospel?

It means this: we do not lean on our own understanding. We do not trust in our own ability to persuade. We do not put our confidence in our arguments, our presentations, our four spiritual laws, or our apologetics training — as helpful as those things may be. We trust in the Lord. With all our heart. And we acknowledge Him — through prayer — in all our ways.

And what does He promise? "He shall direct thy paths." He will guide the conversation. He will prepare the heart. He will arrange the circumstances. He will bring the increase.

Now, let me be clear about something. Spurgeon was not lazy about preparation. He studied harder than almost anyone. He thought carefully about how to present the gospel clearly and winsomely. He believed in using the best tools available. But he held all of that preparation with an open hand... because he knew that the decisive factor was never his skill. It was God's sovereign grace, accessed through believing prayer.

In The Soul Winner, Spurgeon described the kind of prayer that should accompany evangelism. And it was not casual prayer. Not a quick "Lord, bless this conversation" tossed toward heaven as an afterthought. It was agonizing prayer. Travailing prayer. The kind of prayer that Jacob offered when he wrestled with God and said, "I will not let thee go, except thou bless me."

Spurgeon wrote about praying for specific people by name. He encouraged Christians to identify individuals — family members, neighbors, coworkers — and to bring those names before God day after day, week after week, with earnest, persistent, believing prayer. Not giving up. Not losing heart. Trusting that God, in His perfect timing, would do what no human effort could accomplish.

And here's what's beautiful about this. When you pray for someone's salvation... it changes you as much as it prepares them. The act of praying for another person's soul develops in you a love for that person that no self-help technique could produce. You begin to see them the way God sees them — as lost sheep in need of a shepherd, as prodigals waiting to be welcomed home, as dead bones waiting for the breath of God.

Your heart softens toward them. Your patience with them increases. Your conversations with them become more genuine, more compassionate, more rooted in real love rather than a sense of religious duty. Prayer transforms the evangelist before it transforms the listener.

Spurgeon understood this. He taught his students that if you want to win souls, you must first love souls. And if you want to love souls, you must pray for them. Because prayer is the furnace where cold hearts are warmed and hard hearts are melted.

Let me read Proverbs 3:5-6 again, slowly:

"Trust in the Lord with all thine heart; and lean not unto thine own understanding. In all thy ways acknowledge him, and he shall direct thy paths."

"All thine heart." Not half of it. Not most of it. All of it. This kind of trust is radical. It's the kind of trust that says, "God, I have done my best to prepare. I have studied Your Word. I have thought about how to share it clearly. But I know — I know — that none of that will matter if You don't show up. So I'm leaning on You. Not on my understanding. On You."

"In all thy ways acknowledge him." All your ways. Not just in the pulpit. Not just in the Bible study. Not just in the formal evangelistic conversation. In all your ways. At the coffee shop. In the carpool. At the office. Acknowledge God. Invite Him into every interaction. Ask Him to use even the casual, everyday moments of your life as opportunities for His kingdom.

"And he shall direct thy paths." He will guide you to the right person at the right time with the right words. Not because you're clever enough to figure it out... but because you trusted Him enough to ask.

Spurgeon also taught something else in The Soul Winner that I think we need to hear today. He taught that prayer is not a substitute for action... and action is not a substitute for prayer. You need both. The Christian who prays all day but never opens their mouth to share the gospel is not obeying the Great Commission. But the Christian who is always talking about Jesus but never praying for the people they're talking to... is operating in their own strength. And it will show.

The balance is prayer and action. Knees and feet. The closet and the street. This is the rhythm of effective evangelism — and really, the rhythm of the whole Christian life.

Let me paint a picture for you. Imagine that you have a neighbor — let's call him David. David is not a believer. He's a good man by worldly standards — kind, hardworking, decent — but he has no interest in spiritual things. You've invited him to church a few times; he's politely declined. You've tried to bring up matters of faith; he's changed the subject.

Now... the lean-on-your-own-understanding approach would be to give up. "I've tried. He's not interested. I'll move on to someone more receptive." Or maybe you'd double down with a more aggressive approach — a louder argument, a more forceful presentation, a sense of urgency that borders on desperation.

But the trust-in-the-Lord approach looks different. It looks like praying for David every morning. By name. Specifically. "Lord, open David's eyes. Soften his heart. Bring circumstances into his life that make him hunger for something he can't find on his own. And use me — however You see fit — as part of that process."

And then you go about your day. You mow your lawn. You wave hello. You bring over a plate of cookies when his family goes through a hard time. You don't force anything. You don't manipulate. You simply love him, pray for him, and trust that God is at work even when you can't see it.

That's what it means to trust in the Lord with all your heart in the context of evangelism. It's a posture of dependence that frees you from the pressure of producing results... because results were never your job. Your job is faithfulness. God's job is fruit.

Spurgeon saw this principle vindicated again and again. He told stories of conversions that came after years — sometimes decades — of faithful prayer. He knew of cases where a mother prayed for a wayward son for thirty years before that son finally came to Christ. Thirty years. And Spurgeon's response to such stories was not, "What a waste of time." His response was, "What a faithful God."

Because God is faithful. Even when we can't see what He's doing. Even when the heavens seem silent. Even when the person we're praying for seems to be moving further away rather than closer. God is faithful. And He hears every prayer. Every one.

So here is my encouragement to you today, friend.

First... write down the names of three people you want to see come to Christ. Just three. Put that list somewhere you'll see it every day — on your bathroom mirror, in your Bible, on your phone. And begin to pray for those three people daily. Not casually. Earnestly. With faith. With persistence. With the kind of trust that says, "Lord, I believe You can save them, and I will not stop asking until You do."

Second... ask God to create opportunities. You don't have to manufacture them. You don't have to force conversations. Just ask God — "In all thy ways acknowledge him" — and then pay attention. He will open doors. He always does.

And third... release the results. This is the hardest part for most of us. We want to see the fruit. We want the conversion to happen on our timeline, through our efforts, in a way we can measure. But that's leaning on our own understanding. Trusting God means letting go of the outcome and holding onto the process — the process of prayer, of love, of faithfulness, of patient witness.

Spurgeon spent his entire ministry doing this. And the fruit was extraordinary. Not because Spurgeon was extraordinary — though in many ways he was — but because the God he trusted was faithful. Is faithful. Will always be faithful.

Let me close with a prayer. And I want this prayer to be the beginning of something for you — the beginning of a new commitment to pray for the lost by name.

Father, we trust You. Not ourselves. Not our words. Not our strategies. You. You are the one who opens blind eyes and raises dead souls to life. We bring before You now the names on our hearts — the people we love who do not yet know You. Lord, pursue them. Draw them. Break through every barrier of pride and indifference and fear. And use us, Lord — as we lean not on our own understanding but trust in You with all our hearts — use us as instruments of Your grace. Direct our paths to the right people, at the right time, with the right words. And may the fruit be entirely, completely, gloriously Yours. In Jesus' name. Amen.

Until tomorrow. Trust Him today.
