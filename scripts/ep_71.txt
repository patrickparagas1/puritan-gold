PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"Have Mercy Upon Me"
May 10, 2026

---

"Have mercy upon me, O God, according to thy lovingkindness: according unto the multitude of thy tender mercies blot out my transgressions. Wash me throughly from mine iniquity, and cleanse me from my sin. For I acknowledge my transgressions: and my sin is ever before me. Against thee, thee only, have I sinned, and done this evil in thy sight: that thou mightest be justified when thou speakest, and be clear when thou judgest. Behold, I was shapen in iniquity; and in sin did my mother conceive me." -- Psalm 51:1-5

We return today to the great penitential psalm of David, and we fix our attention upon the opening movement of this extraordinary prayer, verses one through nine, where the king of Israel lays bare his soul before the King of heaven. If the fifty-first psalm is the Everest of repentance, then these opening verses are the approach to the summit, where the air grows thin, and every pretension is stripped away, and the soul stands naked and trembling before the holiness of God.

The very first word of David's prayer tells us everything about the ground upon which he stands. "Have mercy." Not "reward me," not "remember my services," not "consider my good intentions," but "have mercy." Mercy is the plea of the guilty. It is the only plea that a sinner can make before a holy God and retain his honesty. For the moment a man asks for mercy, he confesses that he deserves judgment. He acknowledges that if God were to deal with him according to strict justice, he would be undone. And he throws himself upon that quality in God which alone can bridge the infinite chasm between a holy Creator and a sinful creature.

"According to thy lovingkindness: according unto the multitude of thy tender mercies." David does not ask God to measure out His mercy in small, cautious portions. He asks God to give mercy according to the full measure of His own lovingkindness. It is as though the psalmist says, "Lord, I know that my sin is great. I know that my transgression is enormous. But I also know that Thy mercy is greater. And so I ask Thee to measure Thy pardon not by the smallness of my deserving, but by the greatness of Thine own compassion." This is the boldness of faith in the midst of the deepest humiliation. The penitent sinner dares to ask for much, because he knows that the God to whom he prays is rich in mercy.

"Blot out my transgressions." The image here is of a record, a written account of debt, that must be cancelled. In the ancient world, debts were written upon scrolls, and when the debt was paid, the writing was blotted out, erased, made as though it had never existed. David pleads with God to do this with his sins, to cancel the record, to wipe the slate clean, to remove every trace of his transgression from the books of heaven.

"Wash me throughly from mine iniquity, and cleanse me from my sin." Now the image shifts from a ledger to a laundry. David feels the stain of his sin as a deep, ingrained defilement that has penetrated to the very fibers of his being. The word "throughly" in the original Hebrew refers to the process by which a washerwoman would tread upon a garment in water, pounding it again and again to drive out every trace of filth. David is asking God to submit him to whatever process is necessary, however painful, however prolonged, to make him clean.

"For I acknowledge my transgressions: and my sin is ever before me." Here is the honest confession of a man who has stopped running from the truth. For nearly a year, David had tried to hide his sin. He had covered it over with silence, with deceit, even with murder. And for that entire year, the hand of God had been heavy upon him. His vitality had dried up like the heat of summer. His joy had departed. His communion with God had been broken. But now, at last, the truth comes pouring out. "I acknowledge my transgressions." The word "acknowledge" in Hebrew carries the sense of intimate, personal knowledge. David is not merely admitting to a list of offenses. He is acknowledging the full weight and horror of what he has done.

"And my sin is ever before me." This is one of the marks of true conviction. The unconvicted sinner can commit the most atrocious acts and then go about his business as though nothing had happened. But the convicted sinner finds that his sin follows him everywhere. It is before him when he rises in the morning. It is before him when he lies down at night. It meets him in the mirror. It haunts him in his sleep. It poisons every pleasure and embitters every joy. He cannot escape it, because the Holy Spirit will not allow him to escape it, until he has dealt with it at the throne of grace.

"Behold, I was shapen in iniquity; and in sin did my mother conceive me." Here David traces the stream of his corruption to its headwaters. His sin against Bathsheba was not an isolated incident, a single failure in an otherwise spotless life. It was the fruit of a nature that had been sinful from the very moment of his conception. This is the doctrine of original sin, which the Reformed divines have always recognized as one of the most sobering and most necessary truths of Scripture. We are not sinners because we sin; we sin because we are sinners. The corruption runs all the way to the root. The disease is not on the surface but in the blood.

Ezekiel Hopkins, that learned Puritan bishop, wrote that the doctrine of original sin is the key that unlocks the mystery of human wickedness. If we do not understand that every son and daughter of Adam enters this world with a nature already inclined toward evil, we shall never understand why the world is the way it is, why men do the things they do, why even the best of men, like David, are capable of the worst of sins. But this doctrine, rightly understood, does not drive us to despair. It drives us to Christ. For if the disease is in the root, then the cure must also reach the root. And that is precisely what the blood of Christ does. It does not merely cleanse the symptoms. It purifies the source. It makes the tree good, and then the fruit shall be good also.

"Purge me with hyssop, and I shall be clean: wash me, and I shall be whiter than snow." Hyssop was the plant used to sprinkle the blood of the Passover lamb upon the doorposts of the Israelites in Egypt. David is reaching back to that great type of redemption and pleading for the application of atoning blood to his guilty conscience. And the result, he declares with the confidence of faith, shall be a cleanness that exceeds the whiteness of snow. What a promise! What a Saviour! Amen.
