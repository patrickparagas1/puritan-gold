PURITAN GOLD -- Family Devotional, Day 24
"Discipline That Heals"
Richard Baxter — The Christian Directory

---

Good evening, dear family. Welcome back to Puritan Gold. Tonight Richard Baxter tackles one of the most difficult aspects of parenting — discipline. How do we correct our children in a way that heals rather than harms, that builds rather than breaks? Let us seek the Lord's wisdom together.

Let us pray. Lord, we need Your wisdom tonight on a subject that challenges every parent. Help us to understand the purpose of correction, the spirit in which it should be given, and the fruit it is meant to bear. Make us instruments of Your redeeming love in our children's lives. In Christ's name, Amen.

Our Scripture reading this evening comes from Hebrews 12:5-11. Let us hear the Word of the Lord:

"And ye have forgotten the exhortation which speaketh unto you as unto children, My son, despise not thou the chastening of the Lord, nor faint when thou art rebuked of him: For whom the Lord loveth he chasteneth, and scourgeth every son whom he receiveth. If ye endure chastening, God dealeth with you as with sons; for what son is he whom the father chasteneth not? Furthermore we have had fathers of our flesh which corrected us, and we gave them reverence: shall we not much rather be in subjection unto the Father of spirits, and live? For they verily for a few days chastened us after their own pleasure; but he for our profit, that we might be partakers of his holiness. Now no chastening for the present seemeth to be joyous, but grievous: nevertheless afterward it yieldeth the peaceable fruit of righteousness unto them which are exercised thereby."

That is Hebrews 12:5-11. Let those words settle in your heart for a moment before we continue.

--- Teaching ---

The writer to the Hebrews gives us the theology of discipline in a single phrase: "Whom the Lord loveth he chasteneth." Discipline is an expression of love, not a contradiction of it. A parent who never corrects a child does not love more — he loves less. For discipline, rightly administered, is not cruelty but care. It is the loving intervention of a parent who sees danger in a child's path and acts to redirect, to protect, and to train.

Richard Baxter builds on this theology with pastoral precision. In "A Christian Directory," he offers what may be the most balanced and practical guide to parental discipline in all of Puritan literature. His overarching principle is that discipline should always aim at the child's good — never at the parent's convenience, never at the parent's emotional release, and never at the child's humiliation.

Baxter's first rule of discipline is that it should flow from love, not anger. A parent who disciplines in anger is serving himself, not his child. He is venting his frustration rather than addressing the child's need. Baxter counsels parents: when you feel anger rising, stop. Wait. Pray. Calm yourself before you speak or act. Discipline administered in anger almost always goes too far, says too much, and wounds unnecessarily. The goal is correction, not punishment for its own sake.

Baxter's second rule is that discipline should be proportionate. The correction should fit the offense. Not every infraction requires the same response. A minor act of carelessness calls for a gentle reminder. A repeated act of willful disobedience calls for a firmer response. And a serious moral failing calls for a serious conversation accompanied by appropriate consequences. Parents who treat every offense with the same severity will find that their children become either terrified or indifferent — and neither response serves the purposes of godly discipline.

Baxter's third rule is that discipline should be accompanied by instruction. The child must understand not only that he has done wrong but why it is wrong. He must understand not only the consequence but the principle behind it. Simply punishing a child without explanation produces external compliance without internal transformation. But when correction is paired with clear, patient, biblical instruction, the child's conscience is engaged, and true moral growth becomes possible.

Baxter's fourth rule is that discipline should be followed by reconciliation. After the correction is given and received, the parent should restore the child to full fellowship — with warmth, with affection, and with assurance of continued love. A child who is corrected but not reconciled may begin to believe that love is conditional, that acceptance depends on performance, and that one failure can permanently separate him from his parents' affection. The gospel teaches us otherwise. God corrects us and then gathers us back into His arms. Parents should do the same.

The writer to the Hebrews gives us the ultimate purpose of discipline: "that we might be partakers of his holiness." Discipline is not about producing well-behaved children — though good behavior is a blessing. It is about producing holy children — children whose hearts are oriented toward God, who love righteousness and hate sin, who have learned through the experience of correction that the path of obedience leads to life and the path of disobedience leads to ruin. The "peaceable fruit of righteousness" is the harvest that grows from the soil of loving, faithful, consistent discipline.

Baxter closes with a word to children: do not despise the correction of your parents. They discipline you because they love you. They correct you because they see dangers you cannot yet see. Receive their correction with humility, knowing that "afterward it yieldeth the peaceable fruit of righteousness."

--- Discussion Questions ---

Let us pause and talk as a family. Here are tonight's discussion questions. Everyone gets a turn to share.

Question 1: Hebrews says, "Whom the Lord loveth he chasteneth." How does knowing that discipline comes from love change the way we receive it?

Question 2: Baxter says discipline should always be followed by reconciliation. Why is restoring the relationship after correction so important?

Question 3: Children: can you think of a time when correction from your parents helped you grow? Parents: can you think of a time when God's correction helped you?

Take a few minutes to talk about these questions together. Let everyone share, and listen to one another with patience and love.

--- Memory Verse ---

Now, here is our memory verse for today. Let us say it together:

"Now no chastening for the present seemeth to be joyous, but grievous: nevertheless afterward it yieldeth the peaceable fruit of righteousness unto them which are exercised thereby." -- Hebrews 12:11

Try to say it out loud three times right now. Then, before bed tonight, see if you can say it from memory.

--- Closing ---

Let us pray together. Lord, we thank You that You love us enough to correct us. Help us as parents to discipline with love, patience, and wisdom. Help us as children to receive correction with humility and trust. And after every correction, restore us to one another with the warmth and tenderness that reflects Your own unfailing love. In Jesus' name, Amen.

Sleep well tonight, knowing that God watches over your family.
