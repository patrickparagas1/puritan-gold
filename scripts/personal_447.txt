PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Guided Puritan Reading, Day 16
"The Existence of God"
Stephen Charnock — The Existence and Attributes of God, Introduction


Good evening. One of the great losses of the modern church is the loss of slow reading. The Puritans read deeply, digesting every paragraph, pausing to pray, pausing to think. Tonight, we recover that practice as we walk through a treasured text together.

Tonight we draw from the work of Stephen Charnock, specifically from "The Existence and Attributes of God." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Psalm 14:1:

"The fool hath said in his heart, There is no God. They are corrupt, they have done abominable works, there is none that doeth good."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


We turn now to one of the most monumental works in all of theological literature: Stephen Charnock's 'The Existence and Attributes of God.' Charnock, a Nonconformist minister who died in 1680, left behind a work of such intellectual depth and devotional warmth that it has been rightly called the greatest treatment of the divine attributes in the English language. For the remaining reading days of this month, we will journey through this masterpiece, allowing Charnock to enlarge our conception of God.

Charnock begins where all theology must begin: with the existence of God. Before we can speak of what God is like, we must establish that God is. The psalmist declares that 'the fool hath said in his heart, There is no God.' Charnock notes that the denial of God's existence is not an intellectual conclusion but a moral corruption. The fool says this 'in his heart,' not merely in his mind. Atheism, Charnock argues, is ultimately a disease of the will, not the intellect. Men deny God not because the evidence is insufficient but because the implications are unwelcome.

Charnock marshals multiple lines of evidence for God's existence. First, the evidence of creation. The universe displays a design so intricate, a beauty so overwhelming, and an order so precise that it is irrational to attribute it to blind chance. Charnock writes that every creature, from the smallest insect to the grandest constellation, bears the signature of its Maker. The man who looks at creation and sees no Creator is like a man who reads a book and denies the existence of an author.

Second, Charnock points to the evidence of conscience. Every human being possesses an innate awareness of right and wrong, a moral law written on the heart. This moral law points beyond itself to a Moral Lawgiver. Charnock writes that conscience is God's voice within the soul — not infallible in its specific judgments, but unmistakable in its testimony that there is a God to whom we are accountable.

Third, Charnock appeals to the universal consent of mankind. Every culture, every civilization, every tribe throughout human history has possessed some awareness of the divine. This universality, Charnock argues, cannot be explained by cultural transmission alone; it points to an innate knowledge of God planted in the human soul by God Himself. The apostle Paul affirms this in Romans 1: 'That which may be known of God is manifest in them; for God hath shewed it unto them' (Romans 1:19).

But Charnock's purpose is not merely to prove that God exists. Any philosopher could attempt that. Charnock wants to show that the existence of God has profound implications for every aspect of human life. If God exists, then we are not autonomous beings free to define our own purpose and morality. We are creatures, dependent on our Creator for every breath, accountable to Him for every thought and deed. If God exists, then the ultimate questions of life — Why am I here? What is my purpose? What happens when I die? — have answers that are not constructed by human opinion but revealed by divine authority.

Charnock also draws out the devotional implications of God's existence. To know that God exists is not merely to hold an intellectual conviction; it is to stand in the presence of infinite majesty. Charnock writes that the right response to God's existence is not merely belief but worship — a profound, reverent, joyful acknowledgment that we exist because He exists, that we are sustained by His power, and that our lives find their meaning in relation to Him.

Charnock closes his introduction with a searching question: do you believe in God merely as a proposition, or do you live as though God is real? The practical atheist is not the man who denies God with his lips but the man who ignores God with his life. He may affirm the existence of God in the creed but deny it in the calendar, the budget, and the daily choices of the heart. True belief in God transforms everything: how we spend our time, how we treat our families, how we pursue our vocations, and how we face our deaths.

Charnock also warns against what he calls 'speculative atheism' versus 'practical atheism.' Speculative atheism is the explicit denial of God's existence — relatively rare in Charnock's day, though increasingly common in ours. Practical atheism is far more widespread and far more dangerous because it is so easily concealed. The practical atheist attends church, recites the creed, and would be offended if you called him an unbeliever. But his daily life is organized as though God did not exist. His decisions are made without reference to divine authority. His time is spent without consciousness of divine presence. His affections are set on earthly things without regard for eternal realities. Charnock writes that practical atheism is the great sin of the professing church, and the remedy is not merely correct theology but a living, felt, operational awareness of the God who is.

As we begin this journey with Charnock, let us pray that our knowledge of God will move from the mind to the heart, from theory to practice, from intellectual assent to life-shaping, worship-producing, holiness-empowering reality.


Before we close, take a few moments with these reflection questions.

Question 1: Is my belief in God's existence a living reality that shapes my daily decisions, or has it become a mere intellectual assumption that I rarely consider?

Question 2: In what areas of my life am I functionally living as a 'practical atheist' — affirming God's existence but ignoring His authority?

Question 3: How does the evidence of creation and conscience strengthen my confidence in the reality of God?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


O God, who art the source and ground of all existence, I worship You as the One who truly is. Forgive me for the times I have lived as though You were not real — making decisions without consulting You, spending my days without acknowledging You, treating Your existence as a proposition rather than a living reality. Open my eyes to see Your fingerprints in creation, Your voice in my conscience, and Your authority over every area of my life. In the name of Christ, Amen.

Until next time. May the Lord deepen your faith with every passing day.
