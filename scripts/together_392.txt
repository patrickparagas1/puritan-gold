PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 31
"A Good Wife Sent from the Lord — Thomas Gataker"
Puritan Marriage Wisdom — Thomas Gataker's Marriage Sermons


Welcome, beloved couple. We close this month in the Song of Solomon and Puritan marriage wisdom with Thomas Gataker (1574-1654), whose two wedding sermons — A Good Wife God's Gift and Marriage Duties — are among the most tender, practical, and beautiful pieces of Puritan writing on the subject of married love.

Let us read Proverbs 18:22 and 19:14 (KJV):

"Whoso findeth a wife findeth a good thing, and obtaineth favour of the Lord." "House and riches are the inheritance of fathers: and a prudent wife is from the Lord."


Thomas Gataker was a member of the Westminster Assembly and one of the most respected Puritan divines of his generation. But it is his marriage sermons that have earned him a permanent place in the treasury of Christian wisdom on the household. Gataker preached with a warmth, a tenderness, and a practical insight that make his words feel remarkably contemporary.

Gataker began with a fundamental principle: marriage is not merely a human arrangement. It is a divine gift. "A prudent wife is from the Lord" — and by extension, a faithful husband is from the Lord. Your spouse is not an accident, not a mistake, and not merely the product of your own choosing. God's providence brought you together. He knew what you needed, and He provided it — even if the provision sometimes comes wrapped in unexpected packaging.

From this principle of divine gift, Gataker drew several practical applications.

First, the duty of gratitude. If your spouse is a gift from God, then ingratitude toward your spouse is ingratitude toward God. Gataker wrote that the happiest marriages are those where both partners wake each morning with a fundamental posture of thankfulness: "God gave me this person. I did not deserve them. They are a grace to me." This posture of gratitude transforms the atmosphere of a home. When you are grateful, you are less likely to criticize, less likely to compare, less likely to take for granted.

Second, the duty of cherishing. Gataker distinguished between loving and cherishing. To love is to act for the good of the other. To cherish is to hold the other person as precious, as a treasure to be guarded and protected and delighted in. Gataker wrote that many couples love each other dutifully but fail to cherish each other tenderly. The husband provides but does not delight. The wife serves but does not celebrate. The marriage functions but does not flourish. Cherishing adds the warmth that love alone cannot supply.

Third, the duty of companionship. Gataker's most famous phrase about marriage is this: marriage is "the sweetest society of life." Not merely a society — an arrangement of shared responsibilities — but the sweetest society. He argued that God designed marriage to be the most enjoyable, most satisfying, most deeply pleasurable of all human relationships. When marriage becomes drudgery, something has gone wrong — not with the institution but with the practice. Gataker urged couples to recover the sweetness by investing in the friendship at the core of their marriage.

He offered remarkably specific counsel on companionship. Eat together — not just in the same room but in actual conversation. Walk together — not just to the car but for the pleasure of each other's company. Laugh together — for shared laughter is the music of a happy marriage. Pray together — for nothing binds two souls more tightly than shared communion with God. Read together — for a shared intellectual life enriches every other dimension of the relationship.

Fourth, the duty of kindness in speech. Gataker devoted significant attention to the way husbands and wives speak to each other. He wrote that "the tongue is the rudder of the marriage — a small member that steers the whole ship." Words of kindness turn the ship toward peace. Words of contempt turn it toward destruction. He urged couples to eliminate four specific toxins from their speech: contempt — speaking as if your spouse is beneath you; criticism — attacking your spouse's character rather than addressing a specific behavior; defensiveness — refusing to accept any responsibility; and stonewalling — withdrawing from conversation entirely. These four patterns, Gataker argued, are the little foxes that spoil the vine.

Fifth, the duty of perseverance. Gataker acknowledged that marriage is long. The years stretch out, and the initial excitement inevitably fades into something quieter. But quieter is not lesser. Gataker wrote that "the love of many years has a depth that the love of many months cannot fathom." The couple who perseveres through decades of shared life — who weathers storms, celebrates joys, grieves losses, and grows old together — possesses a treasure that no amount of money can buy. The love of autumn has its own beauty, different from the love of spring but no less glorious.

As we close this month, let Gataker's vision of marriage rest upon you: a divine gift, cherished with gratitude, enjoyed with sweetness, seasoned with kindness, and sustained with perseverance. This is the vision of the Song of Solomon set to the key of Puritan wisdom. This is what your marriage can be.


Turn to each other and consider these questions.

Question 1: Gataker says your spouse is a gift from God. Take a moment right now to thank God — out loud, in each other's hearing — for the gift of your spouse. Be specific.

Question 2: Is there more cherishing or more functioning in your marriage right now? What would it look like to add more tender delight to your daily life together?

Question 3: As this month closes, what is the single most important thing you want to carry forward from the Song of Solomon and the Puritan marriage writers? Name it and commit to it.


Tonight's prayer focus: Pray a prayer of gratitude for your marriage — for the Song of Solomon's vision of love, for the Puritan wisdom that has guided you, and for the spouse who sits beside you. Ask God to write the lessons of this month upon your hearts and to make your marriage, in Gataker's words, "the sweetest society of life."


Beloved couple, you have traveled through thirty-one days of the Song of Solomon and the wisdom of the Puritans. You have heard the bridegroom's praise and the bride's longing. You have endured the dark night and celebrated the dawn of reconciliation. You have heard God Himself bless your love. You have sat at the feet of Baxter, Gouge, and Gataker. Now go and live what you have heard.

Set each other as a seal upon your hearts. For love is strong as death, and the coals thereof are coals of fire — the very flame of the Lord. Many waters cannot quench it. Nothing in this world or the next can quench it. It is yours. Guard it. Feed it. Let it blaze.

May the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, the love of God, and the communion of the Holy Spirit rest upon your marriage now and forevermore. Amen. Goodnight.
