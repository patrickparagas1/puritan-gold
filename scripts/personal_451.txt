PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Pastoral Encouragement, Day 20
"Walking with God in Spiritual Darkness"
Samuel Rutherford — Pastoral Counsel for Dark Seasons


Welcome, dear listener. The Puritans were master pastors. They knew how to comfort the afflicted and afflict the comfortable. Tonight, in that tradition, we bring a word of pastoral encouragement and challenge.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Isaiah 50:10:

"Who is among you that feareth the LORD, that obeyeth the voice of his servant, that walketh in darkness, and hath no light? let him trust in the name of the LORD, and stay upon his God."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


Isaiah asks a question that every serious Christian will eventually need to answer: What do you do when you fear the Lord, obey His voice, and yet walk in darkness with no light? This is not the darkness of rebellion but the darkness of spiritual desolation — the experience of the godly soul who cannot feel God's presence, cannot sense His favor, and cannot see His purpose. It is the experience that the mystics call 'the dark night of the soul' and that the Puritans knew intimately.

Samuel Rutherford, the Scottish Presbyterian minister and theologian, is the Puritan who speaks most eloquently to this experience. Rutherford's life was marked by extraordinary suffering: he lost his first wife and several children, was exiled from his beloved congregation at Anwoth, and spent his final years under political persecution. Yet his letters, written during the darkest periods of his life, radiate with a love for Christ that is almost unbearable in its intensity.

Rutherford writes that spiritual darkness is not evidence of God's displeasure but of His deeper purposes. God sometimes withdraws the sense of His presence — not His actual presence, which is guaranteed by covenant — in order to accomplish things in the soul that cannot be accomplished in the light. In the light, we tend to rest in our experiences of God — our feelings of closeness, our seasons of fruitfulness, our spiritual highs. In the darkness, we are forced to rest in God Himself, stripped of every experience and left with nothing but the bare promise of His Word.

Rutherford offers several counsels for the believer walking in spiritual darkness. First, do not interpret the darkness as punishment. This is the instinct of the natural heart: if I cannot feel God, He must be angry with me. But Rutherford insists that some of God's most beloved children have walked the longest in darkness. The darkness is not a mark of divine displeasure but of divine trust: God entrusts the darkness only to those whom He knows can bear it.

Second, continue in the disciplines of grace even when they yield no felt benefit. Pray when prayer feels empty. Read Scripture when it feels like dead words on a page. Worship when worship feels hollow. These duties are not dependent upon our feelings for their efficacy. Rutherford writes that the man who prays without feeling is doing something greater than the man who prays with feeling, for he is praying by faith alone — and faith is more pleasing to God than feeling.

Third, Rutherford counsels patience. The darkness will not last forever. The sun that is hidden behind the clouds has not been extinguished. Weeping may endure for a night, but joy comes in the morning. Rutherford's own experience testified to this: his darkest seasons were invariably followed by seasons of extraordinary closeness with Christ, and he came to regard the darkness as the necessary prelude to a deeper light.

Fourth, Rutherford encourages the sufferer to remember past mercies. When you cannot see God's hand in the present, remember the times when His hand was clearly visible. Build memorial stones from past deliverances and visit them when the night is long. The God who met you then has not abandoned you now.

Rutherford's most famous expression captures the essence of his counsel. He writes of Christ with the language of a lover who cannot bear separation. Even in his darkest moments, Rutherford's pen burns with desire for the One who seems distant but whom he knows is near. His letters are a testimony to the possibility of loving God passionately even when you cannot feel Him at all.

Patrick, if you find yourself in a season of spiritual darkness, take heart. You are not alone. The greatest saints have walked this path before you. Continue in your duties. Trust the promises. Wait patiently. And know that the darkness itself is doing a work in your soul that the light could never accomplish.


Reflection is where truth becomes personal. Consider these questions carefully.

Question 1: Have I experienced seasons of spiritual darkness — times when God seemed distant despite my obedience — and how did I respond?

Question 2: Am I willing to continue in the spiritual disciplines even when they produce no felt benefit, trusting that God is at work beneath my awareness?

Question 3: Do I tend to interpret spiritual dryness as punishment, and how does Rutherford's perspective challenge that tendency?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Lord, I do not always feel Your presence, and there are days when the darkness is thick and my faith is thin. But I trust in Your name and stay upon You, my God. Even when I cannot see Your hand, help me to trust Your heart. Keep me faithful in the darkness, and in Your good time, bring the morning. I wait for You, O Lord, more than they that watch for the morning. Amen.

May the Lord guard your mind and your heart tonight. Until we meet again.
