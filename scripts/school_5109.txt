"Of Justification and Adoption"
The Westminster Standards -- Lesson 9

SEGMENT 1: MORNING DEVOTION
----------------------------------------

Good morning, students. Welcome to the Puritan Academy. Let us begin this day as the Puritans did, by acknowledging God as the source of all knowledge and wisdom. Before we open our textbooks, let us open our hearts.

Almighty and most gracious Father, who art rich in mercy and plenteous in redemption, we come before Thee this morning with hearts that need Thy light. We confess that we are by nature sinners, deserving only Thy wrath and condemnation. Yet Thou hast been pleased, in Thine infinite grace, to justify the ungodly and to adopt strangers into Thy family. As we study these glorious doctrines today, open our minds to comprehend, our hearts to receive, and our wills to embrace the riches of Thy grace in Christ Jesus. Let not these truths remain mere words on a page but let them sink deep into our souls and transform the way we live. Through Jesus Christ our Lord, who is our righteousness and our elder Brother. Amen.

Let us meditate on a portion of Scripture before we begin our main lesson.

Our Scripture for today is Romans 8:1, 15-17 and Galatians 4:4-7:

"There is therefore now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus, who walk not after the flesh, but after the Spirit. For ye have not received the spirit of bondage again to fear; but ye have received the Spirit of adoption, whereby we cry, Abba, Father. The Spirit itself beareth witness with our spirit, that we are the children of God: And if children, then heirs; heirs of God, and joint-heirs with Christ; if so be that we suffer with him, that we may be also glorified together. But when the fulness of the time was come, God sent forth his Son, made of a woman, made under the law, To redeem them that were under the law, that we might receive the adoption of sons. And because ye are sons, God hath sent forth the Spirit of his Son into your hearts, crying, Abba, Father. Wherefore thou art no more a servant, but a son; and if a son, then an heir of God through Christ."

Pause and let these words settle upon your heart. Consider the staggering movement of these texts — from "no condemnation" to "heirs of God." The sinner who deserved eternal punishment now stands without a single charge against him and, more than that, has been received into the very family of the living God. He is no longer a servant trembling before a master but a son crying out to a Father. He is no longer a condemned criminal awaiting sentence but an heir awaiting an inheritance that is incorruptible, undefiled, and that fadeth not away. The Puritan Thomas Goodwin (born 1600 in Rollesby, Norfolk, England) once remarked that there is a vast distance between a pardoned rebel and a beloved child — and that in Christ, God has not merely pardoned us but made us His own children. This is grace upon grace, mercy upon mercy, love beyond all measuring.

As we prepare for today's lesson, remember that everything we study flows from this one fountain: the Word of God. Our lesson today is part of The Westminster Standards, and it is titled: Of Justification and Adoption. Let us proceed with minds engaged and hearts open.

SEGMENT 2: MAIN LESSON PART 1 -- BIBLICAL FOUNDATION
----------------------------------------

Now let us turn to our main lesson for today.

The first part of our lesson establishes the biblical foundation for today's topic. We will examine what God's Word teaches us about the themes of justification and adoption as presented in the Westminster Standards.

Today we study two of the most glorious doctrines in the entire Christian faith — justification and adoption. These two doctrines describe what God does for the sinner who comes to Christ in faith. In justification, God declares the sinner righteous — pardoning all his sins and crediting to his account the perfect righteousness of Christ. In adoption, God receives the sinner into His family — making him a child of God, an heir of heaven, and a brother of Jesus Christ. Together, these doctrines reveal a grace so vast, so undeserved, and so transforming that the Puritans never tired of preaching, writing, and meditating upon them. The doctrine of justification was the central issue of the Protestant Reformation — the article upon which the church stands or falls, as Martin Luther declared. The doctrine of adoption was, for the Puritans, the crowning jewel of the application of redemption — the doctrine that reveals not only God's justice but His fatherly love.

Before we examine justification and adoption individually, we need to understand where they fit within the broader framework of the application of redemption. Christ accomplished salvation on the cross. But the benefits of His work must be applied to individual sinners by the Holy Spirit. The Shorter Catechism, Question 29, asks: "How are we made partakers of the redemption purchased by Christ?" and answers: "We are made partakers of the redemption purchased by Christ, by the effectual application of it to us by his Holy Spirit."

The application of redemption follows a divinely ordered sequence that the Puritans called the "order of salvation" or ordo salutis. This sequence includes effectual calling, regeneration, faith and repentance, justification, adoption, sanctification, and glorification. Each step flows from the preceding one and leads to the next, and the entire process is the work of the triune God — the Father who planned it, the Son who purchased it, and the Spirit who applies it.

Justification and adoption come at a particular point in this sequence. They are the immediate consequences of faith — the blessings that God bestows upon the sinner at the moment he believes in Christ. Justification changes the sinner's legal status before God — from condemned to accepted. Adoption changes the sinner's relational status before God — from stranger to child. Both occur simultaneously, both are acts of God's free grace, and both are received by faith alone.

Before a sinner can be justified or adopted, he must first be brought to faith by the sovereign work of the Holy Spirit. This is the doctrine of effectual calling. The Shorter Catechism, Question 31, asks: "What is effectual calling?" and answers: "Effectual calling is the work of God's Spirit, whereby, convincing us of our sin and misery, enlightening our minds in the knowledge of Christ, and renewing our wills, he doth persuade and enable us to embrace Jesus Christ, freely offered to us in the gospel."

Notice the comprehensive nature of this work. The Spirit convinces us of our sin — showing us that we are guilty, corrupt, and helpless before God. The Spirit enlightens our minds — opening our eyes to see the beauty, sufficiency, and willingness of Christ as Savior. The Spirit renews our wills — changing the fundamental disposition of our hearts from enmity against God to love for God. And the Spirit persuades and enables us to embrace Christ — making it not merely possible but actual that we come to Christ in faith.

This calling is "effectual" because it always accomplishes its intended purpose. It is not a mere invitation that human beings are free to accept or reject at their own pleasure. It is a sovereign, powerful, life-giving work of the Spirit that actually brings the sinner from death to life, from darkness to light, and from rebellion to faith. "For whom he did predestinate, them he also called: and whom he called, them he also justified: and whom he justified, them he also glorified" (Romans 8:30, KJV). The chain is unbreakable — every person whom God calls will be justified, and every person whom God justifies will be glorified.

The Puritan Thomas Goodwin taught that effectual calling is the moment when the eternal decree of election becomes a personal, experiential reality in the life of the individual sinner. In eternity, God chose His people. In time, Christ redeemed them. And in the moment of effectual calling, the Spirit brings them into actual possession of the blessings that the Father planned and the Son purchased.

Through effectual calling, the sinner is brought to faith and repentance. The Shorter Catechism, Question 86, teaches that "faith in Jesus Christ is a saving grace, whereby we receive and rest upon him alone for salvation, as he is offered to us in the gospel." Faith is not a meritorious work — it is a receiving grace. It is the empty hand that takes the gift. It is the eye that looks to the Savior. It is the foot that steps onto the solid rock. And it is through this faith that the sinner receives the great blessings of justification and adoption.

The biblical foundation for justification is extraordinarily rich. The apostle Paul develops the doctrine with magnificent clarity in the book of Romans. In Romans 3:21-26, Paul declares: "But now the righteousness of God without the law is manifested, being witnessed by the law and the prophets; Even the righteousness of God which is by faith of Jesus Christ unto all and upon all them that believe: for there is no difference: For all have sinned, and come short of the glory of God; Being justified freely by his grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus: Whom God hath set forth to be a propitiation through faith in his blood, to declare his righteousness for the remission of sins that are past, through the forbearance of God; To declare, I say, at this time his righteousness: that he might be just, and the justifier of him which believeth in Jesus" (KJV). Notice the phrase "that he might be just, and the justifier" — God does not justify sinners by ignoring His justice but by satisfying it. The cross of Christ is the place where God's justice and God's mercy meet — where the full penalty for sin is paid and the full pardon for the sinner is granted.

In Romans 4, Paul uses Abraham as the supreme Old Testament example of justification by faith: "For what saith the scripture? Abraham believed God, and it was counted unto him for righteousness. Now to him that worketh is the reward not reckoned of grace, but of debt. But to him that worketh not, but believeth on him that justifieth the ungodly, his faith is counted for righteousness" (Romans 4:3-5, KJV). Abraham was not justified because of his works — he was justified because he believed God's promise, and God credited that faith as righteousness. The word "counted" or "reckoned" is the language of imputation — it is an accounting term, meaning to credit to someone's account.

Paul drives the point home with the dramatic contrast between Adam and Christ in Romans 5:18-19: "Therefore as by the offence of one judgment came upon all men to condemnation; even so by the righteousness of one the free gift came upon all men unto justification of life. For as by one man's disobedience many were made sinners, so by the obedience of one shall many be made righteous" (KJV). In Adam, sin and condemnation came to all. In Christ, righteousness and justification come to all who believe. This is the heart of the gospel.

SEGMENT 3: MAIN LESSON PART 2 -- PURITAN TEACHING
----------------------------------------

Now we turn to the Puritan perspective. The Westminster divines and the great Puritan theologians wrote extensively on justification and adoption. The Puritans were not merely theologians in ivory towers. They were pastors, teachers, and parents who applied God's truth to every dimension of life.

The Westminster Confession addresses justification in Chapter 11 — one of the most important chapters in the entire document. Section 1 states: "Those whom God effectually calleth he also freely justifieth; not by infusing righteousness into them, but by pardoning their sins, and by accounting and accepting their persons as righteous; not for anything wrought in them, or done by them, but for Christ's sake alone; not by imputing faith itself, the act of believing, or any other evangelical obedience, to them as their righteousness; but by imputing the obedience and satisfaction of Christ unto them, they receiving and resting on him and his righteousness by faith; which faith they have not of themselves; it is the gift of God."

This single paragraph is one of the most carefully crafted and theologically precise statements in the history of Christian theology. It defines justification with a series of affirmations and denials that guard the doctrine against every distortion and error. The Shorter Catechism, Question 33, provides a more concise definition: "Justification is an act of God's free grace, wherein he pardoneth all our sins, and accepteth us as righteous in his sight, only for the righteousness of Christ imputed to us, and received by faith alone."

Justification is, first of all, a legal or forensic act. It is a declaration — not a transformation. God declares the sinner righteous; He does not make the sinner righteous. This distinction is fundamental. The Roman Catholic Church taught — and still teaches — that justification involves the infusion of righteousness into the soul of the believer, making him progressively more righteous over time. The Reformers and the Westminster divines rejected this view emphatically. Justification is not about what God does in us but about what God declares about us. It is a verdict, not a process. It is a change of legal status, not a change of moral character.

The Confession explicitly denies that justification occurs "by infusing righteousness into them." The righteousness by which we are justified is not our own internal righteousness — however real that may become through sanctification. It is the external, alien righteousness of Christ, which is imputed (credited, reckoned) to us through faith. When God justifies the sinner, He does not look at the sinner's own moral condition — which is corrupt and sinful — but at the perfect righteousness of Christ, which has been credited to the sinner's account.

This is the Protestant doctrine of imputation — the teaching that Christ's righteousness is reckoned to the believer's account by a divine act of grace. Just as our sin was imputed to Christ on the cross — He bore the penalty for sins He never committed — so His righteousness is imputed to us in justification — we receive the credit for an obedience we never performed. This is the "great exchange" of the gospel: our sin for His righteousness, our guilt for His innocence, our condemnation for His acquittal. The prophet Isaiah foresaw this exchange: "All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned every one to his own way; and the LORD hath laid on him the iniquity of us all" (Isaiah 53:6, KJV). And again: "He was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed" (Isaiah 53:5, KJV).

Justification includes two great benefits: the pardon of sin and the imputation of righteousness. The pardon of sin means that God forgives all our transgressions — past, present, and future. The guilt of every sin we have ever committed or will ever commit is removed, canceled, and blotted out. "As far as the east is from the west, so far hath he removed our transgressions from us" (Psalm 103:12, KJV). "I, even I, am he that blotteth out thy transgressions for mine own sake, and will not remember thy sins" (Isaiah 43:25, KJV). This pardon is not partial but complete. It does not cover some sins while leaving others unpardoned. It covers all sins — every thought, every word, every deed, every omission — and it covers them forever.

The imputation of righteousness means that God credits to our account the perfect obedience of Christ. This is not merely a negative benefit (the removal of guilt) but a positive one (the bestowal of righteousness). We are not merely declared "not guilty" — we are declared "righteous." We are not merely pardoned criminals; we are treated as if we had perfectly obeyed every commandment of God's law throughout our entire lives. This is because the righteousness that is credited to us is the righteousness of Christ — His lifelong, perfect, unwavering obedience to the Father's will.

The Puritan Thomas Goodwin described justification using the illustration of a debtor and a benefactor. A man owes a debt of ten thousand talents — a debt he can never pay. A wealthy benefactor comes and pays the debt in full, removing the obligation entirely. But the benefactor does more — he also deposits a vast fortune into the man's account, making him fabulously wealthy. In the same way, Christ's sacrifice cancels our debt of sin, and Christ's righteousness enriches us with a positive standing before God that we could never have achieved on our own.

The doctrine of justification was the central issue of the Protestant Reformation — the doctrine over which Luther, Calvin, and the other Reformers were willing to break with the Roman Catholic Church and, if necessary, to lay down their lives. Martin Luther called justification by faith alone "the article by which the church stands or falls." John Calvin called it "the main hinge on which religion turns." And the Westminster divines placed it at the very heart of their doctrinal system, giving it a precision and fullness that built upon the work of the Reformers and brought it to its mature expression.

The Puritan Thomas Watson (born c. 1620 in England, exact birthplace uncertain) described the Reformation recovery of justification like opening a window in a dark room and letting the sunlight flood in. Suddenly, the gospel was clear: sinners are accepted by God not because of what they have become but because of what Christ has done. They are declared righteous not because of any righteousness within them but because of the perfect righteousness of Christ that is credited to their account.

The Confession is emphatic that justification is grounded solely in the work of Christ. We are justified "not for anything wrought in them, or done by them, but for Christ's sake alone." The ground of our acceptance before God is not our own faith, our own repentance, our own obedience, our own suffering, our own sincerity, or any other quality or action of our own. The sole ground is the righteousness of Jesus Christ — His perfect obedience and His atoning sacrifice. This was the great battle cry of the Reformation: sola fide — by faith alone. Not by faith plus works. Not by faith plus baptism. Not by faith plus church membership. Not by faith plus any human contribution whatsoever. Faith alone is the instrument by which we receive justification — but even faith is not the ground of justification. The ground is Christ and Christ alone.

The Confession explicitly denies that faith itself — "the act of believing" — is imputed as our righteousness. Some theologians had argued that God accepts faith in place of perfect obedience — that because we cannot render perfect obedience, God graciously accepts our faith as a substitute form of obedience. The Westminster divines rejected this view. Faith is not a work that replaces the works of the law. Faith is the instrument that receives the righteousness of Christ. It is the empty hand, not the payment. It is the channel, not the water. It is the eye that sees the Savior, not the Savior Himself.

The Puritan John Owen (born 1616 in Stadham, Oxfordshire, England) spent much of his theological career defending the doctrine of justification by faith alone against every form of compromise or corruption. In his massive work "The Doctrine of Justification by Faith," Owen argued that any view of justification that introduces human merit as a partial ground of acceptance before God destroys the gospel. For if our standing before God depends even in the slightest degree upon our own performance, then we can never have assurance — for our performance is always imperfect. But if our standing depends entirely upon the finished work of Christ, then our assurance is as secure as Christ's own righteousness — which is perfect, complete, and unchangeable.

While faith is not the ground of justification, it is the divinely appointed instrument by which justification is received. The Confession states that the justified receive and rest on Christ "and his righteousness by faith; which faith they have not of themselves; it is the gift of God." Faith is a receiving and a resting. It receives Christ — taking hold of Him as He is offered in the gospel, with all His benefits and blessings. And it rests on Christ — trusting in His finished work as the sole basis of acceptance before God, ceasing from all attempts at self-justification, and finding peace in the sufficiency of what He has done.

The Puritans were careful to distinguish saving faith from various counterfeits. Historical faith — the mere intellectual assent to the facts of the gospel — is not saving faith. Temporary faith — the emotional response that withers under trial — is not saving faith. Presumptuous faith — the careless assumption that one is saved without genuine trust in Christ — is not saving faith. True saving faith involves knowledge (understanding the gospel), assent (believing the gospel to be true), and trust (personally relying upon Christ for salvation). The Puritan William Ames (born 1576 in Ipswich, Suffolk, England) defined saving faith as "the resting of the heart upon God" — a deep, personal, life-transforming trust in the God who has promised salvation through His Son.

Furthermore, the Confession emphasizes that even this faith "is the gift of God." Faith is not something we generate from within ourselves by an act of our own will. It is a gift of divine grace — produced in us by the Holy Spirit through the preaching of the gospel. "For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: Not of works, lest any man should boast" (Ephesians 2:8-9, KJV). From beginning to end, justification is a work of God's free grace.

The Confession teaches that justification is an instantaneous act, not a gradual process. At the moment of faith, the sinner is fully and completely justified. There are no degrees of justification. The newest believer is as fully justified as the apostle Paul. The weakest Christian is as fully justified as the strongest saint. Justification does not increase or decrease with the believer's spiritual growth. It is a permanent, irreversible, once-for-all declaration that can never be undone.

The Larger Catechism, Question 77, confirms this understanding: "Wherein do justification and sanctification differ?" The answer distinguishes between justification as a single act and sanctification as a continuing process — between justification as a change of status and sanctification as a change of character. The Puritan Anthony Burgess (born c. 1600 in Watford, Hertfordshire, England) preached an extensive series of sermons on justification at the Westminster Assembly, in which he developed the distinction between justification and sanctification with meticulous care. Burgess argued that confusing these two doctrines is one of the most dangerous errors in theology. If we treat justification as a process (like sanctification), we will never have assurance — for the process is always incomplete. And if we treat sanctification as unnecessary (because justification is complete), we will fall into antinomianism — using grace as a license for sin.

The Confession, Chapter 11, Section 2, addresses the relationship between justification and good works: "Faith, thus receiving and resting on Christ and his righteousness, is the alone instrument of justification; yet is it not alone in the person justified, but is ever accompanied with all other saving graces, and is no dead faith, but worketh by love." While faith alone justifies, the faith that justifies is never alone. True saving faith is always accompanied by repentance, love, obedience, and good works. These are not the ground of justification — they do not contribute one particle to our legal standing before God. But they are the evidence of justification — the fruit that demonstrates that genuine faith is present.

The apostle James addresses this truth in his epistle: "What doth it profit, my brethren, though a man say he hath faith, and have not works? can faith save him? Even so faith, if it hath not works, is dead, being alone" (James 2:14, 17, KJV). James is not contradicting Paul's teaching that justification is by faith alone. He is exposing the fraud of those who claim to have faith but show no evidence of it in their lives.

The Puritan Ezekiel Hopkins (born 1634 in Sandford, Devon, England) expressed this relationship with characteristic precision: we are justified by faith without works, but we are not justified by a faith that is without works. Faith is the root; works are the fruit. The root produces the fruit, and the fruit demonstrates the vitality of the root. But it is always the root — faith in Christ — that connects us to the source of life, not the fruit of our own efforts.

One more vital aspect of justification must be noted: it is a once-for-all act that can never be reversed or repeated. The Confession, Chapter 11, Section 5, states: "God doth continue to forgive the sins of those that are justified; and although they can never fall from the state of justification, yet they may by their sins fall under God's fatherly displeasure, and not have the light of his countenance restored unto them, until they humble themselves, confess their sins, beg pardon, and renew their faith and repentance."

Justified believers still sin — sometimes grievously. But their sins do not reverse their justification. Their standing before God remains secure — founded not on their own fluctuating holiness but on the unchanging righteousness of Christ. However, sin does affect the believer's experience of fellowship with God. When believers sin, they come under God's "fatherly displeasure" — not the judicial wrath of a condemning Judge (for that has been satisfied by Christ) but the corrective discipline of a loving Father. The Puritan John Flavel (born c. 1627 in Bromsgrove, Worcestershire, England) compared this to the experience of a child who disobeys his father. The child does not cease to be the father's child. The relationship is not broken. But the warmth, the intimacy, and the joy of that relationship are temporarily disrupted until the child comes back in penitence and is restored to full fellowship.

Now we turn to the doctrine of adoption. The Westminster Confession addresses adoption in Chapter 12: "All those that are justified, God vouchsafeth, in and for his only Son Jesus Christ, to make partakers of the grace of adoption; by which they are taken into the number and enjoy the liberties and privileges of the children of God; have his name put upon them; receive the Spirit of adoption; have access to the throne of grace with boldness; are enabled to cry, Abba, Father; are pitied, protected, provided for, and chastened by him as by a father; yet never cast off, but sealed to the day of redemption, and inherit the promises, as heirs of everlasting salvation."

The Shorter Catechism, Question 34, provides the concise definition: "Adoption is an act of God's free grace, whereby we are received into the number, and have a right to all the privileges, of the sons of God."

Adoption is one of the most glorious and yet most underappreciated doctrines in the Christian faith. In justification, we are pardoned criminals standing before a Judge. In adoption, we are beloved children embraced by a Father. Justification addresses the legal problem of guilt. Adoption addresses the relational problem of alienation. Justification restores our standing before God's court. Adoption restores our standing in God's family.

The privileges of adoption, as enumerated by the Confession, are breathtaking in their scope and beauty. First, adopted children are "taken into the number" of God's family. They become members of the household of God — brothers and sisters of Christ, fellow heirs with Him of eternal glory. Second, they "have his name put upon them." To bear God's name is to be identified with Him, to represent Him in the world, and to share in His honor and dignity. "Behold, what manner of love the Father hath bestowed upon us, that we should be called the sons of God" (1 John 3:1, KJV).

Third, they "receive the Spirit of adoption." The Holy Spirit takes up residence within the believer, testifying to his soul that he is a child of God. "The Spirit itself beareth witness with our spirit, that we are the children of God" (Romans 8:16, KJV). This inner witness is the foundation of Christian assurance. Fourth, they "have access to the throne of grace with boldness." Adopted children can approach God without fear — not as slaves cowering before a tyrant but as children running to a loving Father. "For ye have not received the spirit of bondage again to fear; but ye have received the Spirit of adoption, whereby we cry, Abba, Father" (Romans 8:15, KJV). The word "Abba" is the intimate, affectionate address of a child to its father — the equivalent of "Papa" or "Daddy." That the infinite, sovereign God of the universe invites His adopted children to address Him with such intimacy is a marvel that should fill us with wonder and joy.

Fifth, they are "pitied, protected, provided for, and chastened by him as by a father." God deals with His adopted children as a perfect Father. He pities them in their weakness — "like as a father pitieth his children, so the LORD pitieth them that fear him" (Psalm 103:13, KJV). He protects them from danger. He provides for their needs. And He disciplines them when they stray — not out of anger but out of love. "For whom the Lord loveth he chasteneth, and scourgeth every son whom he receiveth" (Hebrews 12:6, KJV).

Sixth, adopted children are "never cast off." The security of adoption is as firm as the covenant of grace itself. God does not adopt children temporarily or conditionally. Once a person is adopted into God's family, he is a child of God forever. "Who shall separate us from the love of Christ? For I am persuaded, that neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor principalities, nor powers, nor things present, nor things to come, nor height, nor depth, nor any other creature, shall be able to separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord" (Romans 8:35, 38-39, KJV).

Seventh, adopted children are "sealed to the day of redemption." The Holy Spirit is the seal — the divine mark of ownership — that guarantees the believer's final inheritance. "In whom also after that ye believed, ye were sealed with that holy Spirit of promise, which is the earnest of our inheritance until the redemption of the purchased possession" (Ephesians 1:13-14, KJV).

Eighth, adopted children "inherit the promises, as heirs of everlasting salvation." "And if children, then heirs; heirs of God, and joint-heirs with Christ" (Romans 8:17, KJV). The inheritance that awaits the adopted children of God is beyond imagination — an eternal weight of glory that surpasses all present suffering and all earthly treasure.

The relationship between justification and adoption is close but distinct, and the Westminster divines were careful to treat them as separate doctrines in separate chapters of the Confession. The Puritan Thomas Goodwin argued that this distinction is vital for understanding the fullness of what God has done for His people. In justification, the dominant metaphor is legal — the courtroom, the judge, the verdict, the law. In adoption, the dominant metaphor is familial — the home, the father, the family, the inheritance. A judge may acquit a defendant without taking him into his family. But God does both — He acquits and He adopts. He justifies and He makes sons. Justification opens the prison door. Adoption opens the Father's arms.

The Puritan Samuel Willard (born 1640 in Concord, Massachusetts) in his massive theological work "A Compleat Body of Divinity," devoted extensive attention to the distinction between justification and adoption. Willard argued that while justification gives the believer a legal title to heaven, adoption gives him a family relationship to the God of heaven. Both are necessary. Both are precious. And both are received by faith in Christ at the same moment.

The Puritans had a special love for the doctrine of adoption. John Owen called adoption "the greatest privilege that any creature is capable of receiving." Thomas Watson described it as the highest honor in the universe — higher than the honor of kings, higher than the honor of angels, for angels are servants of God, but adopted believers are children of God. The Puritan Jeremiah Burroughs (born c. 1600 in England) preached that the doctrine of adoption should be the Christian's greatest source of daily comfort. When trials come, the believer can say, "I am a child of God — He will not forsake me." When temptation comes, the believer can say, "I am a child of God — I will not disgrace my Father's name." When death comes, the believer can say, "I am a child of God — I am going home to my Father's house."

The Puritan Richard Sibbes (born 1577 in Tostock, Suffolk, England) in his beloved devotional work "The Bruised Reed," meditated at length upon the privileges of adoption. Sibbes was particularly moved by the truth that adopted children receive the Spirit of adoption — the inner witness of the Holy Spirit who cries "Abba, Father" within the believer's heart. Sibbes taught that this inner witness is the most intimate and personal form of assurance available to the Christian — more powerful than logical arguments, more persuasive than evidences, more comforting than human counsel. It is the Spirit of God Himself testifying directly to the believer's spirit that he belongs to God and that God belongs to him.

Sibbes also emphasized that adoption is a present reality, not merely a future hope. We are children of God now — not merely candidates for future adoption. The relationship is established, the privileges are operative, and the inheritance is secured. While we do not yet enjoy the fullness of our inheritance — that awaits the resurrection and the new creation — we possess the firstfruits of that inheritance in the indwelling presence of the Holy Spirit. "Beloved, now are we the sons of God, and it doth not yet appear what we shall be: but we know that, when he shall appear, we shall be like him; for we shall see him as he is" (1 John 3:2, KJV).

The Puritan approach reminds us that theology is never abstract. It is always personal and always practical. What we believe about God shapes everything we do.

SEGMENT 3B: DEEPER THEOLOGICAL ANALYSIS
----------------------------------------

Let us now press deeper into the theological foundations of justification and adoption by examining additional Scripture passages and drawing from the broader Puritan tradition.

The relationship between justification and the active and passive obedience of Christ deserves careful attention. The Puritans distinguished between Christ's active obedience — His perfect keeping of the law of God throughout His entire life — and His passive obedience — His suffering and death on the cross as the penalty for sin. Both are essential to justification. Christ's passive obedience satisfies the penalty of the law — the curse that we deserved for our disobedience. "Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us: for it is written, Cursed is every one that hangeth on a tree" (Galatians 3:13, KJV). Christ's active obedience fulfills the precepts of the law — the perfect righteousness that we could never achieve. "For as by one man's disobedience many were made sinners, so by the obedience of one shall many be made righteous" (Romans 5:19, KJV).

The Puritan Robert Traill (born 1642 in Elie, Fife, Scotland) argued that if Christ only died for our sins (passive obedience) but did not also perfectly fulfill the law on our behalf (active obedience), we would be left in the position of Adam before the fall — innocent but not yet confirmed in righteousness, without guilt but without merit. But because Christ both suffered the penalty we deserved and performed the obedience we owed, we receive not only the removal of guilt but the bestowal of a positive righteousness that entitles us to eternal life. Traill insisted that both aspects of Christ's obedience must be imputed to the believer for justification to be complete.

John Owen explored this theme with characteristic depth in his treatise on justification. Owen demonstrated from Scripture that the righteousness imputed to believers in justification is not a bare legal fiction — as critics sometimes charged — but a real righteousness, truly performed by a real person, Jesus Christ, in our nature and on our behalf. Owen wrote that when God declares the believer righteous, He is not pretending that the sinner has obeyed when he has not. Rather, God is declaring that the believer is united to Christ by faith, and that Christ's obedience is as truly credited to the believer's account as the believer's sin was truly credited to Christ's account on the cross. The imputation is real because the union with Christ is real.

The apostle Paul uses the language of imputation with precision in 2 Corinthians 5:21, one of the most theologically dense verses in the New Testament: "For he hath made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin; that we might be made the righteousness of God in him" (KJV). Christ was made sin — not by becoming sinful in His own character, but by having our sin imputed to Him. And we are made the righteousness of God — not by becoming righteous in our own character, but by having Christ's righteousness imputed to us. This double imputation — our sin to Christ and His righteousness to us — is the very heart of the gospel.

Let us also explore the theological relationship between justification and union with Christ. The Puritans taught that justification does not occur in a vacuum — it takes place within the context of the believer's union with Christ. The believer is justified not as an isolated individual but as one who is "in Christ" — united to Him by faith and by the bond of the Holy Spirit. This union is the foundation of every blessing of salvation. We are justified in Christ, adopted in Christ, sanctified in Christ, and will be glorified in Christ. Apart from union with Christ, there is no justification, no adoption, no spiritual blessing of any kind.

The Puritan John Flavel devoted extensive attention to the doctrine of union with Christ, which he considered the foundation of all applied redemption. Flavel taught that just as a branch derives its life from the vine, so the believer derives every spiritual blessing from Christ. The branch does not produce its own sap — it receives the sap from the vine. In the same way, the believer does not generate his own righteousness — he receives the righteousness of Christ through the living union of faith. "I am the vine, ye are the branches: He that abideth in me, and I in him, the same bringeth forth much fruit: for without me ye can do nothing" (John 15:5, KJV).

Regarding adoption, we must explore the Trinitarian dimensions of this doctrine more fully. Adoption is the work of the entire Trinity. The Father adopts — He is the one who receives the sinner into His family and bestows the privileges of sonship. The Son makes adoption possible — it is through His mediatorial work, His incarnation, obedience, death, and resurrection, that the way is opened for sinners to become sons. And the Spirit effects adoption experientially — He indwells the believer, bears witness to his adoption, and enables him to cry "Abba, Father."

The Puritan Thomas Goodwin devoted an entire treatise to the work of the Holy Spirit in relation to adoption. Goodwin distinguished between the objective reality of adoption — the legal act by which God receives the sinner as His child — and the subjective experience of adoption — the inner witness of the Spirit by which the believer comes to know and feel that he is God's child. Goodwin taught that these two aspects do not always coincide in time. A believer may be objectively adopted — truly a child of God — long before he receives the inner witness of the Spirit that assures him of his sonship. Goodwin urged believers who lacked assurance not to doubt their adoption but to seek the Spirit's witness through prayer, obedience, and meditation on Scripture.

The Puritan Stephen Charnock (born 1628 in the parish of St. Katherine Cree, London, England) added another dimension to the doctrine of adoption in his monumental work "The Existence and Attributes of God." Charnock argued that adoption reveals the character of God as no other doctrine can. In creation, God reveals His power. In providence, God reveals His wisdom. In the law, God reveals His holiness. In judgment, God reveals His justice. But in adoption, God reveals His love — a love so extravagant, so overflowing, so freely given that it takes rebels and makes them children, takes enemies and makes them heirs, takes strangers and makes them family. Charnock wrote that if we would know what God is truly like — not merely what He can do, or what He demands, but who He is in the depths of His being — we must look at the doctrine of adoption, for there we see the heart of a Father whose love surpasses all knowledge and exceeds all expectation.

Consider also the eschatological dimension of adoption. While adoption is a present reality — we are children of God now — it also has a future consummation. Paul writes in Romans 8:23: "And not only they, but ourselves also, which have the firstfruits of the Spirit, even we ourselves groan within ourselves, waiting for the adoption, to wit, the redemption of our body" (KJV). The full realization of our adoption awaits the resurrection, when our bodies will be glorified and we will enter into the complete inheritance that has been prepared for us. The present experience of adoption is real but partial — like a down payment on a much larger purchase. The Spirit who indwells us now is the "earnest" or guarantee of the full inheritance yet to come. We taste the firstfruits now, but the full harvest awaits us in glory.

The Puritan William Guthrie (born 1620 in Pitforthy, Angus, Scotland) in his classic work "The Christian's Great Interest" — a book that John Owen declared was the best small treatise on practical theology in all of Christian literature — provided pastoral counsel for believers struggling with assurance of their adoption. Guthrie outlined several marks by which believers can discern whether they are truly adopted children of God: a genuine love for God and His people, a sincere hatred of sin, a desire for holiness, a spirit of prayer, a willingness to suffer for Christ, and the inner testimony of the Holy Spirit. Guthrie urged believers not to build their assurance solely upon their feelings — which are changeable — but upon the objective promises of God in Scripture, confirmed by the inner witness of the Spirit.

SEGMENT 3C: PRACTICAL APPLICATION
----------------------------------------

Before we move to our discussion questions, let us apply these truths to our daily lives.

First, justification should produce deep and settled peace. "Therefore being justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ" (Romans 5:1, KJV). The war between God and the sinner is over. The verdict has been rendered. The gavel has fallen. We are declared righteous in the court of heaven, and no charge can ever be brought against us again. "Who shall lay any thing to the charge of God's elect? It is God that justifieth. Who is he that condemneth? It is Christ that died, yea rather, that is risen again, who is even at the right hand of God, who also maketh intercession for us" (Romans 8:33-34, KJV). When doubt assails you, when your conscience accuses you, when Satan whispers that you are too sinful to be accepted — return to this truth: it is God that justifieth. The Judge of all the earth has declared you righteous, and no one can overturn His verdict. Thomas Watson counseled troubled believers to make this truth the anchor of their souls: "When your sins accuse you, let the blood of Christ excuse you. When the law condemns you, let the gospel acquit you. When Satan tempts you to despair, let Christ's righteousness give you hope."

Second, adoption should transform your prayer life utterly and completely. We do not pray to a distant deity but to a loving Father. We do not approach God with servile fear but with filial confidence. We cry "Abba, Father" — knowing that He hears us, loves us, and delights to give good things to His children. "If ye then, being evil, know how to give good gifts unto your children, how much more shall your Father which is in heaven give good things to them that ask him?" (Matthew 7:11, KJV). The Puritan Thomas Manton (born 1620 in Lawrence Lydiat, Somerset, England) preached that a right understanding of adoption would revolutionize the prayer life of every believer. When you know that the God to whom you pray is your Father — not merely your Creator, not merely your Judge, but your Father who loves you with an everlasting love — you will come to prayer with boldness, with expectation, and with joy.

Third, both doctrines should produce profound humility. We did nothing to earn justification or adoption. Both are acts of God's free grace. We were rebels who deserved judgment, and we have been pardoned and adopted entirely by divine mercy. "Not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to his mercy he saved us, by the washing of regeneration, and renewing of the Holy Ghost; Which he shed on us abundantly through Jesus Christ our Saviour; That being justified by his grace, we should be made heirs according to the hope of eternal life" (Titus 3:5-7, KJV). Observe how Paul weaves justification and adoption together in this single passage — justified by grace, made heirs of eternal life. Both are free. Both are unmerited. Both should drive us to our knees in gratitude and wonder.

Fourth, justification should produce unshakeable assurance. Because our standing before God rests not on our fluctuating performance but on Christ's unchanging righteousness, we can have confidence that we are accepted — not because we are good enough, but because Christ is good enough. William Guthrie urged believers to build their assurance not on their own spiritual experiences or moral achievements but on the objective truth of justification by faith alone. Your justification does not depend on the strength of your faith — it depends on the object of your faith. Even weak faith, if it lays hold of Christ, receives the full benefit of justification.

Fifth, adoption should produce love for fellow believers. If God is our Father, then every other believer is our brother or sister. "Beloved, if God so loved us, we ought also to love one another" (1 John 4:11, KJV). The family of God is not a metaphor — it is a spiritual reality that should shape the way we treat one another. Jeremiah Burroughs preached that a believer who despises his fellow Christian despises his own brother, dishonors his Father's name, and grieves the Spirit of adoption. Let the reality of adoption soften your heart toward every member of God's family — especially those who are different from you, who are difficult to love, or who have wronged you. They bear the same Father's name, they have been purchased by the same Savior's blood, and they are indwelt by the same Spirit.

Sixth, both doctrines should produce holiness. Justified and adopted believers are motivated to live lives of grateful obedience — not in order to earn God's favor, but because they have already received it. "For the grace of God that bringeth salvation hath appeared to all men, Teaching us that, denying ungodliness and worldly lusts, we should live soberly, righteously, and godly, in this present world" (Titus 2:11-12, KJV). The child who knows his father's love does not want to grieve his father's heart. The pardoned criminal who has been adopted into the king's family does not want to return to a life of crime. Grace does not produce carelessness — it produces gratitude, and gratitude produces obedience. John Owen wrote that the strongest motive for holiness is not fear of punishment — which the law provides — but love for the Father — which adoption provides.

SEGMENT 4: DISCUSSION AND APPLICATION
----------------------------------------

Now let us pause and consider what we have learned. Discussion is where understanding deepens.

Discussion Question 1: The Westminster Confession defines justification as "not by infusing righteousness" but "by imputing the obedience and satisfaction of Christ." Why is this distinction between infusion and imputation so important? What difference does it make practically for the believer's assurance?

Discussion Question 2: Explain the "great exchange" of the gospel using 2 Corinthians 5:21. How does the double imputation — our sin to Christ and His righteousness to us — demonstrate both God's justice and His mercy?

Discussion Question 3: The Confession teaches that faith is the "alone instrument of justification" but is "not alone in the person justified." How do we reconcile justification by faith alone with James 2:17 which says "faith, if it hath not works, is dead"? Are Paul and James contradicting each other?

Discussion Question 4: What is the difference between justification and adoption? Why did the Westminster divines treat them as separate doctrines in separate chapters? Why is it important not to collapse adoption into justification?

Discussion Question 5: The Puritans taught that adoption should be the Christian's greatest daily comfort. How should the knowledge that you are a child of God affect the way you respond to trials, temptation, and the fear of death?

Take time with each question. The Puritans valued what they called "holy conference," the practice of discussing spiritual truths together. Do not settle for quick, surface answers. Push deeper. Ask follow-up questions. Listen carefully to one another.

Application is where truth becomes life. It is not enough to know what the Westminster Standards say about justification and adoption. We must ask: how does this change the way I live today? How does this shape my thoughts, my words, my actions? The Puritans insisted that a truth that does not transform is a truth that has not been truly understood.

SEGMENT 6: CROSS-SUBJECT CONNECTIONS
----------------------------------------

One of the great insights of the Puritan educational tradition is that all subjects are interconnected under the lordship of Christ. There is no such thing as a purely secular subject. History, literature, and even the natural sciences all reveal the glory and wisdom of God. Let us explore those connections now.

History Connection:

The doctrine of justification by faith alone was the central issue of the Protestant Reformation. Study the historical development of this doctrine from the early church through the medieval period to the Reformation. Begin with the Council of Orange in 529 AD, which affirmed a broadly Augustinian view of grace. Then trace the gradual drift toward a synergistic view of justification in the medieval church, culminating in the Council of Trent (1545-1563), which condemned the Protestant doctrine of justification by faith alone. Examine how the Westminster Assembly (1643-1649) brought the Reformation understanding of justification to its fullest and most precise expression. Create a timeline showing the key events, councils, and theological figures in the history of this doctrine: Augustine, Anselm, Peter Lombard, Thomas Aquinas, Luther, Calvin, the Council of Trent, and the Westminster Assembly. Compare the Roman Catholic doctrine of justification as defined at Trent with the Protestant doctrine as defined in the Westminster Confession. What are the key points of agreement and disagreement?

Literature Connection:

John Bunyan's "The Pilgrim's Progress" provides one of the most vivid literary portrayals of justification in the English language. In the famous scene at the cross, the burden of sin falls from Christian's back and rolls into the sepulchre, never to be seen again. Three Shining Ones then appear: one declares his sins forgiven, another clothes him with a new garment, and the third places a mark on his forehead and gives him a sealed scroll. Analyze this scene as a literary depiction of justification — the pardon of sin (the burden falling away), the imputation of righteousness (the new garment), and the assurance of salvation (the sealed scroll). Compare Bunyan's use of allegory with the parable of the prodigal son in Luke 15:11-32, which is one of the most powerful biblical illustrations of adoption — the father who runs to meet the returning son, clothes him with the best robe, places a ring on his finger, and declares, "This my son was dead, and is alive again; he was lost, and is found." How do Bunyan and Christ use narrative to make abstract theological truths concrete and personal?

Science Connection:

The concept of imputation involves the crediting or reckoning of one thing to another's account — a principle that has analogies in the natural sciences. In chemistry, consider the concept of a catalyst — a substance that enables a reaction without being consumed by it. Faith functions somewhat like a catalyst in justification: it is the instrument that brings the sinner into contact with Christ's righteousness, enabling the "transfer" of righteousness to the sinner's account, without faith itself being the righteousness. In accounting and mathematics, consider the concept of a ledger — a record of debits and credits. The language of imputation is accounting language: our sin is debited to Christ's account, and His righteousness is credited to ours. Discuss how the precision of mathematical and scientific thinking can illuminate theological concepts, and how the Puritan commitment to clear, precise theological language reflected the same intellectual rigor that was producing the scientific revolution of the seventeenth century. Figures like Robert Boyle and Isaac Newton, who were deeply influenced by Reformed theology, saw no conflict between scientific precision and theological devotion.

As you continue your studies in other subjects, look for connections to what you have learned today. The Puritans saw every branch of knowledge as a thread in the great tapestry of God's truth. Train your eye to see those connections, and your education will be richer for it.

Memory Verse:

"Therefore being justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ." -- Romans 5:1 (KJV)

Read this verse aloud five times. Then close your eyes and try to recite it from memory. Write it out by hand on a card or in your journal. The Puritans memorized vast portions of Scripture, and it was one of the great sources of their spiritual strength. Even one verse, truly hidden in the heart, can sustain you in times of trial.

This verse is the golden chain that links the doctrine of justification to the experience of peace. Notice the logical connection: "therefore" — because of justification — "we have peace with God." The peace is not a feeling that comes and goes but a settled state of reconciliation that has been established once for all by the verdict of justification. Whether you feel peaceful or troubled, the objective reality remains: you have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ. This peace is not a ceasefire — it is a permanent treaty, signed in the blood of Christ, ratified by the resurrection, and sealed by the Holy Spirit. No enemy can break it. No sin can nullify it. No circumstance can diminish it. You have peace with God — not because of anything you have done, but because of everything Christ has done.

Additional Memory Verse:

"Behold, what manner of love the Father hath bestowed upon us, that we should be called the sons of God: and such we are." -- 1 John 3:1 (KJV)

The apostle John does not merely state the fact of adoption — he marvels at it. "Behold!" — stop and look at this. "What manner of love!" — this is no ordinary love. This is a love that defies comprehension, a love so extravagant that even the apostle who leaned on the Savior's breast at the Last Supper stands astonished before it. The love that adopts rebels as children, that takes enemies and makes them heirs, that bridges the infinite gap between the holy Creator and sinful creatures — this love is beyond anything we could have imagined or deserved. And John adds the emphatic affirmation: "and such we are." This is not wishful thinking. This is not a poetic exaggeration. This is a statement of present, settled, irreversible reality. We are the children of God. Now. Today. Already. The adoption is complete. The relationship is established. The inheritance is secured. And nothing — absolutely nothing — can change it.

Supplemental Reading Assignment:

For students who wish to go deeper, read Chapters 11 and 12 of the Westminster Confession of Faith carefully, along with the corresponding Larger Catechism questions (Questions 70-74 on justification and Question 74 on adoption). Then read Thomas Watson's treatment of justification in "A Body of Divinity," where Watson expounds the Shorter Catechism's definition with his characteristic clarity, warmth, and practical application. Watson (born c. 1620) was one of the most beloved Puritan preachers in London, ejected from his pulpit in 1662 along with nearly two thousand other nonconforming ministers. His writings combine doctrinal precision with pastoral tenderness in a way that few authors have ever matched. Pay particular attention to Watson's discussion of the grounds and evidences of justification — how he guards the doctrine against both legalism (earning acceptance through works) and antinomianism (claiming acceptance without works).

For those who desire still further study, examine the opening chapters of John Owen's "The Doctrine of Justification by Faith," where Owen develops the biblical doctrine of imputation with a thoroughness and precision that has never been surpassed. Owen's prose is demanding, but the reward is worth the effort. Owen demonstrates from Scripture, from reason, and from the history of theology that the Protestant doctrine of justification by imputation is not merely one possible interpretation of Scripture but the only interpretation that does full justice to the witness of the apostolic writings. Owen's treatment of the relationship between justification and sanctification is particularly valuable — he shows how these two doctrines are distinguished without being separated, and how both flow from the believer's union with Christ.
