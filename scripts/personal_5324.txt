PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Guided Puritan Reading, Day 24
"The Saints' Everlasting Rest"
Richard Baxter — A Classic Meditation on the Heavenly Glory

---

Good evening, friend. Tonight we turn to one of the great masterworks of Puritan literature: Richard Baxter's "The Saints' Everlasting Rest." Written in 1650 while Baxter believed himself to be dying, this book is a sustained meditation on the heavenly rest that awaits all who belong to Christ. Few books in the history of the church have done more to lift the eyes of weary pilgrims toward their eternal home.

--- Scripture ---

Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Hebrews 4:9-11:

"There remaineth therefore a rest to the people of God. For he that is entered into his rest, he also hath ceased from his own works, as God did from his. Let us labour therefore to enter into that rest, lest any man fall after the same example of unbelief."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.

--- Guided Reading ---

Baxter wrote "The Saints' Everlasting Rest" during a period of severe illness. He believed death was near, and his mind turned naturally to the question that every believer must eventually face: what awaits us on the other side? Baxter's answer, drawn from a lifetime of pastoral ministry and deep study of Scripture, is one of the most beautiful and compelling descriptions of heaven ever penned.

Baxter defines the saints' rest as the perfect, endless enjoyment of God by the perfected saints. Notice each word carefully. It is perfect — free from every defect, every sorrow, every limitation that mars our present experience. It is endless — not a temporary respite but an eternal state. It is the enjoyment of God — not merely a place of comfort but a relationship of unbroken communion. And it belongs to the perfected saints — those who have been made complete in Christ, freed at last from the remaining corruption that clings to them in this life.

One of Baxter's most striking arguments is that the heavenly rest is primarily about the vision of God. The saints in glory will see God as He is. They will know Him not through a glass darkly but face to face. Every question will be answered. Every mystery will be resolved. Every longing of the heart will find its satisfaction in the infinite beauty and goodness of God. Baxter writes that all the pleasures of this world combined are but a shadow compared to the joy of beholding the face of God.

Baxter also emphasizes the rest from sin. This, he says, is one of the sweetest aspects of heaven for the believer who has fought long against indwelling corruption. In glory, the battle will be over. The flesh will no longer war against the spirit. Temptation will cease. The devil will have no access. The believer will be perfectly holy, not by his own effort but by the completing work of grace. John Owen once wrote that the greatest burden of the Christian life is not suffering but sin. If Owen is right, then the greatest relief of heaven is not comfort but holiness — a holiness so complete that the very capacity for sin is removed.

Perhaps the most practically influential section of Baxter's work is his treatment of heavenly meditation. Baxter argued that believers ought to spend regular time meditating on heaven — not as an escape from present duties but as a means of strengthening present faithfulness. He counseled Christians to set aside time each day to lift their thoughts above the cares of this world and fix them upon the glory that is to come. He called this practice "heavenly meditation" and considered it one of the most neglected duties in the Christian life.

Baxter's method of heavenly meditation involves the deliberate engagement of the mind and the affections. First, the believer considers the truths of Scripture concerning heaven. Then he applies those truths to his own soul, asking: what will it be like for me to see Christ face to face? What will it be like to be free from sin? What will it be like to join the company of the perfected saints? As the mind dwells on these realities, the affections are stirred — love for God increases, desire for holiness grows, and the hold of this world upon the heart is loosened. Thomas Watson echoed this counsel when he wrote that the man who keeps his eye on eternity will hold this world with a loose hand.

Baxter closes with a pastoral appeal that has moved readers for nearly four centuries. He urges every believer to live in light of eternity. The trials of this life are temporary. The sorrows of this world will pass. But the rest that awaits the people of God is everlasting. No suffering can compare to it. No sacrifice is too great for it. No delay in its arrival can diminish its glory. Press on, pilgrim. The rest is real, and it is coming.

--- Application ---

First, practice Baxter's discipline of heavenly meditation this week. Set aside ten minutes each day to think deliberately about the glory that awaits you. Read a passage of Scripture about heaven — Revelation 21, John 14, or 1 Corinthians 2:9 — and let your mind and heart dwell upon it.

Second, let the hope of heaven loosen your grip on this world. Where are you holding too tightly to earthly comforts, earthly reputation, or earthly security? The saints' rest is your inheritance; let that certainty free you from anxiety about what you might lose here.

Third, use the hope of heaven to comfort a fellow believer who is suffering. Baxter wrote for the weary and the dying. If someone you know is walking through a difficult season, share with them the promise that there remaineth a rest to the people of God.

--- Closing Prayer ---

O Father, we thank Thee for the rest that Thou hast prepared for Thy people. When the journey is long and the burden is heavy, lift our eyes to the glory that awaits. Teach us to meditate upon heaven until our hearts burn with desire for Thy presence. And when at last we arrive, may we find that the reality exceeds everything we dared to imagine. Through Christ, who has gone before to prepare a place for us, Amen.

Sleep well tonight, friend. The best is yet to come.
