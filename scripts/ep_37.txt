PURITAN GOLD -- Study Devotional, Day 6
Series: Romans
"The Righteousness of God Apart from the Law"
Justification by faith through the propitiation of Christ


Welcome. The practice of sustained Bible study is one of the greatest gifts a Christian can give themselves. Tonight we continue to build our understanding, one passage at a time, guided by the Holy Spirit and the wisdom of the Puritans.

We are studying the book of Romans together. Tonight's lesson is titled "The Righteousness of God Apart from the Law." Each session builds on what came before, so if you have been following along, you will see how tonight's passage connects to the larger argument of the book.


Let us read our passage for tonight. It comes from Romans 3:21-31:

"But now the righteousness of God without the law is manifested, being witnessed by the law and the prophets; Even the righteousness of God which is by faith of Jesus Christ unto all and upon all them that believe: for there is no difference: For all have sinned, and come short of the glory of God; Being justified freely by his grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus: Whom God hath set forth to be a propitiation through faith in his blood, to declare his righteousness for the remission of sins that are past, through the forbearance of God; To declare, I say, at this time his righteousness: that he might be just, and the justifier of him which believeth in Jesus. Where is boasting then? It is excluded. By what law? of works? Nay: but by the law of faith. Therefore we conclude that a man is justified by faith without the deeds of the law."

Read that passage again to yourself. Mark any words or phrases that stand out. The Puritans read with a pen in hand, always ready to note what God was showing them.


One verse in particular demands our attention tonight. Let us read it carefully.

"Being justified freely by his grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus: Whom God hath set forth to be a propitiation through faith in his blood." -- Romans 3:24-25

This verse is the lens through which we will examine the rest of tonight's passage. Keep it in the front of your mind as we proceed.


Let us now exposit this passage carefully, as the Puritans would have done.

John Owen offers valuable commentary on this passage. The Puritans were expositors of the highest order, and their insights remain remarkably fresh.

John Owen's treatise 'The Doctrine of Justification by Faith' is one of the most thorough defenses of the Protestant doctrine of justification ever written, and Romans 3:21-31 is its beating heart. Owen argued that this passage contains the most glorious words in all of Scripture — the answer to the question that haunted humanity since the fall: how can a sinner be right with a holy God?

Owen focused with particular intensity on the word 'propitiation' in verse 25. He insisted that this is no mere metaphor but a precise theological term denoting the satisfaction of divine justice. Christ did not merely demonstrate God's love or provide a moral example; He actually bore the wrath that sinners deserved, absorbing the full penalty of the law in His own person on the cross. Owen argued that without propitiation, there can be no justification. If God's wrath against sin is not satisfied, then either God's justice is compromised or sinners remain under condemnation. The cross resolves this dilemma: Christ bears the wrath, God's justice is vindicated, and believing sinners are set free.

Thomas Goodwin complemented Owen's analysis by emphasizing the phrase 'freely by his grace.' Goodwin delighted in the redundancy — 'freely' and 'by his grace' say the same thing twice for emphasis. Justification is absolutely gratuitous. There is no mixture of grace and merit. The sinner contributes nothing to his justification except the sin that makes it necessary. Goodwin also stressed the present tense force of the passage: justification is not merely a future hope but a present reality. The moment a sinner believes, they are declared righteous — fully, finally, and irrevocably. Owen agreed, adding that this righteousness is not infused into the believer (as Rome taught) but imputed — credited to the believer's account, as a judge pronounces a verdict of 'not guilty.' The righteousness that justifies us is the righteousness of Christ Himself, received by faith alone.

If Romans 1:18-3:20 is the darkest passage in the Bible, Romans 3:21-31 is among the brightest. After the long, relentless indictment of human sin, Paul utters two of the most glorious words in Scripture: 'But now.' These words mark the great turning point of the epistle and indeed of all human history. The verdict has been rendered — all guilty. But now God acts.

'But now the righteousness of God without the law is manifested.' This is not a new righteousness; it is a newly revealed righteousness. It was 'witnessed by the law and the prophets' — foretold throughout the Old Testament. Abraham believed God and it was counted to him for righteousness (Genesis 15:6). Isaiah spoke of the servant who would 'justify many' (Isaiah 53:11). Habakkuk declared that 'the just shall live by his faith' (Habakkuk 2:4). The entire Old Testament pointed forward to this moment.

This righteousness is 'without the law' — apart from law-keeping. It does not come through human effort, obedience, or moral achievement. It comes 'by faith of Jesus Christ unto all and upon all them that believe.' Notice the universality: it is available to all who believe, and it is effective upon all who believe. And then the great equalizer: 'for there is no difference: For all have sinned, and come short of the glory of God.' Whether Jew or Gentile, moral or immoral, religious or pagan — all have sinned. The word 'sinned' is aorist tense, pointing to the historical fact of human sin. And we 'come short' — present tense — we continually fall short of the glory for which God created us.

But the condemnation is not the last word. Verse 24 explodes with grace: 'Being justified freely by his grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus.' Every word here is loaded with theological significance. 'Justified' — declared righteous, a legal verdict of acquittal. 'Freely' — the Greek word dorean means 'as a gift,' the same word used in John 15:25 where Jesus was hated 'without a cause.' Just as there was no cause in the world for hating Christ, there is no cause in us for God's favor. 'By his grace' — the unmerited favor of God. 'Through the redemption' — the word apolutrosis means liberation by payment of a ransom. We were slaves, and Christ paid the price to set us free. And it is all 'in Christ Jesus' — He is the sphere, the source, and the ground of our justification.

Verse 25 takes us to the heart of the atonement: 'Whom God hath set forth to be a propitiation through faith in his blood.' The word 'propitiation' (Greek: hilasterion) is one of the most important words in the New Testament. In the Old Testament, the hilasterion was the mercy seat — the golden lid of the Ark of the Covenant where the blood of atonement was sprinkled on the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16). It was the place where God's wrath met God's mercy. Christ is our mercy seat. On the cross, God's holy wrath against sin and God's infinite love for sinners met in the person of His Son. The blood of Christ did not merely cover sin (as the old covenant sacrifices did temporarily); it removed sin's guilt and satisfied God's justice completely.

This propitiation accomplishes something extraordinary: it declares God's righteousness 'for the remission of sins that are past, through the forbearance of God.' Throughout the Old Testament, God had been passing over sins — forgiving them without fully punishing them. Abraham was a liar, Jacob was a deceiver, David was a murderer and adulterer, yet God forgave them. How? Was God unjust to let these sins go unpunished? No — because He was looking forward to the cross. The blood of bulls and goats could never take away sin (Hebrews 10:4), but they pointed forward to the one sacrifice that could and would. Christ's death retroactively justified God's patience with Old Testament sinners. It was the deferred payment that had been promised all along.

Verse 26 states the magnificent result: 'That he might be just, and the justifier of him which believeth in Jesus.' This is the solution to what is perhaps the greatest theological problem in the universe: how can God be both just and the justifier of sinners? Justice demands that sin be punished. Love desires that sinners be saved. At the cross, both are fully satisfied. God punishes sin — in the person of His Son. God saves sinners — through faith in that substitutionary sacrifice. Justice and mercy kiss (Psalm 85:10). The righteousness of God is vindicated, and believing sinners are declared righteous. It is the most perfect resolution imaginable.

Verses 27-28 draw the immediate implication: 'Where is boasting then? It is excluded.' If justification is freely by grace, through faith, without works, then there is absolutely no room for human boasting. No one can stand before God and claim, 'I earned this. I deserved this. My efforts contributed to my salvation.' The law of faith excludes all boasting. 'Therefore we conclude that a man is justified by faith without the deeds of the law.' This is the thesis of the Reformation: sola fide, by faith alone. Not faith plus works. Not faith plus circumcision. Not faith plus baptism. Faith alone, in Christ alone, by grace alone.

Verses 29-30 extend this to all people: 'Is he the God of the Jews only? is he not also of the Gentiles? Yes, of the Gentiles also: Seeing it is one God, which shall justify the circumcision by faith, and uncircumcision through faith.' There is one God, one way of salvation, and it is the same for all: faith in the Lord Jesus Christ.

Verse 31 addresses the final objection: 'Do we then make void the law through faith? God forbid: yea, we establish the law.' Justification by faith does not abolish the law; it fulfills it. The law demanded perfect righteousness — Christ provided it. The law demanded death for sin — Christ paid it. The law pointed to a coming Redeemer — Christ is He. Far from overthrowing the law, the gospel is the only thing that truly honors the law by meeting all its demands.


What does this truth look like in the ordinary moments of your life? At work, at home, in your relationships? Let us make it concrete.

Romans 3:21-31 is the heart of the gospel. Here are ways to let this truth reshape your life.

First, rest in the finished work of Christ. If you are justified freely by grace through faith, then your standing before God does not fluctuate with your performance. On your best day, you are no more justified than on your worst day, because your justification rests on Christ's righteousness, not yours. When guilt attacks, do not try to earn your way back into God's favor through increased religious activity. Instead, return to the cross and remind yourself: 'Being justified freely by his grace.'

Second, worship at the cross. Verse 25 says God set forth Christ as a propitiation. Spend time this week meditating on what happened at Calvary. The wrath that you deserved fell on Jesus. The punishment that should have destroyed you was absorbed by the Son of God. This should produce not casual gratitude but profound, tear-filled worship. Read Isaiah 53 slowly alongside Romans 3:24-26 and let the reality of the atonement pierce your heart.

Third, kill your boasting. 'Where is boasting then? It is excluded.' Examine your heart for spiritual pride — pride in your theology, your church, your spiritual disciplines, your moral record. All of it is filthy rags before God (Isaiah 64:6). The only boast the Christian has is in the cross of Christ (Galatians 6:14). Practice ascribing every spiritual blessing to grace alone.

Fourth, share this good news with urgency. If all have sinned and justification is available freely to all who believe, then every person you meet is either under condemnation or under grace. There is no middle ground. Let the urgency of this reality motivate your evangelism. Someone in your life needs to hear Romans 3:24 this week. Be the one to tell them.

Write down one concrete action you will take this week in response to tonight's study. Do not let this truth remain theoretical. The Puritans measured the success of a sermon not by how much the hearer learned but by how much the hearer changed.


Gracious Father, we stand in awe before the mystery of the cross. How can You be both just and the justifier of sinners? Only through the propitiation of Your Son. We thank You that Christ bore our punishment, satisfied Your wrath, and opened the way for us to be declared righteous by faith alone. Kill our boasting. Deepen our worship. Let the reality of free justification produce in us lives of grateful obedience and passionate witness. We glory in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ, by whom the world is crucified unto us and we unto the world. Amen.

Goodnight. Remember, the goal of study is not information but transformation.
