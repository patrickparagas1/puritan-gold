"Can We Trust the Bible?"
Apologetics for Young Christians — Lesson 3

Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we address one of the most common and most important challenges to the Christian faith: Can we trust the Bible? Is the book we hold in our hands a reliable record of what actually happened, or has it been corrupted, altered, and distorted over the centuries? We will examine the evidence for the reliability and authority of Scripture from multiple angles, and we will discover that the Bible is the most well-attested, most carefully preserved, and most thoroughly vindicated document in the history of the world. Let us pray and begin.

Lord, Thy Word is truth. As we study the evidence for the trustworthiness of Scripture, strengthen our confidence in the Book that speaks with Thy voice. Thy Word is a lamp unto our feet, and a light unto our path. Help us to see that light more clearly today, and to share it with others who are walking in darkness. In Christ's name, Amen.

--- Lesson ---

The Bible is the foundation of the Christian faith. Everything we believe — about God, about salvation, about the meaning of life, about the future of the world — rests upon the authority and reliability of this Book. If the Bible is unreliable, then Christianity crumbles. But if the Bible is what it claims to be — the inspired, inerrant, trustworthy Word of the living God — then it deserves our complete confidence, our deepest study, and our wholehearted obedience.

The Bible makes extraordinary claims about itself. In 2 Timothy 3:16, the apostle Paul writes, "All scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness." The word translated "inspiration of God" is the Greek word theopneustos, which literally means "God-breathed." Paul is saying that Scripture is not merely a human product — it is the very breath of God, breathed out by the Almighty into the written words of the human authors.

The apostle Peter confirms this in 2 Peter 1:20-21: "Knowing this first, that no prophecy of the scripture is of any private interpretation. For the prophecy came not in old time by the will of man: but holy men of God spake as they were moved by the Holy Ghost." The prophets and apostles did not write according to their own ideas or interpretations. They were "moved by the Holy Ghost" — carried along, superintended, guided by the Spirit of God so that what they wrote was exactly what God intended them to write.

Jesus Himself affirmed the authority and reliability of Scripture in the strongest possible terms. In Matthew 5:18, He declared, "For verily I say unto you, Till heaven and earth pass, one jot or one tittle shall in no wise pass from the law, till all be fulfilled." A jot is the smallest letter in the Hebrew alphabet (yod), and a tittle is a tiny stroke or mark that distinguishes one Hebrew letter from another. Jesus is saying that Scripture is reliable down to the smallest detail — that even the tiniest features of the text are significant and trustworthy.

In John 10:35, Jesus stated simply, "The scripture cannot be broken." It cannot fail. It cannot be overturned. It cannot be shown to be false. This is the testimony of the Son of God Himself about the Book He came to fulfill.

Throughout His ministry, Jesus treated the Old Testament as historically accurate and authoritative. He referred to the creation of Adam and Eve as historical fact (Matthew 19:4-6). He affirmed the historicity of Noah and the flood (Matthew 24:37-39). He treated the account of Jonah and the great fish as a real event, using it as a type of His own death and resurrection: "For as Jonas was three days and three nights in the whale's belly; so shall the Son of man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth" (Matthew 12:40). He quoted from every major section of the Old Testament — the Law, the Prophets, and the Writings — and always treated these texts as the authoritative Word of God.

This is deeply significant for our study. If Jesus Christ is who He claimed to be — the Son of God, the divine Messiah — then His view of Scripture must be correct. And His view of Scripture was clear and unambiguous: it is the trustworthy, authoritative, inerrant Word of God.

The apostle Paul held the same view. In Romans 15:4, he wrote, "For whatsoever things were written aforetime were written for our learning, that we through patience and comfort of the scriptures might have hope." For Paul, every part of Scripture — everything that was "written aforetime" — was written for our instruction and benefit. Paul treated the Old Testament as historically reliable, theologically authoritative, and practically applicable. His letters are saturated with Old Testament quotations and allusions, demonstrating his absolute confidence in the trustworthiness of the sacred text.

The Puritan theologian Francis Turretin, born in 1623 in Geneva, Switzerland, who served as professor of theology at the Academy of Geneva — the institution founded by John Calvin — wrote one of the most comprehensive treatments of the doctrine of Scripture in his Institutes of Elenctic Theology (1679-1685). Turretin defended the inspiration, inerrancy, and infallibility of Scripture with meticulous care, arguing that the Bible is free from error in all that it affirms — not only in matters of faith and practice, but in matters of history, geography, and science as well. Turretin's defense of Scripture became foundational for Protestant theology and remains influential to this day.

The Puritan theologian William Whitaker, born in 1548 in Holme, Lancashire, England, who served as Regius Professor of Divinity at Cambridge, wrote his Disputations on Holy Scripture in 1588 — a work that was considered the most thorough Protestant defense of the authority and sufficiency of Scripture in the sixteenth century. Whitaker argued against Roman Catholic claims that Scripture needed to be supplemented by church tradition, defending the Reformation principle of sola Scriptura — the belief that Scripture alone is the ultimate authority for faith and practice. Whitaker demonstrated from the church fathers, from reason, and from Scripture itself that the Bible is sufficient to guide the believer in all matters pertaining to salvation and godly living.

But today, we are not merely going to assert that the Bible is trustworthy. We are going to examine the evidence — the hard, concrete, verifiable evidence — that demonstrates its trustworthiness. We will examine the evidence from four angles: manuscript evidence, archaeological confirmation, internal consistency, and fulfilled prophecy.

Let us begin with the manuscript evidence. This is one of the most powerful and most overlooked areas of apologetics.

One of the most common objections to the Bible goes something like this: "The Bible was written thousands of years ago. It has been copied and recopied by hand over centuries. How can we know that the text we have today is anything like the original? Haven't the scribes introduced errors, made changes, and corrupted the text beyond recognition?"

This objection sounds reasonable until you actually examine the evidence. And when you do, you discover that the manuscript evidence for the Bible — and especially for the New Testament — is overwhelmingly stronger than the evidence for any other document from the ancient world. It is not even close.

Let us consider the New Testament first. We possess over 5,800 Greek manuscripts of the New Testament — ranging from small fragments containing a few verses to complete copies of the entire New Testament. In addition to these Greek manuscripts, we possess over 10,000 manuscripts in Latin, over 9,000 manuscripts in other ancient languages (Syriac, Coptic, Armenian, Georgian, Ethiopic, Gothic, Slavic), and over 36,000 quotations from the New Testament found in the writings of the early church fathers. If every manuscript of the New Testament were destroyed overnight, we could reconstruct virtually the entire text from the quotations preserved in the writings of the early church fathers alone.

Now compare this to other ancient documents that no one questions. We possess about 1,800 manuscripts of Homer's Iliad — the second-best attested work from the ancient world — and the earliest substantial manuscripts date to the third century BC, roughly five hundred years after the original was composed. We possess only seven manuscripts of Plato's dialogues, the earliest dating roughly 1,200 years after Plato wrote. We have about ten manuscripts of Caesar's Gallic Wars, the earliest dating roughly one thousand years after Caesar's death. We have about eight manuscripts of Thucydides's History of the Peloponnesian War, the earliest dating roughly 1,300 years after the original. We have about twenty manuscripts of the Annals of Tacitus, the earliest dating roughly one thousand years after the original.

No classical scholar doubts the reliability of these texts. They are accepted as substantially accurate representations of what the original authors wrote. And yet the manuscript evidence for every one of these texts combined does not approach the manuscript evidence for the New Testament — not in the number of manuscripts, not in the antiquity of the manuscripts, and not in the closeness of the manuscripts to the original writings.

The earliest fragments of the New Testament date to within decades of the original compositions. The John Rylands Fragment (known as P52), a small papyrus fragment containing portions of the Gospel of John, has been dated to approximately AD 125 — perhaps only thirty to forty years after the Gospel of John was written. The Bodmer Papyri (P66 and P75), containing large portions of the Gospels of John and Luke, date to around AD 175-225. The Chester Beatty Papyri, containing substantial portions of the New Testament, date to the third century.

This is extraordinary. For most ancient documents, there is a gap of hundreds or even thousands of years between the original writing and the earliest surviving copy. For the New Testament, that gap is remarkably small — in some cases, less than a generation.

Furthermore, the variations between manuscripts are overwhelmingly minor. The vast majority are differences in spelling, word order, or the inclusion of a definite article — the kinds of variations that naturally arise when texts are copied by hand. The New Testament scholar Bruce Metzger, who served as professor of New Testament language and literature at Princeton Theological Seminary and who was one of the world's leading authorities on textual criticism, estimated that the New Testament text is 99.5 percent pure — that is, only about half a percent of the text involves any significant variation, and no essential Christian doctrine depends on a disputed reading.

The Puritan scholar Brian Walton, born in 1600 in Cleveland, Yorkshire, England, produced one of the most remarkable scholarly achievements of the seventeenth century: the London Polyglot Bible, published between 1654 and 1657. This massive six-volume work presented the biblical text in multiple languages — Hebrew, Greek, Latin, Syriac, Ethiopic, Arabic, and Persian — with critical notes comparing the various manuscripts and versions. Walton's Polyglot was a monumental contribution to the science of textual criticism, and it demonstrated the commitment of Puritan scholars to establishing the most accurate possible text of Scripture. Walton's work showed that even with all the manuscript variations, the text of Scripture had been transmitted with remarkable fidelity.

Now let us turn to the Old Testament. The preservation of the Old Testament text is itself a remarkable story. The Hebrew scribes who copied the Old Testament manuscripts — particularly the Masoretes, a group of Jewish scholars who worked between the sixth and tenth centuries AD — employed extraordinarily meticulous methods to ensure accuracy. They counted the letters in each book, identified the middle letter, counted the number of times each letter appeared, and compared their results against the previous copies. If a single error was found, the entire manuscript was destroyed and the work begun again. The Masoretes were not merely copyists. They were guardians of the sacred text, and their devotion to accuracy was unmatched in the ancient world.

But the most dramatic confirmation of the accuracy of the Old Testament text came in 1947, with the discovery of the Dead Sea Scrolls. A Bedouin shepherd named Muhammad edh-Dhib was searching for a lost goat in the caves near Qumran, on the northwest shore of the Dead Sea, when he stumbled upon a collection of ancient jars containing ancient scrolls. Over the following years, archaeologists discovered manuscripts in eleven caves near Qumran, including fragments and complete copies of every book of the Old Testament except Esther.

The significance of this discovery cannot be overstated. Before the Dead Sea Scrolls were found, the oldest known Hebrew manuscripts of the Old Testament dated to approximately AD 900 — the Masoretic manuscripts. The Dead Sea Scrolls, dating from approximately 250 BC to AD 68, pushed the manuscript evidence back by roughly a thousand years. And when scholars compared the Dead Sea Scrolls to the Masoretic manuscripts — comparing texts separated by a millennium of copying — they found an astonishing degree of accuracy.

The most dramatic example is the Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsa-a), a complete copy of the book of Isaiah dating to approximately 125 BC. When scholars compared this scroll to the Masoretic text of Isaiah, written over a thousand years later, they found that the two texts were virtually identical. The differences were almost entirely minor variations in spelling and grammar, with no significant changes in meaning. After a thousand years of hand copying, the text of Isaiah had been preserved with extraordinary fidelity.

This discovery confirmed what Christians had long believed: that God had providentially preserved His Word through the faithful labors of His people. The Puritan theologian William Ames, born in 1576 in Ipswich, Suffolk, England, who served as professor of theology at the University of Franeker in the Netherlands, wrote in his Marrow of Theology (1627) that the providential preservation of Scripture was itself evidence of its divine origin. Ames argued that only a Book whose Author is God could have been preserved with such accuracy over such a vast span of time.

The science of textual criticism — the scholarly discipline of comparing manuscripts, identifying variations, and determining the most accurate reading of the original text — has been practiced by Christian scholars for centuries, and its results are overwhelmingly reassuring. The English scholar John Mill, who served as principal of St. Edmund Hall, Oxford, spent thirty years comparing New Testament manuscripts and published his Greek New Testament in 1707, cataloguing approximately thirty thousand textual variants across the manuscript tradition. This number may sound alarming, but when you understand what it means, it is actually a testament to the accuracy of the text. The vast majority of these variants are trivial — differences in spelling, word order, or the inclusion of a common article. Many are found in only one or two manuscripts and are easily identified as scribal errors. Only a tiny fraction involves any meaningful difference in translation, and none of them affects any significant Christian doctrine.

The Puritan scholar John Lightfoot, born in 1602 in Stoke-upon-Trent, Staffordshire, England, who served as Vice-Chancellor of the University of Cambridge and as a member of the Westminster Assembly, was one of the most learned biblical scholars of the seventeenth century. Lightfoot devoted his life to the study of the biblical text in its original languages, producing detailed commentaries and studies that drew on rabbinic literature, historical sources, and textual analysis. His Horae Hebraicae et Talmudicae, published between 1658 and 1678, provided detailed historical and linguistic commentary on the New Testament, demonstrating the careful scholarship that Puritan divines brought to the study of Scripture.

It is also worth noting that the early church fathers quoted the New Testament so extensively in their own writings that scholars have identified over 36,000 New Testament quotations in the works of the church fathers. Justin Martyr, writing around AD 150, quoted from the Gospels, Acts, Romans, 1 Corinthians, Galatians, Ephesians, Colossians, 2 Thessalonians, Hebrews, and 1 Peter. Irenaeus, writing around AD 180, quoted from every book of the New Testament except Philemon, 2 Peter, 3 John, and Jude. Clement of Alexandria, Tertullian, Origen, and many others quoted the New Testament extensively. These quotations provide an independent witness to the text of the New Testament and confirm that the books we have today are the same books that the early church read and revered.

Let us now turn to our second line of evidence: archaeological confirmation.

Over the past two centuries, the science of archaeology has provided remarkable confirmation of the historical accuracy of the Bible. Again and again, discoveries in the field have vindicated biblical claims that critics once dismissed as fictional or legendary.

Consider some specific examples. The Hittite civilization, mentioned frequently in the Old Testament (Genesis 23, 2 Samuel 11, 1 Kings 10), was once considered fictional by critics who pointed out that no evidence of such a civilization had ever been found outside the Bible. Then, in 1906, the German archaeologist Hugo Winckler excavated the ancient city of Hattusa (modern Bogazkoy) in central Turkey and discovered the vast archives of the Hittite Empire — thousands of cuneiform tablets confirming that the Hittites were a major ancient civilization, just as the Bible had described.

The city of Nineveh, the capital of the Assyrian Empire mentioned in the books of Jonah and Nahum, was similarly considered legendary until the British archaeologist Austen Henry Layard excavated it in the 1840s, discovering the magnificent palace of King Sennacherib and confirming the biblical accounts of the Assyrian Empire.

The pool of Bethesda, described in John 5:2 as having "five porches," was once dismissed by critics as a fictional creation of the Gospel writer. Then archaeologists discovered the pool in the northeastern quarter of the Old City of Jerusalem, with its five porticoes, exactly as John had described. The pool of Siloam, mentioned in John 9:7 in connection with the healing of the man born blind, was discovered in 2004 during a sewage repair project in the City of David area of Jerusalem.

The existence of Pontius Pilate, the Roman governor who presided over the trial of Jesus, was confirmed by a stone inscription discovered in 1961 at Caesarea Maritima by the Italian archaeologist Antonio Frova. The inscription, known as the Pilate Stone, bears the name "Pontius Pilatus" and identifies him as the prefect of Judea — exactly as the Gospels describe.

The census described in Luke 2:1-3, which brought Mary and Joseph to Bethlehem, was once questioned by critics who doubted that the Romans conducted such censuses. But papyrus documents discovered in Egypt have confirmed that the Romans did indeed conduct periodic censuses for taxation purposes, and that these censuses sometimes required people to return to their ancestral homes — exactly as Luke describes.

In the New Testament, the evidence is equally impressive. The synagogue at Capernaum, where Jesus taught (Mark 1:21), has been excavated and its first-century foundations identified. The theater at Ephesus, where the riot against Paul took place (Acts 19:29), still stands today. The Gallio inscription, discovered at Delphi in 1905, mentions Gallio as proconsul of Achaia — the same Gallio before whom Paul appeared in Acts 18:12 — and allows scholars to date Paul's stay in Corinth to approximately AD 51-52. The Erastus inscription, found in Corinth, mentions a city official named Erastus — likely the same Erastus mentioned by Paul in Romans 16:23. The Politarch inscription, found in Thessalonica, confirms Luke's use of the unusual title "politarchs" for the city rulers in Acts 17:6 — a title that critics once claimed was inaccurate but that archaeology has now vindicated.

The list goes on and on. The walls of Jericho, the palace of King David, the tunnel of Hezekiah, the seal of Baruch (Jeremiah's scribe), the ossuary of Caiaphas (the high priest who presided over Jesus's trial), the inscription of Gallio (the Roman proconsul mentioned in Acts 18:12) — discovery after discovery has confirmed the historical accuracy of the biblical text.

The great archaeologist Sir William Ramsay, who began his career as a skeptic of the book of Acts, reversed his position after years of archaeological research in Asia Minor. Ramsay declared that Luke, the author of Acts, was "a historian of the first rank" whose accuracy in matters of geography, titles, and historical detail was consistently confirmed by the archaeological evidence.

Nelson Glueck, one of the most distinguished archaeologists of the twentieth century and president of Hebrew Union College, wrote that no archaeological discovery has ever contradicted a biblical reference. This remarkable assessment has been consistently confirmed by subsequent discoveries.

It is important to note that archaeology does not "prove" the Bible in the sense of confirming every spiritual truth it teaches. Archaeology is limited to the material world — it can confirm that a city existed, that a king ruled, or that a battle took place. It cannot confirm that God spoke to a prophet or that a miracle occurred. But what archaeology does demonstrate is that the Bible is historically accurate in the details that can be tested — and this gives us good reason to trust it in matters that lie beyond the reach of archaeological investigation.

The Puritan divine Edward Reynolds, born in 1599 in Southampton, Hampshire, England, who served as Bishop of Norwich and as a member of the Westminster Assembly, wrote about the reliability of Scripture in his Treatise of the Passions and Faculties of the Soul of Man (1640). Reynolds argued that the historical accuracy of Scripture was one of the evidences of its divine authorship. He wrote that God, who cannot lie (Titus 1:2), has given us a Book that is truthful in everything it affirms — in history, in theology, in prophecy, and in all matters upon which it speaks. Reynolds understood that the trustworthiness of Scripture in historical matters is a gateway to trusting it in spiritual matters as well.

The pattern is unmistakable and consistent: wherever the claims of the Bible can be tested against external evidence — manuscript evidence, archaeological discoveries, historical records, geographical details — the Bible has been vindicated. Not once, not twice, but again and again and again. This pattern of consistent accuracy in testable matters gives us strong warrant for trusting the Bible in matters that cannot be directly tested — matters of theology, morality, and spiritual truth.

Now let us turn to our third line of evidence: the internal consistency of the Bible.

The Bible was written by approximately forty different authors over a period of roughly 1,500 years. These authors came from vastly different backgrounds — Moses was a prince of Egypt and a shepherd, David was a king, Solomon was a philosopher, Isaiah was a court prophet, Amos was a herdsman, Daniel was a government official, Matthew was a tax collector, Luke was a physician, Peter was a fisherman, and Paul was a trained rabbi and Roman citizen. They wrote in three languages — Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek. They wrote on three continents — Asia, Africa, and Europe. They wrote in a wide variety of literary genres — history, poetry, prophecy, law, wisdom literature, apocalyptic vision, biography, and personal letters.

And yet, despite this extraordinary diversity of authorship, background, language, geography, and genre, the Bible tells a unified, coherent, harmonious story — the story of God's creation of the world, humanity's fall into sin, God's redemptive plan unfolding through the history of Israel, the coming of the Messiah, the establishment of the church, and the ultimate consummation of all things in the new heaven and new earth.

Consider the overarching narrative. In Genesis, God creates the world, places humanity in a garden, and establishes a covenant relationship with them. When humanity falls into sin, God promises a Redeemer — the seed of the woman who will crush the head of the serpent (Genesis 3:15). From that point forward, the entire Old Testament traces the unfolding of this promise through the patriarchs (Abraham, Isaac, Jacob), through the nation of Israel, through the monarchy (David), through the prophets, and through the exile and return — always pointing forward to the coming Messiah. Then, in the fullness of time (Galatians 4:4), the New Testament records the fulfillment of this promise in the person of Jesus Christ — His birth, His life, His ministry, His death, His resurrection, His ascension, and the establishment of His church. And the final book, Revelation, brings the story to its glorious conclusion — the return of Christ, the final judgment, and the establishment of the new heaven and the new earth.

This is a single, coherent, unified narrative — a story of creation, fall, redemption, and restoration — told across forty authors, fifteen hundred years, three continents, and three languages. The themes interlock. The prophecies connect. The types and shadows of the Old Testament find their fulfillment in the realities of the New. The sacrificial system of Leviticus points to the sacrifice of Christ. The exodus from Egypt foreshadows salvation from sin. The tabernacle and temple anticipate God dwelling with His people in the person of Jesus (John 1:14). The Passover lamb prefigures the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world (John 1:29). Every thread of the biblical tapestry weaves into a coherent, beautiful whole.

This unity is itself powerful evidence of divine authorship. No committee of human authors, separated by centuries and continents and cultures, could have produced such a coherent work without a divine Author guiding the process. The Puritan theologian John Owen made this point with characteristic force in his work The Divine Original of the Scriptures (1659). Owen argued that the unity, harmony, and consistency of Scripture — despite its extraordinary diversity of human authorship — is inexplicable apart from the overarching authorship of the Holy Spirit. Owen wrote that Scripture bears within itself the "self-evidencing power" of divine truth — that it authenticates itself to the believing reader as the Word of God.

The Westminster Confession of Faith, drafted in 1646 by an assembly of Puritan divines meeting at Westminster Abbey in London, expressed this truth beautifully in its first chapter: "The heavens and the earth, common providence, do so far manifest the goodness, wisdom, and power of God, as to leave men inexcusable; yet are they not sufficient to give that knowledge of God, and of His will, which is necessary unto salvation. Therefore it pleased the Lord, at sundry times, and in divers manners, to reveal Himself, and to declare that His will unto His Church; and afterwards for the better preserving and propagating of the truth, and for the more sure establishment and comfort of the Church against the corruption of the flesh, and the malice of Satan and of the world, to commit the same wholly unto writing; which maketh the Holy Scripture to be most necessary."

The Westminster Divines understood that Scripture is not merely a human book about God. It is God's own Word, committed to writing for the preservation, propagation, and establishment of His truth. And its internal consistency is one of the hallmarks of its divine origin.

Now let us turn to our fourth and final line of evidence: fulfilled prophecy.

The Bible contains hundreds of specific, detailed predictions about future events — predictions that were written centuries before the events occurred and that were fulfilled with remarkable precision. This is one of the most distinctive features of the Bible and one of the most powerful evidences of its divine origin.

Consider the prophecies about the Messiah. The Old Testament contains over three hundred specific prophecies about the coming Messiah — predictions about His birthplace, His lineage, His ministry, His manner of death, His burial, and His resurrection. These prophecies were written hundreds of years before Jesus was born, and they were fulfilled in His life with extraordinary precision.

Micah 5:2, written approximately 700 years before Christ, predicted that the Messiah would be born in Bethlehem: "But thou, Bethlehem Ephratah, though thou be little among the thousands of Judah, yet out of thee shall he come forth unto me that is to be ruler in Israel; whose goings forth have been from of old, from everlasting." Jesus was born in Bethlehem (Matthew 2:1).

Isaiah 7:14, written approximately 700 years before Christ, predicted that the Messiah would be born of a virgin: "Therefore the Lord himself shall give you a sign; Behold, a virgin shall conceive, and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel." Jesus was born of the virgin Mary (Matthew 1:18-25).

Genesis 49:10, written approximately 1,700 years before Christ, predicted that the Messiah would come from the tribe of Judah: "The sceptre shall not depart from Judah, nor a lawgiver from between his feet, until Shiloh come; and unto him shall the gathering of the people be." Jesus was of the tribe of Judah (Matthew 1:2-3).

Isaiah 53, written approximately 700 years before Christ, provides an astonishingly detailed description of the Messiah's suffering and death — His rejection, His silence before His accusers, His substitutionary suffering for the sins of others, His death with the wicked, and His burial with the rich. Every detail was fulfilled in the crucifixion and burial of Jesus Christ.

Psalm 22, written by David approximately 1,000 years before Christ, describes the experience of crucifixion in vivid detail — "they pierced my hands and my feet" (v. 16), "they part my garments among them, and cast lots upon my vesture" (v. 18), "all my bones are out of joint" (v. 14) — centuries before crucifixion was even invented as a method of execution. The Romans did not begin practicing crucifixion until the third century BC, and yet David described it with eerie precision a thousand years before Christ.

Daniel 9:25-26, written approximately 530 years before Christ, predicted the precise timing of the Messiah's appearance and death — a prophecy that was fulfilled to the year by the ministry and crucifixion of Jesus.

Zechariah 11:12-13, written approximately 500 years before Christ, predicted that the Messiah would be betrayed for thirty pieces of silver and that the money would be thrown into the house of the Lord and used to purchase a potter's field. This was fulfilled with precise detail in the betrayal of Jesus by Judas Iscariot (Matthew 26:15, 27:3-10).

Hosea 11:1, written approximately 750 years before Christ, declared, "When Israel was a child, then I loved him, and called my son out of Egypt." This was fulfilled when the infant Jesus was taken to Egypt to escape Herod's persecution and then called back to Israel (Matthew 2:14-15). Isaiah 9:1-2 predicted that the Messiah's ministry would begin in Galilee — in "the land of Zebulun and the land of Naphtali" — a prophecy fulfilled when Jesus began His public ministry in Capernaum (Matthew 4:12-16). Zechariah 9:9 predicted that the Messiah would enter Jerusalem "riding upon an ass, and upon a colt the foal of an ass" — fulfilled in Jesus's triumphal entry (Matthew 21:1-7). Psalm 41:9 predicted that the Messiah would be betrayed by a close friend — "Yea, mine own familiar friend, in whom I trusted, which did eat of my bread, hath lifted up his heel against me" — fulfilled in the betrayal by Judas (John 13:18-26).

Isaiah 50:6 predicted that the Messiah would be beaten and spat upon: "I gave my back to the smiters, and my cheeks to them that plucked off the hair: I hid not my face from shame and spitting." This was fulfilled during the trial and abuse of Jesus before His crucifixion (Matthew 26:67, 27:26-30). Numbers 9:12 and Psalm 34:20 predicted that none of the Messiah's bones would be broken — fulfilled when the Roman soldiers, finding Jesus already dead, did not break His legs as they did to the two criminals crucified with Him (John 19:33-36). Zechariah 12:10 predicted that God's people would "look upon me whom they have pierced" — fulfilled when the Roman soldier pierced Jesus's side with a spear (John 19:34-37).

The sheer number and specificity of these fulfilled prophecies is staggering. No other religious text in the history of the world contains anything comparable. The Quran, the Hindu Vedas, the Buddhist sutras, the writings of Confucius — none of these contain specific, dated, verifiable predictions that have been fulfilled with the precision of biblical prophecy. This is uniquely a feature of the Bible, and it powerfully demonstrates its divine origin.

The mathematician Peter Stoner, in his book Science Speaks, calculated the probability of any one person fulfilling just eight of these Messianic prophecies by chance. His conservative estimate was one in ten to the seventeenth power — that is, one in 100,000,000,000,000,000. To illustrate this probability, Stoner asked his readers to imagine covering the entire state of Texas with silver dollars two feet deep, marking one of them, and then asking a blindfolded person to pick the marked one on the first try. Those are the odds of one person fulfilling just eight prophecies by chance. The probability of fulfilling all three hundred-plus Messianic prophecies by chance is so small as to be effectively impossible.

The Puritan commentator Matthew Henry, born in 1662 in Broad Oak, Flintshire, Wales, whose Commentary on the Whole Bible (published 1708-1710) remains one of the most beloved commentaries in the English language, wrote extensively about the fulfillment of prophecy as evidence of divine inspiration. Henry observed that the prophets wrote about events they could not have known about through natural means — events that occurred centuries after their deaths — and that the precise fulfillment of their predictions demonstrates that they were writing under the guidance of the Holy Spirit.

Henry wrote on Isaiah 53, "Who could have described the humiliation and exaltation of the Messiah with such exactness, so long before he came, but He who has all events within his own prescience?" Henry's point is crucial: the fulfillment of prophecy is not something that can be explained by coincidence, guesswork, or human foresight. It requires a supernatural explanation — an Author who knows the end from the beginning and who guided the human writers to record His plan with precision and clarity.

Beyond the Messianic prophecies, the Bible also contains fulfilled prophecies about nations and cities. Ezekiel 26, written in the sixth century BC, predicted with remarkable specificity the destruction of the city of Tyre — that it would be attacked by many nations, that its walls and towers would be broken down, that its rubble would be scraped into the sea, and that it would become a bare rock used for spreading fishing nets. History records that these prophecies were fulfilled in stages — first by Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon, who besieged the mainland city, and then by Alexander the Great, who in 332 BC scraped the rubble of the old city into the sea to build a causeway to the island fortress, exactly as Ezekiel had predicted. Isaiah 13:19-22 predicted the permanent desolation of Babylon — and the once-great city has indeed remained uninhabited for centuries, exactly as the prophet foretold. Daniel 2 and Daniel 7 outlined the succession of world empires — Babylon, Medo-Persia, Greece, and Rome — centuries before the later empires arose. The accuracy of these predictions is so striking that skeptical scholars have attempted to date the book of Daniel to the second century BC, after the events occurred, but this late dating faces serious scholarly objections and has been challenged by conservative scholars with compelling arguments.

The evidence for the reliability of the Bible is powerful and cumulative. No single argument by itself settles the question, but taken together, the manuscript evidence, the archaeological confirmation, the internal consistency, and the fulfilled prophecy create a compelling case that the Bible is exactly what it claims to be: the trustworthy, inspired, infallible Word of God.

As we draw this lesson to a close, let us consider what the Bible itself says about its own enduring power. Isaiah 40:8 declares, "The grass withereth, the flower fadeth: but the word of our God shall stand for ever." The apostle Peter echoes this in 1 Peter 1:24-25: "For all flesh is as grass, and all the glory of man as the flower of grass. The grass withereth, and the flower thereof falleth away: But the word of the Lord endureth for ever. And this is the word which by the gospel is preached unto you." The Word of God is not a fragile, perishable document. It is an eternal, indestructible reality. Empires have risen and fallen. Civilizations have come and gone. Critics have attacked the Bible from every conceivable angle. And yet the Bible stands — more widely read, more deeply studied, more profoundly influential than any other book in history.

The French skeptic Voltaire, writing in the eighteenth century, famously predicted that within a hundred years of his death, Christianity would be swept from existence and the Bible would be found only in museums. Voltaire died in 1778. Within fifty years, the Geneva Bible Society was using Voltaire's own house as a depot for distributing Bibles. The Bible outlasted its most confident critic — as it has outlasted every critic before and since.

The great Puritan pastor Charles Spurgeon, preaching in London in the nineteenth century, said of the Bible, "Defend the Bible? I would as soon defend a lion. Just let it loose; it will defend itself." Spurgeon's confidence was not in human arguments but in the power of the Word itself — the power of God speaking through His written revelation.

The Puritan pastor Thomas Watson, who served at St. Stephen's Walbrook in London, wrote in his Body of Divinity, "The Scripture is the library of the Holy Ghost." It is the place where God has deposited His truth for the benefit of His people and the salvation of the world. We can trust it. We can study it. We can memorize it. We can build our lives upon it. And we can share it with confidence, knowing that it will accomplish the purposes for which God sent it (Isaiah 55:11).
