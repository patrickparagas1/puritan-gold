PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Pastoral Encouragement, Day 5
"Redeeming the Time"
Richard Baxter — Pastoral Counsel on Stewardship of Hours


Good evening. The Christian life is a marathon, not a sprint. And every marathon runner needs a voice along the way saying: keep going, you are not alone, the finish line is real. That is tonight's word for you.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Ephesians 5:15-17:

"See then that ye walk circumspectly, not as fools, but as wise, Redeeming the time, because the days are evil. Wherefore be ye not unwise, but understanding what the will of the Lord is."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


The apostle Paul's command to 'redeem the time' is one of the most searching in all of Scripture. The word 'redeem' means to buy back, to rescue from waste, to make the most of. Time, once spent, cannot be recovered. Every hour that passes is an hour that will never return. And yet how carelessly most of us spend this most precious of commodities.

Richard Baxter, the tireless pastor of Kidderminster, was perhaps the most time-conscious of all the Puritans. His own life was a testimony to the principle he taught: though plagued by chronic illness that often confined him to bed, Baxter produced an astonishing volume of pastoral and theological work. He did so because he understood that time is a sacred trust. Every moment is a gift from God, and we will give account for how we used it.

In 'A Christian Directory,' Baxter addresses the stewardship of time with characteristic thoroughness. He begins with a sobering observation: most people do not waste their time on overtly sinful activities. They waste it on trivial ones. They fritter away hours on idle amusements, aimless conversation, excessive sleep, and activities that are not sinful in themselves but are sinful in their excess because they crowd out greater duties.

Baxter offers several practical counsels for redeeming the time. First, begin each day with God. The man who gives the first hour of his day to Scripture and prayer sets the trajectory for everything that follows. Baxter writes that the morning hour is the rudder of the day: if it is set right, the whole day tends in the right direction. If it is neglected, the day drifts.

Second, Baxter counsels planning. Not the rigid, anxious planning that leaves no room for the unexpected, but the wise ordering of duties that ensures the most important things receive the most attention. Baxter writes that the man who begins his day without a plan is like a soldier who enters battle without a strategy: he may fight bravely, but he will fight inefficiently. Know what your duties are. Know their relative importance. Give the best of your time and energy to the highest duties.

Third, Baxter warns against the tyranny of the urgent. Many men spend their days responding to whatever demand is loudest, most pressing, or most immediately painful. But the urgent is not always the important. The father who spends all his time putting out fires at work while neglecting the spiritual formation of his children has allowed the urgent to devour the important. Baxter counsels deliberate attention to the things that matter most, even when they do not press upon us with the same urgency as lesser matters.

Fourth, Baxter addresses the particular danger of digital distraction — or rather, the seventeenth-century equivalent. In Baxter's day, the temptation was idle company, excessive recreation, and the reading of worthless literature. In our day, it is the smartphone, social media, and the endless scroll. The principle is the same: anything that consumes time without producing spiritual, relational, or vocational fruit is a thief, and we must guard against it with the vigilance of a man guarding his most precious possession.

Patrick, how are you spending your time? This is not a question about productivity in the worldly sense. It is a question about stewardship in the biblical sense. Are the most important things in your life — your walk with God, your marriage, your children, your calling — receiving the best of your time and attention? Or are they getting the leftovers, the scraps that remain after lesser things have consumed the best hours of your day?

Baxter's final counsel is one of hope: it is never too late to begin redeeming the time. The hours that are past are past, but the hours that remain are still yours to steward. Begin today. This very hour. Give it to God and trust Him with the results.


Let these questions search your heart. Answer honestly before God.

Question 1: If I honestly audited how I spend my time each day, would the allocation reflect my stated priorities?

Question 2: What is the single biggest time-waster in my life, and what practical step can I take this week to reclaim that time for greater purposes?

Question 3: Am I giving the first and best of my time to God, or am I offering Him the exhausted remnants at the end of the day?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Lord, teach me to number my days that I may apply my heart unto wisdom. Forgive me for the hours I have wasted on things that do not matter. Help me to redeem the time that remains, giving the best of my energy and attention to the things You value most: my walk with You, my family, and the calling You have placed upon my life. Amen.

Goodnight. Remember: the same God who spoke to the Puritans speaks to you.
