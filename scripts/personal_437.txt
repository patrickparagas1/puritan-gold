PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Doctrine Deep-Dive, Day 6
"The Work of the Holy Spirit"
Pneumatology Explored Through the Puritans

---

Welcome, friend. The Puritans called doctrine the bones of the body of divinity. Without strong bones, the body collapses. Tonight we strengthen those bones as we study a vital truth together.

Tonight we draw from the work of John Owen, specifically from "A Discourse Concerning the Holy Spirit." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.

--- Scripture ---

Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is John 16:13-14:

"Howbeit when he, the Spirit of truth, is come, he will guide you into all truth: for he shall not speak of himself; but whatsoever he shall hear, that shall he speak: and he will shew you things to come. He shall glorify me: for he shall receive of mine, and shall shew it unto you."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.

--- Doctrine Study ---

The doctrine of the Holy Spirit — pneumatology — is among the most neglected and yet most practically vital of all Christian doctrines. Without the Spirit, there is no regeneration, no sanctification, no assurance, no power for service, no communion with God. The Spirit is, as Owen writes, the immediate agent of all divine operations upon the souls of men. Everything that God does in us, He does by His Spirit.

John Owen's 'A Discourse Concerning the Holy Spirit' is the most comprehensive and profound treatment of this doctrine in the English language. Owen wrote it because he saw two equal and opposite errors in the church of his day: those who virtually ignored the Spirit, reducing Christianity to a system of moral duties and intellectual beliefs, and those who claimed extraordinary operations of the Spirit while abandoning sound doctrine and orderly worship. Owen charts a biblical course between these extremes.

Owen begins with the Person of the Spirit. The Holy Spirit is not a force, an influence, or an impersonal power. He is the Third Person of the Trinity, fully God, co-equal and co-eternal with the Father and the Son. He has personal attributes: He knows, He wills, He feels. He can be grieved (Ephesians 4:30), quenched (1 Thessalonians 5:19), resisted (Acts 7:51), and lied to (Acts 5:3). All of these require personhood. Owen insists that right doctrine about the Spirit's Person is essential to right relationship with the Spirit.

Owen then expounds the Spirit's work in four great areas. First, the Spirit's work in creation. The Spirit brooded over the face of the waters at the beginning, bringing order from chaos, beauty from formlessness. All the beauty, complexity, and order of the natural world is the Spirit's handiwork. Owen writes that the man who marvels at creation but does not worship the Spirit who fashioned it has missed the lesson.

Second, the Spirit's work in revelation. The Spirit inspired the Scriptures, not by dictation but by superintending the human authors so that what they wrote was exactly what God intended. Owen is careful to maintain both the divine origin and the human character of Scripture: the Spirit did not erase the personalities of the biblical writers but employed them. The result is a book that is fully human and fully divine — a written word that, like the incarnate Word, bears the marks of both.

Third, and most extensively, Owen addresses the Spirit's work in salvation. The Spirit regenerates the dead sinner, giving him a new heart and new spiritual life. Owen describes regeneration as a supernatural, immediate, and irresistible work of divine power. It is not the result of human decision, moral effort, or religious education, though God may use these as instruments. The decisive act is the Spirit's: He opens blind eyes, unstops deaf ears, and gives life to dead souls.

After regeneration, the Spirit sanctifies the believer, progressively conforming him to the image of Christ. Owen describes sanctification as the Spirit's ongoing war against remaining sin. The Spirit works through means — the Word of God, prayer, the fellowship of believers, the sacraments — but He is never bound to means. He is a sovereign Person who works when, where, and how He pleases.

Fourth, Owen addresses the Spirit's work in assurance. The Spirit 'beareth witness with our spirit, that we are the children of God' (Romans 8:16). This witness is not a mystical experience disconnected from evidence; it is the Spirit's illumination of the objective evidences of grace in the believer's life. The Spirit shows us our faith, our love, our repentance, and testifies that these are genuine works of God in us.

Owen also addresses the Spirit's role in prayer. Romans 8:26 teaches that 'the Spirit also helpeth our infirmities: for we know not what we should pray for as we ought: but the Spirit itself maketh intercession for us with groanings which cannot be uttered.' Owen explains that every genuine prayer is a prayer empowered by the Spirit. When you feel drawn to pray, that drawing is the Spirit's work. When you find words to express your heart to God, those words are given by the Spirit. When you cannot find words at all but simply groan before God, that groaning is the Spirit's intercession within you.

Owen also addresses the Spirit's work in the believer's growth in holiness. It is the Spirit who produces the fruit described in Galatians 5:22-23: love, joy, peace, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, faith, meekness, temperance. These are not natural virtues that can be cultivated by moral effort; they are supernatural graces produced by the Spirit's indwelling presence. The man who tries to produce these qualities by willpower alone will produce only a counterfeit — an external appearance of virtue without the inward reality. True spiritual fruit grows from the inside out, as the Spirit transforms the heart and the transformed heart produces transformed behavior.

The practical question is unavoidable: how much of your Christian life is lived in conscious dependence upon the Holy Spirit? Or have you settled into a Christianity of mere duty, discipline, and effort — all good things, but all powerless without the Spirit who gives them life?

--- Reflection ---

Reflection is where truth becomes personal. Consider these questions carefully.

Question 1: Do I relate to the Holy Spirit as a divine Person — praying to Him, depending on Him, being careful not to grieve Him — or as an impersonal force?

Question 2: In what area of my life am I most trying to operate by my own effort rather than in dependence upon the Spirit?

Question 3: How does Owen's teaching on the Spirit's role in assurance speak to my own struggles with doubt and uncertainty?

Question 4: What would it look like for me to be more consciously dependent on the Spirit in my daily walk?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.

--- Closing Prayer ---

Holy Spirit, Third Person of the blessed Trinity, I confess that I have too often neglected You. I have lived as though the Christian life were a matter of my own effort and discipline, forgetting that every good thing in me is Your work. Teach me to depend upon You for regeneration's fruit, for sanctification's progress, for assurance's comfort, and for prayer's power. Glorify Christ in me today. Amen.
