PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"I Waited Patiently for the Lord"
June 18, 2026

---

"I waited patiently for the Lord; and he inclined unto me, and heard my cry. He brought me up also out of an horrible pit, out of the miry clay, and set my feet upon a rock, and established my goings. And he hath put a new song in my mouth, even praise unto our God: many shall see it, and fear, and shall trust in the Lord." -- Psalm 40:1-3

Patience is not a natural virtue of the human heart. We are creatures of haste, eager for immediate results, impatient with delay, frustrated by the slow workings of providence. And yet patience is one of the cardinal graces of the Christian life, for it is the expression of trust in a God whose ways are not our ways and whose timing is not our timing. David tells us that he waited patiently for the Lord, and the very fact that he mentions it suggests that the waiting was neither easy nor short. The Hebrew text literally reads, "Waiting, I waited" -- an intensive construction that implies a prolonged and deliberate exercise of patience in the face of circumstances that cried out for immediate intervention.

And what was the result of this patient waiting? "He inclined unto me, and heard my cry." God did not ignore David's patience. He did not leave him to wait forever in vain. He inclined -- He leaned down, as it were, from the heights of heaven toward the depths of David's distress, and He heard. There is something profoundly tender in this image of God inclining His ear to the cry of His servant. It speaks of attention, of interest, of sympathy, of a willingness not merely to hear but to listen, not merely to acknowledge but to act.

Spurgeon told his congregation that the patience of the saints is never wasted, for God is never idle during the seasons of our waiting. While David waited, God was working. While the pit seemed inescapable and the clay seemed to grip his feet with unbreakable tenacity, the hand of the Almighty was preparing a deliverance more complete and more glorious than anything David could have imagined. And this is always the way of God. His delays are not denials. His silences are not refusals. He is working all things together for good, even when we cannot see the machinery turning or hear the wheels of providence grinding toward our salvation.

"He brought me up also out of an horrible pit, out of the miry clay." What a vivid picture of the human condition apart from the grace of God. A horrible pit -- a place of darkness, of confinement, of despair, where no ladder reaches and no rope descends and no human hand can pull us free. Miry clay -- a substance that sucks at the feet and drags the struggling victim deeper with every effort to escape. This is what sin does to the soul. It traps us. It holds us fast. It mires us in a quicksand of guilt and shame and spiritual impotence from which no amount of human effort can extricate us. And the more we struggle in our own strength, the deeper we sink.

But God reaches down. "He brought me up." Not David brought himself up, not David's friends pulled him out, not David's own strength and ingenuity found a way of escape, but God. God reached into the pit. God grasped the sinking man. God lifted him out of the miry clay and set his feet upon a rock. And this is the testimony of every truly converted soul. We did not save ourselves. We were saved. We were brought up by a power infinitely greater than our own, a grace that came to us when we were helpless and hopeless and sinking fast.

William Bridge preached upon this psalm with great pastoral warmth, encouraging his hearers who were in the pit of affliction to take comfort from David's testimony. If God delivered David, He can deliver you. If God heard David's cry, He can hear yours. If God was willing to incline His ear to a man trapped in the miry clay, He is willing to incline His ear to you in whatever pit of trouble or sorrow or temptation you may find yourself. The God who saved David has not changed. His arm is not shortened. His ear is not heavy. His compassion has not grown cold. He is the same God today as He was three thousand years ago, and He is as ready to save as He ever was.

"And he hath put a new song in my mouth, even praise unto our God." The deliverance produces praise, and the praise is new because the experience is fresh. David does not recite an old hymn that he has sung a hundred times before. God puts a new song in his mouth -- a song born out of his own recent experience of divine grace, a song that is wet with the tears of the pit and radiant with the joy of deliverance. And this song has a missionary purpose: "Many shall see it, and fear, and shall trust in the Lord." The testimony of one delivered soul becomes the means of drawing other souls to faith. When the world sees a man who has been brought up out of the horrible pit and set upon the rock, singing a new song of praise to God, something happens. Fear comes upon the careless. Curiosity is awakened in the indifferent. And trust is kindled in the seeking heart. The transformed life is the most powerful sermon that can ever be preached.

Let us therefore wait patiently for the Lord, knowing that He hears our cry and that in His own perfect time He will bring us up out of every pit and set our feet upon the rock. And when He does, let us sing the new song that He puts in our mouths, that many may see and fear and trust in the Lord. Amen.
