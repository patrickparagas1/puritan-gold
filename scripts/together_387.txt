PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 26
"Christ and the Song — The Puritan Allegorical Reading"
Song of Solomon Overview — Christ's Love for His Church


Welcome, dear couple. Having walked through the Song of Solomon verse by verse, we now spend three days drawing out the Puritan wisdom that flows from this book. Tonight, we consider what the Puritans saw as the Song's deepest meaning: the love of Christ for His church — and how that divine love is the foundation of every healthy marriage.

Let us read Ephesians 5:25-27:

"Husbands, love your wives, even as Christ also loved the church, and gave himself for it; That he might sanctify and cleanse it with the washing of water by the word, That he might present it to himself a glorious church, not having spot, or wrinkle, or any such thing; but that it should be holy and without blemish."


The Puritans read the Song of Solomon on two levels simultaneously. They saw it as a celebration of human marital love — as we have studied all month — and as an extended allegory of Christ's love for His church. Far from seeing these two readings as contradictory, they saw them as complementary. Richard Sibbes wrote that human marriage and divine love are so deeply intertwined that you cannot fully understand one without the other. Christ's love for His church is the pattern; human marriage is the copy. But the copy, when faithful, reveals the glory of the pattern.

Consider the parallels that the Puritans traced throughout the Song. The bridegroom pursues the bride — just as Christ pursues the sinner. The bride is insecure about her darkness — just as the sinner is aware of his unworthiness. The bridegroom declares "thou art all fair; there is no spot in thee" — just as Christ declares the justified sinner righteous, without spot or blemish. The bride searches through the dark night — just as the believer sometimes feels the absence of Christ's sensible presence. The reconciliation brings deeper love — just as the restoration of communion with Christ after a season of spiritual dryness brings deeper intimacy.

Thomas Goodwin, whose "Heart of Christ" has been one of the richest Puritan works on divine affection, wrote that Christ's heart toward His church is exactly the heart of the bridegroom in the Song. He delights in her. He praises her. He pursues her when she wanders. He forgives her when she hesitates. He speaks over her, "Thou art all fair." And His love is as strong as death — indeed, stronger, for He passed through death and came out the other side, still loving, still pursuing, still calling.

What does this mean for your marriage? It means that your marriage has a script — a divine model to follow. Husbands, you are called to love as Christ loved: sacrificially, unconditionally, with a love that gives itself for the sanctification and flourishing of the beloved. This does not mean perfection. It means a direction, a trajectory, a persistent returning to the pattern of self-giving love.

Wives, you are called to respond as the church responds to Christ: with trust, with delight, with the kind of receptive love that opens the garden gate and says, "Come in." This is not passive submission. It is active, joyful, confident reception of the love that is offered.

William Gouge wrote that the greatest problem in most marriages is that husbands do not love as Christ loved — with patient, pursuing, self-sacrificing devotion — and wives do not respond as the church should respond — with willing, joyful, trusting reception. When both roles are filled, the marriage sings. It becomes a living Song of Solomon.

The Puritans also found comfort in the Song's portrait of imperfect love. The bride hesitates. She fails to open the door. She is insecure. She loses her beloved in the night. And yet the Song ends not with divorce but with longing — still reaching, still calling, still hoping. Your marriage is not disqualified by failure. It is defined by return. The pattern of the Gospel is not sinless perfection but repeated repentance and renewed grace.

Jeremiah Burroughs wrote that the Christian marriage, like the Christian life, is a story of "fallings and risings, wanderings and returnings, departures and reconciliations — all held together by the covenant grace of God." Your marriage is held together not by your performance but by the covenant you made and the God who witnessed it.

Tonight, let the Song of Solomon wash over your marriage one final time as an allegory of divine love. Your marriage is a picture of the greatest love story ever told: the Bridegroom who crossed heaven and earth to claim His bride.


Turn to each other and consider these questions.

Question 1: How does seeing your marriage as a picture of Christ and the church change the way you approach your daily interactions with each other?

Question 2: Where does your marriage most clearly reflect the love of Christ? Where does it most need to grow into that pattern?

Question 3: The Song's portrait of love includes imperfection, failure, and reconciliation. How does this honest picture give you hope for your own marriage?


Tonight's prayer focus: Pray that your marriage would increasingly mirror the love of Christ for His church. Ask for the grace to love sacrificially, to receive joyfully, and to reconcile quickly. Thank God for the divine pattern and pray for the strength to follow it.


Your marriage is a window into the heart of God. Through your love, the world sees — however imperfectly — the love of Christ for His bride. What a calling. What a privilege. Goodnight, dear couple.
