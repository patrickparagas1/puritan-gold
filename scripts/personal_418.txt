PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Doctrine Deep-Dive, Day 18
"A Theology of Prayer"
What the Puritans Taught About Communion with God


Welcome to our doctrine study. What we believe about God matters. It matters infinitely. Tonight we go deep into a truth that has anchored the church through centuries of storm and struggle.

Tonight we draw from the work of Multiple (Bunyan, Owen, Henry), specifically from "Various works on prayer." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Philippians 4:6:

"Be careful for nothing; but in every thing by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known unto God."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


The Puritans were, above all else, men and women of prayer. Their theological rigor was matched by their devotional fervor. They did not merely write about prayer; they practiced it with a discipline and depth that puts most modern Christians to shame. Understanding their theology of prayer will transform the way you approach the throne of grace.

John Bunyan, in 'Prayer' (sometimes titled 'I Will Pray with the Spirit'), provides one of the most stirring Puritan definitions of prayer: 'Prayer is a sincere, sensible, affectionate pouring out of the heart or soul to God, through Christ, in the strength and assistance of the Holy Spirit, for such things as God hath promised, or according to the Word, for the good of the church, with submission to the will of God.' Every phrase of this definition is weighted with theological meaning.

'Sincere' — prayer must be genuine, not performed. Bunyan warns against mere formal repetition of words without the engagement of the heart. The Pharisees prayed publicly and eloquently, but their prayers were an abomination because they lacked sincerity.

'Sensible, affectionate' — prayer engages the whole person: mind and emotions together. The Puritans rejected the notion that prayer is merely intellectual. Bunyan writes that the man who prays without feeling may as well be reading a grocery list. True prayer arises from a heart that feels its need, that delights in God, that aches for holiness, that longs for the coming of Christ's kingdom.

'Through Christ' — all prayer comes to the Father through the mediating work of the Son. Without Christ, we have no access to God. Our prayers are heard not because of our worthiness but because of His. Bunyan insists that the believer must approach God always and only through the merits of the Savior.

'In the strength and assistance of the Holy Spirit' — the Spirit is the engine of prayer. Paul teaches in Romans 8:26 that the Spirit helps our infirmities, making intercession for us with groanings that cannot be uttered. Bunyan argues that prayer without the Spirit's aid is like a bird trying to fly without wings: the form may be present, but the power is absent.

John Owen, in 'Communion with God,' explores the relational dimension of prayer. For Owen, prayer is not merely asking God for things; it is communion with Him. Owen describes the Christian life as a fellowship with all three Persons of the Trinity: the Father's love, the Son's grace, and the Spirit's comfort. Prayer is the primary means by which this fellowship is maintained and deepened.

Owen identifies several types of prayer that the believer should practice. Adoration: praising God for who He is, contemplating His attributes, and delighting in His glory. Confession: honest, specific acknowledgment of sin, not in general terms but in the particular. Thanksgiving: remembering and recounting God's mercies, both common and special. Supplication: bringing specific needs and desires before God. Intercession: praying for others, especially the church, the lost, and the suffering.

Matthew Henry, in 'A Method for Prayer,' provides one of the most practical guides to the content of prayer. Henry structures prayer around the Scriptures, showing how every type of prayer can be enriched by biblical language. He urges believers to pray the Scriptures: to take the promises of God and turn them into petitions, to take the commands of God and turn them into confessions, to take the attributes of God and turn them into praises.

Henry also addresses the common complaint: 'I do not know what to say when I pray.' His answer is simple: let God's Word teach you to pray. If you do not know how to praise, read a psalm aloud to God. If you do not know how to confess, read the law and let it show you your sin. If you do not know what to ask for, read the promises and ask God to fulfill them.

The Puritans also taught the importance of scheduled prayer times. While they affirmed that Christians should 'pray without ceasing' (1 Thessalonians 5:17), they also insisted on set times of concentrated prayer. Most Puritan divines practiced morning and evening prayer as a minimum, with additional times of prayer throughout the day as opportunity allowed.

The challenge for you today is not to become a more eloquent pray-er but a more faithful one. The man who prays briefly but sincerely is nearer to God than the man who prays at length but without heart. Begin where you are. Pray with the Scripture open. Trust the Spirit to help you. And come to the throne of grace with confidence, for Christ has opened the way.


The Puritans kept spiritual journals. Consider writing your answers to these questions.

Question 1: Does my prayer life reflect the richness of Bunyan's definition — sincere, affectionate, through Christ, in the Spirit?

Question 2: Am I practicing all the types of prayer Owen identifies — adoration, confession, thanksgiving, supplication, and intercession — or am I limited to asking for things?

Question 3: How can I incorporate Scripture more deeply into my prayers, following Henry's counsel?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Lord, teach me to pray. I confess that my prayers are often hasty, distracted, and self-centered. Deepen my communion with You. Help me to pray through Christ, in the Spirit, according to Your Word. Make prayer not a duty I perform but a fellowship I cherish. Amen.

Go in peace. The God who began a good work in you will bring it to completion.
