PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Guided Puritan Reading, Day 19
"The Immutability of God"
Stephen Charnock — The Attributes of God: Immutability


Welcome, dear listener. The practice of reading great Christian literature is a form of spiritual formation. It shapes how we think, how we pray, and how we see God. Tonight, let us be formed by what we read.

Tonight we draw from the work of Stephen Charnock, specifically from "The Existence and Attributes of God." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.


Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Malachi 3:6:

"For I am the LORD, I change not; therefore ye sons of Jacob are not consumed."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.


Among all the attributes of God, immutability occupies a special place because it secures all the others. If God could change, then His love might cool, His promises might fail, His power might diminish, His wisdom might err. But because God is immutable — absolutely unchangeable in His being, His perfections, His purposes, and His promises — every other attribute is eternally stable and infinitely reliable.

Stephen Charnock devotes one of his longest and most careful discourses to this attribute. He begins with the text from Malachi: 'I am the LORD, I change not; therefore ye sons of Jacob are not consumed.' Charnock draws out the remarkable connection: it is precisely because God does not change that His sinful people are not destroyed. If God were mutable, His patience with Israel would have exhausted itself long ago. But because He is the same yesterday, today, and forever, His mercy endures to all generations.

Charnock defines immutability with precision. It means that God cannot change for the better, because He is already perfect. He cannot change for the worse, because any diminution of perfection would be inconsistent with His nature. He cannot change from one state to another, because change implies composition, limitation, and potentiality — none of which apply to the absolutely simple, infinite, and actual Being of God.

Charnock distinguishes God's immutability from the immobility of a stone. A stone does not change because it lacks the capacity for activity. God does not change despite possessing infinite activity. He is constantly at work — creating, sustaining, governing, redeeming — and yet in all His activity, His essential nature remains exactly the same. His love does not grow warmer one day and cooler the next. His justice does not relax. His wisdom does not learn. His power does not fluctuate. He is, in every moment and in every action, the fullness of all that He eternally is.

Charnock then addresses the apparent changes in God recorded in Scripture. Does not God repent? Does He not change His mind? Does He not become angry and then relent? Charnock handles these texts with care. He argues that what Scripture describes as God 'repenting' or 'changing His mind' is not a change in God's nature or eternal purpose but a change in His outward dealings with men, corresponding to changes in their condition. When men repent, God relents — not because His disposition has changed but because His unchanging disposition toward repentant sinners now has a new object to which it applies.

The practical implications of God's immutability are enormous. First, it grounds the believer's assurance. The God who loved you before the foundation of the world loves you now with exactly the same love. He has not cooled in His affection, revised His commitment, or reconsidered His choice. The love that chose you is the love that sustains you and the love that will glorify you. It does not change.

Second, it guarantees God's promises. Every promise of God is backed by the immutability of His nature. When God says, 'I will never leave thee, nor forsake thee' (Hebrews 13:5), the guarantee is not merely the sincerity of His intention but the impossibility of His changing. He cannot fail to keep His word because He cannot change from the God who made the promise into a God who breaks it.

Third, Charnock argues that God's immutability should produce in us a holy stability. We are, by nature, fickle and changeable — our moods shift, our affections wander, our resolutions fail. But communion with an unchangeable God produces steadiness of soul. The more we fix our gaze upon the immutable One, the more we are anchored against the storms of circumstance and the fluctuations of feeling.

Charnock further explores the implications of immutability for the believer's struggle with sin. Because God does not change, His commitment to sanctify His people does not waver. The process of sanctification may seem painfully slow from our perspective, but the God who began it will complete it, because He cannot abandon a purpose once formed. Charnock writes that the believer's hope for final victory over sin rests not in the strength of his resolve but in the constancy of God's purpose. He who has begun a good work will perform it until the day of Jesus Christ — not because the work is easy but because the Worker is unchangeable.

Charnock also applies immutability to the question of prayer. Does prayer change God's mind? Charnock answers carefully: prayer does not change God's purpose, but it is the means by which God's unchanging purpose is carried out. God has eternally purposed both the answer and the prayer that precedes it. Prayer is not an attempt to change God but a participation in His unchanging plan.

Charnock closes with a note of warning: the immutability of God is comfort only to those who are in Christ. To those who persist in rebellion, it is a source of terror, for it means that God's justice is as unchangeable as His mercy. The impenitent sinner cannot hope that God will eventually relent, that His standards will soften, or that His judgment will fail to come. He is the LORD; He changes not.


Let these questions search your heart. Answer honestly before God.

Question 1: How does the immutability of God change the way I think about His love for me — especially on days when I feel unlovable?

Question 2: Do I draw comfort from the knowledge that God's promises cannot fail because His nature cannot change?

Question 3: In what ways does my own fickleness and changeableness reveal my need to be anchored in the unchanging character of God?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.


Unchanging God, I praise You that You are the same yesterday, today, and forever. In a world of constant change, You are my anchor. When my feelings fluctuate, Your love stands firm. When my faith wavers, Your promises hold. Teach me to draw stability from Your immutability, confidence from Your constancy, and comfort from the knowledge that the God who began a good work in me will bring it to completion. Through Christ, who is the same forever, Amen.

Goodnight. Remember: the same God who spoke to the Puritans speaks to you.
