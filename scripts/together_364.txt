PURITAN GOLD -- Together Devotional, Day 3
"Behold, Thou Art Fair"
Song of Solomon 1:9-17 — The Bridegroom's Affirmation


Welcome, dear couple. Tonight the bridegroom speaks. After the bride's vulnerable confession of insecurity, the beloved responds — and his response is one of the most beautiful affirmations of love in all of Scripture. Listen well, for how the bridegroom speaks to his bride reveals how we ought to speak to one another.

Song of Solomon 1:9-17:

"I have compared thee, O my love, to a company of horses in Pharaoh's chariots. Thy cheeks are comely with rows of jewels, thy neck with chains of gold. We will make thee borders of gold with studs of silver. While the king sitteth at his table, my spikenard sendeth forth the smell thereof. A bundle of myrrh is my wellbeloved unto me; he shall lie all night betwixt my breasts. My beloved is unto me as a cluster of camphire in the vineyards of Engedi. Behold, thou art fair, my love; behold, thou art fair; thou hast doves' eyes. Behold, thou art fair, my beloved, yea, pleasant: also our bed is green. The beams of our house are cedar, and our rafters of fir."


The bridegroom's first response to the bride's insecurity is not a lecture. It is not a theological correction. It is not even advice. It is admiration. "I have compared thee, O my love, to a company of horses in Pharaoh's chariots." In the ancient world, Pharaoh's chariot horses were the finest, most magnificent creatures alive — bred for beauty, strength, and grace. The bridegroom looks at the woman who just called herself dark and sun-scorched and says: You are magnificent.

This is what the Puritans called "the duty of commendation" in marriage. William Gouge, in his Domestical Duties, wrote that husbands and wives must develop the habit of speaking well of each other — not in flattery, which is hollow, but in genuine appreciation, which is life-giving. Gouge argued that many marriages wither not from great offenses but from the slow starvation of unspoken admiration.

Notice the progression. The bridegroom does not merely say she is adequate or acceptable. He adorns her. "We will make thee borders of gold with studs of silver." He wants to add to her beauty, not because she is lacking, but because she is worthy of adornment. The Puritans saw here a picture of how Christ adorns His church with gifts of the Spirit and graces of sanctification. But they also saw a practical application: husbands, invest in your wife's flourishing. Wives, invest in your husband's dignity. Marriage is a relationship of mutual adornment — each spouse seeking to bring out the best in the other.

Then the bride responds to his affirmation with her own. "While the king sitteth at his table, my spikenard sendeth forth the smell thereof." When he draws near, she becomes fragrant. His attention, his presence, his admiration draws out of her a beauty and a confidence that were always there but had been buried under neglect and insecurity. This is the transformative power of genuine love. When a husband truly sees his wife, she blossoms. When a wife truly honors her husband, he rises.

"A bundle of myrrh is my wellbeloved unto me; he shall lie all night betwixt my breasts." Myrrh was a precious, expensive resin used for anointing and perfume. The bride carries her beloved close to her heart — literally, against her chest — through the night hours. This speaks of sustained intimacy, not the fleeting passion of a moment but the enduring closeness of a night spent together. The Puritans were emphatic that physical closeness in marriage is not merely for procreation. It is for comfort, for bonding, for the deep reassurance that comes from being held by the one who loves you most.

Then comes the climactic exchange. He says: "Behold, thou art fair, my love; behold, thou art fair." She responds: "Behold, thou art fair, my beloved, yea, pleasant." Back and forth they go, mirroring each other's admiration. This is the holy echo of a healthy marriage — each spouse reflecting back to the other the beauty that God has placed in them.

Richard Sibbes, one of the warmest of all Puritan pastors, wrote that the soul grows most under the sunshine of approval, not the frost of criticism. This does not mean we never speak truth to each other. It means that the atmosphere of marriage should be one of warmth, not cold — of celebration, not scrutiny. When your spouse walks into the room, does your face light up? When they speak, do they know that you find them delightful? These small signals of admiration are the daily bread of a thriving marriage.

"Also our bed is green. The beams of our house are cedar, and our rafters of fir." The chapter closes with a picture of their home — a place of life, beauty, and strength. A green bed speaks of vitality and growth. Cedar beams speak of permanence and fragrance. Their home is not a battlefield; it is a garden. Their house is not a cold institution; it is a living, fragrant, growing place of love.

What kind of home are you building together? Not the physical structure — the emotional and spiritual one. Is it green and growing, or dry and withering? Is it fragrant with cedar, or stale with resentment? The Song invites you to build a home that is alive with mutual admiration, saturated with affection, and rooted in the unshakable covenant of God.


Turn to each other and consider these questions together.

Question 1: When was the last time you spoke words of genuine admiration to your spouse — not about what they do, but about who they are? Take a moment right now to say something you find beautiful about each other.

Question 2: The bridegroom responds to insecurity with affirmation. How do you typically respond when your spouse expresses self-doubt or vulnerability? What would change if you made admiration your first response?

Question 3: What is the "atmosphere" of your home right now? Is it more like a green garden or a dry desert? What one thing could you do this week to make your home more fragrant?


Tonight's prayer focus: Pray that God would give you eyes to see the beauty in each other — not the beauty of youth or appearance, but the deeper beauty of character, faithfulness, and grace. Ask Him to fill your mouths with words of admiration and your home with the fragrance of mutual love.


Behold, thou art fair, my love. Behold, thou art fair. May those words echo in your hearts as you close this night together. The God who made you beautiful in His sight has given you to each other as a daily reminder of His delight. Goodnight, dear couple. Rest well.
