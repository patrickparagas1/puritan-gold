PURITAN GOLD — Episode 2
"The Minister's Self-Watch"
Week 1, Day 2 (Tuesday) — Spurgeon's Lectures to My Students & Proverbs 4:23

---

Let me ask you a question this morning. And I want you to really sit with it before you answer.

Who is watching your soul?

Not your ministry. Not your work. Not your reputation or your public life. Your soul. That inner life — the one nobody sees — the part of you that exists between you and God alone. Who is tending to that?

Charles Haddon Spurgeon asked this question of his students at the Pastors' College in London, and the way he asked it should make every one of us — pastor or not — stop and take inventory. In his Lectures to My Students, Spurgeon devoted his very first lecture to a subject that might surprise you. He didn't begin with sermon preparation. He didn't begin with theology or homiletics or church administration. He began with the minister's own spiritual life.

And his reason was devastatingly simple: you cannot give what you do not have.

This is Puritan Gold, and today we're stepping away from our verse-by-verse walk through Proverbs — just for a moment — to sit at the feet of Spurgeon and consider what it means to guard your own soul. And we'll find our way back to Proverbs before we're done, because Solomon had something to say about this too. Something urgent.

But first... Spurgeon.

In that opening lecture, Spurgeon told his students that the minister's own spiritual condition was the most important factor in his ministry. More important than his education. More important than his eloquence. More important than his knowledge of Greek and Hebrew and systematic theology. If the inner life is neglected... everything else becomes hollow.

And Spurgeon didn't mince words about the danger. He warned that it is entirely possible — terrifyingly possible — to preach the gospel to others while your own heart grows cold. To speak of the living water while your own soul is parched and dry. To point others toward Christ while you yourself are drifting away from Him.

Now... you might be thinking, "I'm not a pastor. I'm not a minister. This doesn't apply to me." But friend, I want to gently push back on that. Because every believer is, in some sense, a minister. Every Christian has people watching their life. Every follower of Christ has influence — in their family, in their workplace, in their neighborhood, in their church. And the principle Spurgeon laid down applies to all of us:

You must tend to your own soul first.

Think of it this way. You've heard the safety instructions on an airplane, haven't you? "In the event of a loss of cabin pressure, oxygen masks will drop from above. Put on your own mask first... before helping others." Now, that sounds selfish at first. Why wouldn't you help the child next to you first? But the reason is simple: if you pass out from lack of oxygen, you can't help anyone. You become part of the problem instead of the solution.

Spurgeon understood this spiritual principle deeply. A minister — a Christian — who neglects their own communion with God will eventually have nothing to offer anyone else. The well runs dry. The fire goes out. And what's left is just... performance. Going through the motions. Words without power. Service without joy.

And here is where the danger is most acute. Because when your ministry is going well — when people are praising your work, when the church is growing, when others are being helped — it is so easy to mistake the fruit of ministry for the fruit of the Spirit. It is so easy to think, "Things are going well, so my soul must be healthy." But Spurgeon knew better. He had seen ministers crash and burn. He had watched men who stood in pulpits on Sunday and fell into ruin by Tuesday. And in nearly every case... the collapse didn't happen overnight. It happened slowly. Gradually. One skipped prayer time at a time. One unconfessed sin at a time. One small compromise at a time.

The decay was happening in secret, long before anyone could see it on the surface.

And this brings us to Solomon. Because in Proverbs chapter four, verse twenty-three, we find what may be the most important command in the entire book:

"Keep thy heart with all diligence; for out of it are the issues of life."

Other translations render it this way: "Above all else, guard your heart, for everything you do flows from it."

Above all else. Do you hear the urgency in that? Solomon has given us instruction about wisdom, about avoiding evil companions, about pursuing understanding. But here... here he says, "Above ALL of that — above everything else I've told you — guard your heart."

Why? Because the heart is the wellspring. It's the source. Everything that flows out of your life — your words, your actions, your relationships, your decisions, your ministry — all of it originates in the heart. If the heart is polluted, the stream will be polluted. If the heart is guarded and kept pure, the stream will run clean.

Spurgeon often used the image of a garden to describe the soul. A garden, he said, doesn't tend itself. Left alone, a garden doesn't grow more beautiful. It grows wild. Weeds take over. The flowers are choked out. The paths become overgrown. A garden requires a gardener — someone who is there every day, pulling weeds, watering the good plants, pruning what needs pruning, and protecting the tender shoots from harm.

Your soul is that garden. And you... under the grace and power of the Holy Spirit... are the gardener.

So what does it look like to "keep your heart with all diligence"? What does it look like, practically, to do what Spurgeon urged his students to do — to tend to your own spiritual life with intentional, daily care?

Let me suggest several things.

First... maintain a private prayer life that is real. Not performative. Not rushed. Not a box you check off before getting to the "real work" of the day. Spurgeon spoke often of the necessity of secret prayer — the kind of prayer that happens behind a closed door, where no one sees you, where no one is impressed by your eloquence, where it's just you and God. He said that the minister who does not pray in private will eventually have nothing to say in public. The power drains out.

And friend, this is true for every Christian. If your prayer life has become routine... if you find yourself saying the same words without really meaning them... if prayer has become an obligation rather than a lifeline... then something needs to change. Not because God demands performance from you, but because your soul needs communion with Him the way your lungs need air.

Second... be honest about your sins. Spurgeon was remarkably transparent about his own struggles. He battled depression — what he called "the minister's fainting fits." He knew seasons of doubt. He knew the temptation to pride after a successful sermon and the temptation to despair after a difficult one. And his counsel to his students was always the same: bring it to God. Don't hide it. Don't pretend it isn't there. Don't put on a brave face while your soul is hemorrhaging.

Proverbs tells us, "He that covereth his sins shall not prosper: but whoso confesseth and forsaketh them shall have mercy." That's Proverbs 28:13. And it's as practical a piece of advice as you'll find anywhere. The unconfessed sin is the cancer of the soul. It doesn't go away on its own. It grows. It spreads. And if left untreated, it will destroy everything it touches.

Third... guard what you take in. This is where Solomon's command to "keep thy heart with all diligence" becomes very practical. What are you feeding your mind? What are you reading? What are you watching? What conversations are you allowing to shape your thinking?

Spurgeon was a voracious reader. By some accounts, he read six books a week for much of his life. But he was discerning about what he read. He filled his mind with Scripture, with the Puritans, with sound theology, with great literature. He understood that the mind is not a passive receptacle — it's an active participant in the shaping of the soul. What you take in... you become.

And in our age — an age of endless distraction, of constant noise, of information coming at us from every direction — this counsel is more urgent than ever. Guarding the heart means being intentional about what you allow through the gate. It means saying no to some things so that you can say yes to better things. It means choosing depth over distraction. Substance over stimulation.

Fourth... stay in close fellowship with other believers who will tell you the truth. Spurgeon didn't do ministry alone, and he didn't guard his soul alone either. He had friends — real friends — who could look him in the eye and say, "Charles, something is off." He valued that kind of honesty, even when it stung. Because he knew that the heart is deceitful — Jeremiah told us that — and we need others to help us see what we cannot see in ourselves.

If you don't have someone in your life who has permission to ask you hard questions... find that person. Seek them out. It's not a sign of weakness. It's a sign of wisdom.

And fifth... remember the gospel daily. This might sound obvious, but it's the thing we most often forget. Spurgeon never got over the gospel. After decades of preaching, after thousands of sermons, after becoming the most famous preacher in the English-speaking world... he was still amazed by grace. Still undone by the cross. Still a sinner saved by mercy, and he knew it.

The minister's self-watch — the Christian's self-watch — is not ultimately about self-improvement. It's not about pulling yourself up by your spiritual bootstraps. It's about returning, again and again and again, to the fountain of grace. It's about preaching the gospel to yourself before you preach it to anyone else.

As Spurgeon himself reminded his students: you must be what you preach. Not perfectly — none of us will achieve perfection this side of glory. But genuinely. Authentically. With a heart that is being actively tended by the Holy Spirit, through the means of grace that God has provided.

So let me ask you again... who is watching your soul?

You know, there's a beautiful irony in all of this. Solomon says, "Keep thy heart with all diligence." Spurgeon says, "Tend to your own soul first." And they're both absolutely right. But the deepest truth — the truth beneath the truth — is that the One who is ultimately keeping your soul... is God Himself.

Psalm 121 says it: "He that keepeth thee will not slumber. Behold, he that keepeth Israel shall neither slumber nor sleep."

Your self-watch is important. Your diligence matters. But it rests on a deeper foundation — the faithfulness of a God who never stops watching over you. Who never grows weary of tending to your soul. Who began a good work in you and will be faithful to complete it.

So tend your garden, friend. Pull the weeds. Water the good soil. Guard the gate of your heart. But do it all in the confidence that the Master Gardener is at work in you... and He has never lost a single plant that the Father gave Him.

Let me pray for us.

Lord God, we confess that we have too often neglected the garden of our own souls. We have been busy with outward things while the inner life has grown weedy and dry. Forgive us. Restore us. Give us the discipline to guard our hearts with all diligence, and the humility to know that even our best efforts depend entirely on Your sustaining grace. Keep us, Lord — for we cannot keep ourselves. Watch over our souls today and every day, for the sake of Your Son, Jesus Christ, in whose name we pray. Amen.

I'll see you tomorrow, friend. Keep your heart.
