PURITAN GOLD -- Study Devotional, Day 27
Series: Romans
"Receive the Weak in Faith"
Christian liberty, conscience, and the judgment seat of Christ

---

Welcome. The practice of sustained Bible study is one of the greatest gifts a Christian can give themselves. Tonight we continue to build our understanding, one passage at a time, guided by the Holy Spirit and the wisdom of the Puritans.

We are studying the book of Romans together. Tonight's lesson is titled "Receive the Weak in Faith." Each session builds on what came before, so if you have been following along, you will see how tonight's passage connects to the larger argument of the book.

--- Scripture Reading ---

Let us read our passage for tonight. It comes from Romans 14:1-12:

"Him that is weak in the faith receive ye, but not to doubtful disputations. For one believeth that he may eat all things: another, who is weak, eateth herbs. Let not him that eateth despise him that eateth not; and let not him which eateth not judge him that eateth: for God hath received him. Who art thou that judgest another man's servant? to his own master he standeth or falleth. Yea, he shall be holden up: for God is able to make him stand. One man esteemeth one day above another: another esteemeth every day alike. Let every man be fully persuaded in his own mind. He that regardeth the day, regardeth it unto the Lord; and he that regardeth not the day, to the Lord he doth not regard it... For none of us liveth to himself, and no man dieth to himself. For whether we live, we live unto the Lord; and whether we die, we die unto the Lord: whether we live therefore, or die, we are the Lord's... But why dost thou judge thy brother? or why dost thou set at nought thy brother? for we shall all stand before the judgment seat of Christ. For it is written, As I live, saith the Lord, every knee shall bow to me, and every tongue shall confess to God. So then every one of us shall give account of himself to God."

Read that passage again to yourself. Mark any words or phrases that stand out. The Puritans read with a pen in hand, always ready to note what God was showing them.

--- Key Verse ---

One verse in particular demands our attention tonight. Let us read it carefully.

"For none of us liveth to himself, and no man dieth to himself. For whether we live, we live unto the Lord; and whether we die, we die unto the Lord: whether we live therefore, or die, we are the Lord's." -- Romans 14:7-8

This verse is the lens through which we will examine the rest of tonight's passage. Keep it in the front of your mind as we proceed.

--- Exposition ---

Let us now exposit this passage carefully, as the Puritans would have done.

Charles Hodge offers valuable commentary on this passage. The Puritans were expositors of the highest order, and their insights remain remarkably fresh.

Charles Hodge brought careful exegetical and pastoral wisdom to Romans 14, which he regarded as one of the most practically important passages in the epistle. Hodge argued that Paul is addressing matters that are genuinely indifferent — not issues where Scripture speaks clearly (such as idolatry, immorality, or false doctrine) but matters of conscience where faithful believers may legitimately disagree.

Hodge identified the 'weak' in this passage as those whose consciences are more restrictive — they feel bound by dietary laws, observance of special days, or other practices that the gospel has actually set them free from. The 'strong' are those who understand their liberty in Christ and feel no obligation to observe these ceremonies. Hodge stressed that Paul does not call the weak 'sinners' but 'weak in faith' — their faith is genuine but not yet fully informed. They have not yet grasped the full extent of Christian liberty.

Hodge was particularly forceful on the twin sins that threaten the church over disputable matters: the strong despise the weak (looking down on them as legalistic or unsophisticated), and the weak judge the strong (condemning them as worldly or disobedient). Both attitudes are sinful. Both violate love. Both usurp the role of God, who alone has the right to judge His own servants.

Hodge drew from verse 12 the sobering principle of individual accountability: 'So then every one of us shall give account of himself to God.' We will not give account for other people's consciences; we will give account for our own actions, attitudes, and treatment of brothers and sisters. This truth, Hodge argued, should simultaneously humble us (we have enough to worry about with our own account) and liberate us (we need not carry the burden of policing everyone else's conscience).

Romans 14 addresses one of the most common and most destructive problems in the life of the church: Christians dividing over matters that Scripture does not clearly command or forbid. In Paul's day, the disputes involved food laws and the observance of holy days. In every generation, there are different issues, but the underlying dynamic is the same: believers with different levels of understanding and different sensitivities of conscience clash over disputable matters.

Verse 1 states the principle: 'Him that is weak in the faith receive ye, but not to doubtful disputations.' Welcome the weak believer into full fellowship — do not exclude them because their conscience is more restrictive than yours. But do not welcome them for the purpose of arguing about their scruples. The church should be a place of acceptance, not a debating society over minor issues.

Verses 2-3 illustrate with food: 'For one believeth that he may eat all things: another, who is weak, eateth herbs.' The strong believer understands that all food is clean under the new covenant (Mark 7:19; Acts 10:15). The weak believer, perhaps from a Jewish background, still feels bound by Old Testament dietary restrictions. Paul's instructions apply to both: 'Let not him that eateth despise him that eateth not; and let not him which eateth not judge him that eateth: for God hath received him.' The strong must not despise the weak as legalistic or ignorant. The weak must not judge the strong as worldly or sinful. Both have been received by God. That is what matters.

Verse 4 goes to the heart of the matter: 'Who art thou that judgest another man's servant? to his own master he standeth or falleth.' Each believer is a servant of Christ, not a servant of other believers' opinions. The Lord is the Master; He alone has the right to evaluate His servant's conduct on disputable matters. 'Yea, he shall be holden up: for God is able to make him stand.' The believer who acts in good conscience before God will be upheld by God, even if other believers disapprove.

Verses 5-6 extend the principle to holy days: 'One man esteemeth one day above another: another esteemeth every day alike. Let every man be fully persuaded in his own mind.' Some Christians observe special days — the Sabbath, feast days, fast days. Others regard every day as alike. Paul does not settle the dispute; he insists that each person act from conviction: 'Let every man be fully persuaded in his own mind.' The critical issue is not which position you hold but whether you hold it sincerely before the Lord. 'He that regardeth the day, regardeth it unto the Lord; and he that regardeth not the day, to the Lord he doth not regard it.'

Verses 7-9 provide the theological foundation for everything in this chapter: 'For none of us liveth to himself, and no man dieth to himself. For whether we live, we live unto the Lord; and whether we die, we die unto the Lord: whether we live therefore, or die, we are the Lord's. For to this end Christ both died, and rose, and revived, that he might be Lord both of the dead and living.' We belong to Christ — completely, permanently, in life and in death. This ownership means that Christ is the final judge of our conduct, not our fellow believers.

Verses 10-12 drive the point home with the reality of final judgment: 'But why dost thou judge thy brother? or why dost thou set at nought thy brother? for we shall all stand before the judgment seat of Christ.' Paul quotes Isaiah 45:23: 'As I live, saith the Lord, every knee shall bow to me, and every tongue shall confess to God.' Every person — strong and weak, strict and free — will stand before Christ and give account. 'So then every one of us shall give account of himself to God.' Not of his brother, not of his sister — of himself. This truth should simultaneously humble us and set us free. We have enough to worry about with our own account; we do not need to audit everyone else's.

The principles of this passage apply far beyond food and holy days. In every generation, churches face disputable matters: styles of worship, use of alcohol, entertainment choices, schooling decisions, political preferences, dress codes, musical preferences, homeschooling versus public school. On matters where Scripture does not speak with clear, unmistakable authority, Romans 14 applies: receive one another, do not despise or judge, act from conviction, and remember that you will stand before Christ alone.

--- Application ---

What does this truth look like in the ordinary moments of your life? At work, at home, in your relationships? Let us make it concrete.

Romans 14:1-12 has immediate relevance for church life and Christian relationships.

First, examine your attitude toward believers who differ from you. Do you despise Christians who are more conservative than you — who abstain from things you feel free to enjoy? Do you judge Christians who are more liberal than you — who participate in things your conscience restricts? Either attitude violates this passage. Repent of the contempt or condemnation you have directed at brothers and sisters who hold different convictions on disputable matters.

Second, act from conviction, not from conformity. 'Let every man be fully persuaded in his own mind.' Do not adopt practices simply because others do them, and do not abandon practices simply because others criticize them. Study Scripture, pray, seek counsel, and form your own conviction before the Lord. Then act on that conviction with a clear conscience.

Third, welcome those who are different. The first command of this passage is 'receive' — welcome, accept, embrace. If your church is a place where only people who agree on every minor issue are comfortable, it is not following Romans 14. Cultivate a church culture where believers with different convictions on disputable matters can worship together in unity and love.

Fourth, prepare for the judgment seat of Christ. You will give account of yourself to God. Not of your pastor, not of your theological opponents, not of the weak or the strong — of yourself. How have you used your liberty? How have you treated your brother? Have you lived unto the Lord? Let this coming accountability shape how you live today.

Write down one concrete action you will take this week in response to tonight's study. Do not let this truth remain theoretical. The Puritans measured the success of a sermon not by how much the hearer learned but by how much the hearer changed.

--- Closing ---

Lord Jesus, You are the Master, and we are Your servants. Forgive us for judging one another's servants. Forgive us for despising brothers who are more cautious and condemning brothers who are more free. Teach us to receive one another as You have received us. Give us the humility to hold our convictions without demanding that others share them. Help us to live unto You — whether we eat or abstain, whether we observe or do not observe. And prepare us for the day when we will stand before Your judgment seat, giving account of ourselves alone. May we be found faithful. Amen.

Goodnight. Remember, the goal of study is not information but transformation.
