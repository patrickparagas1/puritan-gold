PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"I Will Praise Thee, O Lord, with My Whole Heart"
June 2, 2026

---

"I will praise thee, O Lord, with my whole heart; I will shew forth all thy marvellous works. I will be glad and rejoice in thee: I will sing praise to thy name, O most High. When mine enemies are turned back, they shall fall and perish at thy presence. For thou hast maintained my right and my cause; thou satest in the throne judging right." -- Psalm 9:1-4

There is a world of difference between the cold, formal acknowledgment of God's goodness and the warm, full-hearted praise that rises from a soul truly sensible of His mercies. David here resolves upon the latter. "I will praise thee, O Lord, with my whole heart." Not with half a heart, not with a divided affection, not with one eye upon the Lord and the other upon the world, but with the entire force and energy of his renewed nature, poured out without reserve at the feet of his great Deliverer. And this is the only praise that is worthy of the name, for God, who searches the heart and tries the reins, will not be satisfied with the mere outward form of worship when the inward reality is wanting.

Charles Spurgeon once observed that many Christians bring to God a praise that is like a body without a soul. The lips move, the words are spoken, the hymns are sung, but the heart is far away, wandering among the cares and pleasures of this present world. Such worship is an abomination to the Lord. He who made the heart demands the heart. He who gave us every faculty of love and joy and gratitude expects that these faculties should be employed in His service. And when a believer truly awakens to the greatness of God's mercies toward him, he finds that half-hearted praise is utterly impossible. The whole heart must be engaged, or the soul feels as though it has not praised at all.

"I will shew forth all thy marvellous works." Here David adds to praise the ministry of testimony. It is not enough to feel gratitude in the secret chamber of the heart; it must overflow into the hearing of others. The marvellous works of God are not to be kept as private treasures, hoarded away in the memory of individual saints. They are to be proclaimed, published, declared abroad, that others also may hear and fear and put their trust in the Lord. Every deliverance, every answered prayer, every signal display of divine faithfulness, is a jewel that ought to be held up to the light so that all who see it may glorify the God who wrought it.

And observe the ground of David's praise: "Thou hast maintained my right and my cause; thou satest in the throne judging right." The psalmist had been oppressed, his enemies had risen against him, injustice had seemed for a season to triumph over righteousness. But God had intervened. The Lord had taken His seat upon the throne of judgment and had pronounced the verdict of heaven in David's favour. And this is ever the way of divine providence, beloved. God may suffer His people to be tried, to be pressed down, to be brought low, even to the very gates of despair. But He never abandons them. He sits upon the throne judging right, and in His own appointed hour He rises up and scatters their adversaries like chaff before the wind.

Stephen Charnock, that profound theologian of the Puritan era, wrote with great depth concerning the justice of God, reminding us that the very throne of God is established upon righteousness and judgment. It is not merely that God happens to be just in this or that particular action; justice is the eternal foundation of His government. He cannot act unjustly any more than the sun can produce darkness. And therefore the believer who commits his cause to the Lord may rest with absolute certainty that the final outcome will be righteous, however dark and perplexing the intermediate chapters of the story may appear.

"When mine enemies are turned back, they shall fall and perish at thy presence." There is a wonderful confidence in these words. David does not say "if" but "when." He has no doubt whatsoever that the enemies of God's people will ultimately be overthrown. And notice where they perish: "at thy presence." It is not by the sword of David, nor by the cunning of human strategy, but by the sheer majesty and power of God's own presence that the wicked are confounded. When the Lord arises, His enemies are scattered as naturally and as inevitably as shadows flee before the rising sun. There is no need for the believer to fret or to scheme or to take vengeance into his own hands. The presence of God is sufficient to undo every adversary and to bring every plot of the wicked to nothing.

Jeremiah Burroughs, that gentle and peaceable Puritan, taught much upon the subject of contentment in the midst of trials, and one of his chief arguments was precisely this: that God sits upon the throne and will certainly bring all things to a righteous conclusion. The believer who truly grasps this doctrine will find his heart filled with a calm and steady joy that no outward circumstance can destroy. He will be able to say with David, "I will be glad and rejoice in thee." Not in his circumstances, which may be dark; not in his own strength, which may be small; but in God Himself, who is the same yesterday, today, and forever, and whose counsels stand sure through all the revolutions of time.

Let us then stir up our hearts to this wholehearted praise. Let us not be content with a tepid and languid worship that merely goes through the motions of godliness while the soul remains cold and unmoved. Let us rather kindle the fire of holy gratitude upon the altar of our hearts, and let us pour out our thanksgiving with the full energy of our redeemed affections. For God has maintained our right and our cause. He has judged righteously on our behalf. He has turned back our enemies and delivered us from those that were too strong for us. And He has done all this not because we deserved it, but because His mercy endureth forever. To Him be praise with the whole heart, now and forevermore. Amen.
