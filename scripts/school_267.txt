PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 7
"Of God's Covenant with Man"
The Westminster Standards — Lesson 7

---

Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we take up one of the most magnificent and far-reaching subjects in all of Reformed theology — the covenants of God with man. The concept of covenant is the golden thread that runs through the entire tapestry of Scripture, from Genesis to Revelation. Without understanding God's covenants, we cannot understand God's plan of salvation. The Westminster Standards give this doctrine a place of central importance, and so shall we. Let us pray and begin.

O Lord our God, Thou art a covenant-keeping God. From before the foundation of the world, Thou hast purposed to save a people for Thyself, and Thou hast bound Thyself by solemn covenant to accomplish that purpose. As we study Thy covenants today, grant us understanding minds and grateful hearts. Show us the riches of Thy grace displayed in Thy dealings with mankind, and lead us to rest with full confidence in Thy promises. In the name of Jesus Christ, our covenant Mediator, Amen.

--- Lesson ---

Part One: What Is a Covenant?

Before we examine the specific covenants that God has made with man, we must understand what a covenant is. In ordinary human affairs, a covenant is a solemn, binding agreement between two or more parties, established upon certain terms and sealed with some form of ratification. Marriage is a covenant. Treaties between nations are covenants. Business partnerships may be covenantal in nature. In each case, there are parties, promises, conditions, and consequences.

But when we speak of God's covenants with man, we must recognize a fundamental difference. In human covenants, the parties are generally equals, or at least they negotiate as approximate equals. In God's covenants with man, the parties are infinitely unequal. God is the sovereign Creator; man is the dependent creature. God does not negotiate with man. He condescends to man. He stoops from His infinite majesty to enter into a relationship with finite, frail, and (after the fall) sinful human beings. This is an act of astonishing grace. As the Psalmist exclaims: "When I consider thy heavens, the work of thy fingers, the moon and the stars, which thou hast ordained; What is man, that thou art mindful of him? and the son of man, that thou visitest him?" (Psalm 8:3-4).

The Westminster Confession, Chapter 7, Section 1, captures this truth beautifully: "The distance between God and the creature is so great that although reasonable creatures do owe obedience unto him as their Creator, yet they could never have any fruition of him, as their blessedness and reward, but by some voluntary condescension on God's part, which he hath been pleased to express by way of covenant."

This opening statement is profoundly important. It teaches us three things. First, there is an infinite distance between God and man. God is self-sufficient. He needs nothing. He lacks nothing. He would have been perfectly happy and perfectly glorious without creating anything at all. Second, although man owes obedience to God by virtue of being God's creature, mere obedience could never earn the reward of eternal blessedness. God owes man nothing. Even if Adam had obeyed perfectly for a million years, he could not have placed God in his debt. Third, the only way man could have any hope of enjoying God as his reward is through God's "voluntary condescension" — His gracious willingness to bind Himself by covenant to reward obedience and to save sinners.

The Puritan theologian Herman Witsius, in his landmark work The Economy of the Covenants Between God and Man, first published in Latin in 1677, defined a divine covenant as "an agreement between God and man, about the way of obtaining consummate happiness, including a commination of eternal destruction, with which the contemner of the happiness, offered in that way, is to be punished." Witsius emphasized that every covenant God makes contains both promises and threats — blessings for obedience and curses for disobedience.

Francis Turretin, the great Reformed scholastic theologian of Geneva who taught at the Academy of Geneva from 1653 to 1687 and whose Institutes of Elenctic Theology profoundly shaped the Westminster tradition, likewise stressed the condescension involved in God's covenant-making. Turretin argued that God was under no obligation to enter into a covenant with man. He could have simply commanded obedience and left man without any promise of reward. The fact that God chose to bind Himself by covenant — to promise blessing in return for obedience — was itself an act of pure, unmerited grace.

Part Two: The Covenant of Works

The Westminster Confession, Chapter 7, Section 2, introduces the first covenant: "The first covenant made with man was a covenant of works, wherein life was promised to Adam, and in him to his posterity, upon condition of perfect and personal obedience."

This is the covenant of works — also called the covenant of life, the covenant of nature, or the Adamic covenant. It was established between God and Adam in the Garden of Eden. Its terms were straightforward: if Adam obeyed God perfectly, he and all his descendants would receive the reward of eternal, confirmed, unlosable life. If Adam disobeyed, he and all his descendants would suffer the penalty of death.

The biblical foundation for the covenant of works is found primarily in Genesis 2:15-17: "And the LORD God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to dress it and to keep it. And the LORD God commanded the man, saying, Of every tree of the garden thou mayest freely eat: But of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, thou shalt not eat of it: for in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die."

Here we see the essential elements of a covenant. There is a sovereign Lord who establishes the terms. There is a human party who is bound by those terms. There is a condition — obedience to God's command regarding the tree. There is a promise implied — life (for God would not threaten death unless life were the alternative). And there is a penalty — death, in all its forms: spiritual, physical, and eternal.

The Shorter Catechism, Question 12, asks: "What special act of providence did God exercise toward man in the estate wherein he was created?" and answers: "When God had created man, he entered into a covenant of life with him, upon condition of perfect obedience; forbidding him to eat of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, upon pain of death."

The Larger Catechism, Question 20, provides additional detail: "The providence of God toward man in the estate in which he was created, was the placing him in paradise, appointing him to dress it, giving him liberty to eat of the fruit of the earth; putting the creatures under his dominion, and ordaining marriage for his help; affording him communion with himself; instituting the Sabbath; entering into a covenant of life with him, upon condition of personal, perfect, and perpetual obedience, of which the tree of life was a pledge; and forbidding to eat of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, upon the pain of death."

Notice the three qualities of the obedience required: it was to be "personal" (Adam himself must obey — no one could obey for him), "perfect" (no sin, no failure, no shortcoming of any kind), and "perpetual" (obedience must be sustained throughout the period of probation). This was not an unreasonable demand. Adam had been created with a righteous nature, a clear mind, a will inclined toward God, and sufficient strength to obey. He was fully equipped for the test.

The Puritan divine Edward Fisher, in his important work The Marrow of Modern Divinity, first published in 1645 and widely circulated among Reformed Christians, described the covenant of works as God saying to Adam, in effect: "I am thy God; thou art my creature. I have made thee a reasonable creature, endued with an understanding mind, and a free will, and have put thee into this pleasant garden, and given thee dominion over all my creatures. Now if thou wilt yield perfect obedience to all my commands, and particularly abstain from eating of the tree of knowledge of good and evil, thou shalt be blessed with the continuation of this happy life, and shall be advanced to a higher degree of happiness. But if thou shalt eat of that tree, thou shalt die the death."

The tree of life in the garden was, according to the Larger Catechism, "a pledge" of the life promised in the covenant. It was a sacramental sign — a visible, tangible assurance that God would keep His promise. Just as the sacraments of the new covenant (baptism and the Lord's Supper) are signs and seals of God's covenant promises, so the tree of life was a sign and seal of the promise of life in the covenant of works.

Part Three: Adam as Federal Head — The Principle of Representation

A crucial feature of the covenant of works is that Adam did not stand alone. He stood as the federal head — the representative — of the entire human race. The Larger Catechism, Question 22, confirms this: "The covenant being made with Adam as a public person, not for himself only, but for his posterity, all mankind descending from him by ordinary generation, sinned in him, and fell with him in that first transgression."

The word "federal" comes from the Latin word foedus, meaning "covenant." Adam was the covenant representative of all who would descend from him by ordinary generation. His obedience would have secured blessing for all; his disobedience secured condemnation for all. This principle of federal representation is central to Reformed covenant theology.

Some have objected to this principle, arguing that it is unjust for one man's sin to be charged to his descendants. But the Westminster divines, following the Apostle Paul, saw in this principle not only justice but also profound wisdom and mercy. For the same principle of representation that explains how Adam's sin was imputed to us also explains how Christ's righteousness can be imputed to us. As Paul writes in Romans 5:18-19: "Therefore as by the offence of one judgment came upon all men to condemnation; even so by the righteousness of one the free gift came upon all men unto justification of life. For as by one man's disobedience many were made sinners, so by the obedience of one shall many be made righteous."

If you reject the principle of federal headship, you lose not only the doctrine of original sin but also the doctrine of justification by faith. The two stand or fall together. If it is unjust for Adam to represent us in the covenant of works, then it is equally unjust for Christ to represent us in the covenant of grace. But if it is right and good for Christ to stand in our place — and every believer knows that it is — then it is also right and good for Adam to have stood in our place in Eden.

The great Puritan theologian John Owen, in his extensive works on the covenants, explained that God's appointment of Adam as the federal head of humanity was an act of wisdom, not of cruelty. God could have made a separate covenant with every individual human being, testing each one independently. But in His wisdom, He chose to test the entire race in its strongest member — the one who had the greatest advantages, the clearest knowledge, the purest heart, and the most favorable circumstances. If any man could have passed the test, it was Adam. And if Adam, with all his advantages, could not stand, what hope would any of his weaker descendants have had?

Part Four: The Breaking of the Covenant of Works

As we studied in our previous lesson, Adam failed the test. Seduced by the serpent, our first parents ate of the forbidden fruit and plunged the entire human race into sin and misery. The covenant of works was broken. Its promise of life was forfeited. Its penalty of death was incurred — not only by Adam and Eve personally, but by all their descendants, who had sinned in them and fallen with them.

The Shorter Catechism, Question 15, asks: "What was the sin whereby our first parents fell from the estate wherein they were created?" and answers: "The sin whereby our first parents fell from the estate wherein they were created, was their eating the forbidden fruit." And Question 16 asks: "Did all mankind fall in Adam's first transgression?" and answers: "The covenant being made with Adam, not only for himself, but for his posterity; all mankind, descending from him, by ordinary generation, sinned in him, and fell with him, in his first transgression."

The consequences were catastrophic. Man lost communion with God. The image of God was defaced, though not entirely destroyed. The understanding was darkened. The will was enslaved to sin. The affections were disordered. The body became subject to disease and death. The entire created order was cursed. And the sentence of eternal death hung over every human head.

But — and this is the turning point of the entire story — the breaking of the covenant of works did not mean the end of God's purposes for man. It meant the beginning of something new and even more glorious: the covenant of grace.

Part Five: The Covenant of Grace — Its Nature and Promise

The Westminster Confession, Chapter 7, Section 3, introduces the covenant of grace: "Man, by his fall, having made himself incapable of life by that covenant, the Lord was pleased to make a second, commonly called the covenant of grace: wherein he freely offereth unto sinners life and salvation by Jesus Christ, requiring of them faith in him, that they may be saved, and promising to give unto all those that are ordained unto eternal life his Holy Spirit, to make them willing and able to believe."

This is one of the most important paragraphs in the entire Confession. Let us examine it with care.

First, notice the occasion for the covenant of grace: "Man, by his fall, having made himself incapable of life by that covenant." The covenant of works still stands in one sense — it still demands perfect obedience, and it still condemns those who fail to render it. But as a means of obtaining eternal life, it is useless to fallen man. We cannot keep it perfectly. We cannot undo our past failures. We cannot earn what we have already forfeited. A new arrangement is needed — not because the first covenant was defective, but because man proved unable to fulfill its terms.

Second, notice the author of the covenant of grace: "the Lord was pleased to make a second." The covenant of grace is God's initiative, not man's. Fallen man did not come to God and propose a new arrangement. God came to fallen man and offered one. This is grace — unmerited, unsolicited, undeserved kindness from a holy God to guilty sinners.

Third, notice the substance of the covenant of grace: God "freely offereth unto sinners life and salvation by Jesus Christ." The covenant of grace is not a lighter version of the covenant of works — a covenant with easier terms that fallen man can now manage. The covenant of grace offers life and salvation freely — as a gift, not as a wage. And it offers this gift "by Jesus Christ" — through His perfect obedience, His atoning death, and His glorious resurrection. Christ is the Mediator of the covenant of grace, and apart from Him, there is no salvation.

Fourth, notice the condition of the covenant of grace: "requiring of them faith in him, that they may be saved." The covenant of grace is not unconditional in the sense that nothing is required of man. Faith is required — personal trust in Jesus Christ as Lord and Saviour. But faith is not a work that earns salvation. It is the instrument that receives salvation. It is the empty hand that grasps the gift.

Fifth, notice the provision for meeting the condition: "promising to give unto all those that are ordained unto eternal life his Holy Spirit, to make them willing and able to believe." Here is the glory of the covenant of grace. God does not merely require faith and then leave us to produce it on our own. He promises to give His Holy Spirit to His elect, to make them willing and able to believe. Even faith itself is a gift of God. Salvation is of the Lord from beginning to end — He plans it, He accomplishes it, He applies it, and He preserves it.

The Shorter Catechism, Question 20, summarizes the covenant of grace in relation to the fall: "God, having out of his mere good pleasure, from all eternity, elected some to everlasting life, did enter into a covenant of grace to deliver them out of the estate of sin and misery, and to bring them into an estate of salvation by a Redeemer."

Part Six: The Covenant of Grace in the Old Testament

The Westminster Confession, Chapter 7, Section 5, teaches that the covenant of grace "was differently administered in the time of the law, and in the time of the gospel." This is an important point. The covenant of grace is one covenant — there are not two separate ways of salvation, one for Old Testament believers and another for New Testament believers. But this one covenant was administered differently under the old dispensation than it is under the new.

Under the old dispensation — from the time of the fall to the coming of Christ — the covenant of grace was administered "by promises, prophecies, sacrifices, circumcision, the paschal lamb, and other types and ordinances delivered to the people of the Jews, all foresignifying Christ to come; which were, for that time, sufficient and efficacious, through the operation of the Spirit, to instruct and build up the elect in faith in the promised Messiah."

Let us trace the unfolding of the covenant of grace through the Old Testament.

The first promise of the covenant of grace is found immediately after the fall, in Genesis 3:15 — the so-called Protoevangelium, or "first gospel." God said to the serpent: "And I will put enmity between thee and the woman, and between thy seed and her seed; it shall bruise thy head, and thou shalt bruise his heel." Here, in the very moment of judgment upon sin, God announced His plan of redemption. The seed of the woman — a descendant of Eve — would crush the head of the serpent, even as the serpent would bruise his heel. This is a prophecy of Christ's victory over Satan through the cross. The cross was Satan's attack upon Christ — the bruising of His heel. But through the cross, Christ destroyed Satan's power — the crushing of his head.

The covenant was further revealed to Noah. After the flood, God established a covenant with Noah and all living creatures, promising never again to destroy the earth with a flood and setting the rainbow as the sign of this promise (Genesis 9:8-17). While this is primarily a covenant of preservation rather than of redemption, it served the purposes of the covenant of grace by ensuring that the world would be preserved until the Redeemer came.

The covenant took a decisive step forward with Abraham. In Genesis 12:1-3, God called Abraham out of Ur of the Chaldees and promised him: "I will make of thee a great nation, and I will bless thee, and make thy name great; and thou shalt be a blessing: And I will bless them that bless thee, and curse him that curseth thee: and in thee shall all families of the earth be blessed." This last promise — "in thee shall all families of the earth be blessed" — is the heart of the Abrahamic covenant and points directly to Christ. As Paul explains in Galatians 3:8: "And the scripture, foreseeing that God would justify the heathen through faith, preached before the gospel unto Abraham, saying, In thee shall all nations be blessed."

In Genesis 15, God confirmed His covenant with Abraham through a remarkable ceremony. Abraham was instructed to cut several animals in half and arrange the pieces opposite each other. Then, in a deep sleep, Abraham saw a smoking furnace and a burning lamp pass between the pieces — symbols of God's presence. In ancient Near Eastern culture, to pass between the pieces of a divided animal was to say, "May I become like these animals if I break this covenant." But notice: it was God alone who passed between the pieces, not Abraham. God was binding Himself unconditionally to fulfill His promise. The covenant of grace depends ultimately on God's faithfulness, not on ours.

In Genesis 17, God gave circumcision as the sign of the covenant with Abraham. Circumcision was a sign of the removal of sin, of separation from the world, and of belonging to God's covenant people. It was administered to Abraham and to his male descendants, including infants, signifying that the covenant promises extended to believers and their children.

The covenant was renewed with Moses at Sinai. The giving of the law on Mount Sinai — that thunderous, terrifying event described in Exodus chapters 19 and 20, when God descended upon the mountain in fire and smoke and spoke the Ten Commandments to the assembled people of Israel — was not a return to the covenant of works. The Puritans were emphatic on this point. The moral law given at Sinai was a transcript of the original law written on Adam's heart, and it continued to serve as the standard of righteousness. But for believers, the law was now a guide to grateful obedience, not a means of earning salvation. The ceremonial laws — the sacrifices, the priesthood, the tabernacle, the festivals — all pointed forward to Christ, the ultimate sacrifice, the great high priest, the true tabernacle, the fulfillment of every feast.

Samuel Rutherford, the Scottish commissioner to the Westminster Assembly who had previously served as Professor of Divinity at St. Andrews from 1639 and who was one of the most brilliant theologians of his generation, insisted that the Mosaic covenant was fundamentally a covenant of grace, not of works. Rutherford argued that the ceremonies and sacrifices of the Old Testament were "gospel in figures" — vivid, tangible illustrations of the salvation that Christ would accomplish. Every lamb that was slain on the altar pointed to "the Lamb of God, which taketh away the sin of the world" (John 1:29).

The covenant was further developed in God's promise to David. In 2 Samuel 7:12-16, God promised David that his throne would be established forever: "And when thy days be fulfilled, and thou shalt sleep with thy fathers, I will set up thy seed after thee, which shall proceed out of thy bowels, and I will establish his kingdom. He shall build an house for my name, and I will stablish the throne of his kingdom for ever." This Davidic covenant finds its ultimate fulfillment in Jesus Christ, the son of David, whose kingdom shall have no end.

The Old Testament prophets also foretold a new and better administration of the covenant. Jeremiah 31:31-34 contains one of the most remarkable prophecies in all of Scripture: "Behold, the days come, saith the LORD, that I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel, and with the house of Judah: Not according to the covenant that I made with their fathers in the day that I took them by the hand to bring them out of the land of Egypt; which my covenant they brake, although I was an husband unto them, saith the LORD: But this shall be the covenant that I will make with the house of Israel; After those days, saith the LORD, I will put my law in their inward parts, and write it in their hearts; and will be their God, and they shall be my people. And they shall teach no more every man his neighbour, and every man his brother, saying, Know the LORD: for they shall all know me, from the least of them unto the greatest of them, saith the LORD: for I will forgive their iniquity, and I will remember their sin no more."

Part Seven: The Covenant of Grace in the New Testament

The Westminster Confession, Chapter 7, Section 6, teaches that under the gospel, "the covenant of grace was and is still to be administered in the preaching of the Word and the administration of the sacraments of baptism and the Lord's Supper; in which grace and salvation are held forth in more fulness, evidence, and efficacy, to all nations."

The coming of Christ was the great turning point in the administration of the covenant of grace. All the types and shadows of the Old Testament found their fulfillment in Him. The sacrificial lamb was fulfilled by the Lamb of God. The priesthood was fulfilled by the great High Priest. The temple was fulfilled by the One who said, "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up" (John 2:19). The prophets were fulfilled by the Prophet who spoke not merely the words of God but was Himself the Word of God. The Davidic king was fulfilled by the King of kings and Lord of lords.

At the Last Supper, Jesus explicitly connected His death to the covenant. Taking the cup, He said: "This cup is the new testament in my blood, which is shed for you" (Luke 22:20). The word "testament" is the same word as "covenant." Jesus was declaring that His blood — His sacrificial death on the cross — was the inauguration of the new covenant prophesied by Jeremiah. The old ceremonies were passing away. The substance had arrived.

The book of Hebrews provides the most extended theological reflection on the relationship between the old and new administrations of the covenant. The author of Hebrews argues that Christ is the Mediator of a "better covenant, which was established upon better promises" (Hebrews 8:6). The old ceremonies were "a shadow of good things to come, and not the very image of the things" (Hebrews 10:1). Now that the reality has come, the shadows are no longer needed.

Part Eight: The New Covenant Fulfilled in Christ

When the Lord Jesus Christ came into the world, He came as the fulfillment of every covenant promise. He was the seed of the woman promised in Genesis 3:15. He was the offspring of Abraham in whom all nations would be blessed. He was the prophet like Moses whom God would raise up (Deuteronomy 18:15). He was the son of David whose kingdom would have no end. He was the suffering servant of Isaiah 53 who would bear the iniquities of His people. He was the Mediator of the new covenant prophesied by Jeremiah.

The writer to the Hebrews makes this connection explicit: "But now hath he obtained a more excellent ministry, by how much also he is the mediator of a better covenant, which was established upon better promises" (Hebrews 8:6). The new covenant is "better" not because the old covenant was bad, but because the new covenant brings to full realization what the old covenant only foreshadowed. The blood of bulls and goats could not take away sin (Hebrews 10:4), but the blood of Christ cleanses from all sin (1 John 1:7). The Levitical priests served in an earthly tabernacle, but Christ serves as High Priest in the heavenly sanctuary itself (Hebrews 9:24). The old sacrifices had to be repeated endlessly, but Christ offered one sacrifice for sins forever and sat down at the right hand of God (Hebrews 10:12).

At the institution of the Lord's Supper, Jesus took the cup and said, "This is my blood of the new testament, which is shed for many for the remission of sins" (Matthew 26:28). With these words, Jesus declared that His death on the cross would be the covenant sacrifice that ratified the new covenant — just as the blood of animals had ratified the old covenant at Sinai (Exodus 24:8). The new covenant was sealed not with the blood of animals but with the precious blood of the Son of God.

The Puritan John Flavel, who ministered faithfully at Dartmouth in Devon from 1656 until the Great Ejection of 1662 and continued his ministry in secret thereafter, wrote movingly about Christ as the covenant surety. A surety is one who takes responsibility for another's debts. Christ, as the surety of the new covenant, took upon Himself the debts that we owed to God — our sins, our guilt, our punishment — and paid them in full on the cross. Flavel marveled that the eternal Son of God would willingly bind Himself to bear the curse that we deserved, and he urged his readers to respond with adoring gratitude and wholehearted obedience.

Part Nine: The Covenant of Redemption — The Eternal Foundation

Some of the Westminster divines and many Puritan theologians distinguished between the covenant of grace (made between God and the elect in time) and the covenant of redemption (made between the Father and the Son in eternity). The covenant of redemption, sometimes called the pactum salutis or the counsel of peace, is the eternal agreement within the Trinity by which the Father appointed the Son to be the Mediator and Redeemer of the elect, and the Son voluntarily accepted this appointment.

While the Westminster Confession does not use the phrase "covenant of redemption" explicitly, several passages in the Standards imply it. The Confession, Chapter 8, Section 1, declares: "It pleased God, in his eternal purpose, to choose and ordain the Lord Jesus, his only begotten Son, to be the Mediator between God and man; the Prophet, Priest, and King; the Head and Saviour of his church; the Heir of all things; and Judge of the world; unto whom he did, from all eternity, give a people to be his seed."

Patrick Gillespie, the Scottish Puritan who served as Principal of the University of Glasgow from 1653 to 1660, wrote an entire treatise on the covenant of redemption titled The Ark of the Covenant Opened, published in 1677. Gillespie argued that the covenant of redemption was the foundation upon which the entire covenant of grace rested. Before time began, before the world was created, before man had fallen, the Father and the Son entered into an eternal agreement for the salvation of the elect. The Father would give a people to the Son. The Son would assume human nature, obey the law perfectly, and bear the penalty of sin on behalf of His people. And the Father would reward the Son by raising Him from the dead, exalting Him to the highest place, and giving Him a redeemed people who would be His forever.

This eternal covenant gives the covenant of grace its absolute security. The covenant of grace does not depend on the faithfulness of sinful human beings. It depends on the faithfulness of the triune God — Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — who made an eternal, unbreakable covenant before the world began. As Jesus said in John 6:37-39: "All that the Father giveth me shall come to me; and him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out. For I came down from heaven, not to do mine own will, but the will of him that sent me. And this is the Father's will which hath sent me, that of all which he hath given me I should lose nothing, but should raise it up again at the last day."

Part Nine: The Practical Significance of Covenant Theology

Understanding God's covenants is not merely an academic exercise. It has profound implications for how we live the Christian life.

First, covenant theology gives us assurance of salvation. If salvation depended on our performance — on our ability to keep a covenant of works — we would have no hope, for we fail daily. But because salvation rests on the covenant of grace, sealed by the blood of Christ and guaranteed by the faithfulness of God, we can have confidence that our salvation is secure. As Paul writes: "Being confident of this very thing, that he which hath begun a good work in you will perform it until the day of Jesus Christ" (Philippians 1:6).

Second, covenant theology shapes our understanding of the family. God's covenant promises extend to believers and their children. This is why the Westminster Standards teach infant baptism — not because infants can exercise faith, but because the children of believers are members of the covenant community and are entitled to receive the sign of the covenant. Just as circumcision was given to the children of Abraham as a sign that they belonged to the covenant people, so baptism is given to the children of believers as a sign that they belong to the new covenant community.

Third, covenant theology shapes our understanding of the church. The church is not a voluntary association of like-minded individuals. It is the covenant community of God — the gathered people whom God has called out of the world and bound to Himself by solemn covenant. The preaching of the Word, the administration of the sacraments, the exercise of discipline, the practice of fellowship — all of these are covenant activities, performed by a covenant people, under the authority of a covenant God.

Fourth, covenant theology shapes our reading of Scripture. When we understand the covenantal structure of the Bible, the Old and New Testaments cease to be two separate books and become one unified story — the story of God's covenant faithfulness to His people across the ages. Abraham, Moses, David, the prophets, the apostles, and the church today are all part of one covenant people, saved by one covenant Mediator, through one covenant of grace.

The great Puritan pastor Richard Sibbes, who served as the Master of St. Catharine's Hall, Cambridge, from 1626 until his death in 1635, and who was known as "the heavenly Doctor Sibbes" for his tender and comforting preaching, wrote beautifully about the security that covenant theology provides: "If God be in covenant with us, we have God Himself. His wisdom is ours to direct us, His power is ours to protect us, His mercy is ours to pity us, His love is ours to embrace us. All that God is, and all that God has, is ours by covenant."

Thomas Brooks, another beloved Puritan pastor who ministered in London during the turbulent decades of the 1650s and 1660s, urged his readers to "plead the covenant" in prayer. When you are burdened by sin, plead the covenant promise of forgiveness. When you are overwhelmed by trials, plead the covenant promise of preservation. When you are discouraged by your weakness, plead the covenant promise of the Spirit's power. God has bound Himself by covenant, and He will not break His word.

Part Ten: The Unity of the Covenant of Grace

One of the most important teachings of the Westminster Standards regarding the covenants is that the covenant of grace is one covenant, not two or more. The Confession, Chapter 7, Section 6, states that under the gospel, "there are not therefore two covenants of grace differing in substance, but one and the same under various dispensations."

This means that Old Testament believers were saved in exactly the same way as New Testament believers — by grace alone, through faith alone, in Christ alone. Abraham was justified by faith, not by works (Romans 4:1-5). David was forgiven by grace, not by sacrificial performance (Romans 4:6-8). The Old Testament saints looked forward to a Redeemer they had not yet seen; we look back to a Redeemer who has already come. But in both cases, salvation is by grace through faith in the promised Messiah.

The Puritan theologian John Ball, whose Treatise of the Covenant of Grace was published posthumously in 1645 — the very year the Westminster Assembly was completing its work — argued at length for the unity of the covenant of grace. Ball demonstrated from Scripture that the same promises of forgiveness, justification, adoption, and eternal life that are offered in the New Testament were also offered, in shadowy form, in the Old Testament. The substance was always the same; only the administration differed.

This unity of the covenant of grace across the ages of redemptive history is one of the glories of Reformed theology. It means that we worship the same God as Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. We trust in the same Saviour as David and Isaiah. We are sustained by the same Spirit who empowered Moses and Elijah. We belong to the same covenant people that has existed from the beginning of the world and will continue until Christ returns. And the promise that God made to Abraham — "I will be their God, and they shall be my people" — is the same promise that He makes to every believer today.

As the Apostle Paul declares in Galatians 3:29: "And if ye be Christ's, then are ye Abraham's seed, and heirs according to the promise."

Let us conclude this lesson by returning to where we began — with the wonder of God's condescension. The infinite, eternal, self-sufficient God did not need to make a covenant with man. He did not need to save anyone. He could have justly condemned the entire human race and remained perfectly righteous in doing so. But in His mercy and love, He chose to condescend. He chose to bind Himself by covenant. He chose to promise salvation. He chose to send His Son. He chose to pour out His Spirit. He chose to gather a people for Himself from every nation, tribe, and tongue. And every one of those choices was free, sovereign, and undeserved.

The Puritan pastor Thomas Manton, who served as one of the clerks of the Westminster Assembly and later pastored a large congregation in Covent Garden, London, until his ejection in 1662, summarized the glory of covenant theology in these words: "The covenant is the very marrow and soul of all God's dealings with man." Manton taught that without the concept of covenant, the Bible is a confusing collection of disconnected stories. But with the concept of covenant, the Bible becomes a unified, coherent, breathtaking narrative of God's faithfulness to His promises and His people.

May this truth anchor your soul and gladden your heart. You serve a God who keeps His covenants. What He has promised, He will perform. What He has begun, He will finish. What He has sealed with the blood of His Son, nothing in heaven or earth or hell can overturn.

--- Closing ---

The God we serve is a covenant-keeping God. He bound Himself by promise to save a people for His glory, and He has never broken His word. From the garden of Eden to the cross of Calvary, from the first promise to the last trumpet, the covenant of grace runs like a river of mercy through the landscape of human history. Rest in this covenant. Trust in this covenant. Build your life upon this covenant. For the God who made it is faithful, and what He has promised, He will perform. Until next time, students.
