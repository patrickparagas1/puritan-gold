--- Lesson ---

The most fundamental question in all of philosophy is this: Does God exist? Everything else depends on the answer. If God exists, then life has meaning, morality has a foundation, and human beings have a destiny beyond the grave. If God does not exist, then we are cosmic accidents, morality is an illusion, and death is the end of the story. The stakes could not be higher.

The Christian answer, of course, is that God does exist, and that His existence is evident to everyone who has eyes to see. The apostle Paul states this clearly in Romans 1:20: "For the invisible things of him from the creation of the world are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made, even his eternal power and Godhead; so that they are without excuse." God's existence is not a matter of blind faith. It is demonstrated by the evidence of creation itself.

Let us examine three arguments from creation that have been used by Christian thinkers for centuries.

The first is the Cosmological Argument — the argument from the existence of the universe. This argument can be stated simply: Everything that begins to exist has a cause. The universe began to exist. Therefore, the universe has a cause. Modern science has confirmed what theologians have long affirmed: the universe is not eternal. It had a beginning. The Big Bang theory, the second law of thermodynamics, and the expansion of the universe all point to a definite starting point. But if the universe had a beginning, something must have caused it. And that cause must be outside the universe — uncaused, timeless, immaterial, and unfathomably powerful. In other words, the cause must have the attributes of God.

The second is the Teleological Argument — the argument from design. This argument observes that the universe displays an astonishing degree of order, complexity, and purpose that points to an intelligent Designer. Consider the fine-tuning of the universe: the physical constants that govern the behavior of matter and energy are calibrated with extraordinary precision. If the gravitational constant were different by one part in ten to the fortieth power, stars could not form. If the strong nuclear force were slightly different, atoms could not exist. The number of these finely tuned parameters runs into the dozens, and the probability of all of them having their exact values by chance is essentially zero.

Or consider the complexity of living organisms. A single human cell contains more information than the entire Encyclopedia Britannica. The DNA molecule is a code — a language — that carries instructions for building and maintaining every organism on earth. Codes do not arise by chance. Languages are not produced by random processes. Wherever we find specified complexity — information arranged for a purpose — we infer the existence of an intelligent mind behind it. The design we see in nature points to a Designer of incomprehensible wisdom and creativity.

The third is the Moral Argument — the argument from the existence of objective moral values. This argument observes that all human beings have a deep, intuitive awareness of right and wrong. We know that torturing innocent children is evil. We know that selfless love is good. We know that justice matters and that injustice is an outrage. But where do these moral convictions come from?

If there is no God, then morality is a human invention — a product of evolution, culture, or personal preference. But if morality is merely invented, then it has no binding authority. If one culture says slavery is wrong and another says it is acceptable, there is no objective standard by which to adjudicate between them. Moral outrage becomes meaningless, because there is nothing truly wrong with anything.

But we know better. We know that some things are genuinely right and some things are genuinely wrong — not just for us, but for everyone, everywhere, at all times. This objective moral law points to an objective moral Lawgiver — a being who is Himself the standard of goodness and who has inscribed His moral law on the human heart. That being is God.

These arguments do not prove every detail of the Christian faith. They do not, by themselves, establish the Trinity, the incarnation, or the resurrection. But they do establish the most fundamental truth: that a powerful, intelligent, and moral Being exists behind the universe. And once that truth is established, the door is open to consider the specific claims of Christianity.

--- Review Questions ---

Review Question 1: Explain the Cosmological Argument in your own words. What does the beginning of the universe imply about its cause?

Review Question 2: What is the Teleological Argument? How does the fine-tuning of the universe point to a Designer?

Review Question 3: How does the Moral Argument work? Why does the existence of objective moral values require a moral Lawgiver?

--- Activity ---

Choose one of the three arguments studied today (Cosmological, Teleological, or Moral) and write a two-paragraph summary explaining it to an imaginary friend who does not believe in God. Use simple, clear language. Avoid jargon. Include at least one specific example or illustration. Then have a family member or friend play the role of a skeptic and raise one objection, and practice responding to it respectfully and thoughtfully.

--- Closing ---

The evidence for God's existence surrounds us. Creation itself testifies to its Creator. As you grow in your ability to articulate these arguments, you will be better equipped to help others see what has been clearly revealed. Until next time, students.
