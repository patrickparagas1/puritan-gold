PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 12
"Does God Exist? Arguments from Creation"
Apologetics for Young Christians — Lesson 2


Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we examine the foundational question of all apologetics — indeed, the foundational question of all human thought: Does God exist? We will explore the powerful arguments from creation that point to the reality of a divine Creator, arguments that have been articulated by some of the greatest minds in the history of human civilization. Let us pray and begin.

Creator God, the heavens declare Thy glory and the firmament showeth Thy handiwork. Day unto day uttereth speech, and night unto night showeth knowledge. As we study the evidence of Thy existence in the world Thou hast made, strengthen our faith and equip us to share these truths with others who are searching for answers. Open our eyes to see Thy fingerprints upon all of creation. In Christ's name, Amen.


The most fundamental question in all of philosophy, in all of science, in all of human inquiry, is this: Does God exist? This is not merely one question among many. It is the question upon which everything else depends. If God exists, then life has ultimate meaning, morality has an objective foundation, human beings possess inherent dignity, and there is hope beyond the grave. If God does not exist, then we are cosmic accidents drifting through a purposeless universe, morality is an illusion, human dignity is a convenient fiction, and death is the final and irreversible end of every person's story. The stakes could not be higher.

The Christian answer, of course, is that God does exist — that His existence is real, that it is knowable, and that it is evident to everyone who has eyes to see and a mind willing to follow the evidence where it leads. The apostle Paul states this truth with remarkable clarity and force in Romans 1:19-20: "Because that which may be known of God is manifest in them; for God hath shewed it unto them. For the invisible things of him from the creation of the world are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made, even his eternal power and Godhead; so that they are without excuse."

Notice the strength of Paul's language here. The knowledge of God is not hidden. It is "manifest" — openly displayed, clearly visible. God has not left Himself without witness. He has "shewed" His existence through the things He has made. His "invisible things" — His eternal power and divine nature — are "clearly seen" through the creation. And the result is that all people are "without excuse." No one can stand before God on the Day of Judgment and say, "I did not know You existed. There was no evidence." The evidence is everywhere. Creation itself is a massive, unmistakable witness to the existence of its Creator.

The Psalmist declared this same truth in Psalm 19:1-4: "The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork. Day unto day uttereth speech, and night unto night sheweth knowledge. There is no speech nor language, where their voice is not heard. Their line is gone out through all the earth, and their words to the end of the world." The heavens are speaking. They are declaring — loudly, clearly, incessantly — the glory of the God who made them. This is a universal testimony that transcends language, culture, and geography. Every person on earth, in every generation, has access to this evidence.

Now, while the existence of God is clearly revealed in creation and conscience, Christian thinkers throughout history have also developed formal arguments that articulate and organize the evidence from creation in logical form. These arguments do not replace faith. They do not substitute for the regenerating work of the Holy Spirit. But they do demonstrate that faith in God is not irrational — that it is supported by powerful evidence and sound reasoning. Let us examine four of the most important of these arguments in detail.

The first and perhaps the most intuitively powerful is the Cosmological Argument — the argument from the existence of the universe. This argument has a long and distinguished history. It was formulated in various forms by the ancient Greek philosophers, refined by medieval Islamic philosophers such as Al-Kindi and Al-Ghazali in the ninth and eleventh centuries, developed with great sophistication by Thomas Aquinas in the thirteenth century, and presented with clarity and force by modern philosophers such as William Lane Craig, who has done more than perhaps any living thinker to bring this argument to public attention.

The argument can be stated in a simple and elegant syllogism — a logical argument with two premises and a conclusion:

Premise 1: Everything that begins to exist has a cause.
Premise 2: The universe began to exist.
Conclusion: Therefore, the universe has a cause.

Let us examine each premise carefully.

Premise 1 — Everything that begins to exist has a cause — is one of the most fundamental principles of human reasoning. It is the basis of all scientific inquiry. When a scientist observes a new phenomenon, the first question is always, "What caused this?" The idea that something could come into existence from nothing, by nothing, for no reason, is not just counterintuitive — it is absurd. As the ancient maxim puts it, "Ex nihilo nihil fit" — out of nothing, nothing comes. This principle has never been falsified by any observation or experiment. It is as solid a foundation as any argument could rest upon.

The Puritan theologian Stephen Charnock, born in 1628 in the parish of St. Katherine Cree, London, addressed this principle in his monumental work The Existence and Attributes of God, published posthumously in 1682. Charnock wrote with remarkable depth about the impossibility of self-creation, arguing that nothing can bring itself into existence because it would have to exist before it existed in order to cause its own existence — a logical impossibility. Charnock declared, "It is the first principle that nothing can give being to itself. If it had not being, how could it give what it had not?" Charnock's rigorous reasoning demonstrates that the first premise of the cosmological argument is not merely plausible but undeniable.

Premise 2 — The universe began to exist — has been confirmed by multiple lines of scientific evidence in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries. Let us consider the most important of these.

First, the expansion of the universe. In 1929, the American astronomer Edwin Hubble, working at the Mount Wilson Observatory in California, discovered that distant galaxies are moving away from us in every direction — that the universe is expanding. If you reverse this expansion and trace it backward in time, you arrive at a point where all the matter and energy of the universe was concentrated in an infinitely dense, infinitely hot state — a singularity. This is the beginning of the universe — the moment when space, time, matter, and energy came into existence. Scientists call this event the Big Bang, and it occurred approximately 13.8 billion years ago according to current estimates.

Second, the cosmic background radiation. In 1965, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, working at Bell Laboratories in New Jersey, accidentally detected a faint microwave radiation permeating the entire universe. This radiation — the cosmic microwave background — is the residual heat from the initial explosion of the Big Bang. Its discovery provided powerful confirmation that the universe had a beginning.

Third, the second law of thermodynamics. This fundamental law of physics states that the usable energy in a closed system is always decreasing — that entropy (disorder) is always increasing. If the universe were eternal — if it had existed for an infinite amount of time — then all its usable energy would have been exhausted long ago. The universe would have reached what physicists call "heat death" — a state of maximum entropy where no work can be done and no life can exist. The fact that the universe still contains vast amounts of usable energy, and that stars are still burning and life is still possible, demonstrates that the universe has not existed forever. It had a beginning.

The great astronomer Robert Jastrow, who served as the founding director of NASA's Goddard Institute for Space Studies and who was himself an agnostic, acknowledged the theological implications of these discoveries. Jastrow wrote in his 1978 book God and the Astronomers, "For the scientist who has lived by his faith in the power of reason, the story ends like a bad dream. He has scaled the mountains of ignorance, he is about to conquer the highest peak; as he pulls himself over the final rock, he is greeted by a band of theologians who have been sitting there for centuries." Jastrow recognized that science was confirming what theologians had always taught — that the universe had a beginning.

Now, if both premises are true — if everything that begins to exist has a cause, and if the universe began to exist — then the conclusion follows necessarily: the universe has a cause. And what must this cause be like? It must be outside the universe, since it caused the universe to exist. It must be timeless, since it existed before time began. It must be immaterial, since it existed before matter existed. It must be enormously powerful, since it brought the entire universe into existence from nothing. And it must be personal, since the decision to create a universe is a choice — an act of will.

In other words, the cause of the universe must have exactly the attributes that the Bible ascribes to God — eternal, immaterial, all-powerful, and personal. The cosmological argument does not prove every detail of Christian theology, but it establishes the most fundamental truth: a being with the attributes of God exists.

The second argument is the Teleological Argument — the argument from design. The word "teleological" comes from the Greek word telos, meaning "end" or "purpose." This argument observes that the universe displays an astonishing degree of order, complexity, and purpose that points to an intelligent Designer.

This argument has ancient roots. The Psalmist expressed it poetically when he wrote, "I will praise thee; for I am fearfully and wonderfully made: marvellous are thy works; and that my soul knoweth right well" (Psalm 139:14). The prophet Jeremiah heard God declare, "Before I formed thee in the belly I knew thee; and before thou camest forth out of the womb I sanctified thee" (Jeremiah 1:5). The biblical writers saw design everywhere they looked — in the heavens, in the earth, in the human body, in the cycles of nature — and they attributed it to the wisdom and purpose of God.

In the modern era, the teleological argument has been strengthened enormously by discoveries in physics, cosmology, and biology. Consider the fine-tuning of the universe — one of the most remarkable discoveries in the history of science.

The physical constants that govern the behavior of matter and energy — the gravitational constant, the electromagnetic force constant, the strong nuclear force constant, the weak nuclear force constant, the cosmological constant, and many others — are calibrated with extraordinary precision. If any of these constants were different by even a tiny amount, the universe as we know it could not exist.

If the gravitational constant were stronger by one part in ten to the fortieth power, stars would burn too quickly and no planets capable of supporting life could form. If it were weaker by the same amount, stars could not form at all. If the strong nuclear force were slightly stronger, all hydrogen in the universe would have been converted to helium shortly after the Big Bang, and no water — essential for life — could exist. If it were slightly weaker, atoms heavier than hydrogen could not form, and the periodic table of elements would consist of a single entry. If the cosmological constant — the energy density of empty space — were larger by one part in ten to the one hundred and twentieth power, the universe would have expanded too rapidly for galaxies, stars, or planets to form. If it were smaller by the same amount, the universe would have collapsed back on itself almost immediately.

The number of these finely tuned parameters runs into the dozens, and the probability of all of them having their exact values by chance is so incomprehensibly small that some scientists have compared it to firing a bullet from one side of the observable universe and hitting a one-inch target on the other side. The physicist Roger Penrose calculated that the odds of the initial conditions of the universe arising by chance are one in ten to the power of ten to the power of 123 — a number so vast that it has more digits than there are particles in the observable universe.

Sir Fred Hoyle, the British astronomer who coined the term "Big Bang" (ironically, as a term of derision, since he did not initially accept the theory), was so impressed by the fine-tuning of the physical constants that he wrote, "A common sense interpretation of the facts suggests that a superintellect has monkeyed with physics, as well as chemistry and biology, and that there are no blind forces worth speaking about in nature." Hoyle was not a Christian, but the evidence compelled him to acknowledge that the universe looks designed.

Or consider the complexity of living organisms. A single human cell contains approximately three billion base pairs of DNA — a molecular code that carries the instructions for building and maintaining every structure and function of the organism. The information content of a single cell has been estimated to be equivalent to several encyclopedias. The DNA molecule is not merely complex — it carries specified complexity, meaning that its complexity is organized according to a pattern that performs a function. This is the hallmark of intelligent design.

In every other area of human experience, when we encounter specified complexity — information arranged for a purpose — we infer the existence of an intelligent mind behind it. When we see a book, we infer an author. When we see a computer program, we infer a programmer. When we see a building, we infer an architect. The specified complexity of living organisms — the intricate molecular machines, the precisely calibrated biochemical pathways, the elegant feedback mechanisms — all point to a Designer of incomprehensible wisdom and creativity.

William Paley, the English clergyman and philosopher born in 1743, articulated the classic version of this argument in his 1802 work Natural Theology. Paley used the famous analogy of finding a watch on a heath. If you found a watch lying on the ground, he argued, you would not conclude that it had assembled itself by natural forces. The intricate arrangement of its gears, springs, and hands — all working together for the purpose of telling time — would compel you to conclude that the watch had been designed by an intelligent watchmaker. In the same way, Paley argued, the far more intricate design of living organisms compels us to conclude that they were designed by an intelligent Creator.

The Puritan pastor and theologian John Howe, born in 1630 in Loughborough, Leicestershire, England, who served as chaplain to Oliver Cromwell from 1656 to 1658, wrote about the evidence of design in creation in his work The Living Temple of God. Howe argued that the order and beauty of the created world are a temple — a dwelling place of the divine presence — that testifies to the wisdom, power, and goodness of God. He wrote that creation is "a glass wherein we may behold the glory of the Creator" and urged his readers to look at the natural world with eyes of wonder and worship.

The third argument is the Moral Argument — the argument from the existence of objective moral values. This argument addresses not the physical world but the moral world — the deep, universal human awareness of right and wrong.

The argument can be stated as follows:

Premise 1: If God does not exist, objective moral values and duties do not exist.
Premise 2: Objective moral values and duties do exist.
Conclusion: Therefore, God exists.

Let us examine each premise.

Premise 1 observes that without God, there is no foundation for objective morality. If human beings are merely the accidental products of blind evolutionary processes, then our moral intuitions are simply the result of natural selection — survival strategies that helped our ancestors pass on their genes. But survival strategies are not moral truths. The fact that a behavior helped our ancestors survive does not make that behavior right. If evolution had taken a different course, we might have developed very different moral intuitions — and on a purely naturalistic view, those alternative intuitions would be neither better nor worse than the ones we actually have.

Without God, morality is reduced to personal preference, cultural convention, or evolutionary accident. But personal preferences are subjective — they differ from person to person. Cultural conventions are relative — they differ from society to society. And evolutionary accidents are arbitrary — they could have turned out differently. None of these provides a foundation for moral truths that are binding on all people, at all times, in all places.

The great Puritan theologian Richard Sibbes, born in 1577 in Tostock, Suffolk, England, and who served as Master of St. Catherine's Hall, Cambridge, and as preacher at Gray's Inn, London, wrote about the moral law inscribed on the human heart. Sibbes, in his work The Bruised Reed and Smoking Flax (1630), argued that God has planted in every human heart an awareness of His moral standards — what Paul calls "the work of the law written in their hearts" (Romans 2:15). This moral awareness, Sibbes taught, is part of what it means to be made in God's image. It is not the product of evolution or culture. It is the signature of the Creator upon His creation.

Premise 2 affirms that objective moral values and duties do, in fact, exist. This is not a controversial claim for most people. We know — with a certainty that runs deeper than any philosophical argument — that torturing innocent children for amusement is evil. We know that selfless love is good. We know that justice matters and that injustice is an outrage. We know that honesty is a virtue and that deception is a vice. These moral convictions are not merely our personal preferences. They are truths about the nature of reality — truths that would remain true even if every person on earth denied them.

If a society were to declare that torturing innocent children is good, we would not conclude that their moral standards are equally valid. We would conclude that they are wrong — objectively, really, truly wrong. And this judgment — the judgment that some things are genuinely right and some things are genuinely wrong, regardless of what anyone thinks or feels about them — presupposes an objective moral standard that transcends human opinion.

Where does this objective moral standard come from? It cannot come from human beings, because human beings are the ones being judged by it. It cannot come from nature, because nature is morally indifferent — a hurricane does not commit evil, and a sunset does not perform good. The objective moral law must come from a source that transcends both humanity and nature — a being who is Himself the standard of goodness and who has inscribed His moral law on the human heart. That being is God.

C.S. Lewis developed this argument with extraordinary clarity in his book Mere Christianity, which began as a series of BBC radio talks delivered during the Second World War between 1941 and 1944. Lewis observed that every culture in history has recognized a moral law — not the same in every detail, but remarkably consistent in its broad outlines. Murder, theft, lying, and betrayal are condemned in virtually every society. Courage, honesty, fairness, and kindness are admired in virtually every society. This universal moral awareness, Lewis argued, is best explained by the existence of a moral Lawgiver who has built the moral law into the fabric of human nature.

Before we move to our fourth argument, let us pause to consider some of the common objections raised against the arguments we have already examined, and how Christian thinkers have responded to them.

Against the Cosmological Argument, some skeptics have asked, "If everything that begins to exist has a cause, then what caused God?" This is perhaps the most common objection, and at first glance it seems powerful. But it actually misunderstands the argument. The first premise does not say that everything has a cause. It says that everything that begins to exist has a cause. God, by definition, did not begin to exist. He is eternal — He has always existed. The argument establishes that anything that comes into existence needs a cause, and since the universe came into existence, it needs a cause. But God did not come into existence, so the question "What caused God?" does not apply to Him.

The Puritan theologian John Owen addressed this in his work A Brief Declaration and Vindication of the Doctrine of the Trinity, where he wrote about the self-existence of God — what theologians call the aseity of God. Owen explained that God exists by the necessity of His own nature. He does not depend on anything else for His existence. He is, as God declared to Moses from the burning bush, "I AM THAT I AM" (Exodus 3:14) — the self-existent, self-sufficient, eternal Being who is the ground of all other existence. The universe needs a cause because it began to exist. God does not need a cause because He never began — He simply is, and always has been, and always will be.

Another objection to the Cosmological Argument is the suggestion that the universe might have caused itself — that it popped into existence from nothing by means of quantum fluctuations. This objection has been popularized by certain physicists, but it involves a fundamental confusion. Quantum fluctuations do not occur in nothing — they occur in a quantum vacuum, which is not nothing. A quantum vacuum is a rich, structured physical state governed by the laws of quantum mechanics. It is something, not nothing. The question remains: Where did the quantum vacuum come from? Where did the laws of quantum mechanics come from? The appeal to quantum fluctuations merely pushes the question back one step without answering it.

Against the Teleological Argument, skeptics sometimes appeal to the multiverse hypothesis — the idea that our universe is just one of an unimaginably vast number of universes, each with different physical constants, and that we happen to live in the one that is fine-tuned for life simply because we could not exist in any of the others. This is an interesting speculation, but it faces serious problems. First, there is currently no empirical evidence for the existence of other universes. The multiverse is a theoretical construct, not an observed reality. Second, even if a multiverse existed, it would itself require an explanation. Where did the multiverse come from? What mechanism generates the different universes? The multiverse hypothesis does not eliminate the need for a Designer — it merely expands the scope of what needs to be designed. Third, the multiverse hypothesis actually violates the principle of Occam's Razor — the principle that we should not multiply explanations beyond what is necessary. Positing an infinite number of unobservable universes to avoid the conclusion that our universe was designed is an extraordinary act of faith — far more extraordinary, one might argue, than simply acknowledging the Designer that the evidence points to.

Against the Moral Argument, some skeptics claim that morality can be explained by evolution — that our moral intuitions are simply the product of natural selection, evolved because they helped our ancestors survive and reproduce. But as we noted earlier, this evolutionary account of morality actually undermines morality rather than explaining it. If our moral beliefs are merely the product of evolutionary pressures, then they are not about truth — they are about survival. And there is no guarantee that what helps us survive is actually true or actually good. Evolution selects for fitness, not for truth. If believing a falsehood helped our ancestors survive, evolution would select for that belief. This means that on an evolutionary account, we have no reason to trust our moral intuitions — including the moral intuition that tells us we ought to be honest, compassionate, and just.

The philosopher Alvin Plantinga, born in 1932 in Ann Arbor, Michigan, who served as professor of philosophy at the University of Notre Dame, developed a related argument known as the Evolutionary Argument Against Naturalism. Plantinga argued that if our cognitive faculties — including our moral faculties — are the product of unguided evolution, then we have no reason to trust them, because evolution selects for survival, not for truth. This means that naturalism (the belief that only natural things exist) actually undermines itself — because if naturalism is true, then we have no reason to trust the cognitive faculties that led us to believe naturalism. This is a devastating internal critique of the naturalistic worldview.

The fourth argument I wish to introduce today is the Argument from Consciousness — the argument from the existence of the human mind. This argument has gained increasing attention in recent decades as scientists and philosophers have grappled with what is known as the "hard problem of consciousness."

The hard problem of consciousness is this: How do physical processes in the brain give rise to subjective, conscious experience? We know that brain activity correlates with conscious experience — that certain patterns of neural firing are associated with seeing red, hearing music, or feeling pain. But correlation is not explanation. How does a physical process — the firing of neurons, the movement of molecules — produce the subjective experience of seeing, hearing, or feeling? This is perhaps the deepest unsolved mystery in all of science.

On a purely materialistic worldview — a worldview that says only physical things exist — consciousness is deeply mysterious and arguably inexplicable. If the universe consists of nothing but matter and energy, governed by impersonal physical laws, then how did subjective experience arise? How did a universe of blind, unconscious matter produce beings who see, think, feel, love, wonder, and worship?

The Christian worldview provides a natural and elegant answer: consciousness exists because we were created by a conscious God. We have minds because we are made in the image of a God who has a mind. We are capable of thought, reason, creativity, and love because we were designed by a being who thinks, reasons, creates, and loves. Consciousness is not an inexplicable accident in a material universe. It is the expected result of being created by a personal, conscious God.

The Puritan philosopher and theologian Nathanael Culverwell, who was a fellow of Emmanuel College, Cambridge, in the 1640s, wrote about the light of reason in his work An Elegant and Learned Discourse of the Light of Nature, published posthumously in 1652. Culverwell argued that the human capacity for reason is itself a gift from God — a "candle of the Lord" (borrowing the language of Proverbs 20:27) that illuminates the mind and enables us to apprehend truth. For Culverwell, the very existence of human consciousness and rationality was evidence of a rational Creator who had designed us for the pursuit of truth.

Now, these four arguments — the Cosmological Argument, the Teleological Argument, the Moral Argument, and the Argument from Consciousness — are not the only arguments for God's existence, but they are among the most powerful and most widely discussed. Taken individually, each provides a strong reason to believe that God exists. Taken together, they create a cumulative case that is extraordinarily compelling.

It is important to understand what these arguments do and do not prove. They do not, by themselves, prove every detail of the Christian faith. They do not establish the Trinity, the incarnation, the atonement, or the resurrection. But they do establish the most fundamental truth of all: that a powerful, intelligent, moral, and personal Being exists behind the universe. They establish that the universe is not the product of blind, purposeless forces, but of a mind — a will — a purpose. And once that truth is established, the door is open to consider the specific claims of Christianity — claims that rest on historical evidence, fulfilled prophecy, and the testimony of Scripture.

As the Puritan divine Thomas Goodwin, born in 1600 in Rollesby, Norfolk, England, who served as President of Magdalen College, Oxford, from 1650 to 1660, wrote, "God has set the world in man's heart, and man in the world, so that man might seek God through the world, and find God through seeking." Creation is designed to lead us to the Creator. The arguments from creation are signposts pointing us toward the God who made all things.

Let us also consider some additional biblical passages that speak to the knowledge of God through creation. In Acts 14:17, Paul and Barnabas tell the people of Lystra that God "left not himself without witness, in that he did good, and gave us rain from heaven, and fruitful seasons, filling our hearts with food and gladness." Even the regular cycles of nature — rain, seasons, food, joy — are witnesses to the existence and goodness of God. In Acts 17:27-28, Paul tells the Athenians that God designed the world so "that they should seek the Lord, if haply they might feel after him, and find him, though he be not far from every one of us: for in him we live, and move, and have our being." God is not far from any of us. He has designed creation to be a signpost pointing to Himself, and He is closer to us than we often realize.

The great theologian Jonathan Edwards, born in 1703 in East Windsor, Connecticut, who later became president of the College of New Jersey (now Princeton University), wrote extensively about how creation reveals God's glory. In his dissertation Concerning the End for Which God Created the World, Edwards argued that God created the universe to display His perfections — His power, His wisdom, His beauty, His goodness — and that the proper response of rational creatures is to perceive these perfections and glorify God for them. Edwards wrote that the creation is like a mirror reflecting the glory of God, and that every aspect of the natural world — from the grandeur of mountains to the intricacy of a snowflake — reveals something about the character of the Creator.

Edwards also developed what might be called an argument from beauty. He observed that human beings have a deep, universal capacity to perceive and appreciate beauty — in nature, in music, in art, in mathematics, in the harmony of ideas. This capacity for aesthetic experience is difficult to explain on a purely materialistic worldview. Why would blind evolutionary processes produce beings who find sunsets beautiful, who are moved by music, who appreciate the elegance of a mathematical proof? On a Christian worldview, the answer is clear: we were created by a God who is Himself the source of all beauty, and our capacity to appreciate beauty is part of what it means to be made in His image.

The Puritan poet and pastor Edward Taylor, born around 1642 in Leicestershire, England, who emigrated to the American colonies and served as pastor in Westfield, Massachusetts, from 1671 until his death in 1729, expressed this sense of divine beauty throughout his Preparatory Meditations — a series of poems written before each celebration of the Lord's Supper. Taylor marveled at the beauty and intricacy of God's creation and saw in it a reflection of the infinite beauty of God Himself. His poetry is a powerful reminder that the arguments for God's existence are not merely logical exercises — they engage the whole person, including the aesthetic sensibility that responds to beauty and recognizes in it the signature of the divine Artist.

Let us also remember that the evidence for God's existence is not merely intellectual. It is personal. Every sunset, every starry night, every newborn child, every act of kindness, every experience of beauty, every moment of wonder — these are not mere data points in a philosophical argument. They are encounters with the living God who has revealed Himself in the world He has made. Psalm 34:8 invites us, "O taste and see that the Lord is good: blessed is the man that trusteth in him." The knowledge of God is not merely a conclusion to be reached by logic. It is a reality to be experienced by faith.

Let me also address a question that may be troubling some of you. If the evidence for God's existence is so strong, why do so many intelligent people not believe in God? This is a fair question, and the Bible provides a clear answer. The issue is not primarily intellectual but moral and spiritual. Romans 1:18 tells us that men "hold the truth in unrighteousness" — they suppress the truth, not because the evidence is insufficient, but because they do not want to submit to the God the evidence points to. Unbelief is not the result of insufficient evidence. It is the result of the human will in rebellion against God.

This does not mean that every individual atheist is being consciously dishonest. Many unbelievers are sincere and thoughtful people who have been influenced by the intellectual culture of their time. But the Bible's diagnosis of the human condition tells us that apart from the grace of God, the human heart is inclined to resist the truth — to look for reasons not to believe, to interpret the evidence in ways that allow us to maintain our independence from God. This is why apologetics, while important, is never sufficient by itself. The arguments can remove intellectual obstacles, but only the Holy Spirit can change a heart.

The Puritan pastor Thomas Brooks, born in 1608 and who served churches in London during the turbulent years of the English Civil War and the Interregnum, wrote about this in his work Precious Remedies Against Satan's Devices, published in 1652. Brooks catalogued the strategies that Satan uses to keep people from the truth, including the strategy of persuading sinners that they need more evidence before they can believe. Brooks warned that this is often a delaying tactic — a way of avoiding the decision that the evidence already demands. He urged his readers to respond to the evidence they already have, rather than waiting for evidence they do not need. Brooks wrote, "God's hearing of our prayers does not depend on the worthiness of our persons, but upon the worthiness of His Son." In the same way, the strength of the arguments for God's existence does not depend on the cleverness of the apologist, but on the reality of the God to whom they point.

Let us also consider the testimony of former atheists who came to faith through the evidence. We have already mentioned C.S. Lewis, who described himself as "the most dejected and reluctant convert in all England." Lewis did not come to faith easily or eagerly. He was dragged to it, kicking and screaming, by the weight of the evidence. But when he finally surrendered to the truth, he found that it was not a prison but a paradise — not the loss of freedom but the discovery of the deepest freedom there is.

Another remarkable conversion story is that of Lee Strobel, the former legal editor of the Chicago Tribune, who was an avowed atheist when his wife became a Christian in 1979. Strobel, trained as a journalist and lawyer, set out to investigate the evidence for Christianity with the intention of disproving it. Instead, after nearly two years of research, he was compelled by the evidence — the evidence for the resurrection, the reliability of the New Testament, the fine-tuning of the universe, and the moral argument — to conclude that Christianity was true. His book The Case for Christ, published in 1998, has become one of the most widely read apologetics books of the modern era.

These testimonies remind us that the arguments for God's existence are not merely theoretical. They have the power, under the blessing of the Holy Spirit, to change minds and transform lives. The God to whom these arguments point is not a distant, abstract deity. He is the living God who seeks and saves the lost, who draws sinners to Himself through the evidence of creation, the testimony of conscience, and the proclamation of the gospel.

As we close this lesson, I want to draw your attention to one final Scripture passage that ties together everything we have studied today. In Psalm 8:3-9, David writes, "When I consider thy heavens, the work of thy fingers, the moon and the stars, which thou hast ordained; What is man, that thou art mindful of him? and the son of man, that thou visitest him? For thou hast made him a little lower than the angels, and hast crowned him with glory and honour. Thou madest him to have dominion over the works of thy hands; thou hast put all things under his feet: All sheep and oxen, yea, and the beasts of the field; The fowl of the air, and the fish of the sea, and whatsoever passeth through the paths of the seas. O Lord our Lord, how excellent is thy name in all the earth!"

David looked at the heavens and was moved to worship. He saw the vastness of the cosmos and marveled not at the insignificance of man, but at the grace of God — that the Creator of such a vast universe would be mindful of human beings, would crown them with glory and honour, would give them dominion over the works of His hands. This is the proper response to the evidence for God's existence — not merely intellectual assent, but worship. Not merely the conclusion that God exists, but the joyful acknowledgment that this God is our Lord, and His name is excellent in all the earth.

The Puritan pastor Thomas Manton, born in 1620 in Lawrence-Lydiard, Somerset, England, who served as one of Oliver Cromwell's chaplains and later as rector of St. Paul's, Covent Garden, London, preached a magnificent series of sermons on Psalm 119 in which he reminded his congregation that the study of God's works and God's Word should always lead to deeper love and worship. Manton wrote, "The more we know God, the more we shall love Him; and the more we love Him, the more we shall desire to know Him." Let this be the spirit in which you study apologetics — not as a mere intellectual exercise, but as a pathway to deeper knowledge of God and deeper love for the One who made all things.

Review Question 3: How does the Moral Argument work? Why does the existence of objective moral values require a moral Lawgiver rather than being explainable by evolution, culture, or personal preference?

Review Question 4: What is the Argument from Consciousness, and why does the existence of subjective experience pose a challenge for purely materialistic worldviews?

Review Question 5: What do these arguments for God's existence establish, and what do they leave for further investigation?


Choose one of the four arguments studied today (Cosmological, Teleological, Moral, or Consciousness) and write a two-paragraph summary explaining it to an imaginary friend who does not believe in God. Use simple, clear language. Avoid jargon. Include at least one specific example or illustration. Then have a family member or friend play the role of a skeptic and raise one objection, and practice responding to it respectfully and thoughtfully.

Additionally, on a clear night this week, go outside and look at the stars. Read Psalm 19:1-6 and Psalm 8:3-4 aloud. Then write a brief journal entry reflecting on how the experience of looking at the night sky connects to the arguments studied in today's lesson.


The evidence for God's existence surrounds us. Creation itself testifies to its Creator. The universe shouts it from the heavens, the fine-tuning of physical constants whispers it in the laboratory, the moral law declares it in the human conscience, and the mystery of consciousness points to it in the depths of the human mind. As you grow in your ability to articulate these arguments, you will be better equipped to help others see what has been clearly revealed — that the God who made all things is real, and that He may be known. Until next time, students.
