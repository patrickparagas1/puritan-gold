PURITAN GOLD -- Daily Study
"Truly God Is Good to Israel"
May 12, 2026

---

"Truly God is good to Israel, even to such as are of a clean heart. But as for me, my feet were almost gone; my steps had well nigh slipped. For I was envious at the foolish, when I saw the prosperity of the wicked. For there are no bands in their death: but their strength is firm. They are not in trouble as other men; neither are they plagued like other men." -- Psalm 73:1-5

There is perhaps no psalm in the entire Psalter that deals more honestly and courageously with one of the most agonizing questions that afflicts the people of God: Why do the wicked prosper while the righteous suffer? This question has tormented believing hearts in every generation, and the seventy-third psalm gives us not merely a theoretical answer but a personal testimony, the account of a man who nearly lost his faith over this very question, and who found his way back to solid ground only by entering the sanctuary of God.

Asaph, the author of this psalm, begins with his conclusion. "Truly God is good to Israel, even to such as are of a clean heart." This is the end of the matter, the settled conviction to which the entire psalm is driving. But Asaph is honest enough to admit that he did not always hold this conviction with such confidence. There was a season when he nearly abandoned it altogether. "But as for me, my feet were almost gone; my steps had well nigh slipped." Almost gone. Well nigh slipped. How many of us have stood on that precipice! How many of us have felt the ground giving way beneath our faith, as we looked out at a world in which the wicked seem to flourish like green bay trees while the righteous are afflicted and despised!

"For I was envious at the foolish, when I saw the prosperity of the wicked." Here is the root of Asaph's spiritual crisis: envy. He looked at the ungodly and saw them living in ease, in health, in abundance. They had no troubles, no pains, no struggles. Their eyes stood out with fatness. They had more than heart could wish. And meanwhile, Asaph, who had given his life to the service of God, who had sought to keep his heart clean and his hands washed in innocence, found himself plagued all the day long and chastened every morning.

This was not merely an intellectual puzzle for Asaph. It was a spiritual crisis of the first order. He was tempted to conclude that serving God was a waste of time. "Verily I have cleansed my heart in vain, and washed my hands in innocency." What profit is there in godliness, if the ungodly have all the prosperity and the godly have all the affliction? This is the question that lies behind every form of practical atheism, every decision to abandon the ways of God in pursuit of worldly success, every compromise of principle in the name of expediency.

But then comes the turning point. "When I thought to know this, it was too painful for me; until I went into the sanctuary of God; then understood I their end." The sanctuary of God. The place of worship. The place where the Word of God is opened and the truth of God is proclaimed. It was here, and only here, that Asaph found the answer to his tormenting question. Not in the library of philosophy. Not in the court of public opinion. Not in the marketplace of worldly wisdom. But in the sanctuary, where God reveals things as they truly are, not as they appear to the natural eye.

And what did Asaph see when he entered the sanctuary? He saw the end of the wicked. "Surely thou didst set them in slippery places: thou castedst them down into destruction. How are they brought into desolation, as in a moment! they are utterly consumed with terrors." The prosperity of the wicked, which had seemed so solid and so permanent, was in reality as insubstantial as a dream from which one awakes. Asaph had been looking at the wicked through a telescope that showed only the present moment. In the sanctuary, God gave him an eternal perspective, and what he saw shattered his envy forever.

Jonathan Edwards, that towering intellect of the Reformed tradition, preached with devastating clarity on the theme of the sinner's false security. The wicked man, said Edwards, is like a man walking upon a sheet of ice over a bottomless abyss. The ice may be smooth. The walking may be pleasant. The sun may be shining. But beneath his feet is a chasm of destruction, and the ice is cracking with every step he takes. His prosperity is not a sign of God's favour but of God's patience, and that patience has a limit.

But Asaph did not merely see the end of the wicked. He also saw the blessedness of his own relationship with God. "Nevertheless I am continually with thee: thou hast holden me by my right hand. Thou shalt guide me with thy counsel, and afterward receive me to glory." Here is the true wealth of the believer, a wealth that no earthly prosperity can equal and no earthly loss can diminish. The believer has God Himself as his continual companion, his unfailing guide, and his eternal inheritance. The wicked may have their ease and their abundance for a season. But the believer has God, not merely for a season, but forever.

"Whom have I in heaven but thee? and there is none upon the earth that I desire beside thee." This is the language of a man who has passed through the fires of spiritual testing and has come out on the other side with pure gold. He has weighed the treasures of earth against the treasure of knowing God, and he has found the whole world wanting. If he has God, he needs nothing else. If he loses God, everything else is worthless. This is the settled conviction that the sanctuary produced in Asaph's heart, and it is the conviction that every believer must reach if he is to walk steadily through a world where the wicked prosper and the righteous are afflicted.

"But it is good for me to draw near to God: I have put my trust in the Lord God, that I may declare all thy works." Let this be our resolution, beloved. When envy assails us, when the prosperity of the wicked tempts us to question the goodness of God, let us flee to the sanctuary. Let us open the Word. Let us draw near to God in prayer. And let us wait there until He shows us things as they truly are, in the light of eternity. For truly, God is good to Israel. Amen.
