PURITAN GOLD -- Study Devotional, Day 5
Series: Romans
"None Righteous, No Not One"
The universal condemnation of humanity and the purpose of the law

---

Good evening, student of the Word. Every time we open the Scriptures, we stand on holy ground. The God of the universe has spoken, and He invites us to listen, to understand, and to respond. Let us listen well tonight.

We are studying the book of Romans together. Tonight's lesson is titled "None Righteous, No Not One." Each session builds on what came before, so if you have been following along, you will see how tonight's passage connects to the larger argument of the book.

--- Scripture Reading ---

Let us read our passage for tonight. It comes from Romans 3:1-20:

"What advantage then hath the Jew? or what profit is there of circumcision? Much every way: chiefly, because that unto them were committed the oracles of God... What then? are we better than they? No, in no wise: for we have before proved both Jews and Gentiles, that they are all under sin; As it is written, There is none righteous, no, not one: There is none that understandeth, there is none that seeketh after God. They are all gone out of the way, they are together become unprofitable; there is none that doeth good, no, not one. Their throat is an open sepulchre; with their tongues they have used deceit; the poison of asps is under their lips: Whose mouth is full of cursing and bitterness: Their feet are swift to shed blood: Destruction and misery are in their ways: And the way of peace have they not known: There is no fear of God before their eyes. Now we know that what things soever the law saith, it saith to them who are under the law: that every mouth may be stopped, and all the world may become guilty before God. Therefore by the deeds of the law there shall no flesh be justified in his sight: for by the law is the knowledge of sin."

Read that passage again to yourself. Mark any words or phrases that stand out. The Puritans read with a pen in hand, always ready to note what God was showing them.

--- Key Verse ---

Here is the key verse for tonight's study. Let it anchor everything else we discuss.

"Therefore by the deeds of the law there shall no flesh be justified in his sight: for by the law is the knowledge of sin." -- Romans 3:20

This verse is the lens through which we will examine the rest of tonight's passage. Keep it in the front of your mind as we proceed.

--- Exposition ---

Having read the text, let us explore its meaning in depth.

Robert Haldane offers valuable commentary on this passage. The Puritans were expositors of the highest order, and their insights remain remarkably fresh.

Robert Haldane, the Scottish Reformed expositor whose commentary on Romans became one of the most influential in the nineteenth century, regarded Romans 3:1-20 as the climax of Paul's indictment of humanity. Haldane observed that Paul begins this section by answering the natural objection that arises from chapter 2: if the outward Jew has no advantage, what was the point of God choosing Israel? Paul's answer is emphatic — 'Much every way.' The Jews possessed the incomparable privilege of the oracles of God, the sacred Scriptures. Haldane stressed that this is no small thing: to have God's revealed truth committed to your care is the greatest national privilege any people can possess.

But Haldane was equally emphatic that privilege does not equal innocence. When Paul asks 'Are we better than they?' and answers 'No, in no wise,' he levels the entire human race. Haldane saw in the catena of Old Testament quotations in verses 10-18 a complete anatomy of human depravity. The chain of citations — drawn from Psalms 14, 5, 140, 10, 36, Isaiah 59, and Ecclesiastes 7 — systematically condemns every part of the human person: the character (none righteous), the mind (none that understandeth), the will (none that seeketh after God), the conduct (all gone out of the way), the speech (throat, tongues, lips, mouth), the actions (feet swift to shed blood), and the fundamental orientation (no fear of God).

Haldane gave particular weight to verse 20: 'Therefore by the deeds of the law there shall no flesh be justified in his sight: for by the law is the knowledge of sin.' He argued that this verse is the tombstone over every system of works-righteousness. The law was never designed to justify sinners; it was designed to convict them. It is a mirror that shows us our filth but has no power to wash it away. The law's purpose is to shut every mouth and bring every soul to the feet of Christ.

Romans 3:1-20 brings Paul's great indictment of humanity to its devastating conclusion. He has shown that the Gentile world is guilty (1:18-32), that the moralist is guilty (2:1-16), and that the religious Jew is guilty (2:17-29). Now he gathers all the evidence and delivers the verdict: the entire human race stands condemned before a holy God.

The chapter opens with an important objection. If outward Jewishness means nothing (2:28-29), what advantage does the Jew have? Was God's election of Israel pointless? Paul answers with a resounding 'Much every way.' The chief advantage is that 'unto them were committed the oracles of God.' The Jews were entrusted with the Holy Scriptures — the law, the prophets, the psalms — God's very words. This is an immeasurable privilege. Through Israel, God preserved His truth for all nations. Through Israel came the covenants, the promises, and ultimately the Messiah Himself (Romans 9:4-5).

But in verses 3-4, Paul addresses the related question: does Jewish unfaithfulness nullify God's faithfulness? 'God forbid: yea, let God be true, but every man a liar.' This is a fundamental principle of theology: God's character does not depend on human response. His faithfulness stands even when all humanity proves faithless. Paul quotes Psalm 51:4 — David's confession — to show that God is always justified in His words and prevails in judgment. Human sin cannot overthrow divine truth; it can only confirm it.

Verses 5-8 address a subtle philosophical objection: if our unrighteousness demonstrates God's righteousness, is God unjust to punish us? Paul dismisses this with horror: 'God forbid: for then how shall God judge the world?' The idea that sin can serve as an excuse because God uses it for His purposes is morally absurd. Paul explicitly rejects the slanderous charge that his gospel teaches 'Let us do evil, that good may come' — a charge he says those who make deserve their condemnation.

Then in verse 9 comes the great leveling: 'What then? are we better than they? No, in no wise: for we have before proved both Jews and Gentiles, that they are all under sin.' 'All under sin' — not merely that all have committed sins, but that all are under sin's dominion, its authority, its enslaving power. Sin is not just what we do; it is what we are by nature. We are born into it (Psalm 51:5), shaped by it, enslaved to it.

To prove this universal indictment, Paul strings together a chain of Old Testament quotations in verses 10-18. This is one of the most concentrated collections of Scripture in the entire Bible. Each citation is carefully chosen to condemn a different aspect of human nature.

'There is none righteous, no, not one' (Psalm 14:1-3) — the universal negative. Not most are unrighteous, not many, but all. The addition of 'no, not one' removes any possible exception. 'There is none that understandeth' — the mind is darkened. 'There is none that seeketh after God' — the will is perverted. Left to ourselves, we do not seek God; we flee from Him, as Adam and Eve hid in the garden (Genesis 3:8). Every apparent seeking after God by the natural man is actually seeking after the benefits of God — comfort, meaning, security — while avoiding God Himself.

'They are all gone out of the way, they are together become unprofitable' — like sheep that have gone astray, turning every one to his own way (Isaiah 53:6). The human race collectively has abandoned the path of righteousness. 'There is none that doeth good, no, not one' — not that humans are incapable of any outward good, but that nothing done apart from faith and the Spirit meets God's standard of true goodness.

Verses 13-14 turn to speech: 'Their throat is an open sepulchre' (Psalm 5:9) — the imagery is grotesque, a gaping grave belching out corruption and death. 'With their tongues they have used deceit' — speech becomes an instrument of manipulation. 'The poison of asps is under their lips' (Psalm 140:3) — words that wound and kill. 'Whose mouth is full of cursing and bitterness' (Psalm 10:7) — speech that curses rather than blesses. James 3 will later develop this theme extensively.

Verses 15-17 turn to actions: 'Their feet are swift to shed blood: Destruction and misery are in their ways: And the way of peace have they not known' (Isaiah 59:7-8). From Cain's murder of Abel onward, human history is a chronicle of violence, destruction, and war.

Verse 18 delivers the root diagnosis: 'There is no fear of God before their eyes' (Psalm 36:1). This is the fountainhead of all sin. Where there is no fear of God — no reverent awe, no holy trembling before the Almighty — every other sin follows naturally. The fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom (Proverbs 9:10); its absence is the beginning of folly and wickedness.

Verses 19-20 draw the legal conclusion. 'Now we know that what things soever the law saith, it saith to them who are under the law.' The law speaks first and foremost to those who possess it — the Jews. If they are condemned by their own Scriptures, who can escape? The purpose: 'that every mouth may be stopped, and all the world may become guilty before God.' The courtroom imagery is vivid. The accused stands before the judge, the evidence has been presented, and there is nothing left to say. Every mouth is stopped. No defense can be offered. The entire world is guilty — hupodikos, liable to punishment, answerable to God.

Verse 20 is the epitaph over human self-salvation: 'Therefore by the deeds of the law there shall no flesh be justified in his sight: for by the law is the knowledge of sin.' The law cannot justify because it was never designed to justify. It was designed to reveal sin, to make sin 'exceeding sinful' (Romans 7:13), to drive the sinner to despair of self-effort and cast himself entirely upon the mercy of God. The law is a schoolmaster to bring us to Christ (Galatians 3:24). It does its work perfectly — but its work is condemnation, not salvation.

This is where Paul wants every reader to be as he prepares to unfold the glory of justification by faith in the next section. You must know you are sick before you will welcome the physician. You must know you are condemned before you will receive the pardon. Every mouth stopped, every excuse silenced, the whole world guilty — and now, at last, we are ready to hear the gospel.

--- Application ---

Knowledge puffs up, Paul warns, but love builds up. Let us ensure that what we have studied tonight leads not to pride but to faithful, loving obedience.

The universal condemnation of Romans 3:1-20 is not meant to leave us in despair but to prepare us for grace. Here are practical applications.

First, let the law do its work in your heart. When you read God's commandments and feel convicted, do not rush past that conviction. Sit with it. Let the weight of your failure press you to Christ. The danger of cheap grace is that it skips over the law's work of conviction. Martin Luther said the law must thunder before the gospel can comfort. This week, read the Ten Commandments slowly (Exodus 20) and ask the Holy Spirit to show you where you fall short — not just in action but in thought and desire.

Second, abandon self-righteousness completely. 'There is none righteous, no, not one.' This includes you. It includes your pastor, your parents, your heroes. The ground is level at the foot of the cross. Stop keeping a mental scorecard of your spiritual achievements. Stop comparing your sins to others' sins. Acknowledge with Paul that you are the chief of sinners (1 Timothy 1:15) and cast yourself entirely on Christ.

Third, cultivate the fear of God. Verse 18 identifies the root problem: 'There is no fear of God before their eyes.' We live in a culture that has largely lost any sense of God's holiness, majesty, and authority. Counter this by meditating on passages that reveal God's awesome greatness: Isaiah 6, Revelation 4-5, Psalm 97. Begin your prayers not with requests but with worship. Let the fear of the Lord recalibrate your entire life.

Fourth, use this passage in evangelism. Many people believe they are basically good. This passage shatters that illusion. When sharing the gospel, gently but honestly present the bad news before the good news. Ask people: 'Do you believe you are righteous enough to stand before a perfectly holy God?' Let the law do its convicting work so that the gospel of grace will shine all the brighter.

Write down one concrete action you will take this week in response to tonight's study. Do not let this truth remain theoretical. The Puritans measured the success of a sermon not by how much the hearer learned but by how much the hearer changed.

--- Closing ---

Sovereign God, we stand before You with every mouth stopped and every excuse silenced. We confess that there is none righteous among us, no, not one. We have all gone out of the way. Our speech, our thoughts, our actions — all fall short of Your glory. Thank You that the purpose of this conviction is not our destruction but our deliverance. Thank You that You gave the law not to save us by our works but to drive us to the Savior. We abandon every shred of self-righteousness and cast ourselves wholly upon Your mercy in Christ. Amen.

Until our next study, walk in the truth you have received.
