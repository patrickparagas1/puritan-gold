PURITAN GOLD -- My Growth, Day 3
"Justification by Faith Alone"
Theological Day — The Doctrine of Justification

---

Welcome. Today is a Theological day — a day for doctrinal depth. We begin with the doctrine that Martin Luther called "the article upon which the church stands or falls": justification by faith alone. The Puritans inherited this doctrine from the Reformers and developed it with extraordinary precision and pastoral warmth.

Let us read Romans 3:23-26 (KJV):

"For all have sinned, and come short of the glory of God; Being justified freely by his grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus: Whom God hath set forth to be a propitiation through faith in his blood, to declare his righteousness for the remission of sins that are past, through the forbearance of God; To declare, I say, at this time his righteousness: that he might be just, and the justifier of him which believeth in Jesus."

--- Teaching ---

What is justification? The Puritans defined it with careful precision. Justification is the judicial act of God by which He declares the sinner righteous on the basis of Christ's righteousness, received through faith alone. Notice the elements of this definition.

First, justification is judicial. It is a legal declaration, not a moral transformation. When God justifies a sinner, He does not make the sinner righteous in character — that is sanctification, a separate work. Rather, He declares the sinner righteous in standing. The distinction is crucial. Justification changes your status before God's law; sanctification changes your nature before God's holiness. Both are necessary, but they must not be confused.

John Owen, the greatest theologian among the Puritans, devoted an entire treatise to this doctrine. Owen argued that justification addresses the deepest problem of the human condition: guilt. Every human being stands before God's holy law as a lawbreaker. The wages of sin is death. The sentence is just. And no amount of moral improvement, religious ritual, or good works can change the verdict. The guilty person cannot earn a "not guilty" verdict by behaving better after the crime. A new basis for the verdict is needed.

That basis is the righteousness of Christ. Owen explained that Christ accomplished two things on behalf of His people. First, He suffered the penalty for their sins — bearing the curse of the law, enduring the wrath of God, dying the death that sinners deserved. This is sometimes called the passive obedience of Christ. Second, He perfectly obeyed the law of God throughout His entire life — fulfilling every commandment, satisfying every requirement, achieving the perfect righteousness that Adam failed to achieve. This is the active obedience of Christ.

In justification, both of these achievements are credited to the believer. Christ's death pays the debt. Christ's life provides the righteousness. The sinner receives both through faith — not as a reward for faith, but through faith as the instrument by which Christ's work is received.

Thomas Watson, in his popular Body of Divinity, used a vivid illustration. He compared justification to a royal pardon combined with a royal robe. The pardon removes the criminal's guilt. The robe adorns the pardoned criminal with the dignity of a prince. In justification, the pardon is Christ's atoning death, and the robe is Christ's perfect righteousness. The sinner who was condemned now stands before God wearing the robe of a prince — not because of anything he has done but because of everything Christ has done.

The Puritans were careful to distinguish between the ground of justification and the instrument of justification. The ground — the basis, the foundation — is Christ's work alone. It is His righteousness, not ours, that satisfies God's justice. The instrument — the means by which we receive it — is faith alone. Not faith plus works. Not faith plus sacraments. Not faith plus moral effort. Faith alone.

But what is faith? The Puritans insisted that saving faith is not mere intellectual assent. It is not simply believing that certain doctrines are true. Saving faith includes knowledge (understanding the Gospel), assent (agreeing that it is true), and trust (personally resting upon Christ and His work for your salvation). Owen wrote that "many believe the doctrine of justification who have never been justified, because they assent to the truth without trusting in the Savior."

The pastoral implications of justification are immense. If you are justified by faith in Christ, then your standing before God does not fluctuate with your performance. On your best day, you are not more justified. On your worst day, you are not less justified. Your justification rests on the unshakable foundation of Christ's finished work, and nothing — not your failures, not your doubts, not your sins — can alter the verdict that God has declared over you in Christ.

Richard Sibbes, the tender pastor, applied this doctrine to the anxious conscience. He wrote that many Christians live in constant fear because they are unconsciously trying to earn their justification through good behavior. When they fail, they feel condemned. When they succeed, they feel proud. Both responses reveal the same error: they have placed the ground of their acceptance in themselves rather than in Christ. The cure is to return, again and again, to the cross — to hear the verdict, to receive the robe, and to rest.

--- Closing ---

You are justified. Not by your works but by His grace. Not by your righteousness but by His. This is the article upon which everything stands. Rest in it today. Lord, I thank You that my standing before You does not depend on my performance but on Christ's. When guilt accuses me, remind me of the robe. When doubt whispers, remind me of the verdict. I am justified freely by Your grace. Amen.
