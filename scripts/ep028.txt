PURITAN GOLD — Episode 28
"Justification by Faith Alone"
Week 4, Day 7 (Sunday) — Devotional Episode

---

If you could stand before God today — right now, this very moment — and He asked you, "Why should I let you into My heaven?"... what would you say?

Think carefully. Because your answer to that question reveals everything about what you truly believe.

Would you point to your church attendance? Your good deeds? Your moral track record? Would you say, "Lord, I've tried my best. I've been a decent person. I've helped others when I could"?

Or would you say something entirely different? Would you say, "Lord, I have nothing to offer You. Nothing. My righteousness is filthy rags. My best efforts fall infinitely short of Your holiness. But I stand before You clothed in a righteousness that is not my own — the righteousness of Jesus Christ, received by faith alone"?

That... is the doctrine of justification by faith alone. And it is, as Martin Luther called it, "the article upon which the church stands or falls." It is the beating heart of the Reformation. It is the doctrine that turned the world upside down five hundred years ago. And it is the truth that will sustain your soul when everything else gives way.

This is Puritan Gold, and today — on this Lord's Day — we're going to sit with this doctrine. Not as an academic exercise, but as a devotional meditation. Because justification isn't just a theology to be debated. It's a reality to be received. It's a truth to be treasured. It's the foundation on which every other blessing of the Christian life is built.

---

Let's begin with Scripture. Romans 3:21-24: "But now the righteousness of God has been manifested apart from the law, although the Law and the Prophets bear witness to it — the righteousness of God through faith in Jesus Christ for all who believe. For there is no distinction: for all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God, and are justified by his grace as a gift, through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus."

Read that again slowly in your mind. "Justified by his grace as a gift."

Justification is a legal declaration. It's God the Judge declaring the guilty sinner to be righteous — not because the sinner has become righteous in himself, but because the righteousness of Christ has been credited to his account. Theologians call this "imputation." Christ's perfect obedience — His sinless life, His fulfillment of every jot and tittle of God's law — is imputed to the believer. It's placed on your record. It's counted as yours.

And in exchange, your sin — all of it, past, present, and future — was placed on Christ at the cross. Second Corinthians 5:21: "For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God."

This is what the Reformers called the "great exchange." Your sin for His righteousness. Your guilt for His innocence. Your condemnation for His acquittal.

And it's received by faith alone. Not by works. Not by effort. Not by moral improvement. By faith. Trust. Resting your entire weight on the finished work of Christ, the way you'd rest your entire weight on a chair without checking first to see if it will hold.

---

Now, let me bring in Proverbs — because even the wisdom literature points us toward this truth. Proverbs 11:4 says: "Riches do not profit in the day of wrath, but righteousness delivers from death."

On the day of wrath — the day when every human being stands before the holy God — your riches won't save you. Your accomplishments won't save you. Your reputation won't save you. Only righteousness delivers from death. But whose righteousness? Not yours. Yours is insufficient. Isaiah 64:6 tells us that "all our righteous deeds are like a polluted garment." Even our best works are tainted by mixed motives, by pride, by self-interest.

The righteousness that delivers from death is the righteousness of Christ — received by faith. This is what Solomon is pointing toward, even if he couldn't see the full picture as clearly as we can on this side of the cross. The entire Old Testament is groaning toward the gospel — toward the day when God would provide a righteousness that sinners could receive as a gift.

---

Martin Luther understood this. And it changed everything.

Luther was an Augustinian monk. He was devout, disciplined, ascetic. He fasted. He confessed his sins for hours at a time. He punished his body. He did everything he could to earn God's favor — and the more he tried, the more terrified he became. Because he knew — with an honesty that many of us lack — that his best efforts were not enough. The law demanded perfection. And Luther was not perfect.

He later described his spiritual breakthrough with the simplicity of a man who had been rescued from drowning. While studying the book of Romans, he came upon the phrase "the righteousness of God" — and suddenly, what had terrified him became the sweetest truth he'd ever heard. The righteousness of God was not the standard by which God would condemn him. It was the gift by which God would save him. A righteousness from God, received by faith.

Luther wrote: "I felt myself to be reborn and to have gone through open doors into paradise. The whole of Scripture took on a new meaning, and whereas the phrase 'the righteousness of God' had before filled me with hate, now it became to me inexpressibly sweet in greater love."

That's what justification does. It takes the thing you feared most — God's righteousness, God's justice, God's holiness — and transforms it into the thing you love most. Because God's righteousness is no longer against you. It's for you. In Christ, God's justice and God's mercy meet. The cross satisfies both.

---

John Calvin built on Luther's foundation and gave the Reformed tradition its most careful articulation of justification. In his Institutes, Calvin wrote that justification is "the principal hinge by which religion is supported." He argued that without justification by faith alone, the entire Christian life collapses into either despair or self-righteousness. Either you'll be crushed by the weight of trying to earn God's favor, or you'll be puffed up with the delusion that you've succeeded.

But with justification — with the knowledge that you are accepted by God, not because of what you've done but because of what Christ has done — you are set free. Free from the tyranny of performance. Free from the anxiety of never being enough. Free to obey God, not in order to earn His love, but because you already have it.

The Puritans took this even further. They didn't just believe in justification as a doctrinal proposition — they lived in it. They preached it to their own hearts daily. They made it the foundation of their devotional lives.

Thomas Goodwin, the great Puritan, wrote about what he called "the heart of Christ in heaven towards sinners on earth." He argued that Christ's love for His justified people is not a cold, legal transaction. It's warm. It's personal. It's ongoing. Christ doesn't merely acquit you and then walk away. He embraces you. He delights in you. He intercedes for you. He is, even now, at the right hand of the Father, praying for you by name.

And that... that is the wonder of justification. It's not just a change in your legal status. It's the beginning of a relationship with the living God — a relationship built not on your performance, but on His grace.

---

Romans 3:25-26 completes the thought: "God put forward Christ Jesus as a propitiation by his blood, to be received by faith. This was to show God's righteousness, because in his divine forbearance he had passed over former sins. It was to show his righteousness at the present time, so that he might be just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus."

Just and the justifier. That's the miracle. God doesn't compromise His justice to save you. He satisfies His justice fully — at the cross — and then justifies you freely. The penalty has been paid. The debt has been settled. The wrath that your sin deserved was poured out on Christ. And now, there is no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus. None. Zero. The verdict has been rendered, and it is: not guilty.

Not "barely guilty." Not "guilty but forgiven." Not guilty. Period. Because the Judge has looked at Christ's record and credited it to your account.

Spurgeon, preaching on justification, once thundered from his pulpit: "I bear my witness that I never knew what true peace meant until I understood the doctrine of substitution. When I saw that my sins were laid on Christ, and that His righteousness was made mine, then my heart found rest."

That's what this doctrine does. It gives rest. Rest to the anxious conscience. Rest to the weary striver. Rest to the soul that has tried and failed, tried and failed, tried and failed to be good enough.

You're not good enough. You never will be. But Christ is. And His goodness is yours, by faith.

---

Now... let me say something to those of you who struggle with assurance. Maybe you believe in justification intellectually, but you don't feel justified. You don't feel forgiven. The weight of your sin still presses down on you, and you wonder if God really accepts you.

The Puritans wrestled with this deeply. They called it the "trial of assurance," and they wrote volumes about it. And here is what they concluded: your assurance does not rest on your feelings. It rests on the promises of God. You may not feel justified on any given day — but if you are trusting in Christ, you are justified. The declaration has been made. The verdict has been rendered. And no feeling — no matter how dark, no matter how persistent — can overturn the verdict of the divine Judge.

John Bunyan, the author of The Pilgrim's Progress, spent years in a dungeon of spiritual doubt before he finally broke through to assurance. And when he did, it was not because his feelings changed. It was because he stopped looking at himself and started looking at Christ. He later wrote: "One day, as I was passing into the field, the sentence fell upon my soul: 'Thy righteousness is in heaven.' And I saw that it was not my good frame of heart that made my righteousness better, nor yet my bad frame of heart that made my righteousness worse; for my righteousness was Jesus Christ Himself."

Your righteousness is in heaven. It's not in your heart — where it would fluctuate daily. It's in Christ — where it remains perfect forever.

---

So, friends, as we close this week — this rich, full week of Proverbs and theology and pastoral reflection — let me point you to the one truth that holds everything else together.

You are justified. Not by your works. Not by your wisdom. Not by your religious performance. By grace. Through faith. In Christ alone.

This is what the Puritans believed. This is what Luther discovered. This is what Calvin taught. This is what Spurgeon preached. This is what the apostle Paul proclaimed in chains. And this is what I want you to carry with you into the new week: you stand before God clothed in a righteousness that is not your own. And it is enough. More than enough. It is perfect.

Rest in that. Rejoice in that. And let it change everything.

Let's pray.

O God of all grace, we bow before You in wonder at the doctrine of justification. That You — the holy, just, and righteous God — would declare sinners righteous through faith in Your Son... this is beyond our comprehension. But we receive it. We rest in it. We stake our eternal lives upon it. Thank You for the cross, where justice and mercy met. Thank You for Christ, whose righteousness is our only hope. Thank You for the gift of faith, by which we lay hold of what we could never earn. Forgive us for the times we've tried to add our works to Your grace. Forgive us for the times we've doubted Your verdict. And seal upon our hearts this blessed truth: that there is now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus. May this truth humble us, comfort us, and send us out into the world as people who have been set free. In the matchless name of Jesus Christ, our righteousness and our redeemer. Amen.
