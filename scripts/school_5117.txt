PURITAN GOLD -- Homeschool, Lesson 18
"Answering Moral Objections to Christianity"
Apologetics for Young Christians — Lesson 8

---

Welcome back to Puritan Gold's homeschool program. Today we address some of the most common and emotionally charged moral objections raised against the Christian faith — the charge that the Bible endorses violence, that Christians are hypocrites, and that religion has done more harm than good in the world. These are objections that you will encounter frequently in conversations with non-Christians, in media portrayals of Christianity, and perhaps even in your own moments of doubt. We will examine each objection honestly, acknowledge the legitimate concerns behind it, and demonstrate that the Christian faith, when properly understood, provides a more compelling moral vision than any alternative. Let us pray and begin.

Lord, we come before Thee knowing that Thy church has not always lived up to the standards Thou hast set. We acknowledge the failures of those who have borne Thy name. But we also know that Thou art perfect, even when Thy people are not, and that Thy Word is true, even when Thy followers fall short. Give us wisdom to answer difficult moral questions about our faith with honesty and grace, courage to acknowledge the failings of Christians while pointing people to the perfection of Christ, and humility to see that we ourselves are sinners saved by grace, not paragons of virtue who look down on others. In Christ's name, Amen.

--- Lesson ---

Some of the most common objections to Christianity are not intellectual but moral. People do not merely doubt whether Christianity is true — they question whether it is good. They look at the violence in the Old Testament, the hypocrisy of Christians, and the wars fought in the name of religion, and they conclude that Christianity is not a force for good in the world but a force for harm. These are serious objections, and they deserve serious, honest, and careful responses.

The Puritan tradition, for all its strengths, was also characterized by a relentless commitment to self-examination and honest acknowledgment of sin. The Puritans did not deny the failures of the church. They confronted them openly and honestly, in the light of Scripture, trusting that the truth would ultimately vindicate God's Word even when God's people fell short. We must follow their example today. Defensiveness is not a Christian virtue. Honesty is.

Let us begin with the first objection: "The Bible endorses violence and is morally outdated."

This objection usually points to Old Testament passages involving divine judgment — the destruction of the Canaanites in the book of Joshua, the flood of Noah in Genesis 6-9, the plagues of Egypt in Exodus 7-12, or the imprecatory psalms that call down God's judgment on the wicked. "How can you worship a God who commands genocide?" the critic asks. "How can you call a book morally inspired that contains such violence?"

This is a serious question, and it requires a thorough response. Let us consider several important points.

First, we must understand these passages in their proper context. The judgments described in the Old Testament are not random acts of violence or unprovoked aggression. They are acts of divine justice — God's righteous response to extreme, prolonged, and grievous wickedness. The Canaanite nations, for example, had practiced child sacrifice — burning their own children alive as offerings to the idol Molech. They practiced ritual prostitution in their temples. They engaged in every form of moral abomination described in Leviticus 18. And they had done so for centuries. God's judgment on them was not arbitrary cruelty. It was the just response of a holy God to profound, persistent, and unrepentant moral corruption.

Consider also that God was extraordinarily patient with the Canaanites before judgment fell. In Genesis 15:16, God told Abraham that his descendants would not inherit the land of Canaan for four hundred years, because "the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet full." God waited four centuries — four hundred years of patience and forbearance — before executing judgment on the Canaanite nations. This is not the behavior of a capricious tyrant. This is the behavior of a patient and longsuffering God who gives every opportunity for repentance before judgment falls.

The book of Jonah illustrates this divine patience beautifully. God sent Jonah to preach to Nineveh, a city of notorious wickedness, and when the Ninevites repented, God relented from the judgment He had threatened. God is always willing to show mercy to those who repent. The Canaanites had centuries of opportunity to turn from their wickedness, and they refused. The judgment that fell on them was just.

Second, we must recognize that God is not obligated to tolerate evil indefinitely. The critic who objects to divine judgment in the Old Testament is often the same person who objects to the problem of evil — who asks, "Why doesn't God do something about all the evil in the world?" But when God does act against evil — when He executes justice against the wicked — the same critic objects that God is too harsh. This is an inconsistency. You cannot simultaneously demand that God act against evil and then condemn Him for doing so.

The Puritan theologian Thomas Watson, born in Yorkshire, England, around 1620, who served as rector of St. Stephen's Church in Walbrook, London, addressed this issue with characteristic clarity in his Body of Divinity. Watson argued that God's justice is an essential and glorious attribute — as essential to His character as His love and mercy. A God who never punished evil would not be a good God but a morally indifferent God. Watson wrote, "God's justice is the rectitude or perfection of His nature, whereby He is infinitely righteous and holy in Himself, and in all His proceedings with the creature." The judgments of the Old Testament, Watson argued, are not contrary to God's goodness — they are expressions of it. A just God cannot be indifferent to wickedness.

Third, we should note that the Old Testament also contains some of the most beautiful, humane, and progressive moral teaching in the ancient world — moral teaching that was centuries ahead of its time and that laid the groundwork for many of the moral convictions that Western civilization takes for granted today.

The command to love your neighbor as yourself appears in Leviticus 19:18 — centuries before any comparable command appeared in any other ancient legal code. The requirement to care for the poor, the widow, the orphan, and the stranger runs throughout the law of Moses (Deuteronomy 10:18-19; 24:17-22). The institution of the Sabbath as a day of rest for all people — including servants and even animals — was revolutionary in the ancient world (Exodus 20:10). The requirement that judges render impartial justice, without favoritism to the rich or the poor, was a groundbreaking legal principle (Leviticus 19:15). The prophets' passionate advocacy for justice, mercy, and humility — "What doth the Lord require of thee, but to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God?" (Micah 6:8) — remains one of the highest expressions of moral aspiration in human literature.

The claim that the Bible is "morally outdated" ignores the fact that many of our most cherished moral convictions — the dignity of every human being, the importance of caring for the vulnerable, the principle of equal justice under law, the duty of compassion — originated in the biblical tradition. The critic who charges the Bible with moral inadequacy is often using moral standards that the Bible itself introduced.

Fourth, the moral objection often rests on a philosophical inconsistency that is important to expose. If there is no God and no objective moral standard, on what basis can someone call anything "immoral"? The very act of moral objection — the act of pointing to something in the Bible and declaring it to be wrong — presupposes an objective moral standard by which the Bible is being measured. But where does that moral standard come from? If it comes from cultural consensus, then it has no binding authority over other cultures or other times. If it comes from individual feeling, then it is merely subjective preference. If it comes from evolution, then it is merely a survival strategy with no genuine moral content.

The only adequate foundation for objective moral standards is the existence of a moral God who has established those standards as reflections of His own character. The critic who charges God with immorality is, ironically, borrowing from the Christian worldview to make his argument — using a concept (objective morality) that only makes sense if the God he is attacking actually exists.

The Puritan theologian Stephen Charnock, born in 1628 in London, England, addressed this philosophical point at length in The Existence and Attributes of God. Charnock argued that the moral law is not an arbitrary set of rules imposed by God but a reflection of God's own eternal, unchanging, perfect moral nature. God does not command things because they are good. Things are good because they reflect the character of God. And human beings have a sense of moral obligation — a conscience — because they are made in the image of a moral God. The atheist who appeals to morality to argue against God is appealing to an authority that only exists if God exists.

Let us also examine the specific case of the flood of Noah, which is frequently cited as an example of divine cruelty. In Genesis 6:5-7, we read, "And God saw that the wickedness of man was great in the earth, and that every imagination of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually. And it repented the Lord that he had made man on the earth, and it grieved him at his heart. And the Lord said, I will destroy man whom I have created from the face of the earth." Notice three things about this passage. First, the wickedness was not ordinary — "every imagination of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually." This describes a civilization so thoroughly and irredeemably corrupted that it had reached a moral point of no return. Second, God was not indifferent to this judgment — "it grieved him at his heart." God did not destroy the world with casual indifference or sadistic pleasure. He did so with sorrow. Third, God provided a means of escape — the ark. Noah preached righteousness for the 120 years during which the ark was being built (2 Peter 2:5), giving the world ample opportunity to repent. No one accepted the invitation. The judgment that fell was just, and the offer of mercy had been extended for over a century.

The Puritan pastor Matthew Henry, born in 1662 in Flintshire, Wales, who produced one of the most widely read commentaries on the entire Bible, wrote about the flood with characteristic pastoral insight. Henry observed that God's judgments are always preceded by warnings and invitations to repent. The flood was not the first resort of an impatient God but the last resort of a patient God whose patience had been exhausted by the utter refusal of mankind to repent. Henry wrote, "God is slow to anger, but He is not slow to justice. When the day of grace is past, the day of judgment will surely come."

The Puritan theologian John Calvin, whose influence on Puritan theology was immense, addressed the moral difficulties of the Old Testament with careful theological reasoning. Calvin argued that God, as the Creator and Sustainer of all life, has the sovereign right to give and take life as He sees fit. Every breath we take is a gift of grace. We are not entitled to life — we receive it as a gift from our Creator. Therefore, when God takes life in judgment, He is not violating anyone's rights. He is exercising His sovereign prerogative as the Author of life. This does not mean that human beings have the right to take life at will — the command "Thou shalt not kill" prohibits human murder precisely because life belongs to God, not to us. But God Himself, as the rightful owner of all life, has the authority to give it and to take it as His justice and wisdom require.

Calvin also argued that the Old Testament judgments must be understood in light of the New Testament revelation of the final judgment. The destruction of the Canaanites, the flood of Noah, and the other divine judgments of the Old Testament are foreshadowings of the final judgment that awaits all humanity. They serve as warnings — vivid, concrete, historical warnings — that God takes sin seriously and that judgment is real. If we object to the temporal judgments of the Old Testament, we must reckon with the eternal judgment described in the New Testament, which Jesus spoke about more frequently and more soberly than any other teacher in Scripture. "And these shall go away into everlasting punishment: but the righteous into life eternal" (Matthew 25:46).

Fifth, we should consider the specific claim that the Bible is "morally outdated" on matters such as slavery, the role of women, and sexual ethics. These are complex topics that require more time than we can give them today, but several general principles are important.

Regarding slavery, the Bible did not invent the institution of slavery — it regulated an institution that was universal in the ancient world and established principles that ultimately undermined it. The Old Testament law required the humane treatment of slaves, provided for their release after a set period (Exodus 21:2; Deuteronomy 15:12-15), and prohibited the return of runaway slaves to their masters (Deuteronomy 23:15-16) — a provision that was revolutionary in the ancient world. The New Testament sowed the seeds that would eventually destroy slavery by declaring that in Christ "there is neither bond nor free" (Galatians 3:28), by calling masters to treat their slaves with justice and fairness (Colossians 4:1), and by presenting the letter to Philemon, in which Paul urged a Christian slave owner to receive his runaway slave back "not now as a servant, but above a servant, a brother beloved" (Philemon 1:16). It was Christians — motivated by these biblical principles — who led the abolition movements in both Britain and America.

Regarding the role of women, the Bible affirms the equal dignity and worth of men and women as image-bearers of God (Genesis 1:27), while also teaching complementary roles within the family and the church. Jesus treated women with a dignity and respect that was extraordinary in His cultural context. He spoke to them publicly, taught them, included them among His followers, and appeared first to women after His resurrection. Christianity elevated the status of women in the ancient world and has continued to do so wherever the gospel has gone.

Regarding sexual ethics, the biblical teaching on sexuality — that sexual intimacy is reserved for the covenant of marriage between a man and a woman — has been consistent from Genesis to Revelation. While this teaching is increasingly countercultural, the question is not whether it is popular but whether it is true. And the Christian argues that it is true — that the biblical vision of sexuality reflects the Creator's design for human flourishing, and that departures from that design, however culturally sanctioned, ultimately lead to brokenness and harm.

The Puritan pastor Richard Sibbes, born in 1577 in Tostock, Suffolk, England, who served as preacher at Gray's Inn and Master of Catherine Hall, Cambridge, wrote about the moral authority of Scripture in his work The Returning Backslider. Sibbes argued that the moral teachings of Scripture are not arbitrary rules imposed by a tyrannical God but loving instructions given by a wise Father who knows what is best for His children. Just as a loving parent sets boundaries for a child's protection, God's moral law is designed for human flourishing. Obedience brings blessing; disobedience brings harm — not because God is vindictive, but because His laws reflect the moral structure of reality itself. To violate God's moral law is to violate the grain of the universe.

Now let us address the second objection: "Christians are hypocrites."

This objection points to the gap between what Christians profess and how they live — and we must be honest: there is often truth to it. Christians have sometimes been guilty of terrible hypocrisy, both individually and institutionally. There have been pastors who preached purity while living in secret sin. There have been churches that proclaimed love while practicing racism. There have been institutions that claimed to serve the vulnerable while exploiting them. These failures are real, they are serious, and they should be acknowledged without excuse or evasion.

The Puritan tradition understood the danger of hypocrisy and warned against it with relentless vigor. The Puritan pastor Richard Baxter, born in 1615 in Rowton, Shropshire, England, wrote in his classic work The Reformed Pastor about the absolute necessity of integrity in the lives of ministers and all Christians. Baxter argued that a minister who does not practice what he preaches is "a traitor to Christ and a murderer of souls." Baxter did not mince words: "It is a palpable error of some ministers who make such a disproportion between their preaching and their living; who study hard to preach exactly, but study not to live exactly." Baxter called for a correspondence between doctrine and life, between profession and practice, that left no room for hypocrisy.

The Puritan pastor John Owen, born in 1616 in Stadham, Oxfordshire, England, wrote extensively about the ongoing battle against sin in the life of the believer. In his treatise On the Mortification of Sin in Believers, published in 1656, Owen argued that every genuine Christian is engaged in a lifelong war against the remaining sin in their own heart. Sanctification — the process of becoming more like Christ — is a real and ongoing process, but it is not complete in this life. Christians are genuinely converted, but they are not yet perfect. They are in the process of being transformed, but the transformation is gradual and sometimes painful. The presence of hypocrisy in the church is not evidence that Christianity is false. It is evidence that the church is full of people who are still in the process of being sanctified — and that the process is sometimes slow and difficult.

Now, having honestly acknowledged the reality of Christian hypocrisy, let us make several important qualifications.

First, hypocrisy does not disprove the truth of Christianity. A mathematics teacher who cannot balance his own checkbook does not thereby prove that mathematics is false. A doctor who smokes does not thereby prove that smoking is healthy. The truth of a belief system is independent of the behavior of its adherents. Christianity should be judged on the basis of its claims, its evidence, and its internal consistency — not on the behavior of every person who claims to be a Christian. Jesus Himself is the standard by which Christianity should be evaluated, not the fallible human beings who follow Him imperfectly.

Second, Christianity itself condemns hypocrisy in the strongest possible terms. Jesus reserved His harshest criticism not for tax collectors and sinners but for the Pharisees — the religious hypocrites of His day. In Matthew 23, Jesus pronounces a series of devastating "woes" against the Pharisees: "Woe unto you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! for ye are like unto whited sepulchres, which indeed appear beautiful outward, but are within full of dead men's bones, and of all uncleanness" (Matthew 23:27). The Bible does not excuse hypocrisy. It condemns it. The fact that Christians sometimes fail to live up to their own standards is not evidence that the standards are wrong — it is evidence that the standards are high, and that we need grace.

Third, while some Christians have been hypocrites, many others have been paragons of extraordinary virtue — men and women whose lives have embodied the love, sacrifice, and holiness that the gospel produces. Consider the following examples.

William Wilberforce, the evangelical Christian member of Parliament who devoted his life to the abolition of the slave trade in the British Empire, enduring decades of opposition, ridicule, and personal sacrifice, finally succeeded in seeing the slave trade abolished in 1807 and slavery itself abolished throughout the British Empire in 1833 — just three days before his death.

The countless monastic orders and missionary organizations that established the first hospitals, orphanages, and schools in Europe and throughout the world — institutions that served the poorest and most vulnerable members of society at a time when no one else would.

Martin Luther King Jr., the Baptist pastor who led the American civil rights movement on the basis of explicitly Christian principles — the dignity of every human being as an image-bearer of God, the demands of divine justice, and the power of sacrificial love.

The tireless work of Christian relief organizations around the world — providing food, medical care, education, and shelter to millions of people in need, regardless of their race, religion, or background.

The Puritan pastor Cotton Mather, born in 1663 in Boston, Massachusetts, who organized charitable societies, promoted public health (including the controversial advocacy for smallpox inoculation), and devoted enormous energy to practical good works in his community.

The fruits of genuine Christian faith — love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control (Galatians 5:22-23) — are undeniable when they appear in the lives of true believers. The existence of counterfeit coins does not disprove the existence of genuine ones. And the existence of hypocrites in the church does not disprove the existence of genuine saints whose lives have been transformed by the power of the Holy Spirit.

The Puritan pastor Thomas Brooks, born in 1608 in London, England, wrote about the difference between genuine and counterfeit Christianity in his work Precious Remedies Against Satan's Devices. Brooks argued that Satan's most effective strategy is not outright opposition to the church but the infiltration of the church with hypocrites — people who wear the mask of Christianity while living in contradiction to its teachings. Brooks warned that the presence of hypocrites in the church should not surprise or scandalize believers, because Jesus Himself predicted it. In the parable of the wheat and the tares (Matthew 13:24-30), Jesus taught that the church would contain both genuine believers and imposters until the final judgment, when God would separate them.

Now let us address the third objection: "Religion has done more harm than good."

This claim is made frequently in our culture, often with reference to the Crusades, the Inquisition, the Wars of Religion, or various other historical episodes of violence associated with Christianity. Again, honesty requires us to acknowledge that terrible things have been done in the name of Christianity. The Crusades involved genuine atrocities. The Inquisition was a dark chapter in church history. Christians have sometimes used the Bible to justify slavery, colonialism, and other forms of oppression. These are facts, and we must not deny them or minimize them.

But a fuller picture of history reveals a very different — and much more balanced — story than the one the critics typically present.

First, the claim that religion causes most of the world's violence is simply not supported by the historical evidence. In fact, the greatest atrocities in human history were perpetrated not by religious people but by explicitly atheistic or anti-religious regimes. The Soviet Union, under the atheistic ideology of Marxism-Leninism, was responsible for the deaths of an estimated 20 million people through executions, forced labor camps (the Gulag), and deliberate famines. Mao Zedong's Communist China, guided by atheistic Marxist ideology, was responsible for an estimated 45 to 65 million deaths during the Great Leap Forward and the Cultural Revolution. The Khmer Rouge regime in Cambodia, inspired by a blend of Marxism and radical nationalism, killed an estimated 1.5 to 2 million people — roughly a quarter of the country's population. Nazi Germany, while not straightforwardly atheist, was deeply anti-Christian in its ideology and was responsible for the systematic murder of 6 million Jews and millions of others.

The twentieth century — the century in which atheistic ideologies held unprecedented political power — was the bloodiest century in human history. Secular ideologies have produced far more bloodshed, far more oppression, and far more human misery than all the religious wars in human history combined. The historian Alister McGrath has noted that "the Inquisition killed perhaps a few thousand people over several centuries. Atheistic regimes killed tens of millions in a few decades."

Second, the claim that religion has done more harm than good ignores the overwhelmingly positive contributions that Christianity has made to human civilization — contributions so pervasive and so fundamental that our culture would be unrecognizable without them.

Christians pioneered education. The first universities in Europe — Oxford, Cambridge, Paris, Bologna — were founded by the church for the purpose of training clergy and advancing knowledge. For centuries, the church was the primary institution responsible for literacy and education in the Western world. The Puritans, in particular, were passionate advocates for education. The Puritan settlers of New England founded Harvard College in 1636 — just sixteen years after arriving in the New World — to ensure a supply of educated ministers and an educated citizenry.

Christians established hospitals. The first institutions dedicated to the care of the sick were founded by Christians in the Roman Empire. Basil of Caesarea established a vast complex of care facilities in the fourth century that has been called the first hospital in history. Throughout the medieval period, monastic orders operated hospitals, leprosariums, and hospices throughout Europe, caring for the sick and the dying when no one else would.

Christians championed human rights. The concept of inherent human dignity — the idea that every human being has intrinsic worth, regardless of race, sex, social status, or ability — is a distinctively Christian idea, grounded in the biblical teaching that every person is made in the image of God (Genesis 1:27). This concept is the foundation of modern human rights discourse, and it originated in the Christian tradition. The abolition of slavery, the rights of women, the protection of children, the care of the disabled — all of these moral advances have their deepest roots in the Christian understanding of human dignity.

The Puritan pastor and theologian Samuel Rutherford, born in 1600 in Nisbet, Scotland, made a remarkable contribution to political theory in his work Lex, Rex — Latin for "The Law Is King" — published in 1644. In this revolutionary work, Rutherford argued that even kings are subject to God's law and that rulers who violate that law may be legitimately resisted. Rutherford's arguments laid important groundwork for the development of constitutional government and the rule of law — ideas that would profoundly influence the American founding fathers a century later. Here was a Puritan pastor, driven by biblical convictions about justice and the sovereignty of God, contributing to one of the most important political developments in human history.

Christians advanced science, as we discussed in our previous lesson. Christians created some of the greatest art, music, architecture, and literature in human civilization — from the cathedrals of the medieval period to the paintings of Rembrandt, from the music of Bach and Handel to the poetry of John Milton and George Herbert, from the novels of Dostoevsky to the philosophical works of Augustine and Aquinas.

Christians have been at the forefront of caring for the poor and the vulnerable around the world. Christian organizations currently provide a significant portion of all humanitarian aid, disaster relief, education, and healthcare in the developing world. The positive contributions of Christianity to human civilization are so vast, so pervasive, and so fundamental that the critic who claims "religion has done more harm than good" is displaying a breathtaking ignorance of history.

The Puritan theologian John Flavel, born around 1627 in Bromsgrove, Worcestershire, England, wrote about the transformative power of the gospel in the lives of individuals and communities. In his work The Method of Grace, Flavel demonstrated that the gospel does not merely save souls — it transforms lives, families, communities, and ultimately civilizations. Wherever the gospel has gone, it has brought light, learning, compassion, justice, and human flourishing. The fruits of the gospel are visible in history, and they far outweigh the failures of those who have distorted or betrayed it.

It is also important to examine the specific historical episodes most commonly cited against Christianity and to understand them in their proper context.

The Crusades, which took place between 1095 and 1291, are frequently cited as evidence that Christianity is inherently violent. But a more careful historical examination reveals a more complex picture. The Crusades were, at their origin, a defensive response to centuries of Islamic military expansion that had conquered vast territories that had been Christian for centuries — including the Holy Land, North Africa, Spain, and much of the Middle East. The first Crusade was launched in response to a request for military assistance from the Byzantine Empire, which was being threatened by the Seljuk Turks. This does not justify the atrocities that were committed during the Crusades — the massacre of Jews in the Rhineland, the sack of Constantinople in 1204, and various other horrific events. These were genuine sins committed by people who claimed to follow Christ while violating His teachings. But it is historically inaccurate to portray the Crusades as unprovoked aggression by Christians against peaceful Muslims. The historical reality is far more complicated.

The Inquisition — particularly the Spanish Inquisition, established in 1478 — is another frequently cited example. Again, honesty requires us to acknowledge that the Inquisition involved genuine injustices, including torture, unjust imprisonments, and executions. However, modern historical scholarship has significantly revised the popular image of the Inquisition. Historians like Henry Kamen have shown that the Spanish Inquisition was primarily a political institution, driven as much by the Spanish crown's desire for national unity as by religious zeal. The total number of people executed by the Spanish Inquisition over its entire three-century history was approximately three to five thousand — a significant number, to be sure, but far less than the millions that popular imagination attributes to it, and a tiny fraction of the deaths caused by the secular revolutionary movements of the eighteenth, nineteenth, and twentieth centuries.

The so-called Wars of Religion in Europe during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries — the French Wars of Religion, the Thirty Years' War, and others — are also frequently cited. These wars were genuine tragedies that caused immense suffering. But historians have increasingly recognized that the causes of these wars were as much political, economic, and dynastic as they were religious. The Thirty Years' War, for example, saw Catholic France allying with Protestant Sweden against the Catholic Holy Roman Empire — hardly a straightforward religious conflict. To blame religion alone for these wars is a gross oversimplification of complex historical events driven by multiple, interacting causes.

The Puritan pastor John Bunyan, born in 1628 in Elstow, Bedfordshire, England, who was imprisoned for twelve years in Bedford jail for the crime of preaching without a license, understood firsthand the danger of using political power to enforce religious conformity. Bunyan's experience of persecution at the hands of a nominally Christian state made him acutely aware of the difference between genuine Christianity and the political misuse of Christianity. In his immortal allegory The Pilgrim's Progress, published in 1678, Bunyan depicted the Christian life as a personal journey of faith, sustained by grace and Scripture, that often brings the believer into conflict with the powers of this world rather than into alliance with them. Bunyan's vision of Christianity was not one of political domination but of humble, faithful pilgrimage — a vision that remains powerful and relevant today.

The Puritan theologian Roger Williams, born around 1603 in London, England, who founded the colony of Rhode Island on the principle of religious liberty, argued powerfully that the attempt to enforce religious belief through political power is not only unjust but contrary to the teaching of Christ. Williams held that civil government has no authority over the conscience and that true faith must be free and uncoerced. His arguments laid important groundwork for the principle of religious freedom that would later be enshrined in the First Amendment to the United States Constitution. Here was a Puritan pastor, driven by deep biblical convictions, arguing for a principle that would benefit people of all faiths and no faith — a powerful example of how genuine Christianity contributes to human freedom and flourishing.

When we consider the full historical record — not the selective, distorted version presented by the critics, but the whole picture — the evidence overwhelmingly supports the conclusion that Christianity has been the greatest force for good in human history. This does not mean that Christians have been perfect. It means that the gospel, when genuinely believed and faithfully practiced, produces extraordinary moral fruit — fruit that has blessed billions of people across thousands of years.

The proper response to all three of these moral objections is not defensiveness but a combination of honesty, perspective, and redirection.

Honesty: We must honestly acknowledge the failures of Christians, past and present, without excuse or evasion. The Puritan practice of self-examination and corporate confession provides a model for this. We do not pretend that the church has been perfect. We confess that it has not been. And we acknowledge that every failure is a genuine sin against God and a genuine injury to His name.

Perspective: We must place these failures in their proper historical context. The failures of Christians, while real, are not the whole story. The positive contributions of Christianity to human civilization are overwhelming and undeniable. And the atrocities committed by atheistic and secular ideologies in the twentieth century dwarf anything that can be attributed to religion.

The Puritan pastor Thomas Manton, born in 1620 in Lawrence-Lydiat, Somerset, England, who served as one of Oliver Cromwell's chaplains and later as minister at Stoke Newington, wrote about the Christian's duty to respond to criticism with meekness and self-examination. In his exposition of the Epistle of James, Manton argued that the Christian should be "swift to hear, slow to speak, slow to wrath" (James 1:19) — even when the criticism is unfair or hostile. Manton counseled that when non-Christians raise moral objections to the faith, the believer's first response should not be defensiveness but self-reflection: "Is there truth in this criticism? Have I contributed to this perception by my own failings? How can I better represent Christ in my life?" This posture of humble self-examination does not weaken our apologetic. It strengthens it — because it demonstrates the very qualities of honesty, humility, and integrity that our critics claim we lack.

The Puritan tradition also emphasized the importance of corporate repentance — the church acknowledging its collective failures and seeking God's forgiveness. The great Puritan days of fasting and humiliation were occasions on which entire congregations would come together to confess their sins, lament their failures, and plead for God's mercy and renewal. This practice stands in sharp contrast to the defensiveness that often characterizes modern Christian responses to criticism. The Puritans understood that acknowledging sin is not a sign of weakness but a sign of spiritual health — and that a church willing to confess its failures is far more credible than one that denies them.

The Puritan pastor Jonathan Edwards, born in 1703 in East Windsor, Connecticut, preached about the marks of genuine Christianity in his work The Religious Affections, published in 1746. Edwards argued that the most reliable evidence of genuine Christian faith is not the absence of sin but the presence of genuine love — love for God and love for neighbor — accompanied by humility, brokenness over sin, and a longing for holiness. Edwards' analysis provides a helpful framework for responding to the charge of hypocrisy: genuine Christians are not people who claim to be sinless but people who are genuinely grieved by their sin, genuinely grateful for grace, and genuinely committed to growing in Christlikeness — however slowly and imperfectly that growth may proceed.

Redirection: We must always redirect the conversation from Christians to Christ. Christianity does not ask people to evaluate the faith on the basis of the behavior of its most flawed adherents. It asks people to look at Jesus — His life, His teaching, His death, His resurrection. Jesus is the standard by which Christianity should be judged, and Jesus is flawless. "Which of you convinceth me of sin?" He asked His critics (John 8:46). No one could answer. No one ever has.

The Puritan pastor William Gurnall, born in 1616 in Lynn, Norfolk, England, wrote in his monumental work The Christian in Complete Armour about the Christian's responsibility to demonstrate the reality of the faith through godly living. Gurnall argued that every believer is a walking apologetic — either confirming or contradicting the gospel by the quality of his or her life. He urged Christians to pursue holiness not merely for their own sake but for the sake of Christ's reputation in the world. "Live so that your life may be a testimony that the gospel is true and that Christ is real."

--- Review Questions ---

Review Question 1: How should Christians understand the violence in the Old Testament? What context helps explain these passages, and how does the patience God showed toward the Canaanites change our understanding of His judgment?

Review Question 2: How should Christians respond to the charge of hypocrisy? Explain why hypocrisy does not disprove the truth of Christianity, and identify what Christianity itself says about hypocrisy.

Review Question 3: Is the claim that "religion has done more harm than good" historically accurate? Provide at least three specific examples of the positive contributions of Christianity to civilization and at least two examples of atrocities committed by atheistic regimes.

Review Question 4: How does the philosophical inconsistency of the moral objection — the need for an objective moral standard to make the objection in the first place — actually support the Christian worldview rather than undermining it?

Review Question 5: What did the Puritan writers teach about honesty, self-examination, and the proper response to moral failures within the church? How can their example guide us in responding to moral objections today?

--- Activity ---

Choose one of the three moral objections discussed in today's lesson. Research the topic further using at least two additional sources beyond this lesson. Then write a two-page response to an imaginary friend who has raised that objection. Your response should do the following: First, acknowledge the legitimate concerns behind the objection honestly and without defensiveness. Second, provide a thoughtful, evidence-based Christian answer that addresses those concerns. Third, redirect the conversation from the failures of Christians to the person and work of Christ. Fourth, include at least two Scripture references and one reference to a Puritan writer. Your response should demonstrate both intellectual rigor and pastoral sensitivity — the ability to speak truth with love. Share your response with a parent or mentor for feedback and discussion.

--- Closing ---

Moral objections to Christianity deserve honest, thoughtful, and humble responses. We must not be defensive, but we must not be dishonest either. The truth is that Christians have sometimes failed terribly — and we should acknowledge that with genuine sorrow. But the truth is also that Christ has never failed — and we should proclaim that with genuine confidence. The gospel is not the story of perfect Christians. It is the story of a perfect Christ who saves imperfect people by His grace and transforms them by His Spirit. And the evidence of that transformation — in changed lives, in sacrificial service, in the advancement of justice and compassion — is written across the pages of history for all who are willing to see it. Judge Christianity not by its worst adherents but by its best — and above all, by its Founder, the Lord Jesus Christ, who alone lived a perfectly sinless life, who alone loved with a perfect love, and who alone demonstrated what God intended humanity to be. As the apostle Peter wrote, Christ "did no sin, neither was guile found in his mouth: Who, when he was reviled, reviled not again; when he suffered, he threatened not; but committed himself to him that judgeth righteously" (1 Peter 2:22-23). He is the standard. He is the answer. He is the glory of the Christian faith. Until next time, students.
