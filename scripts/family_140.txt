PURITAN GOLD -- Family Devotional, Day 9
"The Mystery of Contentment"
Pilgrim Families — Week 2, Day 2


Hello, dear ones. How wonderful it is to sit together as a family and open the Scriptures. Welcome to Puritan Gold's family devotional. Let us begin with thankful hearts.

Let us pray together. Lord God, we come before You as a family, grateful for Your love and Your provision. We ask that You would quiet our minds and soften our hearts as we hear from You tonight. Help us to understand, to remember, and to obey what You teach us. In Christ's name, Amen.

Our Scripture reading this evening comes from 2 Corinthians 12:9-10; Habakkuk 3:17-18. Let us hear the Word of the Lord:

"And he said unto me, My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness. Most gladly therefore will I rather glory in my infirmities, that the power of Christ may rest upon me. Therefore I take pleasure in infirmities, in reproaches, in necessities, in persecutions, in distresses for Christ's sake: for when I am weak, then am I strong. Although the fig tree shall not blossom, neither shall fruit be in the vines; the labour of the olive shall fail, and the fields shall yield no meat; the flock shall be cut off from the fold, and there shall be no herd in the stalls: Yet I will rejoice in the LORD, I will joy in the God of my salvation."

That is 2 Corinthians 12:9-10; Habakkuk 3:17-18. Let those words settle in your heart for a moment before we continue.


Burroughs calls contentment a mystery, and rightly so. It defies human logic. How can a person be joyful when everything goes wrong? How can someone praise God when the cupboard is bare, the bank account is empty, and the future looks bleak? The world cannot understand it. But the Christian who knows this mystery possesses something more valuable than all the world's treasures.

Paul unveils the mystery in 2 Corinthians 12:9-10. He had prayed three times for God to remove a painful "thorn in the flesh." God's answer was not removal but sufficiency: "My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness." Paul's response is staggering: "Most gladly therefore will I rather glory in my infirmities." He does not merely endure weakness — he glories in it. Why? Because when Paul is weak, Christ's power rests upon him. When Paul has nothing left, Christ becomes everything.

Habakkuk paints perhaps the most vivid picture of this mystery in all of Scripture. He lists a catalog of disasters: no figs, no grapes, no olives, no crops, no flocks, no herds. In an agricultural society, this meant total economic ruin. Everything is gone. And yet — "Yet I will rejoice in the LORD, I will joy in the God of my salvation." That word "yet" is the hinge on which contentment swings. Everything may be stripped away, YET God remains. And God is enough.

Burroughs explains that the contented Christian has learned a great secret: contentment comes not from adding to your circumstances but from subtracting from your desires. The world says, "If only I had more money, a bigger house, a better job, I would be content." But the contented Christian says, "I will bring my desires down to match my circumstances, because my circumstances are what God has chosen for me."

This is the opposite of how our culture thinks. We are taught from childhood to want more, to reach for more, to never be satisfied. Advertisements are designed to create dissatisfaction — to make you feel that what you have is not enough. But the Christian who has learned the mystery of contentment looks at what God has provided and says, "This is enough. God is enough."

Paul lists the specific situations where he found contentment: infirmities, reproaches, necessities, persecutions, distresses. These are not minor inconveniences. These are severe trials. Yet Paul takes pleasure in them — not because he enjoys suffering, but because suffering drives him deeper into dependence on Christ, and dependence on Christ is the sweetest place a soul can be.

For families, this mystery is profoundly countercultural. Our children are growing up in a world that constantly tells them they need more to be happy. More screens, more games, more clothes, more experiences. But the happiest families are not the wealthiest families. The happiest families are the ones who have learned to say, "God has given us enough, and we are grateful."

Habakkuk's prayer is a family prayer worth memorizing. When the car breaks down: yet I will rejoice in the Lord. When the job is lost: yet I will rejoice. When the diagnosis is bad: yet I will rejoice. When friends disappoint: yet I will rejoice. The circumstances change; God does not. And if God does not change, then our joy does not have to change either.

Burroughs observed that the contented Christian carries a well of joy inside that does not depend on external rain. The world's joy is like a river that rises and falls with the weather. Christian joy is like an underground spring that flows steadily regardless of the conditions on the surface. This spring is Christ Himself, dwelling in the believer's heart by the Holy Spirit.

The mystery of contentment is ultimately the mystery of Christ's sufficiency. When Christ is your treasure, no one can rob you. When Christ is your joy, no circumstance can steal your happiness. When Christ is your strength, weakness becomes an advantage. This is the mystery the world cannot understand — and it is the rare jewel that every family can possess.


The Puritans were men and women who treasured God's Word above all else. Let us hear from one of them now.

Jeremiah Burroughs, in his work "The Rare Jewel of Christian Contentment," wrote these words:

"A Christian finds contentment not by adding to his circumstances but by subtracting from his desires."

What Jeremiah Burroughs is reminding us is that the truths we have read tonight in 2 Corinthians 12:9-10; Habakkuk 3:17-18 are not new. They have been treasured by faithful Christians for centuries. And they are just as true and just as needed in our family today as they were hundreds of years ago. Let us take these words to heart and let them shape the way we live together as a family.


Let us pause and talk as a family. Here are tonight's discussion questions. Everyone gets a turn to share.

Question 1: What does Habakkuk's 'yet' teach us about faith? How can you say 'yet I will rejoice' when things go wrong?

Question 2: Paul said God's strength is 'made perfect in weakness.' How have you seen God's strength show up most when you felt weakest?

Question 3: Burroughs says contentment comes from subtracting desires, not adding possessions. What desires might our family need to subtract?

Question 4: Why is the world's idea of happiness so different from biblical contentment?

Take a few minutes to talk about these questions together. Let everyone share, and listen to one another with patience and love.


Now, here is our memory verse for today. Let us say it together:

"And he said unto me, My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness." -- 2 Corinthians 12:9

Try to say it out loud three times right now. Then, before bed tonight, see if you can say it from memory. Tomorrow morning, try again. The more we hide God's Word in our hearts, the more it will guide us when we need it most.


To make tonight's lesson come alive, try this activity together as a family:

Practice the 'Yet I Will Rejoice' exercise. Each family member names something difficult they are facing, then says aloud: 'Yet I will rejoice in the Lord!' Repeat this several times as a family and talk about how speaking God's truth changes our perspective.


Let us pray together. Lord God, we thank You for this time as a family. Thank You for speaking to us through Your Word. Plant these truths deep in our hearts, especially in the hearts of our children. Help us to walk in wisdom tomorrow and every day. In Jesus' name, Amen.

Sleep well tonight, knowing that God watches over your family.
