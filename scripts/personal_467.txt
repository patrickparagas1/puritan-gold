PURITAN GOLD -- My Growth, Day 6
"The Doctrine of Sanctification"
Theological Day — Progressive Holiness in the Believer's Life

---

Welcome. Today is a Theological day — a day for doctrinal depth. We examine the doctrine of sanctification: God's ongoing work of making believers holy, transforming them from glory to glory into the image of Christ.

Let us read 2 Corinthians 3:18 (KJV):

"But we all, with open face beholding as in a glass the glory of the Lord, are changed into the same image from glory to glory, even as by the Spirit of the Lord."

--- Teaching ---

Having considered justification on our last theological day, we now examine the doctrine that follows it: sanctification. Justification declares us righteous. Sanctification makes us righteous. Both are the work of God. Both are received through faith. But they operate in distinct ways.

The Westminster Shorter Catechism defines sanctification as the work of God's free grace, whereby we are renewed in the whole man after the image of God, and are enabled more and more to die unto sin and live unto righteousness. Three things stand out in this definition.

First, sanctification is a work of God's free grace. It is not a human project. Just as justification is God's work from first to last, so sanctification originates in divine initiative. John Owen wrote that sanctification is as much a work of sovereign grace as regeneration itself. The Puritans were careful to root sanctification in grace, not in legalism. You are not sanctified by trying harder. You are sanctified by the Spirit of God working within you.

Second, sanctification is progressive. The phrase "more and more" is crucial. Unlike justification, which is instantaneous and complete, sanctification is gradual and ongoing. You are not perfectly holy the moment you believe. You are being made holy over the course of a lifetime. The Puritans used the image of the sunrise. Proverbs 4:18 says, "But the path of the just is as the shining light, that shineth more and more unto the perfect day." The dawn does not become noon in an instant. It brightens gradually, steadily, until the full light of day arrives.

Third, sanctification involves the whole person — the whole man. It touches the mind, the affections, the will, the body, the relationships, the habits, the desires. Thomas Watson wrote that sanctification is a universal change — the renovation of the entire soul. A faith that changes your beliefs but not your behavior, or your behavior but not your affections, is an incomplete sanctification.

The Puritans identified two parts of sanctification. The first is mortification — the killing of sin. The second is vivification — the enlivening of holiness. Both are essential. A gardener who only pulls weeds but never plants flowers has only half a garden. And a gardener who only plants flowers but never pulls weeds will soon find the flowers choked. Mortification without vivification produces grim, negative religion. Vivification without mortification produces shallow, undisciplined religion.

Owen devoted his most important practical work to mortification. He argued that every believer has particular sins — besetting sins — that are especially persistent. These must be identified, confessed, and systematically opposed. But the opposition must be spiritual, not merely moral. You do not kill sin by sheer willpower. You kill it by setting the affections of your heart on Christ. The more you behold the glory of Christ, the less attractive sin becomes.

Vivification — the positive dimension — involves the cultivation of the fruit of the Spirit: love, joy, peace, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, faith, meekness, temperance. These are not virtues you manufacture through effort. They are fruits that grow organically as the Spirit works in you. Your part is to create the conditions for growth: abiding in Christ, feeding on His Word, fellowshipping with His people, and walking in obedience.

The Puritans also taught the seasons of sanctification. Not every season feels like growth. There are winters of dryness and apparent stagnation. There are springs of rapid growth and renewed zeal. There are summers of fruitful service. And there are autumns of harvest and reflection. All are necessary. All are orchestrated by God. The Christian who understands the seasons will not despair in winter or become complacent in summer.

The goal of sanctification, as Paul states in our passage, is transformation into the image of Christ — from glory to glory. This is an ever-increasing conformity to the character of Jesus. It will not be complete in this life. But it will be complete in the next, when glorification finishes what sanctification began.

--- Closing ---

You are being changed. From glory to glory. It is slow, it is painful at times, but it is real. And the One who began this work will complete it. Lord, continue Your work in me. Kill what needs to die. Grow what needs to live. Change me from glory to glory until I stand before You, complete in Christ. Amen.
