PURITAN GOLD -- Study Devotional, Day 28
Series: Romans
"Whatsoever Is Not of Faith Is Sin"
The kingdom of God, the priority of love, and the sacredness of conscience


Good evening. Whether this is your first time joining our study series or you have been with us from the beginning, you are welcome. God's Word is inexhaustible, and there is always more to discover.

We are studying the book of Romans together. Tonight's lesson is titled "Whatsoever Is Not of Faith Is Sin." Each session builds on what came before, so if you have been following along, you will see how tonight's passage connects to the larger argument of the book.


Let us read our passage for tonight. It comes from Romans 14:13-23:

"Let us not therefore judge one another any more: but judge this rather, that no man put a stumblingblock or an occasion to fall in his brother's way. I know, and am persuaded by the Lord Jesus, that there is nothing unclean of itself: but to him that esteemeth any thing to be unclean, to him it is unclean. But if thy brother be grieved with thy meat, now walkest thou not charitably. Destroy not him with thy meat, for whom Christ died. Let not then your good be evil spoken of: For the kingdom of God is not meat and drink; but righteousness, and peace, and joy in the Holy Ghost. For he that in these things serveth Christ is acceptable to God, and approved of men. Let us therefore follow after the things which make for peace, and things wherewith one may edify another... Hast thou faith? have it to thyself before God. Happy is he that condemneth not himself in that thing which he alloweth. And he that doubteth is damned if he eat, because he eateth not of faith: for whatsoever is not of faith is sin."

Read that passage again to yourself. Mark any words or phrases that stand out. The Puritans read with a pen in hand, always ready to note what God was showing them.


The Puritans called the key verse of a passage its 'doctrine text.' Tonight, ours is this:

"For the kingdom of God is not meat and drink; but righteousness, and peace, and joy in the Holy Ghost." -- Romans 14:17

This verse is the lens through which we will examine the rest of tonight's passage. Keep it in the front of your mind as we proceed.


The text is before us. Let us dig into its riches.

John Owen offers valuable commentary on this passage. The Puritans were expositors of the highest order, and their insights remain remarkably fresh.

John Owen treated the conscience with great care in his theological writings, and Romans 14:13-23 was central to his understanding. Owen argued that the conscience is God's deputy in the soul — it is the faculty by which a person judges their own actions in light of what they believe to be true. To violate one's conscience, even in a matter that is objectively permissible, is sin — because it constitutes a willingness to do what one believes to be wrong. The person who eats while doubting commits sin not because eating is wrong but because they are acting against their own moral judgment.

Owen stressed the radical nature of verse 15: 'Destroy not him with thy meat, for whom Christ died.' The strong believer's liberty is a real and precious thing — but it is not the most precious thing. The soul of a weaker brother, for whom Christ shed His blood, is infinitely more valuable than any exercise of freedom. Owen argued that this verse should silence every Christian who insists on their rights at the expense of their brother's spiritual wellbeing. If Christ was willing to die for that brother, surely you can forgo a plate of meat.

Owen also highlighted verse 17 as containing one of the most important definitions in the New Testament: 'For the kingdom of God is not meat and drink; but righteousness, and peace, and joy in the Holy Ghost.' The kingdom is not about external regulations but about internal realities produced by the Spirit. Owen warned that churches which major on minors — making secondary issues the tests of fellowship — have fundamentally misunderstood the nature of God's kingdom. Where righteousness, peace, and joy in the Holy Spirit reign, the kingdom is present. Where quarreling over food and drink reigns, the kingdom is obscured.

Romans 14:13-23 continues Paul's instructions on disputable matters, shifting the focus from the danger of judging others to the responsibility of not causing others to stumble. This passage provides some of the most searching instruction on Christian liberty and love in the entire New Testament.

Verse 13 redirects our judgment: 'Let us not therefore judge one another any more: but judge this rather, that no man put a stumblingblock or an occasion to fall in his brother's way.' Stop judging your brother's convictions and start judging your own behavior. The question is no longer 'is my brother right or wrong?' but 'is my exercise of liberty causing my brother to stumble?'

Verse 14 affirms the strong believer's position: 'I know, and am persuaded by the Lord Jesus, that there is nothing unclean of itself.' Paul agrees with the strong. Under the new covenant, the food laws of the old covenant have been fulfilled in Christ (Mark 7:19; Acts 10:15). No food is inherently unclean. But Paul immediately adds a crucial qualification: 'but to him that esteemeth any thing to be unclean, to him it is unclean.' Objective truth does not override subjective conscience. If a brother believes something is wrong, then for him to do it is wrong — because he would be acting against his own moral conviction.

Verse 15 introduces the law of love: 'But if thy brother be grieved with thy meat, now walkest thou not charitably.' The word 'charitably' is literally 'according to love' (kata agapen). If your exercise of liberty causes pain to a brother whose conscience forbids what you are doing, you are no longer walking in love. Then comes the devastating question: 'Destroy not him with thy meat, for whom Christ died.' Put the price of a steak next to the price of the cross. Christ died for that brother. Will you risk his spiritual wellbeing for a plate of food?

Verse 16 warns the strong: 'Let not then your good be evil spoken of.' Your liberty is genuinely good — but if you exercise it in a way that harms others, it will be regarded as evil. Good things used unwisely become bad things.

Verse 17 provides the definitive perspective: 'For the kingdom of God is not meat and drink; but righteousness, and peace, and joy in the Holy Ghost.' This is one of the most liberating statements in the Bible. The kingdom of God — the reign of God in human lives — has nothing to do with food and drink, holy days and dress codes, minor regulations and cultural preferences. It is about righteousness — right standing with God and right living before Him. Peace — harmony with God, with self, and with others. Joy — the deep, Spirit-produced delight that transcends circumstances. These are what matter. Everything else is secondary.

Verse 18 confirms: 'For he that in these things serveth Christ is acceptable to God, and approved of men.' When we pursue righteousness, peace, and joy in the Holy Spirit, we please both God and man. When we quarrel over secondary issues, we displease both.

Verses 19-20 draw the practical conclusion: 'Let us therefore follow after the things which make for peace, and things wherewith one may edify another. For meat destroy not the work of God.' The church is God's work — His building, His body, His family. Every member is precious. To destroy the faith of a brother over a disputable matter is to demolish what God has been building. 'All things indeed are pure; but it is evil for that man who eateth with offence.' All foods are pure in themselves, but eating becomes sinful when it causes a brother to stumble.

Verse 21 gives a clear guideline: 'It is good neither to eat flesh, nor to drink wine, nor any thing whereby thy brother stumbleth, or is offended, or is made weak.' Love voluntarily limits liberty. This is not legalism; it is love. The strong believer freely chooses to restrict their freedom for the sake of a weaker brother. Paul modeled this himself: 'If meat make my brother to offend, I will eat no flesh while the world standeth' (1 Corinthians 8:13).

Verses 22-23 address the private exercise of faith and the sacred nature of conscience. 'Hast thou faith? have it to thyself before God. Happy is he that condemneth not himself in that thing which he alloweth.' If you have the faith to eat anything, exercise that liberty privately before God — do not flaunt it before a brother who would be distressed. And be sure your own conscience is clear: 'Happy is he that condemneth not himself' — the blessed person is the one who does not feel guilty about the choices they make.

The chapter closes with a principle of enormous scope: 'And he that doubteth is damned if he eat, because he eateth not of faith: for whatsoever is not of faith is sin.' The word 'damned' here means condemned by his own conscience, not consigned to hell. The principle is clear: any action taken in violation of one's own conscience is sin, regardless of whether the action is objectively permissible. Acting against conscience is acting against what you believe God requires — and that is always sinful. Conversely, 'whatsoever is not of faith is sin' establishes that faith must be the animating principle of every action. Whatever we do, we must do it in faith — in conscious dependence on God and in conformity with our understanding of His will.


The true test of understanding is application. If we have truly grasped tonight's passage, it will change the way we live tomorrow.

Romans 14:13-23 transforms how we exercise liberty and relate to fellow believers.

First, use your liberty lovingly. Before exercising your freedom in Christ, ask: 'Will this cause a weaker brother or sister to stumble?' Your liberty is real, but love is greater than liberty. If your freedom would harm another believer's conscience, voluntarily restrict it. This is not legalism; it is the highest expression of Christian love.

Second, pursue what matters. The kingdom of God is righteousness, peace, and joy in the Holy Ghost — not food, drink, music styles, political parties, or secondary cultural issues. Are you spending more energy on secondary matters than on these primary realities? Reorder your priorities. Major on the majors. Let the peace and joy of the Holy Spirit define your Christian experience, not your position on disputable matters.

Third, never violate your conscience. If you believe something is wrong — even if other Christians disagree — do not do it. Acting against your conscience trains you to ignore the moral voice God has placed within you. This is spiritually dangerous. At the same time, work to educate your conscience by studying Scripture, so that your convictions align more and more closely with God's actual will.

Fourth, pursue peace and edification. 'Let us follow after the things which make for peace, and things wherewith one may edify another.' In every interaction with fellow believers, ask two questions: Is this building peace? Is this building up my brother? If the answer to either is no, change course. The church is too precious, and Christ paid too high a price for it, to be torn apart over minor disputes.

Write down one concrete action you will take this week in response to tonight's study. Do not let this truth remain theoretical. The Puritans measured the success of a sermon not by how much the hearer learned but by how much the hearer changed.


Lord of the kingdom, teach us to treasure righteousness, peace, and joy in the Holy Spirit above all secondary matters. Forgive us for quarreling over food and drink while neglecting the weightier matters of Your reign. Give us the love that voluntarily limits its liberty for the sake of weaker brothers and sisters. Guard our consciences and help us never to act in violation of what we believe to be right. Build peace in our churches. Build up our brothers and sisters. And may we never destroy with our freedom what Christ purchased with His blood. Amen.

May the Lord bless your continued study of His Word. Until next time.
