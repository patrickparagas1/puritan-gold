PURITAN GOLD -- My Growth, Day 31
"The Heart of Christ — Thomas Goodwin"
Reading Day — Goodwin on Christ's Present Affections

---

Welcome. Today is a Reading day, and we close this month with one of the most tender and encouraging works in the Puritan library: Thomas Goodwin's The Heart of Christ in Heaven Towards Sinners on Earth. This small book has been called the most comforting treatise ever written about the present disposition of the risen Christ toward His struggling people.

Let us read Hebrews 4:14-16 (KJV):

"Seeing then that we have a great high priest, that is passed into the heavens, Jesus the Son of God, let us hold fast our profession. For we have not an high priest which cannot be touched with the feeling of our infirmities; but was in all points tempted like as we are, yet without sin. Let us therefore come boldly unto the throne of grace, that we may obtain mercy, and find grace to help in time of need."

--- Teaching ---

Thomas Goodwin (1600-1680) was one of the most intellectually formidable of the Puritans — a theologian of immense depth and learning. But his treatise on the heart of Christ is not academic. It is pastoral, devotional, and extraordinarily warm. Its central question is simple: What does Christ feel toward His people right now, at this very moment, as He sits at the right hand of God?

Goodwin's answer, drawn from a careful study of Hebrews 4, is revolutionary in its tenderness: Christ's heart toward sinners on earth is not cold, distant, or indifferent. It is warm, compassionate, moved with sympathy, and overflowing with love. The glorified Christ has not forgotten what it is like to be human. He has not left behind His capacity for empathy. He is touched — genuinely, personally, affectively touched — with the feeling of our infirmities.

Goodwin makes several arguments that build upon one another.

First, Christ's human nature is real and permanent. When the Son of God became man, He did not take on humanity temporarily. He united a human nature to His divine nature forever. The Christ who sits at the right hand of the Father is fully God and fully man — now and for all eternity. This means that He retains a human heart, human sympathies, and a human understanding of what it is like to struggle, to suffer, to be tempted, and to be weak.

Second, Christ's glorification did not diminish His compassion but perfected it. Some might think that Christ, now glorified and exalted above all suffering, would look down on earthly struggles with detachment. Goodwin argues the opposite. Glorification has freed Christ from the limitations of earthly experience — the fatigue, the pain, the sorrow — but it has not freed Him from compassion. His compassion is now perfect, unlimited, and unhindered by the constraints of mortal existence. He feels for you more fully now than He did when He walked beside the disciples on the roads of Galilee.

Third, Christ's present ministry is one of intercession and sympathy, not judgment. Many Christians unconsciously picture Christ in heaven as a stern judge, keeping a record of their failures and waiting to condemn. Goodwin dismantles this image completely. He points to Hebrews 7:25: Christ "ever liveth to make intercession" for those who come to God through Him. His present work is not prosecution but advocacy. He is not against you. He is for you. He is praying for you right now, at this very moment, with perfect knowledge of your situation, perfect compassion for your weakness, and perfect power to help.

Fourth, Christ's heart is moved particularly by the weakness of His people. Goodwin notes that the text says Christ is touched with the feeling of our infirmities — not our strengths. It is not when you are at your strongest that Christ's heart is most engaged. It is when you are at your weakest. When you stumble, He does not turn away in disgust. He turns toward you in compassion. When you doubt, He does not abandon you. He draws nearer. When you sin, He does not revoke His love. He intercedes.

Goodwin draws a beautiful analogy. He writes that a mother's heart is most tender toward the child who is sick, not the child who is well. The well child can run and play without much thought from the mother. But the sick child — the one who is feverish, crying, unable to help herself — that is the child who occupies the mother's heart most fully. And so it is with Christ. Your weakness does not repel Him. It draws Him closer.

This is the truth that closes our month of growth. Whatever your spiritual condition — strong or weak, fervent or cold, confident or doubting — the heart of Christ is toward you. He is not ashamed to call you brother. He is not reluctant to help you. He is not indifferent to your struggles. His heart burns with compassion, intercession, and love.

Come boldly to the throne of grace. Not because you deserve it. Not because you have earned it. But because the One who sits on that throne has a heart that is touched with the feeling of your infirmities. He understands. He cares. And He is able to help.

--- Closing ---

This month has taken us through Bunyan's pilgrimage, Sibbes's tenderness, Watson's providence, Owen's mortification, Flavel's heart-keeping, Edwards's affections, Baxter's heaven, Charnock's attributes, and Goodwin's Christ. All of these great teachers point to one reality: a God who is for you, not against you. A Christ whose heart burns with love for sinners on earth. Let that truth carry you into the month ahead. Lord Jesus, I come boldly — not because I am worthy but because You are willing. Your heart is toward me. In my weakness, You draw near. In my doubt, You intercede. In my failure, You love. I rest in Your heart tonight and always. Amen.
