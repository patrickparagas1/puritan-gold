PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Doctrine Deep-Dive, Day 24
"The Perseverance of the Saints"
The Security of the Believer in God's Hands

---

Good evening. The Puritans were theologians of the highest order, but they were also the most practical of people. They knew that theology untethered from life is useless, and that life untethered from theology is dangerous. Tonight, we hold the two together.

Tonight we draw from the work of Multiple (Owen, Goodwin, Perkins), specifically from "Various works on perseverance." This text has been a source of spiritual nourishment for Christians across the centuries, and it remains profoundly relevant today.

--- Scripture ---

Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is John 10:28-29:

"And I give unto them eternal life; and they shall never perish, neither shall any man pluck them out of my hand. My Father, which gave them me, is greater than all; and no man is able to pluck them out of my Father's hand."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.

--- Doctrine Study ---

The doctrine of the perseverance of the saints — sometimes called 'the preservation of the saints' — teaches that all who are truly regenerated by the Spirit of God will persevere in faith to the end and will be finally saved. No true believer will ultimately and finally fall away from grace. This doctrine is grounded not in the strength of the believer's faith but in the faithfulness of the God who saves.

John Owen provides the most thorough Puritan defense of this doctrine in his work 'The Doctrine of the Saints' Perseverance Explained and Confirmed.' Owen grounds the doctrine in the eternal purposes of God. He argues that if God has elected a person to salvation from before the foundation of the world (Ephesians 1:4), if Christ has died specifically for that person and purchased their redemption (John 10:15), and if the Holy Spirit has regenerated that person and sealed them for the day of redemption (Ephesians 1:13-14), then it would be a failure of the triune God if that person were ultimately lost. Owen writes that 'the perseverance of saints is nothing but the faithfulness of God in fulfilling His promises.'

Owen carefully addresses the biblical texts that seem to warn against falling away. He argues that these warnings serve a real purpose: they are the means by which God preserves His people. The warning against falling away is God's instrument for preventing His people from falling away. It is like a sign on a cliff edge that says 'Danger: do not approach.' The sign does not prove that people will fall over the cliff; it is one of the means by which they are kept from doing so.

Thomas Goodwin, in 'The Object and Acts of Justifying Faith,' approaches perseverance from the perspective of Christ's intercessory work. Goodwin points to Jesus' prayer in John 17: 'Holy Father, keep through thine own name those whom thou hast given me.' Christ prays for the preservation of His people, and His prayers are always answered. Goodwin also points to Hebrews 7:25: Christ 'ever liveth to make intercession' for those who come to God through Him. The believer's security rests not on the believer's grip on Christ but on Christ's grip on the believer.

William Perkins, an earlier Puritan, addresses the practical implications. Perkins writes that the doctrine of perseverance should produce three effects in the believer's life. First, gratitude: the knowledge that your salvation is secure should fill you with overflowing thankfulness to the God who keeps you. Second, humility: the fact that your perseverance depends on God's faithfulness rather than your own should humble you deeply. You did not save yourself, and you cannot keep yourself. Third, diligence: the doctrine of perseverance does not produce laziness but energy. The man who knows that God will finish what He started is emboldened to fight sin, pursue holiness, and serve the church with confidence.

Perkins warns against two misapplications of this doctrine. The first is careless presumption: the attitude that says, 'If I am elect, it does not matter how I live.' This is a grotesque distortion. The elect are characterized by their pursuit of holiness, not by their indifference to it. The man who uses the doctrine of perseverance as an excuse for sin has reason to question whether he is truly among the persevering.

The second misapplication is morbid introspection: the constant, anxious questioning of whether one is truly elect. Perkins counsels the troubled soul to look not inward but upward. Look to Christ. Believe His promises. Trust His intercession. Use the means of grace. And let the fruit of your life, however imperfect, serve as evidence of the Spirit's work within you.

The Westminster Confession summarizes the doctrine beautifully: believers 'can neither totally nor finally fall away from the state of grace: but shall certainly persevere therein to the end, and be eternally saved.' This is not because they are strong, but because God is faithful. This is not because their faith never wavers, but because the One who holds them never lets go.

Rest in this truth today. Your salvation is as secure as the character of God. Nothing in all creation can separate you from His love. You are held in the double grip of the Son and the Father, and no one — not even yourself — can pluck you from their hands.

--- Reflection ---

Here are some questions to carry with you into the coming days.

Question 1: Does the doctrine of perseverance fill me with gratitude and humility, or have I misapplied it toward either presumption or anxiety?

Question 2: How does knowing that Christ is interceding for me right now change the way I face my struggles and temptations?

Question 3: Am I resting in God's faithfulness to preserve me, or am I trying to secure my own salvation through effort?

Do not rush past these questions. Sit with them. Journal about them if you can. The Puritans believed that truth must be digested, not merely tasted.

--- Closing Prayer ---

Father, I thank You that my salvation rests in Your hands, not mine. I thank You that Christ intercedes for me and that nothing can pluck me from Your grip. Let this doctrine produce in me not presumption but gratitude, not laziness but diligence, not anxiety but peace. Amen.

Rest in His grace tonight. Tomorrow is a new day of growth.
