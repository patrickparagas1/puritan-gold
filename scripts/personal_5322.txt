PURITAN GOLD -- Personal Growth: Pastoral Encouragement, Day 22
"Evangelism and Personal Witness"
Sharing the Gospel Boldly — The Believer's Great Privilege

---

Good evening, friend. Tonight we turn to a subject that stirs both excitement and anxiety in the hearts of many believers: the call to share the gospel with those who do not yet know Christ. The Puritans were not only theologians and pastors — they were evangelists. They believed that every Christian bears a responsibility to make the good news known. Let us consider together how we might do this with both boldness and tenderness.

--- Scripture ---

Let us ground ourselves in God's Word before we go further. Our passage tonight is Romans 1:16:

"For I am not ashamed of the gospel of Christ: for it is the power of God unto salvation to every one that believeth; to the Jew first, and also to the Greek."

Hold these words in your heart as we continue. They are the foundation upon which everything else tonight is built.

--- Pastoral Word ---

The first thing to say about evangelism is that it flows from gratitude. A man who has been rescued from drowning does not need to be commanded to tell others about his rescuer. A woman who has been healed of a deadly disease does not need to be persuaded to speak of her physician. The gospel is good news — the best news the world has ever heard — and those who have experienced its power should find it the most natural thing in the world to share it. Richard Baxter wrote that a minister who truly believes that souls are perishing will preach with urgency, and the same principle applies to every believer. If we truly believe what we say we believe, our lives should overflow with witness.

And yet, many of us struggle. We fear rejection. We fear saying the wrong thing. We fear being perceived as arrogant or narrow-minded. These fears are real, and they deserve honest attention. The Puritans understood them well. Thomas Watson observed that the devil works hard to seal the lips of believers, for he knows that a silent Christian is an ineffective Christian. Satan does not mind if you believe the gospel in private, so long as you never share it with anyone else.

The remedy for this fear is not greater confidence in ourselves but greater confidence in the gospel. Paul says it plainly: the gospel is the power of God unto salvation. It is not our eloquence that saves people. It is not our ability to answer every objection. It is not our winsome personality or our theological sophistication. It is the gospel itself — the message that Christ died for sinners, was buried, and rose again — that carries the power. Our job is not to be brilliant. Our job is to be faithful.

John Flavel counseled believers to think of evangelism not as a burden but as a privilege. We have been entrusted with the words of eternal life. We know the cure for the disease that is killing every person we meet. To withhold that cure out of social discomfort is not humility; it is a failure of love. Flavel wrote that the greatest kindness you can show your neighbor is to tell him the truth about his condition and point him to the only remedy.

How then shall we witness? The Puritans offer us a model that combines doctrinal clarity with personal warmth. They did not reduce the gospel to a formula or a sales pitch. They presented it in its fullness: the holiness of God, the sinfulness of man, the sufficiency of Christ, and the necessity of faith and repentance. They did not shy away from hard truths — the reality of judgment, the danger of delay, the narrowness of the way — but they spoke these truths with tears in their eyes and love in their voices.

Richard Sibbes reminded believers that the manner of our witness matters as much as its content. A harsh gospel presentation can do more harm than good. We are ambassadors, not prosecutors. We come not to condemn but to invite. We come not with threats but with tidings of great joy. The bruised reed shall not be broken, and the smoking flax shall not be quenched. Our evangelism should reflect the tenderness of the Savior we proclaim.

Finally, let us remember that the results of evangelism are in God's hands, not ours. The Puritans held firmly to the sovereignty of God in salvation. This did not make them passive; it made them bold. Because they knew that God was the one who opened hearts, they were free to speak without the paralyzing pressure of thinking everything depended on them. William Perkins taught his students that the minister's duty is to preach faithfully; the converting of souls belongs to God alone.

--- Application ---

First, identify one person in your life who does not know Christ, and begin praying for that person daily. Ask God to open a door for conversation and to give you the words to speak when that door opens.

Second, prepare a simple, honest account of what Christ has done in your life. You do not need a dramatic testimony. You need a true one. Be ready to share it when the opportunity comes.

Third, remember that your life is itself a witness. The Puritans spoke of "living epistles" — lives that preach the gospel without a word being spoken. Let your kindness, your integrity, your patience in suffering, and your joy in hardship commend the faith you profess.

--- Closing Prayer ---

O Lord, forgive us for our silence when we should have spoken. Give us boldness to proclaim the gospel and tenderness to proclaim it well. Remind us that the power belongs to Thee, not to us, and that our calling is faithfulness, not success. Use us as instruments of Thy grace in a world that desperately needs to hear the good news. Through Jesus Christ, who is Himself the good news, Amen.

Go in peace tonight, friend. Tomorrow may be the day God uses your words to change a life forever.
