PURITAN GOLD -- My Growth, Day 22
"Grace Abounding — John Bunyan's Spiritual Autobiography"
Reading Day — Bunyan's Personal Testimony of Grace

---

Welcome. Today is a Reading day. We return to John Bunyan, this time not for his allegory but for his autobiography — Grace Abounding to the Chief of Sinners, one of the most honest accounts of spiritual struggle and divine deliverance ever written.

Let us read 1 Timothy 1:15 (KJV):

"This is a faithful saying, and worthy of all acceptation, that Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners; of whom I am chief."

--- Teaching ---

Grace Abounding is Bunyan's personal testimony, written from prison, of his journey from darkness to light. Unlike the allegorical Pilgrim's Progress, this is raw autobiography — Bunyan's own experience of temptation, doubt, despair, and ultimate assurance. It is a book for anyone who has struggled with the question: Is my faith real? Am I truly saved? Has God truly accepted me?

Bunyan grew up in poverty and describes his youth as morally careless. But the turning point came when he overheard three or four poor women sitting in the sun, talking about the new birth, about the work of God in their hearts, and about the insufficiency of their own righteousness. Bunyan writes that their conversation was as if they had found a new world. He was captivated and began to seek God in earnest.

But the seeking was agonizing. Bunyan's conversion was not sudden or clean. It was a years-long battle with doubt, despair, and what he feared was the unpardonable sin. He was tormented by the thought that he had committed the sin against the Holy Ghost — that he had blasphemed beyond the reach of grace. He describes moments when Scripture seemed to condemn him utterly, and other moments when a single verse would break through the darkness like a shaft of light.

The most famous episode in Grace Abounding is Bunyan's account of the battle between two texts. On one side: "My grace is sufficient for thee." On the other: "Esau, who for one morsel of meat sold his birthright." For weeks and months, these two texts fought each other in Bunyan's soul. Was he Esau — a man who had sold his birthright and could never recover it? Or was he a sinner for whom grace was sufficient? The resolution came when Bunyan realized that the promise of sufficient grace was spoken by God, while the fear of being Esau was spoken by the devil using Scripture out of context.

What makes Grace Abounding so valuable is its honesty about spiritual struggle. Bunyan does not present the Christian life as a smooth, upward progression. He presents it as a battle — a prolonged, exhausting, sometimes terrifying battle in which the outcome is uncertain from the human perspective, even though it is certain from the divine. This honesty is liberating for anyone who has experienced similar struggles. If Bunyan — the author of Pilgrim's Progress, one of the greatest Christians in history — endured years of doubt and despair, then your own struggles do not disqualify you. They place you in very good company.

Bunyan's resolution came gradually, through the accumulation of Scripture promises that overwhelmed his fears. He came to understand that his salvation did not depend on the quality of his faith but on the object of his faith — Christ. He writes of the moment when the truth of justification by faith alone broke through the clouds: that Christ's righteousness, not his own, was the basis of his acceptance before God. This understanding did not eliminate all doubt forever, but it provided a foundation to which he could return whenever the storms of uncertainty arose.

The lesson of Grace Abounding is not that the Christian life is easy. It is not. The lesson is that grace is sufficient — genuinely, personally, specifically sufficient — for the chief of sinners. Whatever your past, whatever your struggle, whatever your doubt, the grace of God in Christ is bigger. It is bigger than your sin. It is bigger than your fear. It is bigger than the devil's accusations. And it is freely offered to all who come.

--- Closing ---

If Bunyan could doubt so deeply and yet emerge with such strong faith, then there is hope for every struggling soul. Lord, I bring my doubts and fears to You — not as evidence of disqualification but as occasion for grace. Your grace is sufficient. Your grace is sufficient. Your grace is sufficient. Amen.
