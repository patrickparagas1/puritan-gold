PURITAN GOLD — Episode 11
"The Holiness of God"
Week 2, Day 4 (Thursday) — A.W. Pink's The Attributes of God

---

There is a moment in the book of Isaiah — chapter six — that I believe every Christian should return to again and again. The prophet sees the Lord, high and lifted up, seated on His throne. The seraphim — those mighty, burning angelic beings — are hovering around the throne. And they are calling out to one another. But they're not calling out "Love, love, love." They're not calling out "Power, power, power." They're calling out... "Holy, holy, holy, is the Lord of hosts: the whole earth is full of His glory."

Holy. Three times. In all of Scripture, no other attribute of God is repeated three times. Not His love. Not His mercy. Not His power. Only His holiness. As if the angels themselves cannot say it enough. As if one utterance — or even two — cannot do justice to what they see.

And what is Isaiah's response? Does he stand tall? Does he nod with theological appreciation? No. He falls apart. "Woe is me! For I am undone; because I am a man of unclean lips." The vision of God's holiness didn't make Isaiah feel inspired. It made him feel annihilated.

This is Puritan Gold, and today we return to A.W. Pink's The Attributes of God to explore what Pink considered the most essential — and most neglected — attribute of the divine nature: His holiness.

And we'll weave in that great verse from Proverbs, chapter 9, verse 10:

"The fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom: and the knowledge of the holy is understanding."

There it is. The knowledge of the holy is understanding. Not the knowledge of the powerful. Not the knowledge of the successful. The knowledge of the holy. If you want to understand anything rightly — yourself, the world, your purpose, your sin, your salvation — you must begin with the holiness of God.

So what do we mean when we say that God is holy?

Pink devoted an entire chapter to this in The Attributes of God, and he began by noting that holiness is not merely one attribute among many. It is, in a sense, the attribute that qualifies all the others. God's love is a holy love. God's justice is a holy justice. God's power is a holy power. Holiness is not something God has — it is something God is. It is the very essence of His being.

The Hebrew word for holy — qadosh — carries the idea of being set apart, cut off, utterly other. And this is the first thing we must understand about God's holiness: it means He is different. Radically, fundamentally, infinitely different from everything He has made. We are creatures; He is Creator. We are finite; He is infinite. We are stained by sin; He is absolutely, perfectly, eternally pure.

Pink wrote that God's holiness is the perfection of perfections. Every other attribute of God is perfected by His holiness. His power is not raw, unbridled force — it is holy power, always exercised in perfect righteousness. His love is not sentimental indulgence — it is holy love, which cannot and will not turn a blind eye to sin. His mercy is not soft permissiveness — it is holy mercy, which finds a way to forgive without compromising justice.

And this is where so many of us go wrong in our thinking about God. We want a God who is loving but not holy. A God who accepts but never judges. A God who forgives but never requires repentance. A God who is, frankly, safe. But the God of Scripture is not safe. He is good — profoundly, wonderfully, overwhelmingly good — but He is not safe. He is a consuming fire. He dwells in unapproachable light. The seraphim cover their faces in His presence — and they are without sin.

How much more, then, should we tremble?

Proverbs 9:10 calls this "the fear of the Lord." And that word — fear — is one we've domesticated in the modern church. We've turned it into mere "reverence" or "respect," as if it simply means being polite in God's presence. But the biblical concept of the fear of the Lord is far deeper than that. It includes reverence, yes. But it also includes genuine awe. A holy dread. The overwhelming sense that you are standing before a Being of such transcendent purity that your very existence feels presumptuous.

Isaiah felt it. Moses felt it at the burning bush — "Take off your shoes, for the place where you stand is holy ground." Peter felt it when he fell at Jesus' feet and said, "Depart from me, for I am a sinful man, O Lord." Job felt it after his encounter with God: "I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear: but now mine eye seeth thee. Wherefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes."

Do you see the pattern? Every genuine encounter with the holiness of God produces the same response: a profound awareness of one's own sinfulness. Not self-esteem. Not confidence. Brokenness. Because you cannot stand in the light and fail to see the dirt.

And this is why Pink argued that the holiness of God is the most neglected doctrine in the modern church. Because we don't like how it makes us feel. We don't like being broken. We don't like being told we're sinful. We want a Christianity that builds us up, that affirms us, that makes us feel good about ourselves. And there is a place for encouragement in the Christian life, certainly. But it must be built on the foundation of truth. And the truth is that apart from the grace of God, we are utterly, hopelessly, desperately unholy.

Pink put it starkly. He said that the natural man has no love for the holiness of God. In fact, the natural man hates it. Because God's holiness is the standard that condemns him. It is the light that exposes him. It is the truth that strips away every pretense and leaves him standing naked before the throne. And rather than submit to that exposure, the natural man will invent a different god — a god made in his own image, a god without holiness, a god who asks nothing and demands nothing and judges nothing.

But that god... doesn't exist. The real God — the God who actually sits on the throne of the universe — is holy. And we must deal with Him as He is, not as we wish Him to be.

Now — and this is crucial — the holiness of God is not only a doctrine of judgment. It is also a doctrine of hope. Because the same God who is infinitely holy is also infinitely gracious. And the cross of Jesus Christ is the place where holiness and grace meet. At the cross, God did not lower His standard. He did not say, "Well, I suppose holiness isn't that important after all." No — He upheld His standard perfectly. Every sin was punished. Every transgression was atoned for. Justice was fully satisfied. But it was satisfied in the person of His Son, who bore the penalty that we deserved.

This is what makes the gospel so breathtaking. God did not choose between holiness and love. He expressed both — fully, simultaneously, eternally — at Calvary. The holy God poured out His wrath against sin. The loving God poured out that wrath upon Himself, in the person of His Son. And now, through faith in Christ, the sinner can stand before the holy God... forgiven. Cleansed. Clothed not in his own filthy rags but in the perfect righteousness of Jesus.

Pink wrote with deep emotion about this. He said that the more we understand God's holiness, the more we will marvel at His grace. Because grace means nothing apart from holiness. If God is not truly holy, then forgiveness costs Him nothing. But because He is infinitely holy — because every sin is an infinite offense against His infinite purity — the fact that He forgives at all is staggering. The fact that He forgives through the sacrifice of His own Son is beyond comprehension.

But the holiness of God doesn't only affect our justification — our legal standing before God. It also shapes our sanctification — our daily growth in Christlikeness. Because the command of Scripture is clear: "Be ye holy; for I am holy."

God's holiness is not just a standard He holds over us in judgment. It is the goal toward which He is conforming us by His Spirit. And this process — sanctification — is the work of a lifetime. It involves the daily putting to death of sin, the daily cultivation of virtue, the daily bending of our stubborn wills toward the will of the Holy One.

Pink, in Studies in the Scriptures, described sanctification as the progressive recovery of the image of God in the soul of the believer. We were made in God's image. That image was marred — terribly, tragically marred — by the Fall. And now, through the work of the Spirit, that image is being restored. Slowly. Painfully, at times. But surely. Because the God who began a good work in us is faithful to complete it.

And this is where Proverbs 9:10 comes full circle. "The fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom: and the knowledge of the holy is understanding." When you truly know the Holy One — when you've glimpsed even a fraction of His blinding purity — everything else comes into focus. You understand why sin is so serious. You understand why the cross was necessary. You understand why obedience matters. You understand why holiness is not an optional add-on to the Christian life but the very heart of it.

So let me ask you... When was the last time you were genuinely awed by God's holiness? Not just intellectually aware of it. Not just theologically informed about it. Genuinely awed. Genuinely broken. Genuinely moved to worship not because the music was good, but because the God you were worshipping is terrifyingly, beautifully, incomprehensibly holy?

If it's been a while... I understand. We live in a world that trivializes everything. A world of casual faith and comfortable Christianity. A world where God is a buddy, a life coach, a cosmic therapist. And into that world, the doctrine of God's holiness falls like a thunderbolt. It disrupts everything. It upends every casual assumption. It tells us that the God we worship is not tame. He is not manageable. He is not our peer. He is the Holy One of Israel. And to know Him is the beginning — the very foundation — of all wisdom and understanding.

Let me leave you with a practical encouragement. This week, set aside time — even fifteen minutes — to meditate on the holiness of God. Read Isaiah 6. Read Revelation 4. Read Exodus 3, the burning bush. Don't rush through these passages. Sit with them. Let them work on your heart. Ask the Spirit to give you a fresh vision of who God really is — not the comfortable god of your imagination, but the consuming fire of Scripture. And as He reveals His holiness to you, let it drive you to two places: to the cross, where your sin has been dealt with... and to your knees, where your sanctification continues.

Let's pray.

Holy, holy, holy Lord God Almighty. We confess that we have tamed You in our minds. We have made You comfortable, manageable, safe. Forgive us. Open our eyes to see You as You truly are — the God before whom angels hide their faces. Break our casualness. Shatter our complacency. And then, having broken us, heal us with the gospel of Your Son, who bore our unholiness so that we might share in Yours. Make us holy, Lord, as You are holy. Not because we can achieve it in our own strength, but because Your Spirit is at work within us, conforming us to the image of Christ. We worship You in reverent awe. In Jesus' name. Amen.
