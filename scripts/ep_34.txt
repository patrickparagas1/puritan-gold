PURITAN GOLD -- Study Devotional, Day 3
Series: Romans
"The Impartial Judge"
God's righteous judgment and the law written on every heart


Good evening. There is no shortcut to spiritual depth. It comes through sustained, careful engagement with God's Word. That is what we are doing together in this study series, and tonight we take the next step.

We are studying the book of Romans together. Tonight's lesson is titled "The Impartial Judge." Each session builds on what came before, so if you have been following along, you will see how tonight's passage connects to the larger argument of the book.


Let us read our passage for tonight. It comes from Romans 2:1-16:

"Therefore thou art inexcusable, O man, whosoever thou art that judgest: for wherein thou judgest another, thou condemnest thyself; for thou that judgest doest the same things. But we are sure that the judgment of God is according to truth against them which commit such things... Or despisest thou the riches of his goodness and forbearance and longsuffering; not knowing that the goodness of God leadeth thee to repentance? But after thy hardness and impenitent heart treasurest up unto thyself wrath against the day of wrath and the revelation of the righteous judgment of God; Who will render to every man according to his deeds... For when the Gentiles, which have not the law, do by nature the things contained in the law, these, having not the law, are a law unto themselves: Which shew the work of the law written in their hearts, their conscience also bearing witness, and their thoughts the mean while accusing or else excusing one another."

Read that passage again to yourself. Mark any words or phrases that stand out. The Puritans read with a pen in hand, always ready to note what God was showing them.


The Puritans often identified a single verse as the hinge of a passage. Tonight, that verse is this:

"Or despisest thou the riches of his goodness and forbearance and longsuffering; not knowing that the goodness of God leadeth thee to repentance?" -- Romans 2:4

This verse is the lens through which we will examine the rest of tonight's passage. Keep it in the front of your mind as we proceed.


Let us unfold the meaning of this passage together.

Charles Hodge offers valuable commentary on this passage. The Puritans were expositors of the highest order, and their insights remain remarkably fresh.

Charles Hodge, in his magisterial commentary on Romans, gave careful attention to the rhetorical turn Paul executes at the beginning of chapter 2. Having condemned the pagan Gentile world in chapter 1, Paul now turns on the moralist — particularly the Jewish moralist — who was nodding in agreement at the indictment. Hodge noted that Paul's 'Therefore thou art inexcusable, O man, whosoever thou art that judgest' is a devastating stroke because it catches the self-righteous in their own trap. They condemn others for the very sins they themselves commit, perhaps in different or more respectable forms.

Hodge gave particular attention to verses 4-5, arguing that the goodness of God, far from being license for continued sin, is meant to produce repentance. When sinners presume upon God's patience, interpreting His delay in judgment as indifference to their sin, they are 'treasuring up wrath.' This is a terrifying image — every day of unrepentance adds to the weight of coming judgment, like a man stockpiling combustible material in a building already on fire.

On the crucial passage about Gentiles who 'do by nature the things contained in the law' (verses 14-15), Hodge was careful to note that Paul is not teaching that Gentiles can be saved by keeping the natural law. Rather, Paul is demonstrating that God has not left any human being without moral witness. The conscience bears testimony to the reality of God's moral law, even among those who have never heard the Scriptures. This universal moral awareness renders all humanity accountable. Hodge emphasized that conscience is not infallible — it can be seared, distorted, or suppressed — but its very existence testifies to a moral Lawgiver who has stamped His requirements on the human heart.

Romans 2 contains one of the most masterful rhetorical maneuvers in all of literature. Paul has just finished his devastating indictment of pagan Gentile sin in chapter 1. He can almost hear the Jewish reader (or the moral Gentile) saying 'Amen!' to the condemnation of those idolatrous pagans. Then Paul wheels around with a devastating 'Therefore thou art inexcusable, O man, whosoever thou art that judgest.' The finger that was pointing at others now points at the moralist himself.

This is a pattern we see throughout Scripture. Nathan told David a story about a rich man who stole a poor man's lamb, and when David's righteous anger flared, Nathan declared, 'Thou art the man!' (2 Samuel 12:7). Jesus told the Pharisees stories that made them condemn themselves. Paul employs the same strategy here. The person who judges others reveals that they know the moral law — and yet they violate it themselves. 'For thou that judgest doest the same things.' Perhaps not the same outward acts of gross idolatry, but the same inward sins of pride, covetousness, ingratitude, and self-worship.

Verse 2 establishes a critical principle: 'the judgment of God is according to truth.' God does not judge by appearances, by social standing, by religious heritage, or by comparison with others. He judges according to truth — according to reality, according to what actually is. This means no one can hide behind a facade of respectability. God sees the heart (1 Samuel 16:7).

In verses 3-4, Paul asks a penetrating question: 'And thinkest thou this, O man, that judgest them which do such things, and doest the same, that thou shalt escape the judgment of God?' The moralist imagines that because he can identify sin in others, he is exempt from judgment himself. This is self-deception of the highest order. Then comes the dagger of verse 4: 'Or despisest thou the riches of his goodness and forbearance and longsuffering; not knowing that the goodness of God leadeth thee to repentance?' God's patience is not permission. His forbearance is not approval. Every moment of grace that God extends to an unrepentant sinner is meant to draw them to repentance, not to confirm them in their sin.

Peter echoes this same truth: 'The Lord is not slack concerning his promise, as some men count slackness; but is longsuffering to us-ward, not willing that any should perish, but that all should come to repentance' (2 Peter 3:9). When we experience God's patience, we face a choice: will we respond with repentance and gratitude, or will we presume upon His kindness and harden our hearts further?

Verse 5 presents the terrifying alternative: 'But after thy hardness and impenitent heart treasurest up unto thyself wrath against the day of wrath.' The image is of someone making daily deposits into an account of judgment. Every unrepented sin, every presumption upon grace, every self-righteous thought adds to the treasury of wrath that will be opened on the day of judgment. This is what Edwards meant in his famous sermon when he spoke of the bow of God's wrath bent and the arrow made ready.

Verses 6-11 establish the principle that God 'will render to every man according to his deeds.' This is not a contradiction of justification by faith, which Paul will establish in chapters 3-5. Rather, it establishes the standard of divine judgment: perfect righteousness. To those who 'by patient continuance in well doing seek for glory and honour and immortality' — God will give eternal life. To those who 'are contentious, and do not obey the truth, but obey unrighteousness' — indignation and wrath. The point is that no one, Jew or Gentile, meets this standard. God's judgment is absolutely impartial: 'For there is no respect of persons with God' (verse 11).

This principle of impartiality is crucial. The Jewish reader might assume that possession of the law guaranteed favor with God. But Paul demolishes this assumption in verses 12-13: 'For not the hearers of the law are just before God, but the doers of the law shall be justified.' James makes the same point: 'Be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving your own selves' (James 1:22). Mere possession of Scripture, mere church attendance, mere religious knowledge — none of these justify a person before God.

The remarkable passage in verses 14-16 discusses Gentiles who 'do by nature the things contained in the law.' Paul is not suggesting that Gentiles can earn salvation by following their conscience. Rather, he is establishing that God has written His moral law on every human heart. The conscience — that inner faculty that accuses or excuses — bears witness to a moral order that transcends culture and circumstance. This is what theologians call natural law. Even those who have never opened a Bible know that murder is wrong, that lying is wrong, that stealing is wrong. This universal moral awareness is evidence of God's common grace and renders all humanity accountable.

The conscience, of course, is not a perfect guide. It can be trained wrongly, seared by habitual sin (1 Timothy 4:2), or warped by cultural conditioning. But its very existence testifies to God's reality. As C.S. Lewis argued, the universal human sense of 'ought' points beyond itself to a moral Lawgiver.

Verse 16 concludes with a reference to final judgment: 'In the day when God shall judge the secrets of men by Jesus Christ according to my gospel.' Notice: God will judge secrets — not just outward behavior but hidden motives, private thoughts, secret desires. And He will do so 'by Jesus Christ.' The Father has committed all judgment to the Son (John 5:22). The one who died to save sinners is the same one before whom all sinners must stand. This makes the offer of the gospel all the more urgent. The Judge is offering pardon — but the offer will not last forever.


Study without application is like a seed without soil. Let us now plant what we have learned in the ground of daily life.

This passage strips away every hiding place of self-righteousness. Here are practical steps for applying its truth.

First, stop comparing yourself to others. The moralist's fundamental error is measuring himself against other sinners rather than against God's perfect standard. When you catch yourself thinking 'at least I am not as bad as...' recognize this as the very self-deception Paul condemns. The standard is not other people but the holy God who judges according to truth. Let your comparison always be with Christ, not with your neighbor.

Second, respond rightly to God's patience. Are there sins you have been nursing because God has not yet brought consequences? Do not despise the riches of His goodness. His patience is not permission; it is an invitation to repent. Today, identify one area where you have been presuming on God's grace, and turn from it. Confess it specifically: 'Lord, I have taken Your patience as license. I repent.'

Third, let your knowledge produce obedience, not pride. If you are a person of biblical knowledge — if you can quote verses, explain theology, teach others — this passage is especially for you. 'Not the hearers of the law are just before God, but the doers.' Knowledge without obedience is not just useless; it increases condemnation. Take one truth you have learned recently and find a concrete way to obey it this week.

Fourth, cultivate sensitivity of conscience. Since God has written His law on your heart, tend to that inner witness carefully. When your conscience pricks you, do not suppress it; listen to it. Confess sin quickly. Keep short accounts with God. A tender conscience is one of the greatest gifts a Christian can possess.

Write down one concrete action you will take this week in response to tonight's study. Do not let this truth remain theoretical. The Puritans measured the success of a sermon not by how much the hearer learned but by how much the hearer changed.


Righteous Judge, we confess that we have often stood in judgment of others while harboring the same sins in our own hearts. Forgive our hypocrisy and self-righteousness. We thank You for the riches of Your goodness, forbearance, and longsuffering. Lead us to genuine repentance. Sensitize our consciences to Your truth. Make us doers of Your Word, not hearers only. Search us, O God, and know our hearts; try us and know our thoughts, and see if there be any wicked way in us, and lead us in the way everlasting. Through Christ our righteous Judge, Amen.

Continue to meditate on tonight's passage. The more you return to it, the more it will reveal.
